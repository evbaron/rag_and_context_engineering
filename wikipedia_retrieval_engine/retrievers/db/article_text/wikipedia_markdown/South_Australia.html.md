**South Australia** (commonly abbreviated as **SA**) is a [state](/wiki/States_and_territories_of_Australia) in the southern central part of [Australia](/wiki/Australia). With a total land area of 984,314 square kilometres (380,046 sq mi), it is the fourth-largest of Australia's states and territories by area, which includes some of the most [arid](/wiki/Arid) parts of the continent. With 1.9 million people as of June 2025, it is the [17th-largest subdivision in the world](/wiki/List_of_first-level_administrative_divisions_by_area) and the fifth-largest in [Australasia](/wiki/Australasia). It is the second-most highly centralised state in the nation after [Western Australia](/wiki/Western_Australia), with more than 67% of South Australians living in the capital [Adelaide](/wiki/Adelaide) or its environs. Other population centres in the state are relatively small; with [Mount Gambier](/wiki/Mount_Gambier) being the second-largest centre, with a population of 26,878.

South Australia shares borders with all the other mainland states. It is bordered to the west by [Western Australia](/wiki/Western_Australia), to the north by the [Northern Territory](/wiki/Northern_Territory), to the north-east by [Queensland](/wiki/Queensland), to the east by [New South Wales](/wiki/New_South_Wales), and to the south-east by [Victoria](/wiki/Victoria_(state)). To the south, its border is the ocean, the [Great Australian Bight](/wiki/Great_Australian_Bight).

The state comprises less than 6.7% of the Australian population and ranks fifth in population among the six states and two territories. The majority of its people reside in greater Metropolitan Adelaide. Most of the remainder are settled in fertile areas along the south-eastern coast and [River Murray](/wiki/Murray_River). The state's colonial origins are unique in Australia as [a freely settled, planned British province](/wiki/British_Province_of_South_Australia), rather than as a [convict](/wiki/Convictism_in_Australia) settlement. Colonial government commenced on 28 December 1836, when the members of the council were sworn in near the [Old Gum Tree](/wiki/Old_Gum_Tree).

As with the rest of the continent, the region has a long history of [human occupation](/wiki/Aboriginal_Australians) by numerous groups and languages. The [South Australian Company](/wiki/South_Australian_Company) established a temporary settlement at [Kingscote](/wiki/Kingscote,_South_Australia), [Kangaroo Island](/wiki/Kangaroo_Island), on 26 July 1836, five months before Adelaide was founded. The guiding principle behind settlement was that of *systematic colonisation*, a theory espoused by [Edward Gibbon Wakefield](/wiki/Edward_Gibbon_Wakefield) that was later employed by the [New Zealand Company](/wiki/New_Zealand_Company). The goal was to establish the province as a centre of civilisation for free immigrants, promising civil liberties and religious tolerance. Although its history has been marked by periods of economic hardship, South Australia has remained politically innovative and culturally vibrant. Today, it is known for its fine wine and numerous cultural festivals. The state's economy is dominated by the agricultural, manufacturing and mining industries.

## History

Main article: [History of South Australia](/wiki/History_of_South_Australia)

European settlers with Aboriginal Australians, 1850

Evidence of human activity in South Australia dates back as far as 20,000 years, with [flint](/wiki/Flint) mining activity and rock art in the [Koonalda Cave](/wiki/Koonalda_Cave) on the [Nullarbor Plain](/wiki/Nullarbor_Plain). In addition wooden spears and tools were made in an area now covered in [peat](/wiki/Peat) [bog](/wiki/Bog) in the South East. [Kangaroo Island](/wiki/Kangaroo_Island) was inhabited long before the island was cut off by [rising sea levels](/wiki/Sea_level_rise). According to [mitochondrial DNA](/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA) research, Aboriginal people reached [Eyre Peninsula](/wiki/Eyre_Peninsula) 49,000-45,000 years ago from both the east (clockwise, along the coast, from northern Australia) and the west (anti-clockwise).

The first recorded European sighting of the South Australian coast was in 1627 when the Dutch ship the *Gulden Zeepaert*, captained by [François Thijssen](/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Thijssen), examined and mapped a section of the coastline as far east as the [Nuyts Archipelago](/wiki/Nuyts_Archipelago). Thijssen named the whole of the country eastward of the Leeuwin "Nuyts Land", after a distinguished passenger on board; the Hon. [Pieter Nuyts](/wiki/Pieter_Nuyts)].

The coastline of South Australia was first mapped by [Matthew Flinders](/wiki/Matthew_Flinders) and [Nicolas Baudin](/wiki/Nicolas_Baudin) in 1802, excepting the inlet later named the [Port Adelaide River](/wiki/Port_River) which was first discovered in 1831 by Captain [Collet Barker](/wiki/Collet_Barker) and later accurately charted in 1836–37 by Colonel [William Light](/wiki/William_Light), leader of the South Australian Colonisation Commissioners' 'First Expedition' and first Surveyor-General of South Australia.

The land which now forms the state of South Australia was claimed for Britain in 1788 as part of the colony of [New South Wales](/wiki/New_South_Wales). Although the new colony included almost two-thirds of the continent, early settlements were all on the eastern coast and only a few intrepid explorers ventured this far west. It took more than forty years before any serious proposal to establish settlements in the south-western portion of New South Wales were put forward.

On 15 August 1834, the [British Parliament](/wiki/British_Parliament) passed the [South Australia Act 1834](/wiki/South_Australia_Act_1834) (*Foundation Act*), which empowered His Majesty to erect and establish a province or provinces in southern Australia. The act stated that the land between 132° and 141° east longitude and from 26° south latitude to the southern ocean would be allotted to the intended colony, and it would be convict-free.

[Charles Sturt](/wiki/Charles_Sturt)'s expedition leaving Adelaide for central Australia, 1844

In contrast to the rest of Australia, *[terra nullius](/wiki/Terra_nullius)* did not apply to the new province. The [Letters Patent](/wiki/Letters_Patent), which used the enabling provisions of the South Australia Act 1834 to fix the boundaries of the Province of South Australia, provided that "nothing in those our Letters Patent shall affect or be construed to affect the rights of any Aboriginal Natives of the said Province to the actual occupation and enjoyment in their own Persons or in the Persons of their Descendants of any Lands therein now actually occupied or enjoyed by such Natives." Although the patent guaranteed land rights under force of law for the indigenous inhabitants, it was ignored by the South Australian Company authorities and squatters. Despite strong reference to the rights of the native population in the initial proclamation by the Governor, there were many conflicts and deaths in the [Australian Frontier Wars](/wiki/Australian_frontier_wars) in South Australia.

[Nicolas Baudin](/wiki/Nicolas_Baudin), who mapped the coastline of South Australia, along with [Matthew Flinders](/wiki/Matthew_Flinders)

Survey was required before settlement of the province, and the Colonisation Commissioners for South Australia appointed William Light as the leader of its 'First Expedition', tasked with examining 1500 miles of the South Australian coastline and selecting the best site for the capital, and with then planning and surveying the site of the city into one-acre Town Sections and its surrounds into 134-acre Country Sections.

Eager to commence the establishment of their whale and seal fisheries, the South Australian Company sought, and obtained, the Commissioners' permission to send Company ships to South Australia, in advance of the surveys and ahead of the Commissioners' colonists.

The company's settlement of seven vessels and 636 people was temporarily made at Kingscote on Kangaroo Island, until the official site of the capital was selected by William Light, where the City of Adelaide is currently located. The first [immigrants](/wiki/Immigration_to_Australia) arrived at [Holdfast Bay](/wiki/Holdfast_Bay) (near the present day [Glenelg](/wiki/Glenelg,_South_Australia)) in November 1836.

The commencement of colonial government was proclaimed on 28 December 1836, now known as [Proclamation Day](/wiki/Proclamation_Day_(South_Australia)).

South Australia is the only Australian state to have never received British convicts. However, some emancipated or escaped convicts or expirees made their own way there, and constituted 1–2% of the early population. The plan for the province was that it would be an experiment in reform, addressing the problems perceived in British society. There was to be religious freedom and no established religion. Sales of land to colonists created an Emigration Fund to pay the costs of transferring a poor young labouring population to South Australia. In early 1838 the colonists became concerned after it was reported that convicts who had escaped from the eastern states might make their way to South Australia. The [South Australia Police](/wiki/South_Australia_Police) was formed in April 1838 to protect the community and enforce government regulations. Their principal role was to run the first temporary gaol, a two-room hut.

The current [flag of South Australia](/wiki/Flag_of_South_Australia) was adopted on 13 January 1904, and is a British blue ensign [defaced](/wiki/Defacement_(flag)) with the state badge. The badge is described as a [piping shrike](/wiki/Piping_shrike) with wings outstretched on a yellow disc. The state badge is believed to have been designed by Robert Craig of Adelaide's [School of Design](/wiki/School_of_Design_(Adelaide)).

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of South Australia](/wiki/Geography_of_South_Australia)

A satellite image of eastern South Australia. Note the dry lakes (white patches) in the north.

The terrain consists largely of arid and semi-arid rangelands, with several low [mountain ranges](/wiki/Mountain_range). The most important (but not tallest) is the [Mount Lofty](/wiki/Mount_Lofty_Ranges)-[Flinders Ranges](/wiki/Flinders_Ranges) system, which extends north about 800 kilometres (500 mi) from [Cape Jervis](/wiki/Cape_Jervis_(headland)) to the northern end of [Lake Torrens](/wiki/Lake_Torrens). The highest point in the state is not in those ranges; [Mount Woodroffe](/wiki/Mount_Woodroffe) (1,435 metres (4,708 ft)) is in the [Musgrave Ranges](/wiki/Musgrave_Ranges) in the extreme northwest of the state.

The south-western portion of the state consists of the sparsely inhabited [Nullarbor Plain](/wiki/Nullarbor_Plain), fronted by the cliffs of the [Great Australian Bight](/wiki/Great_Australian_Bight). Features of the coast include [Spencer Gulf](/wiki/Spencer_Gulf) and the [Eyre and Yorke Peninsulas](/wiki/Eyre_Yorke_Block) that surround it. The [Temperate Grassland of South Australia](/wiki/Temperate_Grassland_of_South_Australia) is situated to the east of [Gulf St Vincent](/wiki/Gulf_St_Vincent).

The [Barossa Valley](/wiki/Barossa_Valley_(wine)), northeast of Adelaide. South Australia's wine industry is the largest in Australia.

The different [Köppen climate types](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) in South Australia

The principal industries and exports of South Australia are wheat, wine and wool. More than half of Australia's wines are produced in the South Australian wine regions which principally include [Barossa Valley](/wiki/Barossa_Valley_(wine)), [Clare Valley](/wiki/Clare_Valley_wine_region), [McLaren Vale](/wiki/McLaren_Vale), [Coonawarra](/wiki/Coonawarra_wine_region), the [Riverland](/wiki/Riverland_wine_region) and the [Adelaide Hills](/wiki/Adelaide_Hills_wine_region). *See [South Australian wine](/wiki/South_Australian_wine).*

### South Australian boundaries

Further information: [South Australian borders](/wiki/South_Australian_borders)

South Australia has boundaries with every other Australian mainland state and territory except the [Australian Capital Territory](/wiki/Australian_Capital_Territory) and the [Jervis Bay Territory](/wiki/Jervis_Bay_Territory). The [Western Australia border](/wiki/Western_Australia_border) has a history involving the South Australian government astronomer, G.F. Dodwell, and the [Western Australian Government Astronomer](/wiki/Western_Australian_Government_Astronomer), [H.B. Curlewis](/wiki/Harold_Curlewis), marking the border on the ground in the 1920s.

In 1863, that part of New South Wales to the north of South Australia was annexed to South Australia, by letters patent, as the "Northern Territory of South Australia", which became shortened to the Northern Territory on [6 July 1863](/wiki/Territorial_evolution_of_Australia#6_July_1863). The Northern Territory was handed to the federal government in 1911 and became a separate territory.

According to Australian maps, South Australia's south coast is flanked by the [Southern Ocean](/wiki/Southern_Ocean), but official international consensus defines the Southern Ocean as extending north from the pole only to 60°S or 55°S, at least 17 degrees of latitude further south than the most southern point of South Australia. Thus the south coast is officially adjacent to the south-most portion of the Indian Ocean. *See [Southern Ocean: Existence and definitions](/wiki/Southern_Ocean#Existence_and_definitions)*.

### Climate

The southern part of the state has a [Mediterranean climate](/wiki/Mediterranean_climate), while the rest of the state has either an [arid](/wiki/Desert_climate) or [semi-arid climate](/wiki/Semi-arid_climate). South Australia's main temperature range is 29 °C (84 °F) in January and 15 °C (59 °F) in July.
The highest maximum temperature ever recorded was 50.7 °C (123.3 °F) at [Oodnadatta](/wiki/Oodnadatta,_South_Australia) on 2 January 1960, which is the highest official temperature recorded in Australia. The lowest minimum temperature was −8.2 °C (17.2 °F) at [Yongala](/wiki/Yongala,_South_Australia) on 20 July 1976. The region's overall dry weather is owed to the [Australian High](/wiki/Australian_High) on the Great Australian Bight.

## Economy

See also: [Economy of Australia](/wiki/Economy_of_Australia)

[Flinders Medical Centre](/wiki/Flinders_Medical_Centre). The health care sector is a major employer in South Australia's economy.

As of 2016, South Australia had 746,105 people employed out of a total workforce of 806,593, giving an unemployment rate of 7.5%. South Australia's largest employment sector is health care and social assistance, making up 14.8% of the state's total employment, followed by retail (10.7%), education and training (8.6%), manufacturing (8%), and construction (7.6%). South Australia's economy relies on exports more than any other state in Australia.

South Australia's [credit rating](/wiki/Credit_rating) was upgraded to AAA by [Standard & Poor's](/wiki/Standard_%26_Poor%27s) in September 2004 and to AAA by [Moody's](/wiki/Moody%27s) in November 2004, the highest credit ratings achievable by any company or sovereign. The state had previously lost these ratings in the State Bank collapse. However, in 2012 Standard & Poor's downgraded the state's credit rating to AA+ due to declining revenues, new spending initiatives and a weaker than expected budgetary outlook.

South Australia receives the least amount of federal funding for its local road network of all states on a per capita and a per kilometre basis.

Wheat fields at [Nuriootpa](/wiki/Nuriootpa,_South_Australia). Agriculture is a large industry for the state.

Sunflower crop in the Adelaide Hills

During 2019–20: South Australia's gross state product (GSP) fell 1.4% in chain volume (real) terms (nationally, gross domestic product (GDP) fell 0.3%). South Australia came out of the [COVID-19 recession](/wiki/COVID-19_recession) better than the other Australian states, with the economy growing by 3.9% in the 2020–21 financial year. This was the first time since the [Australian Bureau of Statistics](/wiki/Australian_Bureau_of_Statistics) began collecting data in 1990 that South Australia had outperformed the other states. The recovery was driven in part by growth in the agricultural sector, which increased its production by almost 24% thanks to the end of a drought.

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in South Australia](/wiki/Energy_in_South_Australia)

South Australia has the lead over other Australian states for its commercialisation and commitment to [renewable energy](/wiki/Renewable_energy). It is now the leading producer of [wind power](/wiki/Wind_power_in_South_Australia) in Australia. Renewable energy is a growing source of electricity in South Australia, and there is potential for growth from this particular industry of the state's economy. The [Hornsdale Power Reserve](/wiki/Hornsdale_Power_Reserve) is a bank of grid-connected batteries adjacent to the [Hornsdale Wind Farm](/wiki/Hornsdale_Wind_Farm) in South Australia's Mid-North region. At the time of construction in late 2017, it was billed as the largest lithium-ion battery in the world.

### Mining

The [Olympic Dam](/wiki/Olympic_Dam,_South_Australia) mine near [Roxby Downs](/wiki/Roxby_Downs,_South_Australia) in northern South Australia is the largest deposit of [uranium](/wiki/Uranium) in the world, possessing more than a third of the world's low-cost recoverable reserves and 70% of Australia's. The mine, owned and operated by [BHP](/wiki/BHP), presently accounts for 9% of global uranium production. The Olympic Dam mine is also the world's fourth-largest remaining copper deposit, and the world's fifth largest gold deposit. There was a proposal to vastly expand the operations of the mine, making it the largest open-cut mine in the world, but in 2012 the BHP Billiton board decided not to go ahead with it at that time due to then lower commodity prices.

The remote town of [Coober Pedy](/wiki/Coober_Pedy) produces more [opal](/wiki/Opal) than anywhere else in the world. Opal was first discovered near the town in 1915, and the town became the site of an opal rush, enticing immigrants from southern and eastern Europe in the aftermath of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II).

### Education and research

Higher education and research in Adelaide forms an important part of South Australia's economy. The [South Australian Government](/wiki/Government_of_South_Australia) and educational institutions have attempted to position Adelaide as *Australia's education hub* and have marketed it as a *Learning City*. The number of international students studying in Adelaide has increased rapidly in recent years to 30,726 in 2015, of which 1,824 were secondary school students. Foreign institutions have been attracted to set up campuses to increase its attractiveness as an education hub. Adelaide is the birthplace of three Nobel laureates, [more than any other Australian city](/wiki/List_of_Australian_Nobel_laureates#Australian_laureates_by_birthplace): physicist [William Lawrence Bragg](/wiki/William_Lawrence_Bragg) and pathologists [Howard Florey](/wiki/Howard_Florey) and [Robin Warren](/wiki/Robin_Warren), all of whom completed secondary and tertiary education at [St Peter's College](/wiki/St_Peter%27s_College,_Adelaide) and the [University of Adelaide](/wiki/University_of_Adelaide).

## Government

Main article: [Government of South Australia](/wiki/Government_of_South_Australia)

[Parliament House, Adelaide](/wiki/Parliament_House,_Adelaide)

South Australia is a [constitutional monarchy](/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy) with King [Charles III](/wiki/Charles_III) as sovereign, and the [Governor of South Australia](/wiki/Governor_of_South_Australia) as his representative. It is a state of the [Commonwealth of Australia](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Australia). The [bicameral](/wiki/Bicameral) [Parliament of South Australia](/wiki/Parliament_of_South_Australia) consists of the [lower house](/wiki/Lower_house) known as the [House of Assembly](/wiki/South_Australian_House_of_Assembly) and the [upper house](/wiki/Upper_house) known as the [Legislative Council](/wiki/South_Australian_Legislative_Council). [General elections](/wiki/South_Australian_state_elections) are held every four years, the last being the [2022 election](/wiki/2022_South_Australian_state_election).

Initially, the [Governor of South Australia](/wiki/Governor_of_South_Australia) held almost total power, derived from the letters patent of the imperial government to create the colony. He was accountable only to the British [Colonial Office](/wiki/Colonial_Office), and thus democracy did not exist in the colony. A new body was created to advise the governor on the administration of South Australia in 1843 called the Legislative Council. It consisted of three representatives of the British Government and four colonists appointed by the governor. The governor retained total executive power.

In 1851, the Imperial Parliament enacted the [Australian Colonies Government Act](/wiki/Australian_Colonies_Government_Act), which allowed for the election of representatives to each of the colonial legislatures and the drafting of a constitution to properly create representative and responsible government in South Australia. Later that year, propertied male colonists were allowed to vote for 16 members on a new 24 seat Legislative Council. Eight members continued to be appointed by the governor.

Old Parliament House in 1872

The main responsibility of this body was to draft a constitution for South Australia. The body drafted the most democratic constitution ever seen in the British Empire and provided for universal manhood [suffrage](/wiki/Suffrage). It created the bicameral [Parliament of South Australia](/wiki/Parliament_of_South_Australia). For the first time in the colony, the executive was elected by the people, and the colony used the [Westminster system](/wiki/Westminster_system), where the government is the party or coalition that exerts a majority in the House of Assembly. The Legislative Council remained a predominantly conservative chamber elected by property owners.

[Women's suffrage in Australia](/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_Australia) took a leap forward – [enacted in 1895](/wiki/Constitutional_Amendment_(Adult_Suffrage)_Act_1894) and taking effect from the [1896 colonial election](/wiki/1896_South_Australian_colonial_election), South Australia was the first government in Australia and only the second in the world after [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand) to allow women to vote, and the first in the world to allow women to stand for election. In 1897 [Catherine Helen Spence](/wiki/Catherine_Helen_Spence) was the first woman in Australia to be a candidate for political office when she was nominated to be one of South Australia's delegates to the conventions that drafted the constitution. South Australia became an original state of the Commonwealth of Australia on 1 January 1901.

Although the lower house had universal suffrage, the upper house, the Legislative Council, remained the exclusive domain of property owners until the [Labor](/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party) government of [Don Dunstan](/wiki/Don_Dunstan) managed to achieve reform of the chamber in 1973. Property qualifications were removed and the Council became a body elected via [proportional representation](/wiki/Proportional_representation) by a single state-wide electorate.
Since the following [1975 South Australian state election](/wiki/1975_South_Australian_state_election), no one party has had control of the state's upper house with the balance of power controlled by a variety of minor parties and independents.

### Local government

Further information: [Local government areas of South Australia](/wiki/Local_government_areas_of_South_Australia)

Local government in South Australia is established by the *Constitution Act 1934* (SA), the *Local Government Act 1999* (SA), and the *Local Government (Elections) Act 1999* (SA). South Australia contains [68 councils and 6 Aboriginal and outback communities](/wiki/Local_government_areas_of_South_Australia). Local councils, elected on a four-yearly basis, are responsible for local roads and stormwater management, waste collection, planning and development, fire prevention and hazard management, dog and cat management and control, parking control, public health and food inspections, and other services for their local communities. Councils have the power to raise revenue for their activities, which is mostly achieved through "council rates", a tax based on property valuations. Council rates make up about 70% of council revenue, but account for less than 4% of total taxes paid by Australians.

### Religion

At the [2016 census](/wiki/2016_Australian_census), overall 53.9% of responses identified some variant of [Christianity](/wiki/Christianity). 9% of respondents chose not to state a religion. The most commonly nominated responses were ['No Religion'](/wiki/Irreligion) (35.4%), [Catholicism](/wiki/Roman_Catholic) (18%), [Anglicanism](/wiki/Anglican_Church_of_Australia) (10%) and [Uniting Church](/wiki/Uniting_Church_in_Australia) (7.1%).

South Australia was the first Australian colony not to have an official state religion, and the colony became attractive to people who had experienced religious discrimination, including [Methodists](/wiki/Methodism) and [Unitarians](/wiki/Unitarianism). South Australia also had thousands of [Prussian](/wiki/Prussia) [Old Lutheran](/wiki/Old_Lutherans) immigrants, some of whom established their own form of Lutheranism. As a result, the [Lutheran Church of Australia](/wiki/Lutheran_Church_of_Australia) remains separate from the German Lutheran church to this day. South Australia was the location of the first Muslim mosque in Australia.

Most of the state's original colonists were Christian, but of many denominations, most with their own meeting place in the city square. Adelaide has been known as the "City of Churches" since at least 1868. Some of the oldest remaining buildings in the city are churches.

## Education

See also: [Education in South Australia](/wiki/Education_in_South_Australia)

### Primary and secondary

See also: [List of schools in South Australia](/wiki/List_of_schools_in_South_Australia)

On 1 January 2009, the school leaving age was raised to 17 (having previously been 15 and then 16). Education is compulsory for all children until age 17, unless they are working or undergoing other training. The majority of students stay on to complete their [South Australian Certificate of Education](/wiki/South_Australian_Certificate_of_Education) (SACE). School education is the responsibility of the South Australian government, but the public and private education systems are funded jointly by it and the [Commonwealth Government](/wiki/Government_of_Australia).

The South Australian Government provides, to schools on a per student basis, 89% of the total Government funding while the Commonwealth contributes 11%. Since the early 1970s, it has been an ongoing controversy that 68% of Commonwealth funding (increasing to 75% by 2008) goes to private schools that are attended by 32% of the states students. Private schools often counter this by saying that they receive less State Government funding than public schools, and in 2004 the main private school funding came from the Australian government, not the state government.

On 14 June 2013, South Australia became the third Australian state to sign up to the Australian Federal Government's [Gonski Reform Program](/wiki/Gonski_Report#Education). This will see funding for primary and secondary education to South Australia increased by $1.1 billion before 2019.

The academic year in South Australia generally runs from the end of January until mid-December for primary and secondary schools. The SA schools operate on a four-term basis. Schools are closed for the South Australia public holidays.

### Tertiary

[University of Adelaide](/wiki/University_of_Adelaide)

There are two public and two private universities in South Australia. The two public universities are [Adelaide University](/wiki/Adelaide_University)(merged in 2026 from the [University of Adelaide](/wiki/University_of_Adelaide) and [UniSA](/wiki/University_of_South_Australia)) and [Flinders University](/wiki/Flinders_University) (est. 1966). The two private universities are [Torrens University Australia](/wiki/Torrens_University_Australia) (est. 2013), and [CQUniversity](/wiki/CQUniversity_Australia)'s Adelaide Campus. All four have their main campus in the Adelaide metropolitan area: Adelaide University on [North Terrace](/wiki/North_Terrace,_Adelaide) in the [city](/wiki/Adelaide_city_centre); Flinders at [Bedford Park](/wiki/Bedford_Park,_South_Australia); Torrens University on Wakefield St in the city; and CQUniversity's Adelaide Campus in [Wayville](/wiki/Wayville,_South_Australia).

The University of Adelaide is part of the Group of Eight, a company of Australia's eight leading research universities. As of 2022, it is ranked by the Times Higher Education World University Rankings as one of the top 100 universities in the world. It was the first university in Australia to admit women to academic courses, doing so in 1881. In 2018, the University of Adelaide and the University of South Australia announced plans to merge, but these plans did not at that time come to fruition due in part to disagreements over what to name the new university and which of the university's vice-chancellors would become the vice-chancellor of the amalgamated university. The two institutions have since agreed merger terms.

There are also private higher education providers in Adelaide which offer tertiary degrees, such as [Tabor College](/wiki/Tabor_(Australia)), Millswood; and pathway colleges such as [Eynesbury College](/wiki/Eynesbury_College).

#### Vocational education

Main article: [TAFE South Australia](/wiki/TAFE_South_Australia)

Tertiary vocational education is provided by a range of Registered Training Organisations (RTOs) which are regulated at Commonwealth level. The range of RTOs delivering education include public, private and 'enterprise' providers i.e. employing organisations who run an RTO for their own employees or members.

The largest public provider of vocational education is [TAFE South Australia](/wiki/TAFE_South_Australia) which is made up of colleges throughout the state, many of these in rural areas, providing tertiary education to as many people as possible. In South Australia, TAFE is funded by the [state government](/wiki/Government_of_South_Australia) and run by the South Australian [Department of Further Education, Employment, Science and Technology](/wiki/Department_of_Further_Education,_Employment,_Science_and_Technology) (DFEEST). Each TAFE SA campus provides a range of courses with its own specialisation.

## Transport

Main article: [Transport in South Australia](/wiki/Transport_in_South_Australia)

Major highways in South Australia

### Historical transport in South Australia

After settlement, the major form of transport in South Australia was ocean transport. Limited land transport was provided by horses and bullocks. In the mid 19th century, the state began to develop a widespread rail network, although a coastal shipping network continued until the post war period.

Roads began to improve with the introduction of motor transport. By the late 19th century, road transport dominated internal transport in South Australia.

### Railway

South Australia has four interstate rail connections, to [Perth](/wiki/Perth) via the Nullarbor Plain, to [Darwin](/wiki/Darwin,_Northern_Territory) through the centre of the continent, to New South Wales through [Broken Hill](/wiki/Broken_Hill), and to [Melbourne](/wiki/Melbourne)–which is the closest capital city to Adelaide.

Rail transport was important for many mines in the north of the state.

The capital Adelaide has a commuter rail network made of electric and diesel electric powered multiple units, with 7 lines between them.

### Roads

[Eyre Highway](/wiki/Eyre_Highway) west of the [Nullarbor, South Australia](/wiki/Nullarbor,_South_Australia)

South Australia has extensive road networks linking towns and other states. Roads are also the most common form of transport within the major metropolitan areas with car transport predominating. Public [transport in Adelaide](/wiki/Transport_in_Adelaide) is mostly provided by buses and trams with regular services throughout the day.

### Air transport

[Adelaide Airport](/wiki/Adelaide_Airport) provides regular flights to other capitals, major South Australian towns and many international locations. The airport also has daily flights to several Asian hub airports. Adelaide Metro buses J1 and J1X connect to the city (approx. 30 minutes travel time), as well as other services to other parts of Adelaide. Standard fares apply and tickets may be purchased from a ticket machine at the airport bus stop. Maximum charge (September 2016) for Metroticket is $5.30; off-peak and seniors discounts may apply.

A ferry crossing the Murray River towards the town of [Waikerie, South Australia](/wiki/Waikerie,_South_Australia)

### River transport

The [River Murray](/wiki/River_Murray) was formerly an important trade route for South Australia, with [paddle steamers](/wiki/River_Murray_Navigation_Co.#Steaming_on_the_Murray_River) linking inland areas and the ocean at Goolwa.

### Sea transport

South Australia has a container port at [Port Adelaide](/wiki/Port_Adelaide). There are also numerous important ports along the coast for minerals and grains.

The passenger terminal at Port Adelaide intermittently plays host to an array of cruise liners.

Kangaroo Island is dependent on the Sea Link ferry service between [Cape Jervis](/wiki/Cape_Jervis_(headland)) and [Penneshaw](/wiki/Penneshaw).

## Cultural life

See also: [Adelaide § Cultural life](/wiki/Adelaide#Cultural_life), [Department of the Premier and Cabinet (South Australia) § Arts and culture](/wiki/Department_of_the_Premier_and_Cabinet_(South_Australia)#Arts_and_culture), and [List of festivals in Australia § South Australia](/wiki/List_of_festivals_in_Australia#South_Australia)

South Australia has been known as "the Festival State" for many years, for its abundance of arts and [gastronomic](/wiki/Gastronomic) festivals. While much of the arts scene is concentrated in Adelaide, the state government has supported regional arts actively since the 1990s. One of the manifestations of this was the creation of [Country Arts SA](/wiki/Country_Arts_SA), created in 1992.

[Diana Laidlaw](/wiki/Diana_Laidlaw) did much to further the arts in South Australia during her term as Arts Minister from 1993 to 2002, and after [Mike Rann](/wiki/Mike_Rann) assumed government in 2002, he created a strategic plan in 2004 (updated 2007) which included furthering and promoting the arts in South Australia under the topic heading "Objective 4:
Fostering Creativity and Innovation".

In September 2019, with the arts portfolio now subsumed within the [Department of the Premier and Cabinet](/wiki/Department_of_the_Premier_and_Cabinet_(South_Australia)) (DPC) after the election of [Steven Marshall](/wiki/Steven_Marshall) as Premier, and the 2004 strategic plan having been deleted from the website in 2018, the "*Arts and Culture Plan, South Australia 2019–2024*" was created by the department. Marshall said when launching the plan: "The arts sector in South Australia is already very strong but it's been operating without a plan for 20 years". However the plan does not signal any new government support, even after the government's A$31.9 million cuts to arts funding when [Arts South Australia](/wiki/Arts_South_Australia) was absorbed into DPC in 2018. Specific proposals within the plan include an "Adelaide in 100 Objects" walking tour, a new shared ticketing system for small to medium arts bodies, a five-year-plan to revitalise regional art centres, creation of an arts-focussed high school, and a new venue for the [Adelaide Symphony Orchestra](/wiki/Adelaide_Symphony_Orchestra).

South Australia has a [public library system](/wiki/Public_library), and [state library](/wiki/State_Library_of_South_Australia). Some [public libraries in South Australia](/wiki/List_of_libraries_in_South_Australia#Public_libraries) in rural areas are joint school and community libraries.

## Sport

Main article: [Sport in South Australia](/wiki/Sport_in_South_Australia)

### Australian rules football

Main article: [Australian rules football in South Australia](/wiki/Australian_rules_football_in_South_Australia)

The [Showdown](/wiki/Showdown_(AFL)), a [local derby](/wiki/Local_derby) between South Australia's two AFL teams [Adelaide](/wiki/Adelaide_Football_Club) and [Port Adelaide](/wiki/Port_Adelaide_Football_Club)

[Australian rules football](/wiki/Australian_rules_football) is the most popular spectator sport in South Australia. In 2006, South Australians had the highest attendance rate for the sport of any state, with 31% of South Australians attending a match in the previous twelve months. South Australia fields two teams in the [Australian Football League](/wiki/Australian_Football_League) (AFL) and [AFL Women's](/wiki/AFL_Women%27s) (AFLW): the [Adelaide Football Club](/wiki/Adelaide_Football_Club) and [Port Adelaide Football Club](/wiki/Port_Adelaide_Football_Club). The two teams have an intense rivalry called the [Showdown](/wiki/Showdown_(AFL)). The traditional home of Australian rules football in South Australia was [Football Park](/wiki/Football_Park) in the western suburb of [West Lakes](/wiki/West_Lakes,_South_Australia), which was the home ground of both AFL teams until 2013. Since 2014, both teams have used [Adelaide Oval](/wiki/Adelaide_Oval), near the city centre, as their home ground. In addition, it was chosen to host the inaugural 2023 '[Gather Round](/wiki/Gather_Round)' where all AFL matches are played in one state and has retained the event since.

The [South Australian National Football League](/wiki/South_Australian_National_Football_League) (SANFL), which was the premier league in the state before the advent of the Australian Football League, is a popular local league comprising ten teams: Sturt, Port Adelaide, Adelaide, West Adelaide, South Adelaide, North Adelaide, Norwood, Woodville/West Torrens, Glenelg and Central District.

The [Adelaide Footy League](/wiki/Adelaide_Footy_League) comprises 68 member clubs playing over 110 matches per week across ten senior divisions and three junior divisions. It is one of Australia's largest and strongest Australian rules football associations.

### Cricket

[Cricket](/wiki/Cricket) has a long history in South Australia, with the first matches being played in 1839. Regular club matches were organised following the establishment of the [South Australian Cricket Association](/wiki/South_Australian_Cricket_Association) (SACA) in 1871. Presently, SACA governs the [South Australian Premier Cricket](/wiki/South_Australian_Premier_Cricket) competition in addition to all forms of domestic cricket in the state. Cricket is the most popular summer sport in South Australia, with 39,000 club players at both junior and senior levels in 2023.

At the professional level, the [South Australia cricket team](/wiki/South_Australia_cricket_team) represent the state in men's competitions including the [Sheffield Shield](/wiki/Sheffield_Shield) and [One-Day Cup](/wiki/One-Day_Cup_(Australia)). The [South Australia women's cricket team](/wiki/South_Australia_women%27s_cricket_team) represent the state in the [Women's National Cricket League](/wiki/Women%27s_National_Cricket_League). South Australia also hosts the [Adelaide Strikers](/wiki/Adelaide_Strikers) in the [Twenty20](/wiki/Twenty20) [Big Bash League](/wiki/Big_Bash_League) and their [women's team](/wiki/Adelaide_Strikers_(WBBL)) in the [Women's Big Bash League](/wiki/Women%27s_Big_Bash_League). Matches are held at [Adelaide Oval](/wiki/Adelaide_Oval) and [Karen Rolton Oval](/wiki/Karen_Rolton_Oval) in the [Adelaide Parklands](/wiki/Adelaide_Parklands).

International cricket is also held in South Australia, with matches being played at Adelaide Oval. The venue was used to host matches in both the men's [1992 Cricket World Cup](/wiki/1992_Cricket_World_Cup) and [2015 Cricket World Cup](/wiki/2015_Cricket_World_Cup). Adelaide Oval also saw use during the [2022 ICC Men's T20 World Cup](/wiki/2022_ICC_Men%27s_T20_World_Cup). Outside of Adelaide, international cricket has also been played in [Berri, South Australia](/wiki/Berri,_South_Australia) with [Berri Oval](/wiki/Berri_Oval) hosting a singular match during the 1992 Cricket World Cup.

### Association football

[Adelaide United](/wiki/Adelaide_United_FC) represents South Australia in soccer in the men's [A-League](/wiki/A-League) and women's [W-League](/wiki/W-League_(Australia)). The club's home ground is [Hindmarsh Stadium](/wiki/Hindmarsh_Stadium) (Coopers Stadium), but previously played occasional games at Adelaide Oval.

The club was founded in 2003 and are the [2015–16 season](/wiki/2015%E2%80%9316_A-League) champions of the [A-League](/wiki/A-League). The club was also premier in the inaugural 2005–06 A-League season, finishing 7 points clear of the rest of the competition, before finishing 3rd in the finals. Adelaide United was also a Grand Finalist in the 2006–07 and 2008–09 seasons. Adelaide is the only A-League club to have progressed past the group stages of the [Asian Champions League](/wiki/Asian_Champions_League) on more than one occasion.

[Adelaide City](/wiki/Adelaide_City_FC) remains South Australia's most successful club, having won three [National Soccer League](/wiki/National_Soccer_League) titles and three [NSL Cups](/wiki/NSL_Cup). City was the first side from South Australia to ever win a continental title when it claimed the [1987 Oceania Club Championship](/wiki/1987_Oceania_Club_Championship) and it has also won a record 17 [South Australian championships](/wiki/National_Premier_Leagues_South_Australia) and 17 [Federation Cups](/wiki/FFSA_Federation_Cup).

[West Adelaide](/wiki/West_Adelaide_SC) became the first South Australian club to be crowned Australian champion when it won the [1978 National Soccer League](/wiki/1978_National_Soccer_League) title. Like City, it now competes in the [National Premier Leagues South Australia](/wiki/National_Premier_Leagues_South_Australia) and the two clubs contest the [Adelaide derby](/wiki/Adelaide_derby).

### Basketball

Basketball also has a big following in South Australia, with the [Adelaide 36ers](/wiki/Adelaide_36ers) playing in the [Adelaide Entertainment Centre](/wiki/Adelaide_Entertainment_Centre). The 36ers have won four championships in the last 20 years in the [National Basketball League](/wiki/National_Basketball_League_(Australia)). The [Adelaide Entertainment Centre](/wiki/Adelaide_Entertainment_Centre), located in [Hindmarsh](/wiki/Hindmarsh,_South_Australia), is the home of basketball in the state.

Mount Gambier also has a national basketball team – the Mount Gambier Pioneers. The Pioneers play at the Icehouse (Bern Bruning Basketball Stadium), which seats over 1,000 people and is also home to the Mount Gambier Basketball Association. The Pioneers won the South Conference in 2003 and the Final in 2003; this team was rated second in the top five teams to have ever played in the league. In 2012, the club entered its 25th season, with a roster of 10 senior players (two imports) and three development squad players.

### Motorsport

Australia's premier motorsport series, the [Supercars Championship](/wiki/Supercars_Championship), has visited South Australia each year since 1999. South Australia's Supercars event, the [Adelaide 500](/wiki/Adelaide_500), is staged on the [Adelaide Street Circuit](/wiki/Adelaide_Street_Circuit), a temporary track laid out through the streets and parklands to the east of the [Adelaide city centre](/wiki/Adelaide_city_centre). Attendance for the 2022 event totalled 258,200. An earlier version of the [Adelaide Street Circuit](/wiki/Adelaide_Street_Circuit) played host to the [Australian Grand Prix](/wiki/Australian_Grand_Prix), a round of the [FIA Formula One World Championship](/wiki/FIA_Formula_One_World_Championship), each year from 1985 to 1995.

[Mallala Motor Sport Park](/wiki/Mallala_Motor_Sport_Park), a permanent circuit located near the town of [Mallala](/wiki/Mallala,_South_Australia), 58 km north of Adelaide, caters for both state and national level motor sport throughout the year.

[The Bend Motorsport Park](/wiki/The_Bend_Motorsport_Park) is another permanent circuit, located just outside of [Tailem Bend](/wiki/Tailem_Bend,_South_Australia).

### Rugby league

Main article: [Rugby league in South Australia](/wiki/Rugby_league_in_South_Australia)

The state [rugby league](/wiki/Rugby_league) federation is the [South Australian Rugby League](/wiki/South_Australian_Rugby_League). The game traces its roots in the state back to the 1940s, when the Port Adelaide [rugby union](/wiki/Rugby_union) team split in four, and defected to [rugby league](/wiki/Rugby_league).

South Australia's only professional rugby league team, the [Adelaide Rams](/wiki/Adelaide_Rams), had a short but eventful existence. After debuting in the [Super League](/wiki/Super_League_(Australia)) competition in [1997](/wiki/1997_Super_League_(Australia)_season), in [1998](/wiki/National_Rugby_League_season_1998) they were selected to join the 20-team [National Rugby League](/wiki/National_Rugby_League); however they were to be axed from the 1999 season as part of a rationalisation of teams (from 20 to 14) in the competition.

At present however, the [South Australian Rugby League](/wiki/South_Australian_Rugby_League) still operates a local semi-professional competition consisting of both junior and adult teams from across Adelaide.

[Adelaide Oval](/wiki/Adelaide_Oval) hosted [State of Origin series](/wiki/State_of_Origin_series) games in both [2020](/wiki/2020_State_of_Origin_series) and [2023](/wiki/2023_State_of_Origin_series), with the latter drawing a state record rugby league attendance of 48,613.

## Crime

Main article: [Crime in South Australia](/wiki/Crime_in_South_Australia)

**Crime in South Australia** is managed by the [South Australia Police](/wiki/South_Australia_Police) (SAPOL), various state and federal courts in the [criminal justice system](/wiki/Judiciary_of_Australia) and the state [Department for Correctional Services](/wiki/Department_for_Correctional_Services_(South_Australia)), which administers the [prisons](/wiki/Punishment_in_Australia) and [remand centre](/wiki/Remand_centre).

Crime statistics for all categories of offence in the state are provided on the SAPOL website, in the form of rolling 12-month totals. Crime statistics from the 2017–18 national [ABS](/wiki/Australian_Bureau_of_Statistics) Crime Victimisation Survey show that between 2008–09 and 2017–18, the rate of victimisation in South Australia declined for assault and most household crime types.

In 2013, [Adelaide](/wiki/Adelaide) was ranked the safest capital city in Australia.
