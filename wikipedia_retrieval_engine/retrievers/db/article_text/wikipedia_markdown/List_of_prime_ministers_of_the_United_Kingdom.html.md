# List of prime ministers of the United Kingdom

[Robert Walpole](/wiki/Robert_Walpole) is considered the first prime minister of [Great Britain](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain).

[Winston Churchill](/wiki/Winston_Churchill) was prime minister during much of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II).

[Margaret Thatcher](/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher) was the first female prime minister.

[Keir Starmer](/wiki/Keir_Starmer) is the current prime minister.

The [prime minister of the United Kingdom](/wiki/Prime_minister_of_the_United_Kingdom) is the principal minister of the crown of [His Majesty's Government](/wiki/His_Majesty%27s_Government), and the head of the [British Cabinet](/wiki/British_Cabinet).

There is no specific date for when the office of prime minister first appeared, as the role was not created but rather evolved over time through a merger of duties. The term was regularly, if informally, used by [Robert Walpole](/wiki/Robert_Walpole) by the 1730s. It was used in the [House of Commons](/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_Great_Britain) as early as 1805, and it was certainly in parliamentary use by the 1880s, although did not become the official title until 1905, when [Henry Campbell-Bannerman](/wiki/Henry_Campbell-Bannerman) was prime minister.

Historians generally consider [Robert Walpole](/wiki/Robert_Walpole), who led the government of the [Kingdom of Great Britain](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain) for over twenty years from 1721, to be the first prime minister. Walpole is also the longest-serving British prime minister by this definition. The first prime minister of the [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland](/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland) was [William Pitt the Younger](/wiki/William_Pitt_the_Younger) at its creation on 1 January 1801. The first to use the title in an official act was [Benjamin Disraeli](/wiki/Benjamin_Disraeli) who signed the [1878 Treaty of Berlin](/wiki/1878_Treaty_of_Berlin) as "Prime Minister of Her Britannic Majesty".

In 1905, the post of prime minister was officially given recognition in the [order of precedence](/wiki/Orders_of_precedence_in_the_United_Kingdom), with the incumbent [Henry Campbell-Bannerman](/wiki/Henry_Campbell-Bannerman) the first officially referred to as "prime minister". The first prime minister of the current [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland](/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Northern_Ireland) upon its creation in 1922 (when 26 Irish counties seceded and created the [Irish Free State](/wiki/History_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland)) was [Andrew Bonar Law](/wiki/Andrew_Bonar_Law), although the country was not renamed officially until 1927, when [Stanley Baldwin](/wiki/Stanley_Baldwin) was the serving prime minister.

The current prime minister is [Keir Starmer](/wiki/Keir_Starmer), who assumed the office on 5 July 2024.

## Before the Kingdom of Great Britain

Before the [Union of England and Scotland](/wiki/Union_of_England_and_Scotland) in 1707, the [Treasury](/wiki/HM_Treasury) of [England](/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) was led by the [Lord High Treasurer](/wiki/Lord_High_Treasurer). By the late [Tudor period](/wiki/Tudor_period), the Lord High Treasurer was regarded as one of the [Great Officers of State](/wiki/Great_Officers_of_State), and was often (though not always) the dominant figure in government: [Edward Seymour, 1st Duke of Somerset](/wiki/Edward_Seymour,_1st_Duke_of_Somerset) (lord high treasurer, 1547–1549), served as [lord protector](/wiki/Lord_protector) to his young nephew King [Edward VI](/wiki/Edward_VI); [William Cecil, 1st Baron Burghley](/wiki/William_Cecil,_1st_Baron_Burghley) (lord high treasurer, 1572–1598), was the [dominant minister](/wiki/List_of_ministers_to_Elizabeth_I) to Queen [Elizabeth I](/wiki/Elizabeth_I); Burghley's son [Robert Cecil, 1st Earl of Salisbury](/wiki/Robert_Cecil,_1st_Earl_of_Salisbury), succeeded his father as [Chief Minister](/wiki/List_of_English_chief_ministers) to Elizabeth (1598–1603) and was eventually appointed by King [James I](/wiki/James_VI_and_I) as lord high treasurer (1608–1612).

By the late [Stuart period](/wiki/Stuart_period), the Treasury was often run not by a single individual (i.e., the lord high treasurer) but by a [commission](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/commission#Noun) of [lords of the Treasury](/wiki/Lords_of_the_Treasury), led by the [first lord of the Treasury](/wiki/First_lord_of_the_Treasury). The last lords high treasurer, [Sidney Godolphin, 1st Earl of Godolphin](/wiki/Sidney_Godolphin,_1st_Earl_of_Godolphin) (1702–1710) and [Robert Harley, 1st Earl of Oxford](/wiki/Robert_Harley,_1st_Earl_of_Oxford) (1711–1714), ran the government of [Queen Anne](/wiki/Anne,_Queen_of_Great_Britain).

## From 1707 to 1721

Following the succession of [George I](/wiki/George_I_of_Great_Britain) in 1714, the arrangement of a commission of lords of the Treasury (as opposed to a single lord high treasurer) became permanent. For the next three years, the government was headed by [Charles Townshend, 2nd Viscount Townshend](/wiki/Charles_Townshend,_2nd_Viscount_Townshend), who was appointed [Secretary of State for the Northern Department](/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_the_Northern_Department). Subsequently, Lords [Stanhope](/wiki/James_Stanhope,_1st_Earl_Stanhope) and [Sunderland](/wiki/Charles_Spencer,_3rd_Earl_of_Sunderland) ran the government jointly, with Stanhope managing foreign affairs and Sunderland domestic. Stanhope died in February 1721 and Sunderland resigned two months later; Townshend and [Robert Walpole](/wiki/Robert_Walpole) were then invited to form the next government. From that point, the holder of the *office* of first lord also usually (albeit unofficially) held the status of prime minister. It was not until the [Edwardian era](/wiki/Edwardian_era) that the title *[prime minister](/wiki/Prime_minister)* was constitutionally recognised. The prime minister still holds the office of first lord by constitutional convention, the only exceptions being the [Earl of Chatham](/wiki/William_Pitt,_1st_Earl_of_Chatham) and the [Marquess of Salisbury](/wiki/Robert_Gascoyne-Cecil,_3rd_Marquess_of_Salisbury).

Currently, there are eight living former prime ministers of the United Kingdom. The most recent death of a former prime minister was that of [Margaret Thatcher](/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher) (1979–1990) on 8 April 2013.

## Disputed prime ministers

Due to the gradual evolution of the post of prime minister, the title is applied to early prime ministers only retrospectively; this has sometimes given rise to academic dispute. [William Pulteney, 1st Earl of Bath](/wiki/William_Pulteney,_1st_Earl_of_Bath), and [James Waldegrave, 2nd Earl Waldegrave](/wiki/James_Waldegrave,_2nd_Earl_Waldegrave), are sometimes listed as prime ministers. Bath was invited to [form a ministry](/wiki/Short-lived_ministry) by [George II](/wiki/George_II_of_Great_Britain) when [Henry Pelham](/wiki/Henry_Pelham) resigned in 1746, as was Waldegrave in 1757 after the dismissal of [William Pitt the Elder](/wiki/William_Pitt_the_Elder), who dominated the affairs of government [during the Seven Years' War](/wiki/Great_Britain_in_the_Seven_Years%27_War). Neither was able to command sufficient parliamentary support to form a government; Bath stepped down after two days and Waldegrave after four. Modern academic consensus does not consider either man to have held office as prime minister; they are therefore listed separately.
