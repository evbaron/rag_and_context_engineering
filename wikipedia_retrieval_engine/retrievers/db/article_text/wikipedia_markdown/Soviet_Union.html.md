The **Soviet Union**, officially the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics** (**USSR**), was a [transcontinental country](/wiki/List_of_former_transcontinental_countries#Since_1700) that spanned much of [Eurasia](/wiki/Eurasia) from 1922 until [its dissolution](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) in 1991. It was the world's [third-most populous country](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population), the [largest by area](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area), and [bordered twelve countries](/wiki/Geography_of_the_Soviet_Union#Borders_and_neighbors). A diverse [multinational state](/wiki/Multinational_state), it was organized as a [federal union](/wiki/Federal_union) of [national republics](/wiki/Republics_of_the_Soviet_Union), with the largest and most populous being the [Russian SFSR](/wiki/Russian_SFSR). In practice, its [government](/wiki/Government_of_the_Soviet_Union) and [economy](/wiki/Economy_of_the_Soviet_Union) were [highly centralized](/wiki/Soviet-type_economic_planning). As a [one-party state](/wiki/One-party_state) governed by the [Communist Party](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), it was the flagship [communist state](/wiki/Communist_state). Its capital and largest city was [Moscow](/wiki/Moscow).

The Soviet Union's roots lay in the [October Revolution](/wiki/October_Revolution) of 1917. The new government, led by [Vladimir Lenin](/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin), established the Russian SFSR, the world's first constitutionally [communist state](/wiki/Communist_state). Following the [Bolshevik](/wiki/Bolsheviks) victory in the [Russian Civil War](/wiki/Russian_Civil_War), the Russian SFSR and its subordinate republics were [merged into the Soviet Union](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Creation_of_the_Union_of_Soviet_Socialist_Republics) in 1922. Following [Lenin's death](/wiki/Lenin%27s_death) in 1924, [Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) came to power, initiating [rapid industrialization](/wiki/Industrialization_in_the_Soviet_Union) and [forced collectivization](/wiki/Collectivization_in_the_Soviet_Union) that led to significant growth but contributed to [a devastating famine](/wiki/Soviet_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933) in the 1930s that killed millions. The [Soviet forced labour camp](/wiki/Forced_labor_in_the_Soviet_Union) system, known as the [Gulag](/wiki/Gulag), was expanded. During the late 1930s, Stalin's government conducted the [Great Purge](/wiki/Great_Purge) to eliminate opposition, resulting in [deportations](/wiki/Population_transfer_in_the_Soviet_Union), [executions](/wiki/Great_Purge#Number_of_people_executed), and the [Moscow trials](/wiki/Moscow_trials). Failing to build an [anti-Nazi coalition](/wiki/Popular_front#Comintern_policy:_1934–1939) in Europe, the Soviet Union signed [a non-aggression pact](/wiki/Molotov%E2%80%93Ribbentrop_Pact) with [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany) in 1939. However, in 1941, Germany [invaded the Soviet Union](/wiki/Invaded_the_Soviet_Union) in the largest land invasion in history, opening the [Eastern Front of World War II](/wiki/Eastern_Front_of_World_War_II). The Soviets played a decisive role in defeating the [Axis powers](/wiki/Axis_powers) as part of the [Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II), while liberating much of [Central and Eastern Europe](/wiki/Central_and_Eastern_Europe). With around [27 million casualties](/wiki/World_War_II_casualties_of_the_Soviet_Union), it suffered the [most deaths of any country in World War II](/wiki/World_War_II_casualties). In the [war's aftermath](/wiki/Aftermath_of_World_War_II), the Soviet Union consolidated the [territories occupied](/wiki/Military_occupations_by_the_Soviet_Union#After_the_Soviet_Union_entered_the_war_on_the_Allied_side) by the [Red Army](/wiki/Red_Army) into [satellite states](/wiki/Soviet_satellite_states) and undertook [rapid economic development](/wiki/Post%E2%80%93World_War_II_economic_expansion#Soviet_Union), cementing its status as a [superpower](/wiki/Superpower).

Geopolitical tensions [with the United States](/wiki/Soviet_Union%E2%80%93United_States_relations) led to the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War). The US-led [Western Bloc](/wiki/Western_Bloc) coalesced into the [NATO](/wiki/NATO) military alliance in 1949, prompting the [Eastern Bloc](/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) to form the [Warsaw Pact](/wiki/Warsaw_Pact) in 1955. With [little direct combat](/wiki/Air-to-air_combat_losses_between_the_Soviet_Union_and_the_United_States), the blocs engaged in [ideological](/wiki/Cultural_imperialism) and [proxy wars](/wiki/Proxy_war). In 1953, following [Stalin's death](/wiki/Stalin%27s_death), [Nikita Khrushchev](/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev) initiated a campaign of [de-Stalinization](/wiki/De-Stalinization), which led to [ideological tensions](/wiki/Sino-Soviet_split#Onset_of_the_disputes) with [communist China](/wiki/History_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China), under [Mao Zedong](/wiki/Mao_Zedong), and culminated in [an acrimonious split](/wiki/Sino-Soviet_split). The [Soviet military](/wiki/Soviet_Armed_Forces) suppressed [uprisings in East Germany](/wiki/East_German_uprising_of_1953), [Hungary](/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956) and [Czechoslovakia](/wiki/Warsaw_Pact_invasion_of_Czechoslovakia), while the resolution of the [Cuban Missile Crisis](/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis) narrowly averted [a global conflict](/wiki/World_War_III). Under [Leonid Brezhnev](/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev), prosperity [shifted toward stagnation](/wiki/Era_of_Stagnation), although [relations with the US eased](/wiki/D%C3%A9tente). In 1985, [Mikhail Gorbachev](/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev) sought reform through his policies of *[glasnost](/wiki/Glasnost)* and *[perestroika](/wiki/Perestroika)*. In 1989, most Warsaw Pact countries [overthrew their Soviet-backed regimes](/wiki/Revolutions_of_1989), effectively ending the Eastern Bloc. [Nationalist](/wiki/Nationalist) movements across the Soviet republics [declared sovereignty](/wiki/Parade_of_sovereignties), and in 1991, a [successful referendum](/wiki/1991_Soviet_Union_referendum) to establish a [renewed federation](/wiki/New_Union_Treaty) was followed by a [failed coup](/wiki/1991_Soviet_coup_attempt) by [hardliners](/wiki/State_Committee_on_the_State_of_Emergency). This prompted Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus [to secede](/wiki/Belovezha_Accords). On 26 December, Gorbachev officially recognized the dissolution of the Soviet Union. [Boris Yeltsin](/wiki/Boris_Yeltsin), president of the Russian SFSR, oversaw its reconstitution into the [Russian Federation](/wiki/Russian_Federation), the [Soviet Union's successor state](/wiki/Succession,_continuity_and_legacy_of_the_USSR). All fifteen republics [became independent states](/wiki/Post-Soviet_states); all except the Baltic states joined the [Commonwealth of Independent States](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Independent_States). The post-Soviet states [experienced a humanitarian disaster](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union#Economic_decline,_hunger,_and_excess_mortality).

The Soviet Union was one of the world's two superpowers, with [hegemony in Eastern Europe](/wiki/Soviet_empire), [global diplomacy](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_the_Soviet_Union), ideological influence (particularly in the [Global South](/wiki/Global_South)), military might, economic strength, and [scientific and technological accomplishments](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_the_Soviet_Union). [Its space program](/wiki/Soviet_space_program) made [significant achievements](/wiki/List_of_spaceflight_records) in the [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race). It [had the world's second-largest economy](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_past_and_projected_GDP_(nominal)) and the largest [standing military](/wiki/Standing_army). As a [nuclear state](/wiki/Nuclear_state), it wielded the [largest arsenal of nuclear weapons in the world](/wiki/Russia_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction). As an Allied nation, it was a [founding member](/wiki/Member_states_of_the_United_Nations) of the [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) and one of [five permanent members](/wiki/Permanent_members_of_the_United_Nations_Security_Council) of its [Security Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council).

## Etymology

Main article: [Official names of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Official_names_of_the_Soviet_Union)

See also: [Names of Russia](/wiki/Names_of_Russia)

The word "*[soviet](/wiki/Soviet_(council))*" is derived from the [Russian](/wiki/Russian_language) word *sovet* (Russian: совет), meaning 'council', 'assembly', 'advice', ultimately deriving from the [proto-Slavic](/wiki/Proto-Slavic) verbal stem of \**vět-iti* ('to inform'), related to Slavic *věst* ('news') and English *wise*. The word *sovietnik* means 'councillor'. Some organizations in Russian history were called *council* (Russian: совет). In the [Russian Empire](/wiki/Russian_Empire), the [State Council](/wiki/State_Council_(Russian_Empire)), which functioned from 1810 to 1917, was referred to as a Council of Ministers.

The soviets as [workers' councils](/wiki/Workers%27_council) first appeared during the [1905 Russian Revolution](/wiki/1905_Russian_Revolution). Although they were quickly suppressed by the Imperial army, after the [February Revolution of 1917](/wiki/February_Revolution_of_1917), workers' and soldiers' soviets emerged throughout the country and shared power with the [Russian Provisional Government](/wiki/Russian_Provisional_Government). The [Bolsheviks](/wiki/Bolsheviks), led by [Vladimir Lenin](/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin), demanded that all power be transferred to the soviets, and gained support from the workers and soldiers. After the [October Revolution](/wiki/October_Revolution), in which they seized power from the Provisional Government in the name of the soviets, Lenin proclaimed the formation of the [Russian Socialist Federal Soviet Republic](/wiki/Russian_Socialist_Federal_Soviet_Republic) (RSFSR).

During the [Georgian Affair](/wiki/Georgian_Affair) of 1922, Lenin called for the Russian SFSR and other national soviet republics to form a greater union which he initially named as the Union of Soviet Republics of Europe and Asia (Russian: Союз Советских Республик Европы и Азии, romanized: *Soyuz Sovyetskikh Respublik Evropy i Azii*). [Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) initially resisted Lenin's proposal but ultimately accepted it, and with Lenin's agreement he changed the name to the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR), although all republics began as *socialist soviet* and did not change to the other order until [1936](/wiki/1936_Soviet_Constitution). In addition, in the regional languages of several republics, the word *council* or *conciliar* in the respective language was only quite late changed to an adaptation of the Russian *soviet* and never in others, e.g. [Ukrainian SSR](/wiki/Ukrainian_SSR).

СССР (in the Latin alphabet: *SSSR*) is the abbreviation of the Russian-language cognate of USSR, as written in [Cyrillic letters](/wiki/Cyrillic_letters). The soviets used this abbreviation so frequently that audiences worldwide became familiar with its meaning. After this, the most common Russian initialization is Союз ССР (transliteration: *Soyuz SSR*) which essentially translates to *Union of SSRs* in English. In addition, the Russian short form name Советский Союз (transliteration: *Sovyetsky Soyuz*, which literally means *Soviet Union*) is also commonly used, but only in its unabbreviated form. Since the start of the [Great Patriotic War](/wiki/Great_Patriotic_War) at the latest, abbreviating the Russian name of the Soviet Union as СС has been taboo, the reason being that СС as a Russian Cyrillic abbreviation is associated with the infamous *[Schutzstaffel](/wiki/Schutzstaffel)* of [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany), as *SS* is in English. One apparent exception was the Russian abbreviation of the [Communist Party of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), КПСС (transliteration: *KPSS*).

In English-language media, the state was referred to as the Soviet Union or the USSR. The Russian SFSR dominated the Soviet Union to such an extent that, for most of the Soviet Union's existence, it was colloquially, but incorrectly, referred to as **Russia**.

## History

Main article: [History of the Soviet Union](/wiki/History_of_the_Soviet_Union)

See also: [History of Russia](/wiki/History_of_Russia)

The history of the Soviet Union began with the ideals of the [Bolshevik Revolution](/wiki/Bolshevik_Revolution) and ended in dissolution amid economic collapse and political disintegration. Established in 1922 following the [Russian Civil War](/wiki/Russian_Civil_War), the Soviet Union became a one-party state under the [Communist Party](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union). Its early years under [Lenin](/wiki/Lenin) were marked by the implementation of socialist policies and the [New Economic Policy](/wiki/New_Economic_Policy) (NEP), which allowed for market-oriented reforms.

The rise of [Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) in the late 1920s ushered in an era of intense centralization and totalitarianism. Stalin's rule was characterized by the forced [collectivization of agriculture](/wiki/Collectivization_in_the_Soviet_Union), rapid [industrialization](/wiki/Industrialization_in_the_Soviet_Union), and the [Great Purge](/wiki/Great_Purge), which eliminated perceived enemies of the state. The Soviet Union, one of the [Big Four Allied powers](/wiki/Big_Four_(World_War_II)) alongside the United States, the United Kingdom, and China, played a crucial role in the [Allied victory](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) in [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II). It paid a tremendous human cost with millions of Soviet citizens dying in the conflict.

The Soviet Union emerged as one of the world's two superpowers, leading the [Eastern Bloc](/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) in opposition to the [Western Bloc](/wiki/Western_Bloc) during the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War). This period saw the USSR engage in an arms race, the [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race), and [proxy wars](/wiki/Proxy_wars) around the globe. The post-Stalin leadership, particularly under [Nikita Khrushchev](/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev), initiated a [de-Stalinization](/wiki/De-Stalinization) process, leading to a period of liberalization and relative openness known as the [Khrushchev Thaw](/wiki/Khrushchev_Thaw). However, the subsequent era under [Leonid Brezhnev](/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev), sometimes referred to as the [Era of Stagnation](/wiki/Era_of_Stagnation), was marked by economic decline, political corruption, and a rigid [gerontocracy](/wiki/Gerontocracy). Despite efforts to maintain the Soviet Union's superpower status, the economy struggled due to its centralized nature, technological backwardness, and inefficiencies. The vast military expenditures and burdens of maintaining the Eastern Bloc further strained the Soviet economy.

In the 1980s, [Mikhail Gorbachev](/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev)'s policies of *[glasnost](/wiki/Glasnost)* (openness) and *[perestroika](/wiki/Perestroika)* (restructuring) aimed to revitalize the Soviet system but instead accelerated its unraveling. Nationalist movements gained momentum across the [Soviet republics](/wiki/Soviet_republics) and the control of the Communist Party weakened. The failed [coup attempt](/wiki/1991_Soviet_coup_attempt) in August 1991 against Gorbachev by hardline communists hastened the [end of the Soviet Union](/wiki/End_of_the_Soviet_Union), which formally dissolved on 26 December 1991, ending nearly seven decades of Soviet rule.

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Geography_of_the_Soviet_Union)

See also: [Geography of Russia](/wiki/Geography_of_Russia)

With an area of 22,402,200 square kilometres (8,649,500 sq mi), the Soviet Union was the world's largest country, a status that is retained by the [Russian Federation](/wiki/Russian_Federation). Covering a sixth of Earth's land surface, its size was comparable to that of [North America](/wiki/North_America). Two other successor states are also very large — [Kazakhstan](/wiki/Kazakhstan) ranks among the top 10 countries by land area, and [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine) is the largest country entirely in Europe. The [European](/wiki/Europe) portion accounted for a quarter of the country's area and was the cultural and economic center. The eastern part in [Asia](/wiki/Asia) extended to the [Pacific Ocean](/wiki/Pacific_Ocean) to the east and [Afghanistan](/wiki/Democratic_Republic_of_Afghanistan) to the south, and, except some areas in [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia), was much less populous. It spanned over 10,000 kilometres (6,200 mi) east to west across 11 [time zones](/wiki/Time_zone), and over 7,200 kilometres (4,500 mi) north to south. It had five climate zones: [tundra](/wiki/Tundra), [taiga](/wiki/Taiga), [steppes](/wiki/Steppe), [desert](/wiki/Desert) and [mountains](/wiki/Mountain).

The USSR, like [Russia](/wiki/Russia), had the world's longest [border](/wiki/Border), measuring over 60,000 kilometres (37,000 mi), or 1+1⁄2 circumferences of Earth. Two-thirds of it was a [coastline](/wiki/Coast). The country bordered [Afghanistan](/wiki/Democratic_Republic_of_Afghanistan), the [People's Republic of China](/wiki/History_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China_(1976%E2%80%931989)), [Czechoslovakia](/wiki/Czechoslovak_Socialist_Republic), [Finland](/wiki/Finland), [Hungary](/wiki/Hungarian_People%27s_Republic), [Iran](/wiki/Pahlavi_Iran), [Mongolia](/wiki/Mongolian_People%27s_Republic), [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea), [Norway](/wiki/Norway), [Poland](/wiki/Polish_People%27s_Republic), [Romania](/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Romania), and [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey) from 1945 to 1991. The [Bering Strait](/wiki/Bering_Strait) separated the USSR from the United States, while the [La Pérouse Strait](/wiki/La_P%C3%A9rouse_Strait) separated it from Japan.

The country's highest mountain was Communism Peak (now [Ismoil Somoni Peak](/wiki/Ismoil_Somoni_Peak)) in [Tajikistan](/wiki/Tajik_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), at 7,495 metres (24,590 ft). The USSR also included most of the world's largest lakes; the [Caspian Sea](/wiki/Caspian_Sea) (shared with [Iran](/wiki/Iran)), and [Lake Baikal](/wiki/Lake_Baikal), the world's largest (by volume) and deepest freshwater lake that is also an internal body of water in Russia.

## Government and politics

Main articles: [Politics of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Politics_of_the_Soviet_Union) and [Ideology of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Ideology_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union)

The Soviet [communist state](/wiki/Communist_state) system was based on [unified state power](/wiki/Unified_state_power) and [democratic centralism](/wiki/Democratic_centralism). The [highest organ of state authority](/wiki/Highest_organ_of_state_authority), the [Supreme Soviet of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Supreme_Soviet_of_the_Soviet_Union), stood above all other state organs and worked under the leadership of the [Communist Party of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union). The executive organ of the state (synonymous with government), the [Council of Ministers](/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_of_the_Soviet_Union), was an internal organ of the All-Union Supreme Soviet.

### Communist Party

Main article: [Communist Party of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union)

Military parade on the [Red Square](/wiki/Red_Square) in Moscow, 7 November 1964

At the top of the Communist Party was the [Central Committee](/wiki/Central_Committee_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), elected at [Party Congresses](/wiki/Congress_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union) and Conferences. In turn, the Central Committee voted for a [Politburo](/wiki/Politburo_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union) (called the Presidium between 1952 and 1966), [Secretariat](/wiki/Secretariat_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union) and the [general secretary](/wiki/General_Secretary_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union) (First Secretary from 1953 to 1966), the *de facto* highest office in the Soviet Union. Depending on the degree of power consolidation, it was either the Politburo as a collective body or the General Secretary, who always was one of the Politburo members, that effectively led the party and the country (except for the period of the highly personalized authority of Stalin, exercised directly through his position in the Council of Ministers rather than the Politburo after 1941). They were not controlled by the general party membership, as the key principle of the party organization was [democratic centralism](/wiki/Democratic_centralism), demanding strict subordination to higher bodies, and elections went uncontested, endorsing the candidates proposed from above.

The Communist Party maintained its dominance over the state mainly through its control over the [system of appointments](/wiki/Nomenklatura). All senior government officials and most deputies of the Supreme Soviet were members of the CPSU. Of the party heads themselves, Stalin (1941–1953) and Khrushchev (1958–1964) were Premiers. Upon the forced retirement of Khrushchev, the party leader was prohibited from this kind of double membership, but the later General Secretaries for at least some part of their tenure occupied the mostly ceremonial position of [Chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet](/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Presidium_of_the_Supreme_Soviet), the nominal [head of state](/wiki/List_of_heads_of_state_of_the_Soviet_Union). The institutions at lower levels were overseen and at times supplanted by [primary party organizations](/wiki/Kraikom).

However, in practice the degree of control the party was able to exercise over the [state bureaucracy](/wiki/Soviet_bureaucracy), particularly after the death of Stalin, was far from total, with the bureaucracy pursuing different interests that were at times in conflict with the party, nor was the party itself monolithic from top to bottom, although [factions were officially banned](/wiki/Ban_on_factions_in_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union).

### Highest organ of state authority

The [Grand Kremlin Palace](/wiki/Grand_Kremlin_Palace), the seat of the [Supreme Soviet of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Supreme_Soviet_of_the_Soviet_Union), 1982

The Supreme Soviet (successor of the [Congress of Soviets](/wiki/Congress_of_Soviets_of_the_Soviet_Union)) was nominally the [highest organ of state authority](/wiki/Highest_organ_of_state_authority) for most of the Soviet history, at first acting as a rubber stamp institution, approving and implementing all decisions made by the party. However, its powers and functions were extended in the late 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s, including the creation of new state commissions and committees. It gained additional powers relating to the approval of the [Five-Year Plans](/wiki/Five-year_plans_for_the_national_economy_of_the_Soviet_Union) and the [government budget](/wiki/Government_budget). The Supreme Soviet elected a [Presidium](/wiki/Presidium_of_the_Supreme_Soviet) (successor of the [Central Executive Committee](/wiki/Central_Executive_Committee_of_the_Soviet_Union)) to wield its power between plenary sessions, ordinarily held twice a year, and appointed the [Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_Soviet_Union), the [Procurator General](/wiki/Procurator_General_of_the_Soviet_Union) and the [Council of Ministers](/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_of_the_Soviet_Union) (known before 1946 as the [Council of People's Commissars](/wiki/Council_of_People%27s_Commissars_of_the_Soviet_Union)), headed by the [Chairman](/wiki/Premier_of_the_Soviet_Union) (Premier) and managing an enormous bureaucracy responsible for the administration of the economy and society. State and party structures of the [constituent republics](/wiki/Republics_of_the_Soviet_Union) largely emulated the structure of the central institutions, although the Russian SFSR, unlike the other constituent republics, for most of its history had no republican branch of the CPSU, being ruled directly by the union-wide party until 1990. Local authorities were organized likewise into [party committees](/wiki/Raikom), [local Soviets](/wiki/Soviet_(council)) and [executive committees](/wiki/Executive_committee). While the state system was nominally federal, the party was unitary.

The state security police (the [KGB](/wiki/KGB) and [its predecessor agencies](/wiki/Template:Chronology_of_Soviet_secret_police_agencies)) played an important role in Soviet politics. It was instrumental in the [Red Terror](/wiki/Red_Terror) and [Great Purge](/wiki/Great_Purge), but was brought under strict party control after Stalin's death. Under [Yuri Andropov](/wiki/Yuri_Andropov), the KGB engaged in the suppression of political dissent and maintained an extensive network of informers, reasserting itself as a political actor to some extent independent of the party-state structure, culminating in the anti-corruption campaign targeting high-ranking party officials in the late 1970s and early 1980s.

### Unified power and reform

Main article: [Perestroika](/wiki/Perestroika)

Nationalist anti-government [riots in Dushanbe](/wiki/1990_Dushanbe_riots), [Tajikistan](/wiki/Tajik_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), 1990

The [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Soviet_Union), which was promulgated in [1924](/wiki/1924_Soviet_Constitution), [1936](/wiki/1936_Soviet_Constitution) and [1977](/wiki/1977_Soviet_Constitution), did not limit state power. No [separation of powers](/wiki/Separation_of_powers) existed in the Soviet Union, as the state system was based on the [unified state power](/wiki/Unified_power) of the [highest organ of state authority](/wiki/Supreme_state_organ_of_power), that is, the [All-Union Supreme Soviet](/wiki/Supreme_Soviet_of_the_Soviet_Union) which worked under the party's leadership. The system was governed less by statute than by informal conventions, and no settled mechanism of leadership succession existed. Bitter and at times deadly power struggles took place in the Politburo after the deaths of Lenin and Stalin, as well as after Khrushchev's dismissal, itself due to a decision by both the Politburo and the Central Committee. All leaders of the Communist Party before Gorbachev died in office, except [Georgy Malenkov](/wiki/Georgy_Malenkov) and Khrushchev, who were both dismissed from the party leadership amid internal struggle within the party.

Between 1988 and 1990, facing considerable opposition, [Mikhail Gorbachev](/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev) enacted reforms shifting power away from the highest bodies of the party and making the Supreme Soviet less dependent on them. The [Congress of People's Deputies](/wiki/Congress_of_People%27s_Deputies_of_the_Soviet_Union) was established, the majority of whose members were directly elected in competitive elections held in March 1989, the first in Soviet history. The Congress now elected the Supreme Soviet, which became a full-time parliament, and much stronger than before. For the first time since the 1920s, it refused to rubber stamp proposals from the party and Council of Ministers. In 1990, Gorbachev introduced and assumed the position of the [President of the Soviet Union](/wiki/President_of_the_Soviet_Union), concentrated power in his executive office, independent of the party, and subordinated the government, now renamed the [Cabinet of Ministers of the USSR](/wiki/Cabinet_of_Ministers_of_the_USSR), to himself.

Tensions grew between the Union-wide authorities under Gorbachev, reformists led in Russia by [Boris Yeltsin](/wiki/Boris_Yeltsin) and controlling the newly elected [Supreme Soviet of the Russian SFSR](/wiki/Supreme_Soviet_of_the_Russian_SFSR), and communist hardliners. On 19–21 August 1991, a group of hardliners staged a [coup attempt](/wiki/1991_Soviet_coup_attempt). The coup failed, and the [State Council of the Soviet Union](/wiki/State_Council_of_the_Soviet_Union) became the highest organ of state power 'in the period of transition'. Gorbachev resigned as General Secretary, only remaining President for the final months of the existence of the USSR.

### Judicial system

Main article: [Law of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Law_of_the_Soviet_Union)

See also: [Socialist law](/wiki/Socialist_law)

The judiciary was not independent of the [supreme state organ of power](/wiki/Supreme_state_organ_of_power), and the Supreme Court as the [supreme judicial organ](/wiki/Supreme_judicial_organ) supervised the lower courts ([People's Court](/wiki/People%27s_Court_(Soviet_Union))) and applied the law as established by the constitution or as interpreted by the Supreme Soviet. The Constitutional Oversight Committee reviewed the constitutionality of laws and acts. The Soviet Union used the [inquisitorial system](/wiki/Inquisitorial_system) of [Roman law](/wiki/Roman_law), where the judge, [procurator](/wiki/Procurator_General_of_the_Soviet_Union), and defence attorney collaborate to "establish the truth".

### Human rights

Main article: [Human rights in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Human_rights_in_the_Soviet_Union)

[Human rights](/wiki/Human_rights) in the Soviet Union were severely limited. The Soviet Union was a [totalitarian state](/wiki/Totalitarian_state) from [1927 until 1953](/wiki/History_of_the_Soviet_Union_(1927%E2%80%9353)) and a [one-party](/wiki/One-party) state until 1990. [Freedom of speech](/wiki/Freedom_of_speech) was suppressed and dissent was punished. Independent political activities were not tolerated, whether these involved participation in free [labour unions](/wiki/Labour_union), private [corporations](/wiki/Corporation), independent churches or opposition [political parties](/wiki/Political_parties). The [freedom of movement](/wiki/Freedom_of_movement) within and especially outside the country was limited. The state restricted rights of citizens to [private property](/wiki/Private_property).

The remainder of this section is an excerpt from [Human rights in the Soviet Union § Soviet concept of human rights and legal system](/wiki/Human_rights_in_the_Soviet_Union#Soviet_concept_of_human_rights_and_legal_system).[(https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Human_rights_in_the_Soviet_Union&action=edit)]

According to the [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights), [human rights](/wiki/Human_rights) are the "basic [rights](/wiki/Rights) and [freedoms](/wiki/Freedom_(political)) to which all humans are entitled." including the right to [life](/wiki/Life) and [liberty](/wiki/Liberty), [freedom of expression](/wiki/Freedom_of_speech), and [equality before the law](/wiki/Equality_before_the_law); and social, cultural and economic rights, including the right to participate in [culture](/wiki/Culture), the [right to food](/wiki/Right_to_food), the [right to work](/wiki/Right_to_work), and the right to [education](/wiki/Education).

The Soviet conception of human rights was very different from [international law](/wiki/International_human_rights_law). According to [Soviet legal theory](/wiki/Law_of_the_Soviet_Union), "it is the government who is the beneficiary of human rights which are to be asserted *against* the individual". The [Soviet state](/wiki/Soviet_state) was considered as the source of human rights. Therefore, the Soviet legal system considered [law](/wiki/Law) an arm of politics and it also considered courts agencies of the government. Extensive [extrajudicial powers](/wiki/Extrajudicial_punishment) were given to the [Soviet secret police agencies](/wiki/Chronology_of_Soviet_secret_police_agencies). In practice, the Soviet government significantly curbed the [rule of law](/wiki/Rule_of_law), [civil liberties](/wiki/Civil_liberties), [protection of law](/wiki/Criminal_justice) and [guarantees of property](/wiki/Property_rights), which were considered as examples of "bourgeois morality" by Soviet law theorists such as [Andrey Vyshinsky](/wiki/Andrey_Vyshinsky).

The USSR and other countries in the [Soviet Bloc](/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) had abstained from affirming the [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights) (1948), saying that it was "overly juridical" and potentially infringed on national sovereignty. The Soviet Union later signed legally-binding human rights documents, such as the [International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights](/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Civil_and_Political_Rights) in 1973 (and the 1966 [International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights](/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Economic,_Social_and_Cultural_Rights)), but they were neither widely known or accessible to people living under Communist rule, nor were they taken seriously by the Communist authorities. Under [Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin), the [death penalty](/wiki/Death_penalty) was extended to adolescents as young as 12 years old in 1935.

[Sergei Kovalev](/wiki/Sergei_Kovalev) recalled "the famous article 125 of the Constitution which enumerated all basic civil and political rights" in the Soviet Union. But when he and other prisoners attempted to use this as a legal basis for their abuse complaints, their prosecutor's argument was that "the Constitution was written not for you, but for American Negroes, so that they know how happy the lives of Soviet citizens are".

Crime was determined not as the infraction of law, instead, it was determined as any action which could threaten the Soviet state and society. For example, [a desire to make a profit](/wiki/Speculation) could be interpreted as a [counter-revolutionary activity](/wiki/Counter-revolutionary) punishable by death. [The liquidation and deportation of millions of peasants in 1928–31](/wiki/Dekulakization) was carried out within the terms of the Soviet Civil Code. Some Soviet legal scholars even said that "criminal repression" may be applied in the absence of guilt. [Martin Latsis](/wiki/Martin_Latsis), chief of [Soviet Ukraine](/wiki/Soviet_Ukraine)'s [secret police](/wiki/Cheka) explained: "Do not look in the file of incriminating evidence to see whether or not the accused rose up against the Soviets with arms or words. Ask him instead to which [class](/wiki/Social_class) he belongs, what is his background, his [education](/wiki/Education), his [profession](/wiki/Profession). These are the questions that will determine the fate of the accused. That is the meaning and essence of the [Red Terror](/wiki/Red_Terror)."

The purpose of [public trials](/wiki/Show_trial) was "not to demonstrate the existence or absence of a crime – that was predetermined by the appropriate [party authorities](/wiki/CPSU) – but to provide yet another forum for [political agitation and propaganda](/wiki/Soviet_propaganda) for the instruction of the citizenry (see [Moscow Trials](/wiki/Moscow_Trials) for example). Defense lawyers, who had to be [party members](/wiki/CPSU), were required to take their client's guilt for granted..."

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_the_Soviet_Union)

[Gerald Ford](/wiki/Gerald_Ford), [Andrei Gromyko](/wiki/Andrei_Gromyko), [Leonid Brezhnev](/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev) and [Henry Kissinger](/wiki/Henry_Kissinger) speaking informally at the [Vladivostok Summit](/wiki/Vladivostok_Summit) in 1974

Mikhail Gorbachev and [George H. W. Bush](/wiki/George_H._W._Bush) signing bilateral documents during Gorbachev's official visit to the United States in 1990

During his rule, Stalin always made the final policy decisions. Otherwise, Soviet foreign policy was set by the commission on the Foreign Policy of the Central Committee of the [Communist Party of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), or by the party's highest body the [Politburo](/wiki/Politburo_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union). Operations were handled by the separate [Ministry of Foreign Affairs](/wiki/Ministry_of_Foreign_Affairs_(Soviet_Union)). It was known as the People's Commissariat for Foreign Affairs (or Narkomindel), until 1946. The most influential spokesmen were [Georgy Chicherin](/wiki/Georgy_Chicherin), [Maxim Litvinov](/wiki/Maxim_Litvinov), [Vyacheslav Molotov](/wiki/Vyacheslav_Molotov), [Andrey Vyshinsky](/wiki/Andrey_Vyshinsky), and [Andrei Gromyko](/wiki/Andrei_Gromyko). Intellectuals were based in the [Moscow State Institute of International Relations](/wiki/Moscow_State_Institute_of_International_Relations).

- Comintern (1919–1943), or [Communist International](/wiki/Communist_International), was an international communist organization based in the Kremlin that advocated [world communism](/wiki/World_communism). The Comintern intended to 'struggle by all available means, including armed force, for the overthrow of the international bourgeoisie and the creation of an international Soviet republic as a transition stage to the complete abolition of the state'. It was abolished as a conciliatory measure toward Britain and the United States.
- [Comecon](/wiki/Comecon), the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance ([Russian](/wiki/Russian_language): Совет Экономической Взаимопомощи, *Sovet Ekonomicheskoy Vzaimopomoshchi*, СЭВ, *SEV*) was an economic organization from 1949 to 1991 under Soviet control that comprised the countries of the Eastern Bloc along with several communist states elsewhere in the world. Moscow was concerned about the [Marshall Plan](/wiki/Marshall_Plan), and Comecon was meant to prevent countries in the Soviets' sphere of influence from moving towards that of the Americans and Southeast Asia. Comecon was the Eastern Bloc's reply to the formation in Western Europe of the Organization for European Economic Co-Operation (OEEC),
- The [Warsaw Pact](/wiki/Warsaw_Pact) was a [collective defence](/wiki/Collective_defence) alliance formed in 1955 among the USSR and its [satellite states](/wiki/Satellite_state) in Eastern Europe during the Cold War. The Warsaw Pact was the military complement to the Comecon, the regional economic organization for the [socialist states](/wiki/Socialist_state_(communism)) of Central and Eastern Europe. The Warsaw Pact was created in reaction to the integration of [West Germany](/wiki/West_Germany) into [NATO](/wiki/NATO). Although nominally a "defensive" alliance, the Pact's primary function was to safeguard the [Soviet Union's hegemony](/wiki/Soviet_Empire) over its [Eastern European](/wiki/Soviet_Bloc) satellites, with the Pact's only direct military actions having been the invasions of its own member states to keep them from breaking away.
- The [Cominform](/wiki/Cominform) (1947–1956), informally the Communist Information Bureau and officially the Information Bureau of the Communist and Workers' Parties, was the first official agency of the international Marxist-Leninist movement since the dissolution of the Comintern in 1943. Its role was to coordinate actions between Marxist-Leninist parties under Soviet direction. Stalin used it to order Western European communist parties to abandon their exclusively parliamentarian line and instead concentrate on politically impeding the operations of the [Marshall Plan](/wiki/Marshall_Plan), the U.S. program of rebuilding Europe after the war and developing its economy. It also coordinated international aid to Marxist-Leninist insurgents during the Greek Civil War in 1947–1949. It expelled Yugoslavia in 1948 after [Josip Broz Tito](/wiki/Josip_Broz_Tito) insisted on an independent program. Its newspaper, *For a Lasting Peace, for a People's Democracy!*, promoted Stalin's positions. The Cominform's concentration on Europe meant a deemphasis on world revolution in Soviet foreign policy. By enunciating a uniform ideology, it allowed the constituent parties to focus on personalities rather than issues.

#### Early policies (1919–1939)

Further information: [International relations (1919–1939) § Soviet Union](/wiki/International_relations_(1919%E2%80%931939)#Soviet_Union)

Soviet propaganda poster denouncing "[social fascism](/wiki/Social_fascism)," 1932.

[Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) and [Joachim von Ribbentrop](/wiki/Joachim_von_Ribbentrop) exchanging a handshake after the signing of the [Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact](/wiki/Molotov%E2%80%93Ribbentrop_Pact) in August 1939.

The Marxist-Leninist leadership of the Soviet Union intensely debated foreign policy issues and changed directions several times. Even after Stalin assumed dictatorial control in the late 1920s, there were debates, and he frequently changed positions.

During the country's early period, it was assumed that Communist revolutions would break out soon in every major industrial country, and it was the Russian responsibility to assist them. The [Comintern](/wiki/Comintern) was the weapon of choice. A few revolutions did break out, but they were quickly suppressed (the longest lasting one was in Hungary)—the [Hungarian Soviet Republic](/wiki/Hungarian_Soviet_Republic)—lasted only from 21 March 1919 to 1 August 1919. The Russian Bolsheviks were in no position to give any help.

By 1921, Lenin, Trotsky, and Stalin realized that capitalism had stabilized itself in Europe and there would not be any widespread revolutions anytime soon. It became the duty of the Russian Bolsheviks to protect what they had in Russia, and avoid military confrontations that might destroy their bridgehead. Russia was now a pariah state, along with Germany. The two came to terms in 1922 with the [Treaty of Rapallo](/wiki/Treaty_of_Rapallo,_1922) that settled long-standing grievances. At the same time, the two countries secretly set up training programs for the illegal German army and air force operations at hidden camps in the USSR.

Moscow eventually stopped threatening other states, and instead worked to open peaceful relationships in terms of trade, and diplomatic recognition. The United Kingdom dismissed the warnings of [Winston Churchill](/wiki/Winston_Churchill) and a few others about a continuing Marxist-Leninist threat, and opened trade relations and *de facto* diplomatic recognition in 1922. There was hope for a settlement of the pre-war Tsarist debts, but it was repeatedly postponed. Formal recognition came when the new [Labour Party](/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK)) came to power in 1924. All the other countries followed suit in opening trade relations. [Henry Ford](/wiki/Henry_Ford) opened large-scale business relations with the Soviets in the late 1920s, hoping that it would lead to long-term peace. Finally, in 1933, the United States officially recognized the USSR, a decision backed by the public opinion and especially by US business interests that expected an opening of a new profitable market.

In the late 1920s and early 1930s, Stalin ordered Marxist-Leninist parties across the world to strongly oppose non-Marxist political parties, labour unions or other organizations on the left, which they labelled [social fascists](/wiki/Social_fascists). In the usage of the Soviet Union, and of the Comintern and its affiliated parties in this period, the epithet *[fascist](/wiki/Fascist_(insult))* was used to describe capitalist society in general and virtually any [anti-Soviet](/wiki/Anti-Soviet) or anti-Stalinist activity or opinion. Stalin reversed himself in 1934 with the [Popular Front](/wiki/Popular_Front) program that called on all Marxist parties to join with all [anti-Fascist](/wiki/Anti-Fascist) political, labour, and organizational forces that were opposed to [fascism](/wiki/Fascism), especially of the [Nazi](/wiki/Nazi) variety.

The rapid growth of power in Nazi Germany encouraged both Paris and Moscow to form a military alliance, and the [Franco-Soviet Treaty of Mutual Assistance](/wiki/Franco-Soviet_Treaty_of_Mutual_Assistance) was signed in May 1935. A firm believer in collective security, Stalin's foreign minister [Maxim Litvinov](/wiki/Maxim_Litvinov) worked very hard to form a closer relationship with France and Britain.

In 1939, half a year after the [Munich Agreement](/wiki/Munich_Agreement), the USSR attempted to form an anti-Nazi alliance with France and Britain. [Adolf Hitler](/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) proposed a better deal, which would give the USSR control over much of Eastern Europe through the [Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact](/wiki/Molotov%E2%80%93Ribbentrop_Pact). In September, Germany invaded Poland, and the USSR also invaded later that month, resulting in the partition of Poland. In response, Britain and France declared war on Germany, marking the beginning of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II).

#### World War II (1939–1945)

Main articles: [Causes of World War II](/wiki/Causes_of_World_War_II) and [Diplomatic history of World War II § Soviet Union](/wiki/Diplomatic_history_of_World_War_II#Soviet_Union)

Up until his death in 1953, [Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) controlled all foreign relations of the Soviet Union during the [interwar period](/wiki/Interwar_period). Despite the increasing build-up of [Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany)'s war machine and the outbreak of the [Second Sino-Japanese War](/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War), the Soviet Union did not cooperate with any other nation, choosing to follow its own path. However, after [Operation Barbarossa](/wiki/Operation_Barbarossa), the Soviet Union's priorities changed. Despite previous conflict with the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom), [Vyacheslav Molotov](/wiki/Vyacheslav_Molotov) dropped his post war border demands.

#### Cold War (1945–1991)

Main articles: [Origins of the Cold War](/wiki/Origins_of_the_Cold_War) and [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War)

The [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) was a period of [geopolitical](/wiki/Geopolitical) tension between the [United States](/wiki/United_States) and the Soviet Union and their respective allies, the [Western Bloc](/wiki/Western_Bloc) and the [Eastern Bloc](/wiki/Eastern_Bloc), which began following [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) in 1945. The term *[cold war](/wiki/Cold_war_(general_term))* is used because there was no large-scale fighting directly between the two [superpowers](/wiki/Superpowers), but they each supported major regional conflicts known as [proxy wars](/wiki/Proxy_war). The conflict was based around the ideological and geopolitical struggle for global influence by these two superpowers, following their temporary [alliance](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) and [victory](/wiki/Allied-occupied_Germany) against [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany) in 1945. Aside from the [nuclear arsenal development](/wiki/Nuclear_arms_race) and conventional military deployment, the struggle for dominance was expressed via indirect means such as [psychological warfare](/wiki/Psychological_warfare), propaganda campaigns, [espionage](/wiki/Cold_War_espionage), far-reaching [embargoes](/wiki/Economic_sanctions), rivalry at [sports events](/wiki/Politics_and_sports) and technological competitions such as the [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race).

### Administrative divisions

Structure of the Union of the Soviet Socialist Republics (1925)

Main articles: [Subdivisions of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Subdivisions_of_the_Soviet_Union), [Soviet republic (system of government)](/wiki/Soviet_republic_(system_of_government)), and [Republics of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Republics_of_the_Soviet_Union)

Constitutionally, the USSR was a federation of constituent Union Republics, which were either unitary states, such as [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukrainian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) or [Byelorussia](/wiki/Byelorussian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) (SSRs), or federations, such as [Russia](/wiki/Russian_Soviet_Federative_Socialist_Republic) or [Transcaucasia](/wiki/Transcaucasian_Socialist_Federative_Soviet_Republic) (SFSRs), all four being the founding republics who signed the [Treaty on the Creation of the USSR](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Creation_of_the_USSR) in December 1922. In 1924, during the [national delimitation](/wiki/National_delimitation_in_the_Soviet_Union) in Central Asia, [Uzbekistan](/wiki/Uzbek_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) and [Turkmenistan](/wiki/Turkmen_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) were formed from parts of Russia's [Turkestan ASSR](/wiki/Turkestan_ASSR) and two Soviet dependencies, the [Khorezm](/wiki/Khorezm_People%27s_Soviet_Republic) and [Bukharan PSPs](/wiki/Bukharan_People%27s_Soviet_Republic). In 1929, [Tajikistan](/wiki/Tajik_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) was split off from the Uzbekistan SSR. With the constitution of 1936, the Transcaucasian SFSR was dissolved, resulting in its constituent republics of [Armenia](/wiki/Armenian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), [Georgia](/wiki/Georgian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) and [Azerbaijan](/wiki/Azerbaijan_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) being elevated to Union Republics, while [Kazakhstan](/wiki/Kazakh_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) and [Kirghizia](/wiki/Kirghiz_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) were split off from the Russian SFSR, resulting in the same status. In August 1940, [Moldavia](/wiki/Moldavian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) was formed from parts of Ukraine and [Soviet-occupied Bessarabia](/wiki/Soviet_occupation_of_Bessarabia_and_northern_Bukovina), and Ukrainian SSR. [Estonia](/wiki/Estonian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), [Latvia](/wiki/Latvian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) and [Lithuania](/wiki/Lithuanian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic) were also [annexed by the Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_occupation_of_the_Baltic_states_(1940)) and turned into SSRs, which was [not recognized by most of the international community](/wiki/State_continuity_of_the_Baltic_states) and was considered an [illegal occupation](/wiki/Occupation_of_the_Baltic_states). After the [Soviet invasion of Finland](/wiki/Soviet_invasion_of_Finland), the [Karelo-Finnish SSR](/wiki/Karelo-Finnish_SSR) was formed on annexed territory as a Union Republic in March 1940 and then incorporated into Russia as the [Karelian ASSR](/wiki/Karelian_ASSR) in 1956. ## Military

Main article: [Soviet Armed Forces](/wiki/Soviet_Armed_Forces)

See also: [Red Army](/wiki/Red_Army), [Soviet Ground Forces](/wiki/Soviet_Ground_Forces), [Soviet Navy](/wiki/Soviet_Navy), [Soviet Air Forces](/wiki/Soviet_Air_Forces), [Lists of Heroes of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Lists_of_Heroes_of_the_Soviet_Union), and [Military history of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Military_history_of_the_Soviet_Union)

A medium-range [SS-20](/wiki/RSD-10) non-[ICBM](/wiki/ICBM) ballistic missile, the deployment of which by the Soviet Union in the late 1970s launched a new [arms race](/wiki/Arms_race) in Europe when NATO responded by deploying [Pershing II](/wiki/Pershing_II) missiles in [West Germany](/wiki/West_Germany), among other things

Under the Military Law of September 1925, the [Soviet Armed Forces](/wiki/Soviet_Armed_Forces) consisted of the [Land Forces](/wiki/Red_Army), the [Red Army Air Force](/wiki/Red_Army_Air_Force), the [Navy](/wiki/Soviet_Navy), [Joint State Political Directorate](/wiki/Joint_State_Political_Directorate) (OGPU) and the [Internal Troops](/wiki/Internal_Troops). The OGPU later became independent and in 1934 joined the [NKVD](/wiki/NKVD) secret police, and so its internal troops were under the joint leadership of the defense and internal commissariats. After World War II, [Strategic Missile Forces](/wiki/Strategic_Missile_Forces) (1959), [Air Defense Forces](/wiki/Soviet_Air_Defence_Forces) (1948) and National Civil Defense Forces (1970) were formed, which ranked first, third, and sixth in the official Soviet system of importance (ground forces were second, Air Force fourth, and Navy fifth).

The army had the greatest political influence. In 1989, there served two million soldiers divided between 150 motorized and 52 tank divisions. Until the early 1960s, the Soviet navy was a rather small military branch, but after the [Cuban Missile Crisis](/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis), under the leadership of [Sergei Gorshkov](/wiki/Sergei_Gorshkov), it expanded significantly. It became known for its submarine fleet. In 1989, there served 500,000 men. The [Soviet Air Force](/wiki/Soviet_Air_Force) focused on a fleet of [strategic bombers](/wiki/Strategic_bomber) and during war situation was to eradicate enemy infrastructure and nuclear capacity. The air force also had a number of [fighters](/wiki/Fighter_aircraft) and tactical bombers to support the army in the war. Strategic missile forces had more than 1,400 [intercontinental ballistic missiles](/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile) (ICBMs), deployed between 28 bases and 300 command centers.

After 1945, the Soviet Ground Forces suppressed several uprisings in East Europe and was involved in many other operations abroad. These included the suppression of the [uprising in East Germany](/wiki/East_German_uprising_of_1953) (1953), [Hungarian revolution](/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956) (1956) and the [invasion of Czechoslovakia](/wiki/Invasion_of_Czechoslovakia) (1968). The Soviet Union also began the [war in Afghanistan between 1979 and 1989](/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War).

In the Soviet Union, general [conscription](/wiki/Conscription) applied, meaning all able-bodied males aged 18 and older were drafted in the armed forces.

### Weapons of mass destruction

Main article: [Soviet Union and weapons of mass destruction](/wiki/Soviet_Union_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction)

By the end of the Cold War, the Soviet Armed Forces maintained the world's largest arsenals of [nuclear, chemical, and biological weapons](/wiki/Weapon_of_mass_destruction). As dissolution seemed imminent, the United States initiated the [Nunn–Lugar Cooperative Threat Reduction](/wiki/Nunn%E2%80%93Lugar_Cooperative_Threat_Reduction) program to dismantle much of this Soviet infrastructure and secure its personnel and materials.

The Soviet Union had the largest [nuclear weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon) arsenal ever, peaking at over 40,000 warheads in 1986. The country was the second to develop nuclear weapons, conducting its first test [RDS-1](/wiki/RDS-1) in 1949, four years after the US [Trinity](/wiki/Trinity_(nuclear_test)) test. Its primary nuclear testing sites were [Semipalatinsk](/wiki/Semipalatinsk_Test_Site), [Novaya Zemlya](/wiki/Novaya_Zemlya#Nuclear_testing), and [Kapustin Yar](/wiki/Kapustin_Yar). By 1991, it deployed over 10,000 [strategic nuclear weapons](/wiki/Strategic_nuclear_weapons) in its [nuclear triad](/wiki/Nuclear_triad): [Strategic Rocket Forces](/wiki/Strategic_Rocket_Forces)' silo-based and road-mobile [intercontinental ballistic missiles](/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile), the [Soviet Navy](/wiki/Soviet_Navy)'s [submarine-launched ballistic missiles](/wiki/Submarine-launched_ballistic_missile), and [Long Range Aviation](/wiki/Soviet_Long_Range_Aviation)'s [Tu-95MS](/wiki/Tu-95MS) and [Tu-160](/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-160) bombers. Another 11,000 [tactical nuclear weapons](/wiki/Tactical_nuclear_weapons) were assigned to land and naval tactical aircraft, missiles, [nuclear artillery](/wiki/Nuclear_artillery), and [anti-submarine weapons](/wiki/Anti-submarine_weapon).

The [Soviet chemical weapons program](/wiki/Soviet_chemical_weapons_program) became the largest in world history. Russia in 1993 declared 39,967 tons of [chemical weapons](/wiki/Chemical_weapons). The program produced [Novichok](/wiki/Novichok), [VR](/wiki/VR_(nerve_agent)), [sarin](/wiki/Sarin), and [soman](/wiki/Soman) [nerve agents](/wiki/Nerve_agent), as well as [lewisite](/wiki/Lewisite), [mustard](/wiki/Mustard_gas), and [phosgene](/wiki/Phosgene), and others. By comparison, 27,770 metric tons were declared for the [United States chemical weapons program](/wiki/United_States_chemical_weapons_program) in 1997. By the time of the Soviet Union's dissolution in 1991, its research institute, [GosNIIOKhT](/wiki/GosNIIOKhT), employed around 6,000 people across Russia and Uzbekistan. Novichok agents were designed to be undetectable and unprotectable by [NATO](/wiki/NATO) equipment, safer to handle, and circumvent the forthcoming [Chemical Weapons Convention](/wiki/Chemical_Weapons_Convention) list of controlled [precursors](/wiki/Precursor_(chemistry)).

The [Soviet biological weapons program](/wiki/Soviet_biological_weapons_program) was the world's largest, longest, and most sophisticated [biological warfare](/wiki/Biological_warfare) project. It weaponized and stockpiled the [biological agents](/wiki/Biological_agent) that cause [anthrax](/wiki/Anthrax), [plague](/wiki/Plague_(disease)), [tularemia](/wiki/Tularemia), [smallpox](/wiki/Smallpox), [botulism](/wiki/Botulism) and others. [Genetic engineering](/wiki/Genetic_engineering) improved agent stability and [antibiotic resistance](/wiki/Antibiotic_resistance). A civilian cover organization named *[Biopreparat](/wiki/Biopreparat)* operated 40 to 50 military-purposed biological research facilities. An [anti-agriculture](/wiki/Biological_warfare#Anti-agriculture) program, *[Ekologiya](/wiki/Ministry_of_Agriculture_and_Food_(Soviet_Union)#Role_in_offensive_Soviet_biological_warfare_programme)*, targeted crops and livestock. The program employed a peak of 65,000 people and annually produced, for example, 100 tons of smallpox. The [Sverdlovsk anthrax leak](/wiki/Sverdlovsk_anthrax_leak), which led to at least 68 deaths, began to reveal the extent of the program, continued by defectors including [Ken Alibek](/wiki/Ken_Alibek) and [Vladimir Pasechnik](/wiki/Vladimir_Pasechnik).

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Economy_of_the_Soviet_Union)

The Soviet Union adopted a [command economy](/wiki/Command_economy), whereby production and distribution of goods were centralized and directed by the government. For the overwhelming majority of its existence, the USSR did not use GDP or GNP to measure its economy, instead relying on the [Material Product System](/wiki/Material_Product_System). The first Bolshevik experience with a command economy was the policy of [war communism](/wiki/War_communism), which involved the nationalization of industry, centralized distribution of output, coercive or forced requisition of agricultural production, and attempts to eliminate money circulation, private enterprises and [free trade](/wiki/Free_trade). The [barrier troops](/wiki/Barrier_troops) were also used to enforce Bolshevik control over food supplies in areas controlled by the Red Army, a role which soon earned them the hatred of the Russian civilian population. After the severe economic collapse, Lenin replaced war [communism](/wiki/Communism) by the [New Economic Policy](/wiki/New_Economic_Policy) (NEP) in 1921, legalizing free trade and private ownership of small businesses. The economy steadily recovered as a result.

After a long debate among the members of the Politburo about the course of economic development, by 1928–1929, upon gaining control of the country, Stalin abandoned the NEP and pushed for full central planning, starting [forced collectivization of agriculture](/wiki/Collectivization_in_the_Soviet_Union) and enacting draconian labour legislation. Resources were mobilized for [rapid industrialization](/wiki/History_of_the_Soviet_Union_(1927%E2%80%9353)#Industrialization_in_practice), which significantly expanded Soviet capacity in heavy industry and capital goods during the 1930s. The primary motivation for industrialization was preparation for war, mostly due to distrust of the outside capitalist world. As a result, the USSR was transformed from a largely agrarian economy into a great industrial power, leading the way for its emergence as a superpower after [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II). The war caused extensive devastation of the Soviet economy and infrastructure, which required massive reconstruction.

The [DneproGES](/wiki/Dnieper_Hydroelectric_Station), one of many [hydroelectric](/wiki/Hydroelectric) power stations in the Soviet Union and a symbol of Soviet economic progress

By the early 1940s, the Soviet economy had become relatively [self-sufficient](/wiki/Autarky); for most of the period until the creation of [Comecon](/wiki/Comecon), only a tiny share of domestic products was traded internationally. After the creation of the Eastern Bloc, external trade rose rapidly. However, the influence of the [world economy](/wiki/World_economy) on the USSR was limited by fixed domestic prices and a state monopoly on [foreign trade](/wiki/Foreign_trade_of_the_Soviet_Union). Grain and sophisticated consumer manufactures became major import articles from around the 1960s. During the [arms race](/wiki/Arms_race) of the Cold War, the Soviet economy was burdened by military expenditures, heavily lobbied for by a powerful bureaucracy dependent on the arms industry. At the same time, the USSR became the largest arms exporter to the [Third World](/wiki/Third_World). A portion of Soviet resources during the Cold War were [allocated in aid](/wiki/International_relations_within_the_Comecon) to the Soviet-aligned states. The Soviet Union's [military budget](/wiki/Military_budget) in the 1970s was gigantic, forming 40–60% of the entire federal budget and accounting to 15% of the USSR's GDP (13% in the 1980s).

From the 1930s until its dissolution in late 1991, the way the Soviet economy operated remained essentially unchanged. The economy was formally directed by [central planning](/wiki/Economic_planning), carried out by [Gosplan](/wiki/Gosplan) and organized in [five-year plans](/wiki/Five-year_plans_for_the_national_economy_of_the_Soviet_Union). However, in practice, the plans were highly aggregated and provisional, subject to *ad hoc* intervention by superiors. All critical economic decisions were taken by the political leadership. Allocated resources and plan targets were usually denominated in [rubles](/wiki/Soviet_ruble) rather than in physical goods. [Credit](/wiki/Credit) was discouraged, but widespread. The final allocation of output was achieved through relatively decentralized, unplanned contracting. Although in theory prices were legally set from above, in practice they were often negotiated, and informal horizontal links (e.g. between producer factories) were widespread.

A number of basic [services](/wiki/Service_(economics)) were state-funded, such as [education](/wiki/Education_in_the_Soviet_Union) and health care. In the manufacturing sector, heavy industry and defence were prioritized over [consumer goods](/wiki/Consumer_goods_in_the_Soviet_Union). Consumer goods, particularly outside large cities, were often scarce, of poor quality and limited variety. Under the command economy, consumers had almost no influence on production, and the changing demands of a population with growing incomes could not be satisfied by supplies at rigidly fixed prices. A massive unplanned second economy grew up at low levels alongside the planned one, providing some of the goods and services that the planners could not. The legalization of some elements of the decentralized economy was attempted with the [reform of 1965](/wiki/1965_Soviet_economic_reform).

Workers of the [Salihorsk](/wiki/Salihorsk) potash plant, [Belarus](/wiki/Byelorussian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), 1968

Although statistics of the Soviet economy are notoriously unreliable and its economic growth difficult to estimate precisely, by most accounts, the economy continued to expand until the mid-1980s. During the 1950s and 1960s, it had comparatively high growth and was catching up to the West. However, after 1970, the growth, while still positive, [steadily declined](/wiki/Era_of_Stagnation) much more quickly and consistently than in other countries, despite a rapid increase in the capital [stock](/wiki/Stock) (the rate of capital increase was only surpassed by Japan).

Professor of Economic History [Bob Allen](/wiki/Bob_Allen_(economic_historian)) contends that in the era in which the Soviet economy was publicly owned and planned (1928–1989), the Soviet Union's [GDP per capita](/wiki/GDP_per_capita) growth outpaced nearly all other world economies, trailing only Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan. Data shows that Soviet per capita growth expanded by a factor of 5.2, exceeding the growth rates of Western Europe at 4.0, and the US, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand at 3.3. Ultimately, the Soviet model of public ownership proved more effective at raising average incomes during this period than the world's primary industrialized capitalist systems. According to Stephen Gowans, this growth trajectory was eventually undermined by the "accumulated toll on the Soviet economy of the West’s efforts to bring it down, the Reagan administration’s intensification of the Cold War, and the Soviet leadership’s inability to find a way out of the predicament these developments occasioned." Scholar [Christopher Davidson](/wiki/Christopher_Davidson) argued that, during the 1980s, the Reagan administration attempted to weaponize the global energy market against the USSR. At the request of CIA Director [William J. Casey](/wiki/William_J._Casey), Saudi Arabia flooded the market with oil, dropping the price from $28 to $10 per barrel, and draining Soviet foreign currency reserves. This was later described by a former CIA chief of staff as a "body blow to the Soviets. It was the equivalent of stepping on their oxygen tube."

Overall, the growth rate of per capita income in the Soviet Union between 1960 and 1989 was slightly above the world average (based on 102 countries). According to [Stanley Fischer](/wiki/Stanley_Fischer) and [William Easterly](/wiki/William_Easterly), growth could have been faster. By their calculation, per capita income in 1989 should have been twice higher than it was, considering the amount of investment, education and population. The authors attribute this poor performance to the low productivity of capital. Steven Rosefielde states that the [standard of living](/wiki/Standard_of_living) declined due to Stalin's despotism. While there was a brief improvement after his death, it lapsed into stagnation.

In 1987, [Mikhail Gorbachev](/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev) attempted to reform and revitalize the economy with his program of *[perestroika](/wiki/Perestroika)*. His policies relaxed state control over enterprises but did not replace it by market incentives, resulting in a sharp decline in output. The economy, already suffering from [reduced petroleum export revenues](/wiki/1980s_oil_glut), started to collapse. Prices were still fixed, and the property was still largely state-owned until after the country's dissolution. For most of the period after World War II until its collapse, Soviet GDP ([PPP](/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity)) was [the second-largest in the world](/wiki/List_of_regions_by_past_GDP_(PPP)), and third during the second half of the 1980s, although on a [per-capita](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)_per_capita) basis, it was behind that of [First World](/wiki/First_World) countries. Compared to countries with similar per-capita GDP in 1928, the Soviet Union experienced significant growth.

In 1990, the country had a [Human Development Index](/wiki/Human_Development_Index) of 0.920, placing it in the 'high' category of human development. It was the third-highest in the Eastern Bloc, behind [Czechoslovakia](/wiki/Czechoslovak_Socialist_Republic) and [East Germany](/wiki/East_Germany), and the 25th in the world of 130 countries.

### Energy

Main article: [Energy policy of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Energy_policy_of_the_Soviet_Union)

A Soviet stamp depicting the 30th anniversary of the [International Atomic Energy Agency](/wiki/International_Atomic_Energy_Agency), published in 1987, a year following the [Chernobyl USdisaster](/wiki/Chernobyl_nuclear_disaster)

The need for fuel declined in the Soviet Union from the 1970s to the 1980s, both per ruble of gross social product and per ruble of industrial product. The decline was very rapid between 1965 and 1970, then slowed between 1970 and 1975. From 1975 to 1980, the decline continued at an even slower rate, with fuel requirements per ruble of gross social product decreasing by only 2.6%. David Wilson, a historian, believed that the gas industry would account for 40% of Soviet fuel production by the end of the century. His theory did not come to fruition because of the USSR's collapse. According to Wilson, the Soviet Union was, in theory, well-positioned to avoid an energy crisis and could have sustained economic growth rates of 2–2.5% during the 1990s, supported by its energy resources. However, the energy sector faced many difficulties, among them the country's high military expenditure and hostile relations with the [First World](/wiki/First_World).

In 1991, the Soviet Union had a [pipeline](/wiki/Pipeline) network of 82,000 kilometres (51,000 mi) for [crude oil](/wiki/Crude_oil) and another 206,500 kilometres (128,300 mi) for natural gas. Petroleum and petroleum-based products, natural gas, metals, wood, agricultural products, and a variety of manufactured goods, primarily machinery, arms and military equipment, were exported. In the 1970s and 1980s, the USSR heavily relied on fossil fuel exports to earn [hard currency](/wiki/Hard_currency). At its peak in 1988, it was the largest producer and second-largest exporter of crude oil, surpassed only by [Saudi Arabia](/wiki/Saudi_Arabia).

### Science and technology

Main article: [Science and technology in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_the_Soviet_Union)

See also: [Cybernetics in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Cybernetics_in_the_Soviet_Union)

Soviet stamp showing the orbit of [Sputnik 1](/wiki/Sputnik_1)

The Soviet Union placed great emphasis on [science and technology](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_the_Soviet_Union). Lenin believed the USSR would never overtake the developed world if it remained as technologically backward as it was upon its founding. Soviet authorities proved their commitment to Lenin's belief by developing massive networks and research and development organizations. In the early 1960s, 40% of chemistry PhDs in the Soviet Union were attained by women, compared with only 5% in the United States. By 1989, Soviet scientists were among the world's best-trained specialists in several areas, such as energy physics, selected areas of medicine, mathematics, welding, space technology, and military technologies. However, due to rigid state planning and [bureaucracy](/wiki/Nomenklatura), the Soviets remained far behind the [First World](/wiki/First_World) in chemistry, biology, and computer science. Under Stalin, the Soviet government persecuted [geneticists](/wiki/Geneticists) in favour of [Lysenkoism](/wiki/Lysenkoism), a [pseudoscience](/wiki/Pseudoscience) rejected by the scientific community in the Soviet Union and abroad but supported by Stalin's inner circles. Implemented in the USSR and China, it resulted in reduced crop yields and is widely believed to have contributed to the [Great Chinese Famine](/wiki/Great_Chinese_Famine). In the 1980s, the Soviet Union had more [scientists](/wiki/Scientists) and [engineers](/wiki/Engineers) relative to the world's population than any other major country, owing to strong levels of state support. Some of its most remarkable technological achievements, such as launching the [world's first space satellite](/wiki/Sputnik_1), were achieved through military research.

Under the [Reagan administration](/wiki/Reagan_administration), [Project Socrates](/wiki/Project_Socrates) determined that the Soviet Union addressed the acquisition of science and technology in a manner radically different to the United States. The US prioritized indigenous [research and development](/wiki/Research_and_development) in both the public and private sectors. In contrast, the USSR placed greater emphasis on acquiring foreign technology, which it did through both [covert](/wiki/Industrial_espionage) and overt means. However, centralized state planning kept Soviet technological development greatly inflexible. This was exploited by the US to undermine the strength of the Soviet Union and thus foster its reform.

### Space program

Main article: [Soviet space program](/wiki/Soviet_space_program)

See also: [Nedelin catastrophe](/wiki/Nedelin_catastrophe)

From left to right: [Yuri Gagarin](/wiki/Yuri_Gagarin), [Pavel Popovich](/wiki/Pavel_Popovich), [Valentina Tereshkova](/wiki/Valentina_Tereshkova) and [Nikita Khrushchev](/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev) at [Lenin's Mausoleum](/wiki/Lenin%27s_Mausoleum) in 1963

[Soyuz](/wiki/Soyuz_(rocket_family)) rocket at the [Baikonur Cosmodrome](/wiki/Baikonur_Cosmodrome)

At the end of the 1950s, the USSR constructed the first [satellite](/wiki/Satellite)—[Sputnik 1](/wiki/Sputnik_1), which marked the beginning of the [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race)—a competition to achieve superior spaceflight capability with the United States. This was followed by other successful satellites, most notably [Sputnik 5](/wiki/Sputnik_5), where test dogs were sent to space. On 12 April 1961, the USSR launched [Vostok 1](/wiki/Vostok_1), which carried [Yuri Gagarin](/wiki/Yuri_Gagarin), making him the first human to ever be launched into space and complete a space journey. The first plans for [space shuttles](/wiki/Spaceplane) and orbital stations were drawn up in Soviet design offices, but personal disputes between designers and management prevented their development.

In terms of the [Luna program](/wiki/Luna_program), the USSR only had automated spacecraft launches with no crewed spacecraft. The [N1](/wiki/N1_(rocket))—a [Super heavy-lift launch vehicle](/wiki/Super_heavy-lift_launch_vehicle) intended to match the American [Saturn V](/wiki/Saturn_V) for a Soviet crewed moon landing—failed all four of its test launches, and the 'Moon' part of [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race) was [won by the Americans](/wiki/Apollo_11). The Soviet public's reaction to the American moon-landing was mixed. The Soviet government limited the release of information about it, which affected the reaction. A portion of the populace did not give it attention, and another portion was angered.

In the 1970s, specific proposals for the design of a space shuttle emerged, but shortcomings, especially in the electronics industry (rapid overheating of electronics), postponed it till the end of the 1980s. The first shuttle, the [Buran](/wiki/Buran_(spacecraft)), flew in 1988, but without a human crew. Another, *[Ptichka](/wiki/Ptichka)*, endured prolonged construction and was canceled in 1991. For their launch into space, there is today an unused superpower rocket, [Energia](/wiki/Energia_(rocket)), which is the most powerful in the world.

In the late 1980s, the Soviet Union built the *[Mir](/wiki/Mir)* orbital station. It was built on the construction of [*Salyut* stations](/wiki/Salyut_programme) and its only role was civilian-grade research tasks. Mir was the only orbital station in operation from 1986 to 1998. Gradually, other modules were added to it, including American modules. However, the station deteriorated rapidly after a fire on board and was deorbited in 2001, burning up in the Earth's atmosphere.

### Transport

Main article: [Transport in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Transport_in_the_Soviet_Union)

[Aeroflot](/wiki/Aeroflot)'s flag during the Soviet era

Nuclear Icebreaker *[Lenin](/wiki/Lenin_(1957_icebreaker))*

Transport was a vital component of the country's economy. The [economic centralization](/wiki/First_five-year_plan_(Soviet_Union)) of the late 1920s and 1930s led to the development of infrastructure on a massive scale, most notably the establishment of [Aeroflot](/wiki/Aeroflot), an aviation enterprise. The country had a wide variety of modes of transport by land, water and air. However, due to inadequate maintenance, much of the road, water and Soviet civil aviation transport were outdated and technologically backward compared to the First World.

Soviet rail transport was the largest and most intensively used in the world; it was also better developed than most of its Western counterparts. By the late 1970s and early 1980s, Soviet economists were calling for the construction of more roads to alleviate some of the burdens from the railways and to improve the Soviet [government budget](/wiki/Government_budget). The [street network](/wiki/Street_network) and [automotive industry](/wiki/Automotive_industry_in_the_Soviet_Union) remained underdeveloped, and [dirt roads](/wiki/Dirt_road) were common outside major cities. Soviet maintenance projects proved unable to take care of even the few roads the country had. By the early-to-mid-1980s, the Soviet authorities tried to solve the road problem by ordering the construction of new ones. Meanwhile, the automobile industry was growing at a faster rate than road construction. The underdeveloped road network led to a growing demand for public transport.

Despite improvements, several aspects of the transport sector were still riddled with problems due to outdated infrastructure, lack of investment, corruption and bad decision-making. Soviet authorities were unable to meet the growing demand for transport infrastructure and services.

The Soviet [merchant navy](/wiki/Merchant_navy) was one of the largest in the world.

## Demographics

Main article: [Demographics of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Demographics_of_the_Soviet_Union)

Population of the Soviet Union (red) and the [post-Soviet states](/wiki/Post-Soviet_states) (blue) from 1961 to 2009 as well as projection (dotted blue) from 2010 to 2100

Excess deaths throughout [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I) and the [Russian Civil War](/wiki/Russian_Civil_War) (including the [famine of 1921–1922](/wiki/Russian_famine_of_1921%E2%80%931922) that was triggered by Lenin's [war communism](/wiki/War_communism) policies) amounted to a combined total of 18 million, some 10 million in the 1930s, and more than 20 million in 1941–1945. The postwar [Soviet population](/wiki/Population_of_the_Soviet_Union) was 45 to 50 million smaller than it would have been if pre-war demographic growth had continued. According to [Catherine Merridale](/wiki/Catherine_Merridale), '... reasonable estimate would place the total number of excess deaths for the whole period somewhere around 60 million.'

The [birth rate](/wiki/Birth_rate) of the USSR decreased from 44.0 per thousand in 1926 to 18.0 in 1974, mainly due to increasing urbanization and the rising average age of marriages. The [mortality rate](/wiki/Mortality_rate) demonstrated a gradual decrease as well—from 23.7 per thousand in 1926 to 8.7 in 1974. In general, the birth rates of the southern republics in Transcaucasia and Central Asia were considerably higher than those in the northern parts of the Soviet Union, and in some cases even increased in the post–World War II period, a phenomenon partly attributed to slower rates of urbanization and traditionally earlier marriages in the southern republics. Soviet Europe moved towards [sub-replacement fertility](/wiki/Sub-replacement_fertility), while [Soviet Central Asia](/wiki/Soviet_Central_Asia) continued to exhibit population growth well above replacement-level fertility.

The late 1960s and the 1970s witnessed a reversal of the declining trajectory of the rate of mortality in the USSR, and was especially notable among men of working age, but was also prevalent in Russia and other predominantly Slavic areas of the country. An analysis of the official data from the late 1980s showed that after worsening in the late-1970s and the early 1980s, adult mortality began to improve again. The infant mortality rate increased from 24.7 in 1970 to 27.9 in 1974. Some researchers regarded the rise as mostly real, a consequence of worsening health conditions and services. The rises in both adult and infant mortality were not explained or defended by Soviet officials, and the [Soviet government](/wiki/Soviet_government) stopped publishing all mortality statistics for ten years. Soviet demographers and health specialists remained silent about the mortality increases until the late-1980s, when the publication of mortality data resumed, and researchers could delve into the real causes.

### Urbanism

Largest cities of the USSR according to the 1989 census

The Soviet Union imposed heavy controls on city growth, preventing some cities from reaching their full potential while promoting others.

For the entirety of the Soviet Union's existence, the most populous cities were [Moscow](/wiki/Moscow) and [Leningrad](/wiki/Leningrad) (both in [Russian SFSR](/wiki/Russian_SFSR)), with the third far place taken by [Kiev](/wiki/Kiev) ([Ukrainian SSR](/wiki/Ukrainian_SSR)). At the USSR's inception, the fourth and fifth most populous cities were [Kharkov](/wiki/Kharkov) (Ukrainian SSR) and [Baku](/wiki/Baku) ([Azerbaijan SSR](/wiki/Azerbaijan_SSR)), but, by the end of the century, [Tashkent](/wiki/Tashkent) ([Uzbek SSR](/wiki/Uzbek_SSR)), which had assumed the position of capital of Soviet Central Asia, had risen to fourth place. [Minsk](/wiki/Minsk) ([Byelorussian SSR](/wiki/Byelorussian_SSR)) saw rapid growth during the 20th century, rising from the 32nd most populous in the union to the 7th.

### Women and fertility

[Valentina Tereshkova](/wiki/Valentina_Tereshkova), the first woman in space, visiting the [Lvov](/wiki/Lvov) confectionery, Ukrainian SSR, 1967

Under Lenin, the state made explicit commitments to promote the [equality of men and women](/wiki/Gender_equality). Many early Russian feminists and ordinary Russian working women actively participated in the Revolution, and many more were affected by the events of that period and the new policies. Beginning in October 1918, Lenin's government liberalized divorce and abortion laws, decriminalized homosexuality (re-criminalized in 1932), permitted cohabitation, and ushered in a host of reforms. However, without [birth control](/wiki/Birth_control), the new system produced many broken marriages, as well as countless out-of-wedlock children. The epidemic of divorces and extramarital affairs created social hardships when Soviet leaders wanted people to concentrate their efforts on growing the economy. Giving women control over their fertility also led to a precipitous decline in the birth rate, perceived as a threat to their country's military power. By 1936, Stalin reversed most of the liberal laws, ushering in a [pronatalist](/wiki/Natalism) era that lasted for decades.

By 1917, Russia became the first [great power](/wiki/Great_power) to grant women the right to vote. After heavy casualties in World Wars I and II, women outnumbered men in Russia by a 4:3 ratio; this contributed to the larger role women played in Russian society compared to other great powers at the time.

### LGBTQ rights

Main article: [LGBTQ history in the Soviet Union](/wiki/LGBTQ_history_in_the_Soviet_Union)

The Soviet Union repressed [homosexuality](/wiki/Homosexuality). Even during the period when homosexuality was officially legal after the abolition of the Tsarist penal code criminalising it, Soviet courts attempted to repress non-traditional forms of sexuality, which were widely viewed by Russian revolutionaries as a form of capitalist decadence despite more liberal views on homosexuality from Soviet academic sexologists. After Stalin's consolidation of power, homosexuality became officially recriminalised in 1934. The increased homophobia during this time interval was driven by the economic demands of the First Five-Year Plan, as well as the NKVD's view of homosexuals as "socially harmful elements", although even during this heightened period of repression, a clandestine homosexual subculture was able to persist. Homosexuality remained a criminal offence throughout the remainder of the Soviet Union's existence.

### Education

Main article: [Education in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Education_in_the_Soviet_Union)

[Young Pioneers](/wiki/Young_Pioneer_organization_of_the_Soviet_Union) at a Young Pioneer camp in the Kazakh SSR

[Anatoly Lunacharsky](/wiki/Anatoly_Lunacharsky) became the first [People's Commissar](/wiki/People%27s_Commissar) for Education of Soviet Russia. In the beginning, the Soviet authorities placed great emphasis on the [elimination of illiteracy](/wiki/Likbez). All left-handed children were forced to write with their right hand in the Soviet school system. Literate people were automatically hired as teachers. For a short period, quality was sacrificed for quantity. By 1940, Stalin could announce that illiteracy had been eliminated. Throughout the 1930s, [social mobility](/wiki/Social_mobility) rose sharply, which has been attributed to reforms in education. In the aftermath of World War II, the country's educational system expanded dramatically, which had a tremendous effect. In the 1960s, nearly all children had access to education, the only exception being those living in remote areas. [Nikita Khrushchev](/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev) tried to make education more accessible, making it clear to children that education was closely linked to the needs of society. Education also became important in giving rise to the [New Man](/wiki/New_Soviet_man). Citizens directly entering the workforce had the constitutional right to a job and to free [vocational training](/wiki/Vocational-technical_school).

The [education system](/wiki/Education_in_the_Soviet_Union) was highly centralized and universally accessible to all citizens, with [affirmative action](/wiki/Affirmative_action) for applicants from nations associated with [cultural backwardness](/wiki/Cultural_backwardness). However, as part of a general [antisemitic policy](/wiki/Antisemitism_in_the_Soviet_Union), an unofficial [Jewish quota](/wiki/Jewish_quota) was applied in the leading institutions of higher education by subjecting Jewish applicants to harsher entrance examinations. The Brezhnev era also introduced a rule that required all university applicants to present a reference from the local [Komsomol](/wiki/Komsomol) party secretary. According to statistics from 1986, the number of higher education students per the population of 10,000 was 181 for the USSR, compared to 517 for the US.

### Nationalities and ethnic groups

Main articles: [Islam in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Islam_in_the_Soviet_Union), [National delimitation in the Soviet Union](/wiki/National_delimitation_in_the_Soviet_Union), [Korenizatsiia](/wiki/Korenizatsiia), and [Soviet Central Asia](/wiki/Soviet_Central_Asia)

The Soviet Union was an ethnically diverse country, with more than 100 distinct ethnic groups. The total population of the country was estimated at 293 million in 1991. According to a 1990 estimate, the majority of the population were [Russians](/wiki/Russians) (50.78%), followed by [Ukrainians](/wiki/Ukrainians) (15.45%) and [Uzbeks](/wiki/Uzbeks) (5.84%). Overall, in 1989 the ethnic demography of the country showed that 69.8% was [East Slavic](/wiki/East_Slavs), 17.5% was [Turkic](/wiki/Turkic_peoples), 1.6% were [Armenians](/wiki/Armenians), 1.6% were [Balts](/wiki/Balts), 1.5% were [Uralic](/wiki/Uralic_peoples), 1.5% were [Tajik](/wiki/Tajiks), 1.4% were [Georgian](/wiki/Georgians), 1.2% were [Moldovan](/wiki/Moldovans) and 4.1% were of other various ethnic groups.

All citizens of the USSR had their own ethnic affiliation. The ethnicity of a person was chosen at the age of sixteen by the child's parents. If the parents did not agree, the child was automatically assigned the ethnicity of the father. Partly due to Soviet policies, some of the smaller minority ethnic groups were considered part of larger ones, such as the [Mingrelians](/wiki/Mingrelians) of [Georgia](/wiki/Georgian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), who were classified with the linguistically related [Georgians](/wiki/Georgians). Some ethnic groups voluntarily assimilated, while others were brought in by force. Those who refused to assimilate would be mistreated and ostracized. Some ethnic groups suffered more than others, however some groups would be afforded better treatment than others. A person’s quality of life was heavily influenced by their ethnicity. When the Bolsheviks first took power, they wanted to make it seem as if the ethnicities were equal. However, in most cases, Russians were generally preferred over other ethnicities. Russians, [Belarusians](/wiki/Belarusians), and Ukrainians, who were all East Slavic and Orthodox, shared close cultural, ethnic, and religious ties, while other groups did not. With multiple nationalities living in the same territory, [ethnic antagonisms](/wiki/Ethnic_conflicts_in_the_Soviet_Union) developed over the years.

Members of various ethnicities participated in legislative bodies. Organs of power like the Politburo, the Secretariat of the Central Committee etc., were formally ethnically neutral, but in reality, ethnic Russians were overrepresented, although there were also non-Russian leaders in the [Soviet leadership](/wiki/List_of_leaders_of_the_Soviet_Union), such as [Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin), [Grigory Zinoviev](/wiki/Grigory_Zinoviev), [Nikolai Podgorny](/wiki/Nikolai_Podgorny) or [Andrei Gromyko](/wiki/Andrei_Gromyko). During the Soviet era, a significant number of ethnic Russians and Ukrainians migrated to other Soviet republics, and many of them settled there. According to the last census in 1989, the Russian 'diaspora' in the Soviet republics had reached 25 million.

### Health

Main article: [Health care in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Healthcare_in_Russia#Healthcare_in_the_Soviet_Union)

An early Soviet-era poster discouraging unsafe abortion practices

In 1917, before the revolution, health conditions were significantly behind those of developed countries. As Lenin later noted, "Either the lice will defeat socialism, or socialism will defeat the lice". The Soviet health care system was conceived by the [People's Commissariat for Health](/wiki/People%27s_Commissariat_for_Health) in 1918. Under the [Semashko model](/wiki/Semashko_model), health care was to be controlled by the state and would be provided to its citizens free of charge, a revolutionary concept at the time. Article 42 of the [1977 Soviet Constitution](/wiki/1977_Soviet_Constitution) gave all citizens the [right to health](/wiki/Right_to_health) protection and free access to any health institutions in the USSR. Before [Leonid Brezhnev](/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev) became general secretary, the Soviet healthcare system was held in high esteem by many foreign specialists. This changed, however, from Brezhnev's accession and [Mikhail Gorbachev](/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev)'s tenure as leader, during which the health care system was heavily criticized for many basic faults, such as the quality of service and the unevenness in its provision. [Minister of Health](/wiki/Ministry_of_Health_(Soviet_Union)) [Yevgeniy Chazov](/wiki/Yevgeniy_Chazov), during the [19th Congress of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union](/wiki/19th_Congress_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), while highlighting such successes as having the most doctors and hospitals in the world, recognized the system's areas for improvement and felt that billions of [rubles](/wiki/Soviet_ruble) were squandered.

After the revolution, life expectancy for all age groups went up. These improvements continued into the 1960s when statistics indicated that the life expectancy briefly surpassed that of the United States; life expectancy started to decline in the 1970s, possibly because of [alcohol abuse](/wiki/Alcohol_consumption_in_Russia). At the same time, infant mortality began to rise. After 1974, the government stopped publishing statistics on the matter. This trend can be partly explained by the number of pregnancies rising drastically in the Asian part of the country where infant mortality was the highest while declining markedly in the more developed European part of the Soviet Union.

#### Dentistry

Soviet dental technology and dental health were considered extremely bad; in 1991, the average 35-year-old had 12 to 14 cavities, fillings or missing teeth. Toothpaste was often not available, and toothbrushes did not conform to standards of modern dentistry.

### Language

Main articles: [Languages of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Languages_of_the_Soviet_Union) and [Reforms of Russian orthography](/wiki/Reforms_of_Russian_orthography)

Under Lenin, the government gave small language groups their own writing systems. The development of these writing systems was highly successful, even though some flaws were detected. During the later days of the USSR, countries with the same [multilingual](/wiki/Multilingual) situation implemented similar policies. A serious problem when creating these writing systems was that the languages differed [dialectally](/wiki/Dialect) greatly from each other. When a language had been given a writing system and appeared in a notable publication, it would attain 'official language' status. There were many minority languages which never received their own writing system; therefore, their speakers were forced to have a [second language](/wiki/Second_language). There are examples where the government retreated from this policy, most notably under Stalin where education was discontinued in languages that were not widespread. In 1938, Stalin mandated all schools to teach Soviet schoolchildren the Russian language. It did not stop children from getting an education in their native language (the language they grew up speaking), but they would also be required to learn how to speak the Russian language. Stalin saw this as necessary from a military perspective, because it would be easier for everyone to communicate. Despite only being mandated in 1938, children of different ethnicities were already being enrolled in schools teaching Russian beforehand because knowing the Russian language would open up a lot of opportunities for a person. Whether it expanded the person's ability to socialize or gave the person a chance to contribute to the multiethnic goals of the Soviet Union, many people saw the benefit of learning Russian. While other languages were spoken, Russian became the lingua franca of the USSR. During World War II, some minority languages were banned, and their speakers accused of collaborating with the enemy.

As the most widely spoken of the Soviet Union's many languages, Russian *de facto* functioned as an official language, as the 'language of interethnic communication' (Russian: язык межнационального общения), but only assumed the *[de jure](/wiki/De_jure)* status as the official national language in 1990.

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Religion_in_the_Soviet_Union)

The [Cathedral of Christ the Saviour](/wiki/Cathedral_of_Christ_the_Saviour) in Moscow during its demolition in 1931

The [Saviour Church on Sennaya Square](/wiki/Saviour_Church_on_Sennaya_Square) in Leningrad was one of many notable church buildings destroyed during the [Khrushchev Thaw](/wiki/Khrushchev_Thaw).

A [paranja](/wiki/Paranja) burning ceremony in the [Uzbek SSR](/wiki/Uzbek_SSR) as part of Soviet [Hujum](/wiki/Hujum) policies

Major religious groups in the Soviet Union as published by the [CIA](/wiki/CIA)

[Christianity](/wiki/Christianity) and [Islam](/wiki/Islam) had the highest number of adherents among the religious citizens. [Eastern Christianity](/wiki/Eastern_Christianity) predominated among Christians, with Russia's traditional [Russian Orthodox Church](/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church) being the largest [Christian denomination](/wiki/Christian_denomination). About 90% of the Soviet Union's Muslims were [Sunnis](/wiki/Sunni), with [Shias](/wiki/Shias) being concentrated in the [Azerbaijan SSR](/wiki/Azerbaijan_SSR). Smaller groups included [Roman Catholics](/wiki/Roman_Catholics), Jews, [Buddhists](/wiki/Buddhists), and a variety of [Protestant](/wiki/Protestant) denominations (especially [Baptists](/wiki/Baptists) and [Lutherans](/wiki/Lutherans)).

Religious influence had been strong in the Russian Empire. The Russian Orthodox Church enjoyed a privileged status as the church of the monarchy and took part in carrying out official state functions. The immediate period following the establishment of the Soviet state included a struggle against the Orthodox Church, which the revolutionaries considered an ally of the former [ruling classes](/wiki/Ruling_class).

In Soviet law, the 'freedom to hold religious services' was constitutionally guaranteed, although the ruling Communist Party regarded religion as incompatible with the [Marxist](/wiki/Marxist) spirit of [scientific materialism](/wiki/Scientific_materialism). In practice, the Soviet system subscribed to a narrow interpretation of this right, and in fact used a range of official measures to discourage religion and curb the activities of religious groups.

The 1918 [Council of People's Commissars](/wiki/Council_of_People%27s_Commissars) decree establishing the Russian SFSR as a secular state also decreed that 'the teaching of religion in all [places] where subjects of general instruction are taught, is forbidden. Citizens may teach and may be taught religion privately.' Among further restrictions, those adopted in 1929 included express prohibitions on a range of church activities, including meetings for organized [Bible study](/wiki/Bible_study_(Christian)). Both Christian and non-Christian establishments were shut down by the thousands in the 1920s and 1930s. By 1940, as many as 90% of the churches, synagogues, and mosques that had been operating in 1917 were closed; the majority of them were demolished or re-purposed for state needs with little concern for their historic and cultural value.

More than 85,000 Orthodox priests were shot in 1937 alone. Only a twelfth of the Russian Orthodox Church's priests were left functioning in their parishes by 1941. In the period between 1927 and 1940, the number of Orthodox Churches in Russia fell from 29,584 to less than 500 (1.7%).

The Soviet Union was officially a [secular state](/wiki/Secular_state), but a 'government-sponsored program of forced conversion to [atheism](/wiki/Marxist-Leninist_atheism)' was conducted under the doctrine of [state atheism](/wiki/State_atheism). The government targeted religions based on state interests, and while most organized religions were never outlawed, religious property was confiscated, believers were harassed, and religion was ridiculed while atheism was propagated in schools. In 1925, the government founded the [League of Militant Atheists](/wiki/League_of_Militant_Atheists) to intensify the propaganda campaign. Accordingly, although personal expressions of religious faith were not explicitly banned, a strong sense of social stigma was imposed on them by the formal structures and mass media, and it was generally considered unacceptable for members of certain professions (teachers, state bureaucrats, soldiers) to be openly religious. While persecution accelerated following Stalin's rise to power, a revival of Orthodoxy was fostered by the government during World War II and the Soviet authorities sought to control the Russian Orthodox Church rather than liquidate it. During the first five years of Soviet power, the Bolsheviks executed 28 Russian Orthodox bishops and over 1,200 Russian Orthodox priests. Many others were imprisoned or exiled. Believers were harassed and persecuted. Most seminaries were closed, and the publication of most religious material was prohibited. By 1941, only 500 churches remained open out of about 54,000 in existence before World War I.

Convinced that religious [anti-Sovietism](/wiki/Anti-Sovietism) had become a thing of the past, and with the looming threat of war, the Stalin administration began shifting to a more moderate religion policy in the late 1930s. Soviet religious establishments overwhelmingly rallied to support the war effort during World War II. Amid other accommodations to religious faith after the German invasion, churches were reopened. [Radio Moscow](/wiki/Radio_Moscow) began broadcasting a religious hour, and a historic meeting between Stalin and Orthodox Church leader [Patriarch Sergius of Moscow](/wiki/Patriarch_Sergius_of_Moscow) was held in 1943. Stalin had the support of the majority of the religious people in the USSR even through the late 1980s. The general tendency of this period was an increase in religious activity among believers of all faiths.

Under [Nikita Khrushchev](/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev), the state leadership clashed with the churches in 1958–1964, a period when [atheism](/wiki/Atheism) was emphasized in the educational curriculum, and numerous state publications promoted atheistic views. During this period, the number of churches fell from 20,000 to 10,000 from 1959 to 1965, and the number of synagogues dropped from 500 to 97. The number of working mosques also declined, falling from 1,500 to 500 within a decade.

Religious institutions remained monitored by the Soviet government, but churches, synagogues, temples, and mosques were all given more leeway in the [Brezhnev era](/wiki/Brezhnev_era). Official relations between the Orthodox Church and the government again warmed to the point that the Brezhnev government twice honored Orthodox Patriarch [Alexy I](/wiki/Alexy_I) with the [Order of the Red Banner of Labour](/wiki/Order_of_the_Red_Banner_of_Labour). A poll conducted by Soviet authorities in 1982 recorded 20% of the Soviet population as 'active religious believers.'

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Culture_of_the_Soviet_Union)

See also: [Soviet cuisine](/wiki/Soviet_cuisine), [Music of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Music_of_the_Soviet_Union), [Fashion in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Fashion_in_the_Soviet_Union), [Broadcasting in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Broadcasting_in_the_Soviet_Union), [Printed media in the Soviet Union](/wiki/Printed_media_in_the_Soviet_Union), and [Samizdat](/wiki/Samizdat)

The 'Enthusiast's March', a 1930s song famous in the Soviet Union

Soviet singer-songwriter, poet and actor [Vladimir Vysotsky](/wiki/Vladimir_Vysotsky) in 1979

The culture of the Soviet Union evolved through several stages during its existence. During the first decade following the revolution, there was relative freedom and artists experimented with several different styles to find a distinctive Soviet style of art. Lenin wanted art to be accessible to the Russian people. On the other hand, hundreds of intellectuals, writers, and artists were exiled or executed, and their work banned, such as [Nikolay Gumilyov](/wiki/Nikolay_Gumilyov) who was shot for alleged conspiracy against the Bolsheviks, and [Yevgeny Zamyatin](/wiki/Yevgeny_Zamyatin).

The government encouraged a variety of trends. In art and literature, numerous schools, some traditional and others radically experimental, proliferated. Communist writers [Maxim Gorky](/wiki/Maxim_Gorky) and [Vladimir Mayakovsky](/wiki/Vladimir_Mayakovsky) were active during this time. As a means of influencing a largely illiterate society, films received encouragement from the state, and much of director [Sergei Eisenstein](/wiki/Sergei_Eisenstein)'s best work dates from this period.

During Stalin's rule, the Soviet culture was characterized by the rise and domination of the government-imposed style of [socialist realism](/wiki/Socialist_realism), with all other trends being severely repressed, with rare exceptions, such as [Mikhail Bulgakov](/wiki/Mikhail_Bulgakov)'s works. Many writers were imprisoned and killed.

Following the [Khrushchev Thaw](/wiki/Khrushchev_Thaw), censorship was diminished. During this time, a distinctive period of Soviet culture developed, characterized by conformist public life and an intense focus on personal life. Greater experimentation in art forms was again permissible, resulting in the production of more sophisticated and subtly critical work. The government loosened its emphasis on socialist realism; thus, for instance, many protagonists of the novels of author [Yury Trifonov](/wiki/Yury_Trifonov) concerned themselves with problems of daily life rather than with building socialism. Underground dissident literature, known as *[samizdat](/wiki/Samizdat)*, developed during this late period. In architecture, the Khrushchev era mostly focused on functional design as opposed to the highly decorated style of Stalin's epoch. In music, in response to the increasing popularity of forms of popular music like [jazz](/wiki/Jazz) in the West, many jazz orchestras were permitted throughout the USSR, notably the Melodiya Ensemble, named after the principle record label in the USSR.

In the second half of the 1980s, Gorbachev's policies of *[perestroika](/wiki/Perestroika)* and *[glasnost](/wiki/Glasnost)* significantly expanded [freedom of expression](/wiki/Freedom_of_expression) throughout the country in the media and the press.

## Sport

See also: [Voluntary Sports Societies of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Voluntary_Sports_Societies_of_the_Soviet_Union), [CSKA Moscow](/wiki/CSKA_Moscow), [Soviet Union at the Olympics](/wiki/Soviet_Union_at_the_Olympics), and [Soviet Union men's national ice hockey team](/wiki/Soviet_Union_men%27s_national_ice_hockey_team)

[Valeri Kharlamov](/wiki/Valeri_Kharlamov) represented the Soviet Union at 11 [Ice Hockey World Championships](/wiki/Ice_Hockey_World_Championships), winning eight gold medals, two silvers and one bronze.

In summer of 1923 in Moscow was established the [Proletarian Sports Society "Dynamo"](/wiki/Dynamo_Sports_Club) as a sports organization of Soviet secret police [Cheka](/wiki/Cheka).

On 13 July 1925 the [Central Committee of the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks)](/wiki/Central_Committee_of_the_Russian_Communist_Party_(Bolsheviks)) adopted a statement "About the party's tasks in sphere of physical culture". In the statement was determined the role of physical culture in Soviet society and the party's tasks in political leadership of physical culture movement in the country.

The [Soviet Olympic Committee](/wiki/Soviet_Olympic_Committee) formed on 21 April 1951, and the [IOC](/wiki/IOC) recognized the new body in its [45th session](/wiki/List_of_IOC_meetings). In the same year, when the Soviet representative Konstantin Andrianov became an IOC member, the USSR officially joined the [Olympic Movement](/wiki/Olympic_Movement). The [1952 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1952_Summer_Olympics) in Helsinki thus became first Olympic Games for Soviet athletes. The Soviet Union was the biggest rival to the United States at the Summer Olympics, winning [six of its nine appearances at the games](/wiki/Soviet_Union_at_the_Olympics#Medals_by_Summer_Games) and also topping the medal tally at the Winter Olympics six times. The Soviet Union's Olympics success has been attributed to its large investment in sports to demonstrate its superpower image and political influence on a global stage.

The [Soviet Union national ice hockey team](/wiki/Soviet_Union_national_ice_hockey_team) won nearly every [world championship](/wiki/Ice_Hockey_World_Championships) and [Olympic tournament](/wiki/Ice_hockey_at_the_Olympic_Games) between 1954 and 1991 and never failed to medal in any [International Ice Hockey Federation](/wiki/International_Ice_Hockey_Federation) (IIHF) tournament in which they competed.

The Soviet Olympic team was notorious for skirting the edge of amateur rules. All Soviet athletes held some nominal jobs, but were in fact state-sponsored and trained full-time. According to many experts, that gave the Soviet Union a huge advantage over the [United States](/wiki/United_States) and other Western countries, whose athletes were students or real amateurs. Indeed, the Soviet Union monopolized the top place in the medal standings after 1968, and, until its collapse, placed second only once, in the [1984 Winter games](/wiki/1984_Winter_Olympics), after another Eastern bloc nation, the [GDR](/wiki/GDR). Amateur rules were relaxed only in the late 1980s and were almost completely abolished in the 1990s, after the [fall of the USSR](/wiki/Fall_of_the_USSR).

According to British journalist [Andrew Jennings](/wiki/Andrew_Jennings), a [KGB](/wiki/KGB) colonel stated that the agency's officers had posed as anti-doping authorities from the [International Olympic Committee](/wiki/International_Olympic_Committee) (IOC) to undermine [doping tests](/wiki/Doping_test) and that Soviet athletes were "rescued with [these] tremendous efforts". Documents obtained in 2016 revealed the Soviet Union's plans for a statewide doping system in track and field in preparation for the [1984 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics) in Los Angeles. Dated prior to the country's decision to boycott the Games, the document detailed the existing steroids operations of the program, along with suggestions for further enhancements.

In the late 1980s, the government was persuaded to fund construction of a racing yacht specifically to take part in the [1989–1990 Whitbread Round the World Race](/wiki/1989%E2%80%931990_Whitbread_Round_the_World_Race) with a Soviet crew. The 25 metre sloop *[Fazisi](/wiki/Fazisi)* was built in 1989 to the design of Vladislav Murnikov in Poti, Georgia. She came a creditable 11th in a field of 23 boats, but the project was not repeated.

## Environment

Landscape near [Karabash, Chelyabinsk Oblast](/wiki/Karabash,_Chelyabinsk_Oblast), an area that was previously covered with forests until acid rainfall from a nearby copper smelter killed all vegetation

One of the many impacts of the approach to the environment in the USSR and post-Soviet states is the [Aral Sea](/wiki/Aral_Sea). (See status in 1989 and 2014)

Neighbouring countries were aware of the high levels of pollution in the Soviet Union but after the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) it was discovered that its environmental problems were greater than what the Soviet authorities admitted. The Soviet Union was the world's second largest producer of harmful emissions. In 1988, total emissions in the Soviet Union were about 79% of those in the United States. But since the Soviet [GNP](/wiki/GNP) was only 54% of that of the United States, this means that the Soviet Union generated 1.5 times more pollution than the United States per unit of GNP.

The [Chernobyl disaster](/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) in the Ukrainian SSR in 1986 was the first major accident at a civilian [nuclear power plant](/wiki/Nuclear_power_plant). Unparalleled in the world, it resulted in a large number of radioactive isotopes being released into the atmosphere. Radioactive doses were scattered relatively far. Although long-term effects of the accident were unknown, 4,000 new cases of thyroid cancer which resulted from the accident's contamination were reported at the time of the accident, but this led to a relatively low number of deaths (WHO data, 2005). The disaster contributed to the socio-economic crises that resulted in the collapse of the Soviet Union.Another major radioactive accident was the [Kyshtym disaster](/wiki/Kyshtym_disaster).

The [Kola Peninsula](/wiki/Kola_Peninsula) was one of the places with major problems. Around the industrial cities of [Monchegorsk](/wiki/Monchegorsk) and [Norilsk](/wiki/Norilsk), where [nickel](/wiki/Nickel), for example, is mined, all forests have been destroyed by contamination, while the northern and other parts of Russia have been affected by emissions. During the 1990s, people in the West were also interested in the radioactive hazards of nuclear facilities, decommissioned [nuclear submarines](/wiki/Nuclear_submarine), and the processing of [nuclear waste](/wiki/Nuclear_waste) or [spent nuclear fuel](/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel). It was also known in the early 1990s that the USSR had transported radioactive material to the [Barents Sea](/wiki/Barents_Sea) and [Kara Sea](/wiki/Kara_Sea), which was later confirmed by the Russian parliament. The crash of the [K-141 Kursk](/wiki/K-141_Kursk) submarine in 2000 in the west further raised concerns. In the past, there were accidents involving submarines [K-19](/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-19), [K-8](/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-8), a [K-129](/wiki/K-129), [K-27](/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-27), [K-219](/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-219) and [K-278 Komsomolets](/wiki/K-278_Komsomolets).

## Legacy

See also: [Neo-Sovietism](/wiki/Neo-Sovietism) and [Nostalgia for the Soviet Union](/wiki/Nostalgia_for_the_Soviet_Union)

The legacy of the USSR remains a controversial topic. The socio-economic nature of [communist states](/wiki/Communist_state) such as the USSR, especially under Stalin, has also been much debated, varyingly being labelled a form of [bureaucratic collectivism](/wiki/Bureaucratic_collectivism), [state capitalism](/wiki/State_capitalism), [state socialism](/wiki/State_socialism), or a totally unique [mode of production](/wiki/Mode_of_production). The USSR implemented a broad range of policies over a long period of time, with a large amount of conflicting policies being implemented by different leaders. Some have a positive view of it whilst others are critical towards the country, calling it a repressive [oligarchy](/wiki/Oligarchy). The opinions on the USSR are complex and have changed over time, with different generations having different views on the matter as well as on Soviet policies corresponding to separate time periods during its history.

2001 stamp of [Moldova](/wiki/Moldova) shows [Yuri Gagarin](/wiki/Yuri_Gagarin), the first human in space.

Western academicians published various analyses of the post-Soviet states' development, claiming that the dissolution was followed by a severe drop in economic and social conditions in these countries, including a rapid increase in poverty, crime, corruption, unemployment, homelessness, rates of disease, infant mortality and domestic violence, as well as demographic losses, income inequality and the rise of an [oligarchical class](/wiki/Russian_oligarchs), along with decreases in calorie intake, life expectancy, adult literacy, and income. Between 1988–1989 and 1993–1995, the [Gini ratio](/wiki/Gini_ratio) (a measure of inequality) increased by an average of 9 percentage points for all former Soviet republics. According to Western analysis, the economic shocks that accompanied wholesale [privatization](/wiki/Privatization) were associated with sharp increases in mortality, Russia, Kazakhstan, Latvia, Lithuania, and Estonia saw a tripling of unemployment and a 42% increase in male death rates between 1991 and 1994, and in the following decades, only five or six of the post-communist states are on a path to joining the wealthy capitalist West while most are falling behind, some to such an extent that it will take over fifty years to catch up to where they were before the fall of the Soviet Bloc. As of 2011, the experience of the former Soviet republics was mixed, with some having recovered in terms of [gross domestic product](/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) and others not. There are large wealth disparities, and many post-soviet economies are described as oligarchic.

Since the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union), annual polling by the [Levada Center](/wiki/Levada_Center) has shown that over 50% of Russia's population regretted this event, with the only exception to this being in 2012 when support for the Soviet Union dipped below 50 percent. A 2018 poll showed that 66% of [Russians](/wiki/Russians) regretted the fall of the Soviet Union, setting a 15-year record, and the majority of these regretting opinions came from people older than 55. In 2020, polls conducted by the Levada Center found that 75% of Russians agreed that the Soviet era was the greatest era in their country's history.

According to the New Russia Barometer (NRB) polls by the Centre for the Study of Public Policy, 50% of Russian respondents reported a positive impression of the Soviet Union in 1991. This increased to about 75% of NRB respondents in 2000, dropping slightly to 71% in 2009. Throughout the 2000s, an average of 32% of NRB respondents supported the restoration of the Soviet Union.

In a 2021 poll, a record 70% of Russians indicated they had a mostly/very favourable view of [Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin). In [Armenia](/wiki/Armenia), 12% of respondents said the USSR collapse did good, while 66% said it did harm. In [Kyrgyzstan](/wiki/Kyrgyzstan), 16% of respondents said the collapse of the USSR did good, while 61% said it did harm. In a 2018 [Rating Sociological Group](/wiki/Sociological_group_%22RATING%22) poll, 47% of [Ukrainian](/wiki/Ukrainians) respondents had a positive opinion of Soviet leader [Leonid Brezhnev](/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev), who ruled the Soviet Union from 1964 to 1982, while viewing [Lenin](/wiki/Lenin), Stalin, and [Gorbachev](/wiki/Gorbachev) very negatively. A 2021 poll conducted by the Levada Center found that 49% of Russians prefer the USSR's political system, while 18% prefer the current political system and 16% would prefer a [Western democracy](/wiki/Western_democracy). A further 62% of people polled preferred the Soviet system of central planning, while 24% prefer a market-based system. According to the Levada Center's polls, the primary reasons cited for Soviet nostalgia are the advantages of the shared economic union between the Soviet republics, including perceived financial stability. This was referenced by up to 53% of respondents in 2016. At least 43% also lamented the loss of the Soviet Union's global political superpower status. About 31% cited the loss of social trust and capital. The remainder of the respondents cited a mix of reasons ranging from practical travel difficulties to a sense of national displacement.

The 1941–1945 period of World War II is still known in Russia as the '[Great Patriotic War](/wiki/Great_Patriotic_War_(term))'. The war became a topic of great importance in cinema, literature, history lessons at school, the mass media, and the arts. As a result of the [massive losses](/wiki/World_War_II_casualties_of_the_Soviet_Union) suffered by the military and civilians during the conflict, [Victory Day](/wiki/Victory_Day_(9_May)) celebrated on 9 May is still one of the most important and emotional dates in Russia. Catherine Wanner asserts that Victory Day commemorations are a vehicle for Soviet nostalgia, as they "kept alive a mythology of Soviet grandeur, of solidarity among the *Sovietskii narod*, and of a sense of self as citizen of a superpower state".

Russian Victory Day parades are organized annually in most cities, with the central military parade taking place in [Moscow](/wiki/Moscow) (just as during the Soviet times). Additionally, the recently introduced [Immortal Regiment](/wiki/Immortal_Regiment) on 9 May sees millions of Russians carry the portraits of their relatives who fought in the war. Russia also [retains other Soviet holidays](/wiki/Public_holidays_in_Russia), such as the [Defender of the Fatherland Day](/wiki/Defender_of_the_Fatherland_Day) (23 February), [International Women's Day](/wiki/International_Women%27s_Day) (8 March), and [International Workers' Day](/wiki/International_Workers%27_Day#Russia).

### In the former Soviet republics

See also: [Anti-Sovietism](/wiki/Anti-Sovietism), [Anti-Russian sentiment](/wiki/Anti-Russian_sentiment), and [Decommunization in Ukraine](/wiki/Decommunization_in_Ukraine)

People in the [Donetsk People's Republic](/wiki/Donetsk_People%27s_Republic) celebrate the annual [Victory Day](/wiki/Victory_Day_(9_May)) over [Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany), 9 May 2018

Protest against [Ukrainian decommunization policies](/wiki/Decommunization_in_Ukraine) in Donetsk, 2014. The red banner reads, "Our homeland USSR".

In some post-Soviet republics, there is a more negative view of the USSR, although there is no unanimity on the matter. In large part due to the [Holodomor](/wiki/Holodomor), ethnic [Ukrainians](/wiki/Ukrainians) have a negative view of the Soviet Union. [Russian](/wiki/Russian_language_in_Ukraine)-speaking Ukrainians of Ukraine's southern and eastern regions have a more positive view of the USSR. In some countries with internal conflict, there is also nostalgia for the USSR, especially for [refugees](/wiki/Refugee) of the [post-Soviet conflicts](/wiki/Post-Soviet_conflicts) who have been forced to flee their homes and have been displaced. The many Russian enclaves in the former USSR republics such as [Transnistria](/wiki/Transnistria) have in a general a positive remembrance of it.

### For China

As its counterpart as a powerful communist state, the [Chinese Communist Party](/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party) (CCP) has continually placed an emphasis on understanding the Soviet Union and its collapse as lessons for itself. In 2011, the CCP completed a study focusing on four reasons for the Soviet collapse. First, Gorbachev's rapid pursuit of democracy which undermined the centrality of the Communist Party. Second, rapid privatization of state-owned enterprises. Third, the end of the ideological monopoly of the Communist Party, leading to historical nihilism and attacks on socialism. Fourth, the West's promotion of a [peaceful evolution](/wiki/Peaceful_evolution), cultivating a pro-West "[fifth column](/wiki/Fifth_column)" in Soviet society.

A 2023 [Center for Strategic and International Studies](/wiki/Center_for_Strategic_and_International_Studies) report argued modern Chinese scholarship's [attributes the Soviet collapse](/wiki/Historical_nihilism#Collapse_of_the_Soviet_Union) primarily to its concept of [historical nihilism](/wiki/Historical_nihilism), equated to the penetration of Western ideas into society. In December 1989, then-leader [Jiang Zemin](/wiki/Jiang_Zemin) first attributed both the [fall of communism in Eastern Europe](/wiki/Fall_of_communism) and the [Tiananmen Square protests](/wiki/1989_Tiananmen_Square_protests_and_massacre) to historical nihilism. A second current in Chinese writing are from [Sovietologists](/wiki/Sovietologist) who argue the [Communist Party of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union)'s institutions and policies was more responsible for collapse than its ideology. Despite [Xi Jinping](/wiki/Xi_Jinping)'s focus on the historical nihilism current, he stated in 2021 "the Soviet Communist Party separated itself from the people and became a privileged bureaucratic group". The report also noted that between 1960 and 1980, [China's state propaganda](/wiki/Propaganda_in_China) chose to publish "gray-cover books" ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 灰皮书, [romanized](/wiki/Romanization_of_Chinese): *huī pí shū*), a large range of socialist and anti-socialist foreign literature criticizing the Soviet system. These included [Leon Trotsky](/wiki/Leon_Trotsky)'s *[The Revolution Betrayed](/wiki/The_Revolution_Betrayed)* and *[Stalin](/wiki/Stalin:_An_Appraisal_of_the_Man_and_His_Influence)*, works of [Eduard Bernstein](/wiki/Eduard_Bernstein) and [Karl Kautsky](/wiki/Karl_Kautsky), [Milovan Djilas](/wiki/Milovan_Djilas)' *[The New Class](/wiki/The_New_Class:_An_Analysis_of_the_Communist_System)*, and [Friedrich Hayek](/wiki/Friedrich_Hayek)'s *[The Road to Serfdom](/wiki/The_Road_to_Serfdom)*. The report suggested these publications influenced interest in the Soviet failures among Chinese leaders Xi, Jiang, and [Deng Xiaoping](/wiki/Deng_Xiaoping).

### For the political left

#### Criticism

See also: [Criticism of communist party rule § Left-wing criticism](/wiki/Criticism_of_communist_party_rule#Left-wing_criticism)

The left's view of the USSR is complex. While some leftists regard the USSR as an example of state capitalism or that it was an oligarchical state, other leftists admire [Vladimir Lenin](/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin) and the [Russian Revolution](/wiki/Russian_Revolution). [Council communists](/wiki/Council_communists) generally view the USSR as failing to create [class consciousness](/wiki/Class_consciousness), turning into a corrupt state in which the elite controlled society.

[Trotskyists](/wiki/Trotskyists) believe that the ascendancy of the Stalinist bureaucracy ensured a [degenerated](/wiki/Degenerated_workers%27_state) or [deformed workers' state](/wiki/Deformed_workers%27_state), where the capitalist elite have been replaced by an unaccountable bureaucratic elite and there is no true democracy or workers' control of industry. In particular, American Trotskyist [David North](/wiki/David_North_(socialist)) noted that the generation of [bureaucrats](/wiki/Nomenklatura) that rose to power under Stalin's tutelage presided over the [stagnation](/wiki/Stagnation_of_the_Soviet_Union) and [breakdown](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) of the Soviet Union.

Many [anti-Stalinist leftists](/wiki/Anti-Stalinist_left) such as anarchists are extremely critical of Soviet authoritarianism and [repression](/wiki/Nabat#Decline). Much of the criticism it receives is centered around [massacres in the Soviet Union](/wiki/List_of_massacres_in_the_Soviet_Union), the centralized [hierarchy](/wiki/Hierarchy) present in the USSR and mass [political repression](/wiki/Political_repression_in_the_Soviet_Union) as well as violence towards government critics and [political dissidents](/wiki/Soviet_dissidents) such as other leftists. Critics also point towards its failure to implement any substantial [worker cooperatives](/wiki/Worker_cooperatives) or implementing worker liberation, as well as corruption and the Soviet authoritarian nature.

Anarchists are also critical of the country, labeling the Soviet system as *[red fascism](/wiki/Red_fascism)*. Factors contributing to the anarchist animosity towards the USSR included the Soviet destruction of the [Makhnovist movement](/wiki/Makhnovist_movement) after an initial alliance, the suppression of the anarchist [Kronstadt rebellion](/wiki/Kronstadt_rebellion), and the defeat of the rival anarchist factions by the Soviet-supported Communist faction during the [Spanish Civil War](/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War).

[Maoists](/wiki/Maoists) also have a mixed opinion on the USSR, viewing it negatively during the [Sino-Soviet Split](/wiki/Sino-Soviet_Split) and denouncing it as revisionist and reverted to capitalism. The Chinese government in 1963 articulated its criticism of the USSR's system and promoted China's ideological line as an alternative.

After the dissolution of the Soviet Union, the [Japanese Communist Party](/wiki/Japanese_Communist_Party) (JCP) released a press statement titled "We welcome the end of a party which embodied the historical evil of [great power](/wiki/Great_power) [chauvinism](/wiki/Chauvinism) and [hegemonism](/wiki/Hegemonism)".

[Noam Chomsky](/wiki/Noam_Chomsky) called the collapse of the Soviet Union "a small victory for socialism, not only because of the fall of one of the most anti-socialist states in the world, where working people had fewer rights than in the West, but also because it freed the term 'socialism' from the burden of being associated in the propaganda systems of East and West with Soviet tyranny—for the East, in order to benefit from the aura of authentic socialism, for the West, in order to demonize the concept." Some scholars on the left have posited that the end of the Soviet Union and [communism](/wiki/Communism) as a global force allowed [neoliberal](/wiki/Neoliberal) [capitalism](/wiki/Capitalism) to become a global system, which has resulted in rising [economic inequality](/wiki/Economic_inequality).

#### Praise

See also: [Anti anti-communism](/wiki/Anti_anti-communism)

A 1986 study published in the *[American Journal of Public Health](/wiki/American_Journal_of_Public_Health)* claimed that, citing [World Bank](/wiki/World_Bank) data, the Soviet model provided a better [quality of life](/wiki/Quality_of_life) and [human development](/wiki/Human_development_(economics)) than market economies at the same level of economic development in most cases.

In her 2012 book *The Communist Horizon*, [Jodi Dean](/wiki/Jodi_Dean) argued that there is a double standard among all sides of the political spectrum, including [conservatives](/wiki/Conservatives), [liberals](/wiki/Liberalism), and [social democrats](/wiki/Social_democrats), in how communism and capitalism are perceived nearly two decades after the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union). Dean stated that the worst excesses of capitalism are often minimized, while communism is often equated only with the Soviet Union, and experiments in Eastern Europe, Latin America, Africa, and Asia are often ignored, with an emphasis placed on the [Stalin era](/wiki/Stalin_era) and its violent excesses including [gulags](/wiki/Gulags), [purges](/wiki/Purges_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), [droughts and famines](/wiki/Droughts_and_famines_in_Russia_and_the_Soviet_Union), and almost no consideration for the [industrialization](/wiki/Industrialization_in_the_Soviet_Union) and [modernization](/wiki/Modernization) of the [Soviet economy](/wiki/Soviet_economy), the successes of [Soviet science](/wiki/Soviet_science) (such as the [Soviet space program](/wiki/Soviet_space_program)), or the rise in the [standard of living](/wiki/Standard_of_living) for the once predominantly [agrarian society](/wiki/Agrarian_society). The dissolution of the Soviet Union is therefore seen as the proof that communism cannot work, allowing for all left-wing criticism of the excesses of neoliberal capitalism to be silenced, for the alternatives would supposedly inevitably result in economic inefficiency and violent authoritarianism.

[Michael Parenti](/wiki/Michael_Parenti)'s 1997 book *Blackshirts and Reds* takes the controversial position of defending the Soviet Union and other communist countries from reflexive condemnation, arguing that they featured a number of advantages over capitalist countries, e.g., by ensuring less economic inequality. He later argues that the Soviet Union's "well-publicized deficiencies and injustices" were exacerbated by the [Russian Civil War](/wiki/Russian_Civil_War), the [Nazi-led multinational invasion](/wiki/Operation_Barbarossa), and by non-military modes of capitalist intervention against the [Eastern Bloc](/wiki/Eastern_Bloc). Moreover, he claims that "pure socialists" and "[left anticommunists](/wiki/Left-wing_anti-communism)" had failed to specify a viable alternative to the "siege socialism" implemented in the Soviet model. Parenti argued the Soviet Union played a crucial role in "tempering the worst impulses of Western capitalism and imperialism" that in the post-Cold War era is "no longer restrained by a competing system" and is now "rolling back the many gains that working people in the West have won over the years". By offering a rare defense of 20th century Communism, *Blackshirts and Reds* has elicited strong reactions from anarchist and Communist publications.

### For the political right

Both [liberals](/wiki/Liberalism_in_the_United_States) and [neoconservatives](/wiki/Neoconservatism) in the United States celebrated the dissolution of the Soviet Union. Ideas such as [Francis Fukuyama](/wiki/Francis_Fukuyama)'s [end of history](/wiki/End_of_history) and [Charles Krauthammer](/wiki/Charles_Krauthammer)'s [unipolar moment](/wiki/Unipolar_moment) gained prominence to describe the victory of Western [liberal democracy](/wiki/Liberal_democracy). At the same time, both sides of the political spectrum in the US criticized the [George H. W. Bush administration](/wiki/George_H._W._Bush_administration) for its comparative cautiousness and political support of Gorbachev over more pro-democracy and nationalist forces in the Soviet Union.
