# Everybody Wants to Rule the World

"**Everybody Wants to Rule the World**" is a song by the English [pop rock](/wiki/Pop_rock) band [Tears for Fears](/wiki/Tears_for_Fears) from their second studio album *[Songs from the Big Chair](/wiki/Songs_from_the_Big_Chair)* (1985). It was written by [Roland Orzabal](/wiki/Roland_Orzabal), [Ian Stanley](/wiki/Ian_Stanley), and [Chris Hughes](/wiki/Chris_Hughes_(musician)) and produced by Hughes. It was released on 18 March 1985 by [Phonogram](/wiki/Phonogram_Inc.), [Mercury](/wiki/Mercury_Records), and [Vertigo Records](/wiki/Vertigo_Records) as the third single from the album. "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" is a [new wave](/wiki/New_wave_music) and [synth-pop](/wiki/Synth-pop) song with lyrics that detail the desire humans have for control and power, and centres on themes of [corruption](/wiki/Corruption).

An international success, the song peaked at number two in Ireland, Australia, and the United Kingdom and at number one in Canada, New Zealand, and on both the US [*Billboard* Hot 100](/wiki/Billboard_Hot_100) and *[Cashbox](/wiki/Cashbox_(magazine))*. It was certified gold by both [Music Canada](/wiki/Music_Canada) (MC) and the [British Phonographic Industry](/wiki/British_Phonographic_Industry) (BPI). Retrospectively, music critics have praised "Everybody Wants to Rule the World", with some ranking the song among the decade's best. Along with "[Shout](/wiki/Shout_(Tears_for_Fears_song))" (1984), it is one of the band's [signature songs](/wiki/Signature_song).

A music video received promotion from [MTV](/wiki/MTV). In 1986, the song won [Best Single](/wiki/Brit_Award_for_British_Single_of_the_Year) at the [Brit Awards](/wiki/Brit_Awards), and was re-recorded by the band as a charity single for the [Sport Aid](/wiki/Sport_Aid) campaign the same year. "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" has been covered extensively since its release, most notably by New Zealand singer [Lorde](/wiki/Lorde) for the soundtrack to the film adaptation of *[The Hunger Games: Catching Fire](/wiki/The_Hunger_Games:_Catching_Fire)* (2013).

## Background and release

"Everybody Wants to Rule the World" was written by [Roland Orzabal](/wiki/Roland_Orzabal), [Ian Stanley](/wiki/Ian_Stanley) and [Chris Hughes](/wiki/Chris_Hughes_(musician)), and produced by Hughes. The song was a "last-minute" addition during recording sessions of *[Songs from the Big Chair](/wiki/Songs_from_the_Big_Chair)* (1985). The decision to include the song in the album came after Orzabal played two chords on his [acoustic guitar](/wiki/Acoustic_guitar) for Hughes. It was recorded in two weeks and added as the final track on the album. According to Orzabal, the final line in the song's chorus, originally written as "Everybody wants to go to war", contributed to his indifference towards the track.

In an interview with *[Mix](/wiki/Mix_(magazine))* magazine, Hughes said that "as a piece of recording history, [the song is] bland as hell". Orzabal's unimpressed reaction to the track during their songwriting sessions prompted Hughes to convince him to record it, in a calculated effort to garner American chart success. After completing their sessions at 6 p.m., they would spend an hour reviewing each recording many times; this helped Orzabal to create the song's guitar figure and change its title. Orzabal acknowledged that the [shuffle beat](/wiki/Swing_time#Swing_as_a_rhythmic_style) used in the song was "alien" to their way of writing music, stating it was "jolly rather than square and rigid in the manner of "[Shout](/wiki/Shout_(Tears_for_Fears_song))", but it continued the process of becoming more extrovert." In a 2025 [podcast](/wiki/Podcast), Orzabal stated that the basic rhythm track had been inspired in part by [Simple Minds](/wiki/Simple_Minds)' "[Waterfront](/wiki/Waterfront_(song))" and in part by [Linx](/wiki/Linx_(band))'s 1981 hit "Throw Away the Key". [Curt Smith](/wiki/Curt_Smith), the song's lead singer, said the themes were "quite serious — it's about everybody wanting power, about warfare and the misery it causes." In an interview with Guitar World, Orzabal recalled that the vocals were pitched up, as they felt the melody was not fast enough.

"Everybody Wants to Rule the World" was first released on 22 March 1985 through [Phonogram](/wiki/Phonogram_Inc.), [Mercury](/wiki/Mercury_Records) and [Vertigo Records](/wiki/Vertigo_Records) as the third single from *Songs from the Big Chair*. The song was released for sale (as a [7-inch](/wiki/LP_record), [10-inch](/wiki/LP_record) and [12-inch](/wiki/LP_record) vinyl set), which included its B-side, interviews from the band and different versions of the song. To accommodate the vinyl release, a CD video set was also distributed and included the song's music video along with audios of bonus tracks.

## Composition and lyrical interpretation

"Everybody Wants to Rule the World" is a [new wave](/wiki/New_wave_music) and [synth-pop](/wiki/Synth-pop) song. Sheet music for the song is set in the [key](/wiki/Key_(music)) of [D major](/wiki/D_major) (original is slightly detuned) with a [**12**
**8** time signature](/wiki/Time_signature#Compound_time_signatures) and a [tempo](/wiki/Tempo) of 112 beats per minute. The band stated that the driving [shuffle rhythm](/wiki/Swing_(jazz_performance_style)) was influenced by [Simple Minds](/wiki/Simple_Minds)' 1983 song "[Waterfront](/wiki/Waterfront_(song))", and [Linx](/wiki/Linx_(band))'s 1981 song "Throw Away the Key". "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" incorporates [synthesizers](/wiki/Synthesizer), [guitar](/wiki/Guitar), a shuffle groove, two [guitar solos](/wiki/Guitar_solo), and [keyboards](/wiki/Musical_keyboard).

According to [Joe Strummer](/wiki/Joe_Strummer) (of [The Clash](/wiki/The_Clash)) in a 1988 interview, he was in a restaurant and saw Orzabal, whereupon he told him that "you owe me a fiver", explaining that the title of "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" was an exact lift of the first line of the middle eight in "[Charlie Don't Surf](/wiki/Sandinista!)". According to Strummer, Orzabal simply reached into his pocket and gave him a five pound note. The song's lyrics detail the desire humans have for control and power. In 2017, Tal Rosenberg of *[Pitchfork](/wiki/Pitchfork_(website))* stated that its lyrics could be applied in different scenarios such as the environment ("Turn your back on [mother nature](/wiki/Mother_nature)"), short-lived financial success ("Help me make the most of freedom and of pleasure/Nothing ever lasts forever"), [dictatorial](/wiki/Dictatorship) rule ("Even while we sleep/We will find you"), and the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) ("Holding hands while the [walls](/wiki/Berlin_Wall) come tumbling down").

The song's lyrics have elicited different political interpretations. A writer for *[The Economist](/wiki/The_Economist)* called the track "a Cold War anthem" and noted its "timeless message", stating that "the song's lyrics speak to the anxieties of every age". Marc Ambinder from *[The Atlantic](/wiki/The_Atlantic)* used the lyrics "Say that you'll never, never, never need it / One headline, why believe it? / Everybody wants to rule the world" in his article about the United States government's use of "[original classified authority](/wiki/Classified_information_in_the_United_States)" and the abuse of power between the [branches of government](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States). Dominic Pino of *[National Review](/wiki/National_Review)* described the track as a "conservative pop song", noting the lyrics' tension between "personal ambition" and "channeling that personal ambition to good ends", comparing these themes to [James Madison](/wiki/James_Madison)'s concerns about private interest in the [*Federalist Papers.*](/wiki/The_Federalist_Papers) Curt Smith challenged this interpretation.

Tears for Fears revisited the song and its message in a 2017 interview with [Yahoo! Music](/wiki/Yahoo!_Music), stating that the song's themes were still "just as poignant" as they were when they first wrote it. They mentioned that they discussed the Cold War with "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" and *Songs from the Big Chair* but that was the "[U.S. and Russia](/wiki/Soviet_Union%E2%80%93United_States_relations) then, and now the concern is more with the [U.S. and [North] Korea](/wiki/North_Korea%E2%80%93United_States_relations)."

## Reception and legacy

*[Consequence of Sound](/wiki/Consequence_of_Sound)* editor Michael Roffman praised the group for being able to produce a "timeless and influential composition" with minimal effort. Roffman also noted that "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" was appropriate when it was first released, calling it a "meditative commentary on an era that was so corrupt economically and spiritually." [AllMusic](/wiki/AllMusic)'s Stanton Swihart expressed in his retrospective review that the group "perfectly captured the zeitgeist of the mid-'80s while impossibly managing to also create a dreamy, timeless pop classic." *[Pitchfork](/wiki/Pitchfork_(website))* called it a song with "near-universal appeal", as well as a staple for "classic-rock radio, pharmacies, bars, and parties". In their review for the best albums of the 1980s, Eric Henderson from *[Slant Magazine](/wiki/Slant_Magazine)* stated that the song "seems like one of the great indictments of the materialism and false triumphalism of the decade."

"Everybody Wants to Rule the World" was voted the 25th best single in *The Village Voice*'s [Pazz & Jop](/wiki/Pazz_%26_Jop) critics' poll for 1985 with 17 points, sharing the spot with [Tom Petty and the Heartbreakers](/wiki/Tom_Petty_and_the_Heartbreakers)' "[Don't Come Around Here No More](/wiki/Don%27t_Come_Around_Here_No_More)" (1985) and [Sade](/wiki/Sade_(singer))'s "[Smooth Operator](/wiki/Smooth_Operator)" (1984). *Pitchfork* placed the song at number 82 on their list for the best songs of the 80s expressing that "underneath the [synth-pop](/wiki/Synth-pop) sheen, its vague message" and its lesson in how power-driven society could be, the song was able to reach "[Reagan](/wiki/Ronald_Reagan) and [Margaret](/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher)-era youth fed up with political greed". Kevin Korber from *Spectrum Culture* ranked the song at 24 on his 80s decade list, calling the song a "testament to how much of a free-for-all the pop landscape was in the 1980s". Korber dismissed the song's vague lyrics but praised its complex melodic structure, saying it was "both the perfect representation of its time and a timeless composition". *Treble* included the song at number 49 on their decade list. In 2021, *Rolling Stone* ranked the song at number 319 in their updated list of the [500 Greatest Songs of All Time](/wiki/Rolling_Stone%27s_500_Greatest_Songs_of_All_Time).

In 1986, the song won "[Best Single](/wiki/Brit_Award_for_British_Single_of_the_Year)" at the [Brit Awards](/wiki/Brit_Awards). Orzabal argued that the song deserved to win the Ivor Novello International Hit of the Year award, claiming that the winner—"[19](/wiki/19_(song))" by [Paul Hardcastle](/wiki/Paul_Hardcastle)—was not an actual song, but only a "dialogue collage". In 2015, 30 years after its release, the song was honoured at the annual [BMI Awards](/wiki/BMI_Awards) in London for achieving 6 million radio airplays. "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" is regarded as the group's [signature song](/wiki/Signature_song), along with "[Shout](/wiki/Shout_(Tears_for_Fears_song))" (1984).

"Everybody Wants to Rule the World" was banned for broadcast by the [BBC](/wiki/BBC) for the duration of the first [Gulf War](/wiki/Gulf_War) (2 August 1990 – 28 February 1991) due to the song's political themes. For several years, the song was used as the title theme to the political [HBO](/wiki/HBO) talk show, *[Dennis Miller Live](/wiki/Dennis_Miller_Live)*.

## Commercial performance

In the United Kingdom, "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" debuted at number 16 on the [UK Singles Chart](/wiki/UK_Singles_Chart), in the issue dated 24 March 1985. On the week of 14 April 1985, the song peaked at number two, where it stayed for an additional week, both times being blocked from the top spot by "[We Are the World](/wiki/We_Are_the_World)" by [USA for Africa](/wiki/USA_for_Africa). "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" re-entered several times the UK Singles Chart between 2022 and 2026 and received a quintuple platinum certification by the [British Phonographic Industry](/wiki/British_Phonographic_Industry) (BPI) on 30 January 2026 for 3,000,000 sales. In addition, as of May 2023, is the 12th most [streamed](/wiki/Streaming_media) song from the 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s by British artists in the United Kingdom. The song peaked at number three in Belgium, and at number two in both the Netherlands and Ireland.

In the United States, "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" debuted at number 70 on the [*Billboard* Hot 100](/wiki/Billboard_Hot_100) in the issue dated 16 March 1985. On the week of 27 April 1985, the song rose to number 18. On the week of 8 June 1985, the song moved to #1, replacing [Wham!](/wiki/Wham!)'s "[Everything She Wants](/wiki/Everything_She_Wants)" (1984) on the chart; it would spend a total of two weeks in this position. The song was a commercial success in other American markets, peaking at number two on the [Adult Contemporary](/wiki/Adult_Contemporary_(chart)) and [Top Rock Tracks](/wiki/Mainstream_Rock_(chart)) and charting at number one on the [Hot Dance Club Play](/wiki/Dance_Club_Songs), [Hot Dance Music/Maxi-Singles Sales](/wiki/Dance/Electronic_Singles_Sales), and *[Cash Box](/wiki/Cashbox_(magazine))* charts. It ranks as the 345th best-charting single of all time in the United States. In Canada, the song reached the number one spot, earning a gold certification from [Music Canada](/wiki/Music_Canada) (MC) for 40,000 sales shipments on 1 May 1985.

In Australia, the song reached the number two position. On the report dated 19 May 1985, the song debuted at number four on the [New Zealand Top 40 Singles](/wiki/Recorded_Music_NZ) chart; it would later peak at number one. It was certified Gold by the [Recorded Music NZ](/wiki/Recorded_Music_NZ) (RMNZ) for sales of 10,000.

## B-side: "Pharaohs"

"Pharaohs" uses a 1984 recording of [Brian Perkins](/wiki/Brian_Perkins) reading a shipping forecast on various [North Sea](/wiki/North_Sea) locations (*pictured*).

"Pharaohs" is the [B-side](/wiki/A-side_and_B-side) to the "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" single. It samples a recording of [BBC Radio 4](/wiki/BBC_Radio_4) announcer [Brian Perkins](/wiki/Brian_Perkins) reading the [Shipping Forecast](/wiki/Shipping_Forecast) for the [North Sea](/wiki/North_Sea) region of the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom). The title of the song has an identical enunciation to the [Faroe Islands](/wiki/Faroe_Islands) ("Faroes"), one of the places referenced in the forecast. Orzabal, Ian Stanley, [Curt Smith](/wiki/Curt_Smith) and Hughes share writing credits. "Pharaohs" is included on the [Groove Armada](/wiki/Groove_Armada) compilation album *[Back to Mine](/wiki/Back_to_Mine:_Groove_Armada)* (2000). Chris Hughes wrote about the song in the liner notes of *[Saturnine Martial & Lunatic](/wiki/Saturnine_Martial_%26_Lunatic)* (1996), saying:

> No matter how horrifying the conditions may really be, the voice reading the shipping forecast is deliberately calm and relaxed. Recorded at [the Wool Hall](/wiki/The_Wool_Hall) for the B-side of 'Everybody' in a calm and relaxed way.

"Pharaohs" shipping forecast read by [Brian Perkins](/wiki/Brian_Perkins):

> There are warnings of gales in Viking, Forties, Cromarty, Forth, Fisher, Dover, Wight, Portland, Plymouth, Finisterre, Sole, Lundy, Fastnet, Shannon, Rockall, Malin, Hebrides, Bailey, Fair Isle, Faroes and Southeast Iceland.
> The general synopsis at one eight double-O: low just north of Viking, nine double-seven, moving steadily east-northeast.
> Low 300 miles south of Iceland. Atlantic low forming, moving steadily northeast.
> A ridge of high pressure has swayed between North and South Utsire. The area forecast for the next twenty-four hours. Viking, Forties, Cromarty, Forth.

## Music video

The roadside attraction [Cabazon Dinosaurs](/wiki/Cabazon_Dinosaurs) (*pictured*) is shown briefly in the video.

The video for "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" was directed by [Nigel Dick](/wiki/Nigel_Dick). It was filmed in [Los Angeles](/wiki/Los_Angeles), Desert Hot Springs, Palm Springs, and [Cabazon](/wiki/Cabazon,_California), [California](/wiki/California). [Curt Smith](/wiki/Curt_Smith) admitted to *Pitchfork* that the video shoot was a "disaster"; Dick was "in tears" on the second night of shooting. Smith also mentioned that there was an accident while filming the "dirt bikes and four-wheel [*[sic](/wiki/Sic)*] off-road vehicles" scene, with one child flying out of a vehicle and smashing his head, leaving him unconscious.

The video begins with Smith driving a vintage [Austin-Healey 3000](/wiki/Austin-Healey_3000) sports car while a toddler points toy guns in Smith's direction. The car is then seen driving through a desert, on Interstate 10; this scene is intercut with the band performing the song in a studio. In the chorus, a plane is seen. Smith parks the car at the Wheel Inn diner and makes a call from a telephone booth. The camera pans to show a statue of a prospector and his donkey in the foreground. Smith leaves the diner in the car while singing the song's lyrics. The following scene shows two men in suits performing synchronised dance movements in front of two gas pumps. Shots of young people riding [three-wheeled ATVs](/wiki/All-terrain_vehicle#Three-wheeler_era_(1967–1987)) and [dirt bikes](/wiki/Types_of_motorcycles#Off-road) through desert sand dunes are also shown. Smith is then shown singing in the desert wearing black sunglasses as many of the dirt bike and ATV riders approach from behind him and pass to either side.

The music video promoted the group in America, due to [heavy rotation](/wiki/Rotation_(music)) on the music video channel [MTV](/wiki/MTV). *[HuffPost](/wiki/HuffPost)* editor Daryl Deino ranked the video at number three on their year-end list for best music videos of 1985 stating that the video "represents pure Americana as it was in 1985". Deino also mentioned that the video "proves that at times, artists were able to do so much with so little."

"Everybody Wants to Run the World" is a re-recording of the band's song "Everybody Wants to Rule the World". The reworked single was released in May 1986 as the theme song for the [Sport Aid](/wiki/Sport_Aid) campaign, a charitable event held to raise money for famine relief in Africa. It was a success in the [UK Singles Chart](/wiki/UK_Singles_Chart), becoming the band's sixth top 5 hit, peaking at number 5 in June 1986. The song also reached number 4 in Ireland.

| [Producers](/wiki/Record_producer) | - Peter Shurkin - [Joel Little](/wiki/Joel_Little) |

[New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand) singer [Lorde](/wiki/Lorde) recorded a cover of the song for the [soundtrack](/wiki/The_Hunger_Games:_Catching_Fire_%E2%80%93_Original_Motion_Picture_Soundtrack) of *[The Hunger Games: Catching Fire](/wiki/The_Hunger_Games:_Catching_Fire)* (2013). The cover was produced by Peter Shurkin and [Joel Little](/wiki/Joel_Little) with arrangement by [Michael A. Levine](/wiki/Michael_A._Levine) and Lucas Cantor. Orzabal praised Lorde for reinventing the cover, stating that the group finds it interesting when artists take what they do and reinterpret it. For their Rule the World Tour, the duo uses this version to launch their show.

Critics were positively receptive to the cover, some praised it for its darker atmosphere mood although others criticized it for stripping away the song's original upbeat composition. David Haglund from *[Slate](/wiki/Slate_(magazine))* stated that while the song "doesn't top the great original, it does memorably reinvent it". Sam Lansky from *[Idolator](/wiki/Idolator_(website))* called the cover "haunting and melodramatic". Conversely, *Stereogum* editor Nate Patrin criticized the chorus and production but praised the "aloof strangeness" in Lorde's vocals for being able to carry the song "past the usual Dramatic Reenvisionings". *[Paste](/wiki/Paste_(magazine))* and *[Exclaim!](/wiki/Exclaim!)* included the cover in their respective 2013 year-end lists for covers, while the former ranked it 13th on their decade-end list for covers. Her cover appeared on the [New Zealand Singles Chart](/wiki/Official_New_Zealand_Music_Chart) at number 14. It reached number 53 in Australia, number 65 in the United Kingdom, number 93 in France, and number 27 on the U.S. [Hot Rock Songs](/wiki/List_of_number-one_Billboard_Rock_Songs) chart.

### Other versions

[Patti Smith](/wiki/Patti_Smith) made a cover of the song in 2007 for her album of the covers [Twelve](/wiki/Twelve_(Patti_Smith_album)).

[Ted Leo and the Pharmacists](/wiki/Ted_Leo_and_the_Pharmacists) covered the song in 2010 for the inaugural episode of *[The A.V. Club](/wiki/The_A.V._Club)'*s *[A.V. Undercover](/wiki/A.V._Undercover)* web series.

[Relient K](/wiki/Relient_K) recorded a version of the song for their 2011 cover album *[Is for Karaoke](/wiki/Is_for_Karaoke)*.

[Weezer](/wiki/Weezer) covered the song on their 2019 [self-titled cover album](/wiki/Weezer_(Teal_Album)). They were joined by Orzabal and Smith to perform the song live at [Coachella 2019](/wiki/Coachella_2019) on April 14, and again two days later on *[Jimmy Kimmel Live!](/wiki/Jimmy_Kimmel_Live!)*.

[Niall Horan](/wiki/Niall_Horan) covered a live version of the song mixed with his own song "If You Leave Me" from his 2023 album *[The Show](/wiki/The_Show_(Niall_Horan_album)): The Encore*.

Filipino indie rock band [Hey June!](/wiki/Hey_June!) released a cover version as a single in 2024.

The song was [interpolated](/wiki/Interpolation_(popular_music)) by [Miley Cyrus](/wiki/Miley_Cyrus) for a remix of "[Black Skinhead](/wiki/Black_Skinhead)" by [Kanye West](/wiki/Kanye_West) which leaked online in January 2016.

American rapper [Lil Baby](/wiki/Lil_Baby) sampled the song in "The World is Yours to Take" for the [Budweiser](/wiki/Budweiser) anthem of the [2022 FIFA World Cup](/wiki/2022_FIFA_World_Cup) in Qatar and also appeared on the [FIFA World Cup Qatar 2022 Official Soundtrack](/wiki/FIFA_World_Cup_Qatar_2022_Official_Soundtrack).

The song appears at the end of *[Despicable Me 4](/wiki/Despicable_Me_4)*, where [Gru](/wiki/Gru) and Maxime Le Mal are in prison putting on a talent show for all of the prisoners. The franchise's villains from previous films return in this song. The cover version, performed by the film's cast, also appears on [the movie's soundtrack](/wiki/Despicable_Me_4_(soundtrack)).

The song was covered by British singer [Robbie Williams](/wiki/Robbie_Williams) for the 2019 [Trevor Horn](/wiki/Trevor_Horn) album *[Reimagines the Eighties](/wiki/Reimagines_the_Eighties)*.
