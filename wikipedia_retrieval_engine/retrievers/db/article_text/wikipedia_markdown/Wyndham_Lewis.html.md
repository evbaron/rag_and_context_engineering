This article is about the Vorticist painter and author. For others of that name, see [Wyndham Lewis (disambiguation)](/wiki/Wyndham_Lewis_(disambiguation)).

**Percy Wyndham Lewis** (18 November 1882 – 7 March 1957) was a [Canadian-born](/wiki/Canadians_in_the_United_Kingdom) British writer, painter and critic. He was a co-founder of the [Vorticist](/wiki/Vorticist) movement in art and edited *[Blast](/wiki/Blast_(British_magazine))*, the literary magazine of the Vorticists.

His novels include *[Tarr](/wiki/Tarr)* (1916–17) and *The Human Age* trilogy, comprising *The Childermass* (1928), *Monstre Gai* (1955) and *Malign Fiesta* (1955). A fourth volume, *The Trial of Man*, remained unfinished upon his death. He wrote two autobiographical volumes: *[Blasting and Bombardiering](/wiki/Blasting_and_Bombardiering)* (1937) and *Rude Assignment: A Narrative of my Career Up-to-Date* (1950).

## Life and career

### Early life

Percy Wyndham Lewis was born on 18 November 1882, reputedly on his father's yacht off the [Canadian province](/wiki/Canadian_province) of [Nova Scotia](/wiki/Nova_Scotia). His English mother, Anne Stuart Lewis ([née](/wiki/N%C3%A9e) Prickett), and American father, Charles Edward Lewis, separated about 1893. His mother subsequently returned to England. Lewis was educated in England at [Rugby School](/wiki/Rugby_School) and then, from 16, the [Slade School of Fine Art](/wiki/Slade_School_of_Fine_Art), [University College London](/wiki/University_College_London), but left for Paris without finishing his course. He spent most of the 1900s travelling around Europe and studying art in Paris. Whilst there he attended lectures by [Henri Bergson](/wiki/Henri_Bergson) on [process philosophy](/wiki/Process_philosophy).

### Early work and development of Vorticism (1908–1915)

Wyndham Lewis, 1912, *The Dancers*

Wyndham Lewis, c.1914–15, *Workshop* ([Tate](/wiki/Tate), London)

In 1908 Lewis moved to [London](/wiki/London), England, where he would reside for much of his life. In 1909 he published his first work, accounts of his travels in [Brittany](/wiki/Brittany), in [Ford Madox Ford](/wiki/Ford_Madox_Ford)'s *The English Review*. He was a founding member of the [Camden Town Group](/wiki/Camden_Town_Group), which brought him into close contact with the [Bloomsbury Group](/wiki/Bloomsbury_Group), particularly [Roger Fry](/wiki/Roger_Fry) and [Clive Bell](/wiki/Clive_Bell), with whom he soon fell out.

In 1912 he exhibited his work at the second [Post-Impressionist](/wiki/Post-Impressionism) exhibition: [Cubo-Futurist](/wiki/Cubo-Futurism) illustrations to *[Timon of Athens](/wiki/Timon_of_Athens)* and three major oil paintings. In 1912 he was commissioned to produce a decorative mural, a drop curtain, and more designs for [The Cave of the Golden Calf](/wiki/The_Cave_of_the_Golden_Calf), an [avant-garde](/wiki/Avant-garde) nightclub and [cabaret](/wiki/Cabaret) on [Heddon Street](/wiki/Heddon_Street).

From 1913 to 1915 Lewis developed the style of geometric [abstraction](/wiki/Abstraction) for which he is best known today, which his friend [Ezra Pound](/wiki/Ezra_Pound) dubbed "[Vorticism](/wiki/Vorticism)". Lewis sought to combine the strong structure of [Cubism](/wiki/Cubism), which he found was not "alive", with the liveliness of [Futurist](/wiki/Futurism_(art)) art, which lacked structure. The combination was a strikingly dramatic critique of modernity. In his early visual works Lewis may have been influenced by Bergson's [process philosophy](/wiki/Process_philosophy). Though he was later savagely critical of Bergson, he admitted in a letter to Theodore Weiss (19 April 1949) that he "began by embracing his evolutionary system." The German philosopher [Friedrich Nietzsche](/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche) was an equally important influence.

Lewis had a brief tenure at Roger Fry's [Omega Workshops](/wiki/Omega_Workshops), but left after a quarrel with Fry over a commission to provide wall decorations for the [Daily Mail](/wiki/Daily_Mail) [Ideal Home Exhibition](/wiki/Ideal_Home_Exhibition), which Lewis believed Fry had misappropriated. He and several other Omega artists started a competing workshop called the [Rebel Art Centre](/wiki/Rebel_Art_Centre). The Centre operated for only four months, but it gave birth to the Vorticist group and its publication, *[Blast](/wiki/Blast_(British_magazine))*. In *Blast* Lewis formally expounded the Vorticist aesthetic in a manifesto, distinguishing it from other avant-garde practices. He also wrote and published a play, *Enemy of the Stars*. It is a proto-absurdist, [Expressionist](/wiki/Expressionism) drama. The Lewis scholar Melania Terrazas identifies it as a precursor to the plays of [Samuel Beckett](/wiki/Samuel_Beckett).

### World War I (1915–1918)

Wyndham Lewis, photograph by [George Charles Beresford](/wiki/George_Charles_Beresford), 1917

In 1915 the Vorticists held their only British exhibition before the movement broke up, largely as a result of the [First World War](/wiki/First_World_War). Lewis himself joined up under the [Derby Scheme](/wiki/Derby_Scheme) in March 1916 just before conscription was brought in. He was assigned to the [Royal Garrison Artillery](/wiki/Royal_Garrison_Artillery) (RGA) and after training was posted to 183rd Siege Battery, RGA, forming at [Weymouth, Dorset](/wiki/Weymouth,_Dorset), in which he served as a [Bombardier](/wiki/Bombardier_(rank)). At the second attempt he was accepted as an officer cadet and went to the cadet school at [Trowbridge](/wiki/Trowbridge) before his battery deployed overseas. On completing his officer training he was commissioned as a [2nd Lieutenant](/wiki/Second_lieutenant) and in January 1917 was posted to the newly-raised [330th Siege Battery, RGA](/wiki/330th_Siege_Battery,_Royal_Garrison_Artillery). 330th Siege Battery embarked on 24 May 1917 for the [Western Front](/wiki/Western_Front_(World_War_I)). It served on the Flanders coast and then at [Ypres](/wiki/Ypres) during the [Third Ypres offensive](/wiki/Battle_of_Passchendaele). Much of Lewis's time was spent in [Forward Observation Posts](/wiki/Artillery_observer) looking down at apparently deserted German lines, registering targets and calling down fire from batteries massed around the rim of the [Ypres Salient](/wiki/Ypres_Salient). He wrote vivid accounts of narrow misses and deadly artillery duels, though not all of these can be corroborated.

After the Third Battle of Ypres Lewis was appointed an official [war artist](/wiki/War_artist) for the [Canadian](/wiki/Government_of_Canada) and [British governments](/wiki/Government_of_the_United_Kingdom). For the Canadians he painted *[A Canadian Gun-pit](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wyndham_Lewis-A_Canadian_Gun-pit.jpg)* (1918) from sketches made on [Vimy Ridge](/wiki/Vimy_Ridge). For the British he painted one of his best-known works, *[A Battery Shelled](/wiki/A_Battery_Shelled)* (1919), drawing on his own experience at Ypres. Lewis exhibited his war drawings and some other paintings of the war in an exhibition, "Guns", in 1918. Although the Vorticist group broke up after the war, Lewis's patron, [John Quinn](/wiki/John_Quinn_(collector)), organised a Vorticist exhibition at the Penguin Club in [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City) in 1917.

Between 1907 and 1911 Lewis had written what would be his first published novel, *[Tarr](/wiki/Tarr)*, which was revised and expanded in 1914–15 and serialised in the London literary magazine *[The Egoist](/wiki/The_Egoist_(periodical))* from April 1916 to November 1917. It was first published in book form in 1918 by [Alfred A. Knopf](/wiki/Alfred_A._Knopf) in New York and by *The Egoist* in London. It is widely regarded as one of the key texts in [literary modernism](/wiki/Literary_modernism).

Lewis later documented his experiences and opinions of this period of his life in the autobiographical *Blasting and Bombardiering* (1937), which covered the time up to 1926.

### *Tyros* and writing (1918–1929)

*Mr Wyndham Lewis as a Tyro*, self-portrait, 1921

After the war Lewis resumed his career as a painter with a major exhibition, *Tyros and Portraits*, at the [Leicester Galleries](/wiki/Leicester_Galleries) in 1921. "Tyros" were satirical caricatures intended to comment on the culture of the "new epoch" that succeeded the First World War. *A Reading of [Ovid](/wiki/Ovid)* and *Mr Wyndham Lewis as a Tyro* are the only surviving oil paintings from this series. Lewis also launched his second magazine, *The Tyro*, of which there were only two issues. The second (1922) contained an important statement of Lewis's visual aesthetic: "Essay on the Objective of Plastic Art in our Time". It was during the early 1920s that he perfected his incisive draughtsmanship.

By the late 1920s he concentrated on writing. He launched another magazine, *The Enemy* (1927–1929), largely written by himself and declaring its belligerent critical stance in its title. The magazine and other theoretical and critical works he published from 1926 to 1929 mark a deliberate separation from the avant-garde and his previous associates. He believed that their work failed to show sufficient critical awareness of those ideologies that worked against truly revolutionary change in the West, and therefore became a vehicle for these pernicious ideologies. His major theoretical and cultural statement from this period is *The Art of Being Ruled* (1926).

*Time and Western Man* (1927) is a cultural and philosophical discussion that includes penetrating critiques of [James Joyce](/wiki/James_Joyce), [Gertrude Stein](/wiki/Gertrude_Stein) and [Ezra Pound](/wiki/Ezra_Pound) that are still read. Lewis also attacked the [process philosophy](/wiki/Process_philosophy) of Bergson, [Samuel Alexander](/wiki/Samuel_Alexander), [Alfred North Whitehead](/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead) and others. By 1931 he was advocating [the art](/wiki/Art_of_ancient_Egypt) of [ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) as impossible to surpass.

### Fiction and political writing (1930–1936)

Lewis in 1929, photographed by [George Charles Beresford](/wiki/George_Charles_Beresford)

In 1930 Lewis published *[The Apes of God](/wiki/The_Apes_of_God)*, a biting satirical attack on the London literary scene, including a long chapter caricaturing the [Sitwell](/wiki/The_Sitwells) family. The writer [Richard Aldington](/wiki/Richard_Aldington), however, found it "the greatest piece of *writing* since *[Ulysses](/wiki/Ulysses_(novel))*", by [James Joyce](/wiki/James_Joyce). In 1937 Lewis published *The Revenge for Love*, set in the period leading up to the [Spanish Civil War](/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War) and regarded by many as his best novel. It is strongly critical of [communist](/wiki/Communism) activity in Spain and presents English intellectual [fellow travellers](/wiki/Fellow_traveller) as deluded.

Despite serious illness necessitating several operations, he was very productive as a critic and painter. He produced a book of poems, *One-Way Song*, in 1933, and a revised version of *Enemy of the Stars*. An important book of critical essays also belongs to this period: *Men without Art* (1934). It grew out of a defence of Lewis's satirical practice in *The Apes of God* and puts forward a theory of "non-moral", or metaphysical, satire. The book is probably best remembered for one of the first commentaries on [William Faulkner](/wiki/William_Faulkner) and a famous essay on [Ernest Hemingway](/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway).

### Return to painting (1936–1941)

Lewis's [Ezra Pound](/wiki/Ezra_Pound), 1919. The portrait is lost.

After becoming better known for his writing than his painting in the 1920s and early 1930s, he returned to more concentrated work on visual art, and paintings from the 1930s and 1940s constitute some of his best-known work. The *[Surrender of Barcelona](/wiki/The_Surrender_of_Barcelona)* (1936–37) makes a significant statement about the [Spanish Civil War](/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War). It was included in an exhibition at the Leicester Galleries in 1937 that Lewis hoped would re-establish his reputation as a painter. After the publication in *[The Times](/wiki/The_Times)* of a letter of support for the exhibition, asking for something from the show to be purchased for the national collection (signed by, among others, [Stephen Spender](/wiki/Stephen_Spender), [W. H. Auden](/wiki/W._H._Auden), [Geoffrey Grigson](/wiki/Geoffrey_Grigson), [Rebecca West](/wiki/Rebecca_West), [Naomi Mitchison](/wiki/Naomi_Mitchison), [Henry Moore](/wiki/Henry_Moore) and [Eric Gill](/wiki/Eric_Gill)) the [Tate Gallery](/wiki/Tate_Gallery) bought the painting, *Red Scene*. Like others from the exhibition, it shows an influence from [Surrealism](/wiki/Surrealism) and [Giorgio de Chirico](/wiki/Giorgio_de_Chirico)'s [metaphysical painting](/wiki/Metaphysical_painting). Lewis was highly critical of the ideology of Surrealism, but admired the visual qualities of some Surrealist art.

During this period Lewis also produced many of his most well-known portraits, including pictures of [Edith Sitwell](/wiki/Edith_Sitwell) ([1923–1936](/wiki/Edith_Sitwell_(Lewis))), [T. S. Eliot](/wiki/T._S._Eliot) ([1938](/wiki/Portrait_of_T._S._Eliot) and 1949), and [Ezra Pound](/wiki/Ezra_Pound_(Lewis)) ([1939](/wiki/Ezra_Pound_(Lewis))). His 1938 portrait of Eliot was rejected by the selection committee of the [Royal Academy](/wiki/Royal_Academy) for their annual exhibition and caused a furore. [Augustus John](/wiki/Augustus_John) resigned in protest.

### Second World War and North America (1941–1945)

Lewis spent the [Second World War](/wiki/Second_World_War) in the United States and Canada. In 1941 in [Toronto](/wiki/Toronto) he produced a series of [watercolour](/wiki/Watercolor_painting) fantasies centred on themes of creation, [crucifixion](/wiki/Crucifixion) and bathing.

He grew to appreciate the [cosmopolitan](/wiki/Cosmopolitanism) and "rootless" nature of the American [melting pot](/wiki/Melting_pot), declaring that the greatest advantage of being American was to have "turned one's back on race, [caste](/wiki/Caste), and all that pertains to the rooted state." He praised the contributions of [African Americans](/wiki/African_Americans) to American culture, and regarded [Diego Rivera](/wiki/Diego_Rivera), [David Alfaro Siqueiros](/wiki/David_Alfaro_Siqueiros) and [José Clemente Orozco](/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Clemente_Orozco) as the "best North American artists," predicting that when "the [Indian](/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Mexico) culture of Mexico melts into the great American mass to the North, the Indian will probably give it its art." He returned to England in 1945.

### Later life and blindness (1945–1951)

By 1951 he was completely blinded by a [pituitary tumour](/wiki/Pituitary_adenoma) that placed pressure on his [optic nerve](/wiki/Optic_nerve). It ended his career as a painter, but he continued writing until his death. He published several autobiographical and critical works: *Rude Assignment* (1950), *Rotting Hill* (1951), a collection of allegorical short stories about his life in "the capital of a dying empire"; *The Writer and the Absolute* (1952), a book of essays on writers including [George Orwell](/wiki/George_Orwell), [Jean-Paul Sartre](/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre) and [André Malraux](/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Malraux); and the semi-autobiographical novel *Self Condemned* (1954).

The [BBC](/wiki/BBC) commissioned Lewis to complete his 1928 work *The Childermass*, which was published as *The Human Age* and dramatised for the [BBC Third Programme](/wiki/BBC_Third_Programme) in 1955. In 1956 the [Tate Gallery](/wiki/Tate_Gallery) held a major exhibition of his work, "Wyndham Lewis and Vorticism", in the catalogue to which he declared that "Vorticism, in fact, was what I, personally, did and said at a certain period"—a statement which brought forth a series of "Vortex Pamphlets" from his fellow *Blast* signatory [William Roberts](/wiki/William_Roberts_(painter)).

## Personal life

From 1918 to 1921, Lewis lived with [Iris Barry](/wiki/Iris_Barry), with whom he had two children. He is said to have shown little affection for them.

In 1930, Lewis married Gladys Anne Hoskins (1900–1979), who was affectionately known as Froanna. They lived together for 10 years before marrying, and never had children. Lewis did not tell all of his friends about his marriage, as he was jealous of them meeting her. Froanna modelled for some of his work, and characters in his books reflect her.

Plaque dedicated to Lewis at Golders Green Crematorium

Lewis converted to [Catholicism](/wiki/Catholicism) in 1921. He died in 1957 and was cremated at [Golders Green Crematorium](/wiki/Golders_Green_Crematorium). By the time of his death he had written 40 books.

### Political views

In 1931, after a visit to [Berlin](/wiki/Berlin), Lewis published *Hitler*, a book presenting [Adolf Hitler](/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) as a "man of peace", with members of his party being threatened by communist street violence. His unpopularity among liberals and [anti-fascists](/wiki/Anti-fascism) grew, especially after Hitler came to power in 1933. Following a second visit to Germany in 1937, Lewis changed his views and began to retract his previous political comments. He recognised the reality of Nazi treatment of Jews after a visit to Berlin in 1937. In 1939 he published an attack on [antisemitism](/wiki/Antisemitism) titled *The Jews, Are They Human?*, which was favourably reviewed in *[The Jewish Chronicle](/wiki/The_Jewish_Chronicle)*. He also published *The Hitler Cult* (1939), which firmly revoked his earlier support for Hitler.

Politically, Lewis remained an isolated figure through the 1930s. In *Letter to [Lord Byron](/wiki/Lord_Byron)*, [W. H. Auden](/wiki/W._H._Auden) called Lewis "that lonely old volcano of the Right." Lewis thought there was what he called a "left-wing orthodoxy" in Britain in the 1930s. He believed it was against Britain's self-interest to ally with the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union), "which the newspapers most of us read tell us has slaughtered out-of-hand, only a few years ago, millions of its better fed citizens, as well as its whole imperial family."

In *Anglosaxony: A League that Works* (1941), Lewis reflected on his earlier support for [fascism](/wiki/Fascism):

> Fascism – once I understood it – left me colder than communism. The latter at least pretended, at the start, to have something to do with helping the helpless and making the world a more decent and sensible place. It does start from the human being and his suffering. Whereas fascism glorifies bloodshed and preaches that man should model himself upon the wolf.

His sense that America and Canada lacked a British-type [class structure](/wiki/Class_structure) had increased his opinion of [liberal democracy](/wiki/Liberal_democracy), and in the same pamphlet he defends liberal democracy's respect for individual freedom against its critics on both the left and right. In *America and Cosmic Man* (1949) Lewis argued that [Franklin D. Roosevelt](/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt), the [US president](/wiki/US_president) from 1933 to 1945, had successfully managed to reconcile individual rights with the demands of the state.

## Legacy

In recent years there has been renewed critical and biographical interest in Lewis and his work, and he is now regarded as a major British artist and writer of the twentieth century. Rugby School hosted an exhibition of his works in November 2007 to commemorate the 50-year anniversary of his death. The [National Portrait Gallery](/wiki/National_Portrait_Gallery_(London)) in London held a major retrospective of his portraits in 2008. Two years later the Fundación Juan March (Madrid, Spain), held a large exhibition (*Wyndham Lewis 1882–1957*) featuring a comprehensive collection of Lewis's paintings and drawings. As Tom Lubbock pointed out, it was "the retrospective that Britain has never managed to get together."

In 2010 [Oxford World's Classics](/wiki/Oxford_World%27s_Classics) published a critical edition of the 1928 text of *Tarr*, edited by Scott W. Klein of [Wake Forest University](/wiki/Wake_Forest_University). The [Nasher Museum of Art](/wiki/Nasher_Museum_of_Art) at [Duke University](/wiki/Duke_University) held an exhibition entitled "[The Vorticists](/wiki/Vorticism): Rebel Artists in London and New York, 1914–18" from 30 September 2010 through 2 January 2011. The exhibition then travelled to the Peggy Guggenheim Collection, [Venice](/wiki/Venice) (29 January – 15 May 2011: "I Vorticisti: Artisti ribellia a Londra e New York, 1914–1918") and then to [Tate Britain](/wiki/Tate_Britain) under the title "The Vorticists: Manifesto for a Modern World" between 14 June and 4 September 2011.

Several readings by Lewis are collected on *The Enemy Speaks*, an [audiobook](/wiki/Audiobook) published in [compact disc](/wiki/Compact_disc) form in 2007 and featuring extracts from "One Way Song" and *The Apes of God*, as well as radio talks titled "When John Bull Laughs" (1938), "A Crisis of Thought" (1947) and "The Essential Purposes of Art" (1951).

A [blue plaque](/wiki/Blue_plaque) now stands on the house in [Kensington](/wiki/Kensington), London, where Lewis lived, No. 61 Palace Gardens Terrace.

Blue plaque: Wyndham Lewis, 61 Palace Gardens Terrace, London W8

### Critical reception

In his essay "[Good Bad Books](/wiki/Good_Bad_Books)" [George Orwell](/wiki/George_Orwell) presents Lewis as the exemplary writer who is cerebral without being artistic. Orwell wrote, "Enough talent to set up dozens of ordinary writers has been poured into Wyndham Lewis's so-called novels… Yet it would be a very heavy labour to read one of these books right through. Some indefinable quality, a sort of literary vitamin, which exists even in a book like [1921 melodrama] *[If Winter Comes](/wiki/If_Winter_Comes_(novel))*, is absent from them."

In 1932 [Walter Sickert](/wiki/Walter_Sickert) sent Lewis [a telegram](/wiki/A_telegram) in which he said that Lewis's pencil portrait of [Rebecca West](/wiki/Rebecca_West) proved him to be "the greatest portraitist of this or any other time."

#### Anti-semitism

For many years Lewis's novels have been criticised for their satirical and hostile portrayals of Jews. *Tarr* was revised and republished in 1928, giving a new Jewish character a key role in making sure a duel is fought. This has been interpreted as an allegorical representation of [a supposed Zionist conspiracy against the West](/wiki/Stab-in-the-back_myth). His literary satire *The Apes of God* has been interpreted similarly, because many of the characters are Jewish, including the modernist author and editor Julius Ratner, a portrait which blends antisemitic stereotype with the historical literary figures [John Rodker](/wiki/John_Rodker) and James Joyce.

A key feature of these interpretations is that Lewis is held to have kept his conspiracy theories hidden and marginalised. Since the publication of [Anthony Julius](/wiki/Anthony_Julius)'s *T. S. Eliot, Anti-Semitism, and Literary Form* (1995), where Lewis's antisemitism is described as "essentially trivial", this view is no longer taken seriously.
