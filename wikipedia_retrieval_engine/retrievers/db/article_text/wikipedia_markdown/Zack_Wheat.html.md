**Zachariah Davis Wheat** (May 23, 1888 – March 11, 1972), nicknamed "**Buck**", was an American professional [baseball](/wiki/Baseball) player. He played in [Major League Baseball](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball) (MLB) as a [left fielder](/wiki/Left_fielder) from 1909 to 1927, most notably as a member of the [Brooklyn Dodgers](/wiki/Brooklyn_Dodgers) who were known as the Robins at that time. After 18 seasons in Brooklyn, he played his final season with the [Philadelphia Athletics](/wiki/Philadelphia_Athletics).

Although Wheat spent the first part of his career playing in the [Dead ball era](/wiki/Dead_ball_era), he hit over .300 in 13 seasons and won the [National League batting championship](/wiki/List_of_Major_League_Baseball_batting_champions) in 1918. He ended his career with a .317 career batting average and remains the Dodgers all-time franchise leader in games, at bats, plate appearances, hits, doubles, triples, and total bases. Wheat was also known as a stylish and graceful outfielder, leading National League left fielders in putouts seven times and fielding percentage twice.

Wheat was unanimously elected to the [National Baseball Hall of Fame](/wiki/National_Baseball_Hall_of_Fame) in 1959. His brother [McKinley "Mack" Wheat](/wiki/Mack_Wheat) also played in the major leagues, and the two were teammates in Brooklyn for five seasons.

## Early life

Born in [Hamilton, Missouri](/wiki/Hamilton,_Missouri), he was the son of Basil and Julia Wheat. His father was of English descent. For many years, it was believed that Wheat was at least partially of [Cherokee descent](/wiki/Cherokee_descent) but, according to the [Society for American Baseball Research](/wiki/Society_for_American_Baseball_Research), this was likely untrue.

## Career

Wheat began his professional baseball career in 1906 for Enterprise in the Kansas League, followed by Wichita in 1907, the [Shreveport Pirates](/wiki/Shreveport_Sports) of the [Texas League](/wiki/Texas_League) in 1908, and the [Mobile Sea Gulls](/wiki/Mobile_Sea_Gulls) of the [Southern Association](/wiki/Southern_Association) in 1909. It was during that 1909 season that the [Brooklyn Superbas](/wiki/Brooklyn_Superbas) of the [National League](/wiki/National_League_(baseball)) purchased Wheat for $1,200, and he made his major league debut in September. Wheat batted with a corkscrew type of swing, and held his hands down near the end of the bat, unlike most hitters during his time, a time noted as the "[Dead Ball Era](/wiki/Dead_Ball_Era)". Along with his consistently high levels of hitting, he was also noted for his graceful and stylish defense.

> What [Lajoie](/wiki/Nap_Lajoie) was to infielders, Zach Wheat is to outfielders, the finest mechanical craftsman of them all ... Wheat is the easiest, most graceful of outfielders with no close rivals.

— *Baseball Magazine*, 1917

Wheat played his first full season in 1910. He played every game for the Superbas that season as the regular left fielder, leading the league in [games played](/wiki/Games_played). He [batted](/wiki/Batting_average_(baseball)) .284 that season, the second-lowest average of his career, which led the team, and was among the league leaders in [hits](/wiki/Hit_(baseball)), [doubles](/wiki/Double_(baseball)), and [triples](/wiki/Triple_(baseball)). It was in 1911 that his reputation as a slugger began to take hold. Along with hitting .287, Wheat finished eighth in the league with 13 triples and slugged five [home runs](/wiki/Home_run). In an era when players rarely hit double-digit home runs for a season, five was enough for people to take notice.

Wheat continued his steady and consistent climb up the batting charts in 1912, hitting .305 and finishing the season among the league leaders in home runs and [slugging percentage](/wiki/Slugging_percentage). Over the next four seasons, he continued to be among the leaders in many offensive categories, including home runs, batting average, slugging average, hits, doubles, triples, and [RBI](/wiki/Run_batted_in). It was during the 1912 season that Wheat married Daisy Kerr Forsman. His wife quickly became his *de facto* agent, encouraging him to hold out for a better contract each season. Each time Wheat held out, he received more money, the club not wanting to lose one of its best hitters and the team's most popular player. This tactic of threatening to hold out served him well during throughout his career, including during the [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I) era, when he raised and sold [mules](/wiki/Mule) to the [United States Army](/wiki/United_States_Army) as pack animals. Wheat claimed that he was making so much money from his business that he wouldn't need to play baseball at all. The team, fearing that they might lose a great player during the prime of his career, succumbed to his demands every year.

Zack Wheat baseball card, 1911 Gold Borders (T205)

In 1916, Wheat topped off a string of seasons with a finish in the top ten in all the above categories, topping the league in [total bases](/wiki/Total_bases) and slugging. He also had a career-high hitting streak, which reached 29 games. The Brooklyn Robins won the National League pennant that season. In the World Series, they faced the [Boston Red Sox](/wiki/Boston_Red_Sox), who had the formidable pitching rotation of [Ernie Shore](/wiki/Ernie_Shore), [Dutch Leonard](/wiki/Dutch_Leonard_(left-handed_pitcher)), [Carl Mays](/wiki/Carl_Mays), and [Babe Ruth](/wiki/Babe_Ruth). The Red Sox won the series four games to one, holding the Robins to a .200 batting average and Wheat to a paltry .211.

During the 1917 and 1918 seasons, Wheat hit well, but missed many games due to injuries. He had tiny feet, around size 5, and this is believed to be the cause of the many nagging ankle injuries that caused to miss many games in his career. However, Wheat led the league in batting average for the only time in his career with a .335 batting average, his highest average up to that point. Despite being known as a slugger and consistently placing in the top ten in home runs, Wheat hit no home runs that season and had hit just one the season prior. Not until [Rod Carew](/wiki/Rod_Carew) in 1972 would another player win a batting title without hitting a home run during the season.

Starting in 1919, Wheat returned to the league slugging leaders once again as the [baseball](/wiki/Baseball_(ball)) began to become livelier. The Robins made their second World Series appearance in 1920, this time facing off against the [Cleveland Indians](/wiki/Cleveland_Indians). The Robins lost this series as well, 5 games to 2, although Wheat's series average was .333. Wheat's statistics climbed during this new live era of baseball, reaching double-digit home runs for the first time with 14 in 1921, and again three more times in the next four years. Wheat hit .320 or higher every season from 1920 through 1925, topping out with .375 in consecutive seasons. He failed to lead the league in hitting those two seasons, not getting enough [at bats](/wiki/At_bat) in 1923 to qualify, and Hornsby topped the league with .384, and in 1924, his .375 was a distant second to Hornsby's .424.

A subtle but longstanding friction existed between Wheat and his [manager](/wiki/Manager_(baseball)), [Wilbert Robinson](/wiki/Wilbert_Robinson). The friction reportedly stemmed from Robinson's belief that Wheat pursued the manager's job behind his back. When owner [Charles Ebbets](/wiki/Charles_Ebbets) died in 1925, new team president Ed McKeever reassigned Robinson into the front office and named Wheat as [player-manager](/wiki/Player-manager). Newspapers confirm that he managed the Dodgers for two weeks. McKeever caught [pneumonia](/wiki/Pneumonia) at Ebbets' [funeral](/wiki/Funeral), and died soon afterward, and Robinson quickly returned to the manager's position. As it turned out, Wheat never again managed in the majors, much to his disappointment. Moreover, Wheat's 1925 managerial stint never made it into the official records. In 1931, Steve McKeever, Ed's brother, hired Wheat as a [coach](/wiki/Coach_(sport)), leading to widespread speculation that he was being groomed for the manager's spot, threatening Robinson's job for a second time in seven years, and he treated his former star as coldly as ever.

Wheat was signed by the [Philadelphia Athletics](/wiki/Philadelphia_Athletics) after his release from Brooklyn in 1927. After the season, he was released again; this time he signed and played for the minor league [Minneapolis Millers](/wiki/Minneapolis_Millers) of the [American Association](/wiki/American_Association_(20th_century)). Wheat played very little that season due to a heel injury, and retired from playing following the season. He still holds the Dodger franchise records for hits, doubles, triples and total bases.

## Career statistics

In 2,410 games over 19 seasons, Wheat posted a .317 [batting average](/wiki/Batting_average_(baseball)) (2,884-for-9,106) with 1,289 [runs](/wiki/Run_(baseball)), 476 [doubles](/wiki/Double_(baseball)), 172 [triples](/wiki/Triple_(baseball)), 132 [home runs](/wiki/Home_runs), 1,248 [RBI](/wiki/Run_batted_in), 205 [stolen bases](/wiki/Stolen_bases), 650 [bases on balls](/wiki/Bases_on_balls), .367 [on-base percentage](/wiki/On-base_percentage) and .450 [slugging percentage](/wiki/Slugging_percentage). He finished his career with a .966 [fielding percentage](/wiki/Fielding_percentage) as a left fielder. In 12 World Series games (1916,1920), he batted .283 (13-for-46) with 4 runs, 2 doubles, 1 triple, 3 RBI and 3 walks.

## Post-career

After Wheat retired from baseball, he moved back to his 160-acre (0.65 km2) farm in [Polo, Missouri](/wiki/Polo,_Missouri), until the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression) forced him to sell it in 1932. He moved to [Kansas City, Missouri](/wiki/Kansas_City,_Missouri), where he operated a [bowling alley](/wiki/Bowling_alley) with [Cotton Tierney](/wiki/Cotton_Tierney). Wheat later became a [police officer](/wiki/Police_officer). During his duties as an officer in 1936, he crashed his vehicle while pursuing a felon, and nearly died. Following five months in hospital after the accident, he moved his family to [Sunrise Beach, Missouri](/wiki/Sunrise_Beach,_Missouri), a resort town on the [Lake of the Ozarks](/wiki/Lake_of_the_Ozarks), where he opened a 46-acre (190,000 m2) hunting and fishing resort.

> One of the grandest guys ever to wear a baseball uniform, one of the greatest batting teachers I have seen, one of the truest pals a man ever (had) and one of the kindliest men God ever created.

— [Casey Stengel](/wiki/Casey_Stengel), 1965, speaking of Zack Wheat.

Wheat was first voted into the [Baseball Hall of Fame](/wiki/Baseball_Hall_of_Fame) by the [Veterans Committee](/wiki/Veterans_Committee) in [1957](/wiki/1957_Baseball_Hall_of_Fame_balloting), but could not be inducted because he had not been retired for the required 30 years. The committee unanimously elected him in [1959](/wiki/1959_Baseball_Hall_of_Fame_balloting). In 1981, [Lawrence Ritter](/wiki/Lawrence_Ritter) and [Donald Honig](/wiki/Donald_Honig) included him in their book *The 100 Greatest Baseball Players of All Time*. In 2006, the stretch of [Route 13](/wiki/Route_13_(Missouri)) that runs through [Caldwell County, Missouri](/wiki/Caldwell_County,_Missouri) was named the *Zach Wheat Memorial Highway*.

Due to the long-held belief about his supposed Cherokee ancestry, Wheat was featured in "Baseball's League of Nations: A Tribute to Native Americans in Baseball", a 2008 exhibit at the Iroquois Indian Museum in Howes Cave, N.Y.

Wheat died of a heart attack on March 11, 1972.

## Legacy

The American Legion Post in Sunrise Beach, Missouri, where Wheat lived following his retirement, is named "Zachariah (Zack) Davis Wheat Post 624 American Legion, Sunrise Beach, Missouri" in his honor.
