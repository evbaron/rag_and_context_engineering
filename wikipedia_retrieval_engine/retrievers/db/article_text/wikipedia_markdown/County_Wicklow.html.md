**County Wicklow** ([/ˈwɪkloʊ/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [*WIK-loh*](/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key); [Irish](/wiki/Irish_language): *Contae Chill Mhantáin* [[ˈkɔn̪ˠt̪ˠeː ˌçiːl̠ʲ ˈwan̪ˠt̪ˠaːnʲ]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Irish)) is a [county](/wiki/Counties_of_Ireland) in [Ireland](/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland). The last of the traditional 32 counties, having been formed as late as [1606](/wiki/1606_in_Ireland), it is part of the [Eastern and Midland Region](/wiki/Eastern_and_Midland_Region) and the [province](/wiki/Provinces_of_Ireland) of [Leinster](/wiki/Leinster). It is bordered by the [Irish Sea](/wiki/Irish_Sea) to the east and the counties of [Wexford](/wiki/County_Wexford) to the south, [Carlow](/wiki/County_Carlow) to the southwest, [Kildare](/wiki/County_Kildare) to the west, and [South Dublin](/wiki/South_Dublin) and [Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown](/wiki/D%C3%BAn_Laoghaire%E2%80%93Rathdown) to the north.

Wicklow is named after its [county town](/wiki/County_town) of [Wicklow](/wiki/Wicklow), which derives from the name *Víkingaló* ([Old Norse](/wiki/Old_Norse) for "Vikings' Meadow"). [Wicklow County Council](/wiki/Wicklow_County_Council) is the [local authority](/wiki/Local_government_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) for the county, which had a population of 155,258 at the [2022 census](/wiki/2022_census_of_Ireland). Colloquially known as the "Garden of Ireland" for its scenery—which includes extensive woodlands, nature trails, beaches, and ancient ruins while allowing for a multitude of walking, hiking, and climbing options—it is the 17th largest of Ireland's 32 counties by area and the 16th largest by population. It is also the fourth largest of Leinster's 12 counties by size and the fifth largest in terms of population.

## History

### Missions and monasteries

[Saint Kevin's](/wiki/Kevin_of_Glendalough) monastery at [Glendalough](/wiki/Glendalough).

During the 5th century Saint Palladius, a bishop from Britain or Gaul was sent to 'the Irish believing in Christ.' He and his companions landed at Wicklow harbour in the year 431 AD. Palladius founded three ancient churches at Donard, Tigroney and Colbinstown. Along with him came two clerics named St. Sylvester and Solonius who died and were buried at Donard. The local tribe in the area were called Cualann and their chief, a man named Naithi was opposed to these Christian missionaries. Despite this hostility however Palladius seems to have gained support elsewhere as he was able to erect those three churches in the Wicklow area. After a rather unsuccessful stay in the county St. Palladius departed Ireland and journeyed to Scotland.

A large number of early Irish saints male and female founded churches, monasteries and convents in Wicklow. Among them were St. [Kevin](/wiki/Kevin_of_Glendalough), St. [Dagan](/wiki/Dag%C3%A1n), St. [Ernan](/wiki/Ernan), St. [Credan](/wiki/Credan), St. Baotan and the holy women [St. Cainnear](/wiki/Cainnear_(name)), St. Ceoltigherna, St. Fledh, St. Tartinna, St. Coine and St. Croine. The ancient [monastery](/wiki/Monastery) of [Glendalough](/wiki/Glendalough) is located in the county.

### 17th century onwards

County Wicklow was the last of the traditional counties of Ireland to be shired in [1606](/wiki/1606_in_Ireland) from land previously part of counties [Dublin](/wiki/County_Dublin) and [Carlow](/wiki/County_Carlow). Established as a distinct county, it was aimed at controlling local groups such as the [O'Byrnes](/wiki/Fiach_McHugh_O%27Byrne). The *[Military Road](/wiki/R115_road_(Ireland))*, stretching from [Rathfarnham](/wiki/Rathfarnham) to [Aghavannagh](/wiki/Aghavannagh) crosses the mountains, north to south, and was built by government forces to assist them in defeating the rebels still active in the [Wicklow Mountains](/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains) following the failed [Irish Rebellion of 1798](/wiki/Irish_Rebellion_of_1798). It provided them with access to an area that had been a hotbed of Irish rebellion for centuries. Several barracks to house the soldiers were built along the route; in 1974 the [Glencree Centre for Peace and Reconciliation](/wiki/Glencree_Centre_for_Peace_and_Reconciliation) was opened alongside the remains of Glencree barracks. Battalions of the [Irish Army](/wiki/Irish_Army) use firing ranges in County Wicklow for tactical exercises, especially the largest one in the [Glen of Imaal](/wiki/Glen_of_Imaal) which was previously used by the [British Army](/wiki/British_Army) prior to independence.

There was a brief [Wicklow gold rush](/wiki/Wicklow_gold_rush) in the year 1795 near the village of [Woodenbridge](/wiki/Woodenbridge) that lasted for a month, during which as many as 80 kilograms of gold was recovered by unlicensed prospectors before the government took over.

During the [Cromwellian conquest of Ireland](/wiki/Cromwellian_conquest_of_Ireland), local authorities immediately surrendered without a fight. During the Irish Rebellion of 1798, some of the [United Irishmen](/wiki/Society_of_United_Irishmen) insurgents took refuge in the Wicklow Mountains, resulting in clashes between British forces and rebels commanded by [Joseph Holt](/wiki/Joseph_Holt_(rebel)) near Aughrim and later at Arklow.

The boundaries of the county were extended in 1957 by the Local Government Act which "detached lands from the [County of Dublin](/wiki/County_Dublin) and from the jurisdiction and powers of the Council of the County of Dublin" near Bray and added them to the County of Wicklow.

### Irish language

Wicklow was the first county in Ireland to lose the [Irish language](/wiki/Irish_language), with only around 2% of people speaking it in the 1770s. It was spoken on the Western slopes of the [Wicklow Mountains](/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains) in the 1790s. Andrew and Hannah Byrne of [Glenealy](/wiki/Glenealy,_County_Wicklow), who both died in 1830, were among the last native speakers in the county.

## Geography

Further information: [Wicklow Mountains](/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains), [Wicklow Mountains National Park](/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains_National_Park), and [Wicklow Way](/wiki/Wicklow_Way)

### Location

Topographic map of County Wicklow

Wicklow is part of the [Eastern and Midland Region](/wiki/Eastern_and_Midland_Regional_Assembly) of Ireland, within the province of [Leinster](/wiki/Leinster). It is bounded by four counties, [Dublin](/wiki/County_Dublin) to the north; [Kildare](/wiki/County_Kildare) and [Carlow](/wiki/County_Carlow) to the west; [Wexford](/wiki/County_Wexford) to the south; and the [Irish Sea](/wiki/Irish_Sea) to the east. Wicklow is the 17th largest of Ireland's 32 traditional counties by land area, and the 16th most populous. It is the fourth largest of Leinster's 12 counties by size and the fifth most populous. The county is named after the town of [Wicklow](/wiki/Wicklow), located approximately 50 kilometres (31 mi) south of [Dublin](/wiki/Dublin), the capital city of Ireland.

The [Wicklow Mountains](/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains) occupy the whole of central Wicklow

The [Wicklow Mountains](/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains) are Ireland's largest continuous upland area and occupy a significant portion of the county, spanning the entire centre of Wicklow and stretching into Dublin and Wexford at their southern and northern fringes. [Lugnaquilla](/wiki/Lugnaquilla), at 925 metres (3,035 ft) above sea level, is the tallest peak in the range, the highest mountain in Ireland outside of [County Kerry](/wiki/County_Kerry), and Ireland's 13th-highest mountain overall. The [Wicklow Mountains National Park](/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains_National_Park), located in the middle of the county, is a dedicated protected area covering 205 square kilometres (50,657 acres), making it the largest [national park](/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland) in Ireland. The [Wicklow Way](/wiki/Wicklow_Way) was the first [long-distance trail](/wiki/Lists_of_long-distance_trails_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) in the State, and crosses the range for 131 kilometres (81 mi).

### Geology

The Wicklow Mountains are centred on the Leinster [batholith](/wiki/Batholith) and are primarily composed of [granite](/wiki/Granite) surrounded by an envelope of [mica](/wiki/Mica)-[schist](/wiki/Schist) and much older rocks such as [quartzite](/wiki/Quartzite). Covering 1,500 square kilometres (580 mi2), the Leinster batholith is the most expansive mass of [intrusive](/wiki/Intrusive_rock) [igneous rock](/wiki/Igneous_rock) in Ireland or [Britain](/wiki/Great_Britain). The oldest rocks in the county are the quartzites of the Bray Group that include [Bray Head](/wiki/Bray_Head) and the [Little Sugar Loaf](/wiki/Little_Sugar_Loaf) and [Great Sugar Loaf](/wiki/Great_Sugar_Loaf) mountains. These [metamorphosed](/wiki/Metamorphism) from [sandstone](/wiki/Sandstone) deposited in the deep waters of the primeval [Iapetus Ocean](/wiki/Iapetus_Ocean) during the [Cambrian](/wiki/Cambrian) [period](/wiki/Period_(geology)) (542-488 million years ago).

As with much of Ireland, Wicklow's terrain was sculpted by successive periods of glaciation during the [quaternary](/wiki/Quaternary). Weathering and erosion by ice carved out long valleys known as [glens](/wiki/Glens) (from the [Irish](/wiki/Irish_language) *gleann*) such as [Glenmacnass](/wiki/Glenmacnass_Waterfall), [Glen of the Downs](/wiki/Glen_of_the_Downs), [Glenmalure](/wiki/Glenmalure), [Glen of Imaal](/wiki/Glen_of_Imaal), [Glencree](/wiki/Glencree) and [Glendalough](/wiki/Glendalough). The Irish Sea Ice-Sheet began to retreat shortly after the [Last Glacial Maximum](/wiki/Last_Glacial_Maximum) ca. 20,000–23,000 years ago but significant ice masses persisted in the Wicklow Mountains for another 4,000–7,000 years.

### Hydrology

[Powerscourt Waterfall](/wiki/Powerscourt_Waterfall), the second highest in Ireland

Major rivers include the 132-kilometre (82.0 mi) [River Liffey](/wiki/River_Liffey), Ireland's 8th-longest river, which rises near [Tonduff](/wiki/Tonduff) mountain and flows through the centre of [Dublin City](/wiki/Dublin), reaching the Irish Sea at [Dublin Bay](/wiki/Dublin_Bay). Its biggest tributary by volume, the [River Dodder](/wiki/River_Dodder), rises along the northern slope of [Kippure](/wiki/Kippure) in the far north of the county, while the Kings River joins at Blessington Lakes. Multiple other Liffey tributaries flow within the county.

The 117.5-kilometre (73.0 mi) [River Slaney](/wiki/River_Slaney), which starts at Lugnaquilla, flows west and then south before reaching [St George's Channel](/wiki/St_George%27s_Channel) at [Wexford](/wiki/Wexford) town. The [Avonmore](/wiki/Avonmore_River) and [Avonbeg](/wiki/River_Avonbeg) rivers join to form the [Avoca River](/wiki/River_Avoca) at the *Meeting of the Waters* in the Vale of Avoca. The [River Derreen](/wiki/River_Derreen) in the south of the county defines a section of the border with Carlow.

The [Poulaphouca Reservoir](/wiki/Poulaphouca_Reservoir) near [Blessington](/wiki/Blessington) is Ireland's largest artificial lake

Most of the county's lakes (or loughs) are glacially derived [ribbon](/wiki/Ribbon_lake) and [corrie lakes](/wiki/Tarn_(lake)) found in scenic [trough valleys](/wiki/Trough_valley) surrounded by mountains, making them popular with tourists. Notable lakes in the county include [Glendalough Upper Lake](/wiki/Glendalough_Upper_Lake), [Lough Tay](/wiki/Lough_Tay) ('The [Guinness](/wiki/Guinness_Estate) Lake'), [Lough Ouler](/wiki/Tonelagee#Gallery) ('The Heart-shaped Lake'), Lough Bray, [Lough Dan](/wiki/Lough_Dan) and Lough Nahanagan.

[Poulaphouca Reservoir](/wiki/Poulaphouca_Reservoir) is the largest of Wicklow's lakes, covering 2,226 hectares (5,500 acres) in the west of the county. It is the largest artificial lake in Ireland and was created following the damming of the [River Liffey](/wiki/River_Liffey) at [Poulaphouca](/wiki/Poulaphouca) in 1940. The village of Ballinahown was completed submerged by the reservoir and its 70 families were relocated. Ruins of the old village including buildings, fences and farm machinery can be seen during droughts when water levels in the reservoir diminish. The lower [Vartry Reservoir](/wiki/Vartry_Reservoir), constructed between 1862 and 1868, is the county's second largest lake.

### Coast

Beach at [Brittas Bay](/wiki/Brittas_Bay). The county is a popular seaside destination

Wicklow has a relatively [short coastline](/wiki/List_of_Irish_counties_by_coastline), at 64 kilometres (40 miles) in length. Wicklow's coastline is mostly straight, with few sizable bays or inlets and no offshore islands, giving it a shorter coastline than smaller counties like [Louth](/wiki/County_Louth) and Dublin. [Wicklow Head](/wiki/Wicklow_Head) is the county's most prominent coastal headland, and is also the most easterly mainland point of the Republic of Ireland. Wicklow's east coast is a popular domestic summer holiday destination, and the county has numerous beaches including [Brittas Bay](/wiki/Brittas_Bay), Clogga Beach, The Cove, Silver Strand Beach, Sallymount Bay Beach, Ennereilly Beach, Newcastle Beach, Arklow's Porter's Rock Beach and South Beach, Greystones North and South Beaches, Bray Strand, and Magheramore Beach.

### Forest

The county has roughly 37,500 hectares (93,000 acres) of wooded area, the 8th highest total forest cover in Ireland. In terms of forest cover as a proportion of land area, Wicklow ranks second in the country, at 18.5%. Known as the "Garden of Ireland", Wicklow was historically the county with the highest percentage of woodlands. The 2017 National Forestry Inventory revealed that [County Leitrim](/wiki/County_Leitrim) had overtaken it for the first time. Wicklow is in a [temperate broadleaf and mixed forests](/wiki/Temperate_broadleaf_and_mixed_forests) [biome](/wiki/Biome), although the majority of Wicklow's forests are commercial [conifers](/wiki/Conifer). The economic [tree line](/wiki/Tree_line) in the region is around 400 metres (1,300 ft), above which [hill farming](/wiki/Hill_farming) and [blanket bog](/wiki/Blanket_bog) dominates.

### Urban development

In terms of urban development, [Wicklow County Council](/wiki/Wicklow_County_Council) imposes the most stringent planning restrictions in Ireland. In order to build a house outside of the main towns, a person must be "born and bred in the area, or have lived there for a period of 10 years" and must also demonstrate that the house is for their own housing needs, rather than for resale. Further, potential buyers in rural areas must be approved by the council before the homeowner is allowed to sell to them. The primary justification for these restrictions is to avoid [one-off housing](/wiki/One-off_housing) or other poorly planned developments which could put a strain on the county's infrastructure and degrade its natural environment.

Autumn leaves at [Upper Lake](/wiki/Glendalough_Upper_Lake)

The climate of Wicklow is [temperate](/wiki/Temperate) [oceanic](/wiki/Oceanic_climate) ([Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *Cfb* in most areas, and *Cfc* [oceanic subpolar](/wiki/Oceanic_climate#Subpolar_variety_(Cfc,_Cwc)) in some highland areas), with cool, humid summers, and mild, wet winters. The climate of the eastern portion of the county is moderated by the [Irish Sea](/wiki/Irish_Sea) and averages milder winters and cooler summers, while the western inland portion along the [Kildare](/wiki/County_Kildare) border experiences warmer summers and colder winters. The county's upland interior experiences significantly lower temperatures and higher rainfall year-round. Snow typically falls from December to March, but most low-lying and coastal areas see only a few days of lying snow per year, or may see no snow at all during some winters. The Wicklow Mountains region is the snowiest part of Ireland and can experience 50 or more days of snowfall each year.

On average, Wicklow receives more snowfall than any other county

The county experiences a narrow annual temperature range. Typical daytime highs range from 17–22 °C (63–72 °F) throughout the county in July and August, with overnight lows in the 11–14 °C (52–57 °F) range, although temperatures in the mountains can be 5–10 °C (41–50 °F) lower. Mean January temperatures range from 7 °C (45 °F) on the coast to 0 °C (32 °F) at high elevations. Precipitation is fairly evenly distributed throughout the year, although the wettest months (October and November) receive roughly twice as much rain as the driest months (February, March and April). The driest area of Wicklow is the northeast corner of the county, which is protected from the prevailing south-westerly winds by the Wicklow Mountains and receives around 750 mm (30 in) of rainfall per year. The coastal region of [County Dublin](/wiki/County_Dublin) immediately to the north is the driest location in all of Ireland as a result of the [rain shadow](/wiki/Rain_shadow) created by the mountains.

Easterly winds can occur between February and May, and are often associated with extreme snowfall events such as the 2018 "[Beast from the East](/wiki/2018_British_Isles_cold_wave)" and the "[Big Snow of 1947](/wiki/Winter_of_1946%E2%80%9347_in_the_United_Kingdom)". Much of Wicklow is very vulnerable to heavy snowfall and the county is typically the hardest hit by such events, with some areas recording over 2 feet (61 cm) of snow. During the winter of 1947, residents of [Rathdrum](/wiki/Rathdrum,_County_Wicklow) called upon the [Irish Red Cross](/wiki/Irish_Red_Cross_Society) to drop them food parcels by plane as the village had been inaccessible by road for over a month due to heavy snow.

Wicklow is occasionally affected by [hurricanes](/wiki/Tropical_cyclone), although they are usually [extratropical](/wiki/Extratropical) storms by the time they reach Ireland. On 24 August 1986, during [Hurricane Charley](/wiki/Hurricane_Charley_(1986)), 280 mm (11 in) of rain fell over a 24-hour period at [Kippure](/wiki/Kippure) on the Wicklow-Dublin border, the greatest daily rainfall total ever recorded in Ireland. The hurricane caused severe flooding and up to 1,000 homes had to be evacuated in Bray alone. Other major floods in Wicklow which resulted in loss of life occurred in 1886–87, 1931, 1965 and 2011.

### Subdivisions

#### Baronies

There are eight historic [baronies](/wiki/Barony_(Ireland)) in the county. While baronies continue to be officially defined units, they are no longer used for administrative purposes. Their official status is illustrated by Placenames Orders made since 2003, where official Irish names of baronies are listed under "Administrative units". The largest barony in Wicklow is Lower Talbotstown, at 88,704 acres (359 km2), and the smallest barony is Rathdown, at 33,463 acres (135 km2).

As of the [2016 census](/wiki/2016_Republic_of_Ireland_census), the resident population of Wicklow was 142,425, a 4.2% increase since the [Census of Ireland 2011](/wiki/Census_of_Ireland_2011). The county's population is one of the fastest growing in the country, increasing 38.7 percent in the 20 years between 1996 and 2016. However, its share of the [Mid East](/wiki/Mid-East_Region,_Ireland)'s population has steadily fallen as Meath and Kildare have experienced even more rapid population growth. While Bray remains by far the largest town in the county, improved transport links to Dublin such as upgrades to the [M11](/wiki/N11_road_(Ireland)) and the completion of the [M50](/wiki/M50_motorway_(Ireland)) in 2005 encouraged the growth of towns further south. Between 2002 and 2016 Bray grew by 5.3 percent. By contrast, over the same period [Arklow](/wiki/Arklow) grew by 32.2 percent, [Greystones](/wiki/Greystones) grew by 53.3 percent and [Rathnew](/wiki/Rathnew) grew by 133.9%.

The most densely populated areas of the county are concentrated in the northeast, with over 50 percent of Wicklow's residents living within 15 kilometres (9 mi) of the [Dublin](/wiki/County_Dublin) border. Many of the county's largest urban areas are clustered in this region along an axis stretching from [Bray](/wiki/Bray,_County_Wicklow) to [Newtownmountkennedy](/wiki/Newtownmountkennedy), which incorporates [Greystones](/wiki/Greystones), [Delgany](/wiki/Delgany), [Enniskerry](/wiki/Enniskerry), [Kilmacanogue](/wiki/Kilmacanogue), [Kilpedder](/wiki/Kilpedder) and [Kilcoole](/wiki/Kilcoole). Outside of this region, [Wicklow](/wiki/Wicklow), [Rathnew](/wiki/Rathnew) and [Arklow](/wiki/Arklow) are the only sizable towns in the eastern portion of the county, while [Blessington](/wiki/Blessington) is the only large settlement in west Wicklow.

The county's interior is very sparsely populated, with only a few small settlements located in the foothills of the Wicklow Mountains such as [Rathdrum](/wiki/Rathdrum,_County_Wicklow), [Laragh](/wiki/Laragh,_County_Wicklow), [Roundwood](/wiki/Roundwood) and [Tinahely](/wiki/Tinahely). Most [small areas](/wiki/Small_Area_Population_Statistics) in central Wicklow have a population density of less than 20 people per km2, compared with an average of 77 people per km2 in the county as a whole.

The gender makeup of the county in 2016 was 50.74% female and 49.26% male. 29.0% of the population were under the age of 19; 58% were between the ages of 19 and 64; and 13.0% were 65 years of age or older.

### Migration

As of 2016, around 85% of Wicklow's population was born within the Republic of Ireland, and a further 0.9% was born in [Northern Ireland](/wiki/Northern_Ireland). Just over 38% of Wicklow's usually resident population was born within the county, making it the second least indigenous county in Ireland, after Meath. While around 35% of the county's population was born in neighbouring Dublin, it is likely that a significant portion of these are Wicklow natives who were born in one of Dublin's many hospitals.

People born in the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom) are by far the largest immigrant group, comprising 5.9% of Wicklow's population (8,388 people). Of this group, only around half (4,045 people) stated that they were either UK or dual Irish-UK citizens. The [Poles](/wiki/Polish_people) are the second largest immigrant group, constituting 2.0% of the county's population. The census recorded 2,905 Polish citizens in Wicklow, of which 2,759 were born in [Poland](/wiki/Poland). The fastest growing major immigrant groups between 2011 and 2016 were [Romanians](/wiki/Romanians), [Indians](/wiki/Indian_people) and [Filipinos](/wiki/Filipinos). There was also a large increase in the number of [Brazilians](/wiki/Brazilians) (+107%), [Italians](/wiki/Italians) (+59%) and [Spaniards](/wiki/Spaniards) (+53%) living in Wicklow over this period.

The 2016 census showed that religious affiliation in Wicklow was as follows: [Catholic](/wiki/Catholic) 73%, [Irreligion](/wiki/Irreligion) 13.3%, Other Christian 8.8%, Other stated religions 3.1%, and not stated 1.8%.

The single largest religious denomination in 2016 was the Roman Catholic Church, with 103,947 adherents. This is a 4.4% decrease from the 2011 census. Catholicism in Wicklow reached its peak percentage in the 1961 census, when 87.5% of the population identified as Catholic. As of 2016, Wicklow is the second least Catholic county in the State, and among the most irreligious.

Other Christian denominations comprised 8.8% of the population. As a proportion of population, Wicklow has the largest [Church of Ireland](/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) affiliation of any county in the Republic of Ireland, at 6.2%, although this figure was historically much higher. The 1901 census recorded that just under 20% of Wicklow's population was affiliated with the Church of Ireland. In 2016 [Greystones](/wiki/Greystones) had the highest percentage of Protestants in the State (10.5%), and was also Ireland's least religious town, with 18.3% of residents stating they did not follow any religion.

Just over 0.5% of the population adhered to smaller Protestant denominations such as [Presbyterian](/wiki/Presbyterian), [Apostolic](/wiki/Apostolic_Church_(disambiguation)) and [Pentecostal](/wiki/Pentecostal). A further 1.05% of respondents stated that they were "Christian" but did not specify any denomination. [Eastern Orthodox](/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox) is the fastest growing of the major Christian denominations, increasing from 840 adherents in 2011 to 1,317 in 2016, a 56.7% increase.

The largest non-Christian religions were [Islam](/wiki/Islam) (0.4%) and [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism) (0.16%). All other stated religions constituted 2.5% of the population, and 1.8% of respondents did not state their religion.

## Economy

### Built environment

Wicklow is home to several major water supply and hydroelectric facilities. The [Turlough Hill](/wiki/Turlough_Hill) [pumped-storage scheme](/wiki/Pumped-storage_hydroelectricity), a significant [civil engineering](/wiki/Civil_engineering) project, was carried out in the mountains in the 1960s and 1970s.

### Leisure and tourism

Wicklow, often called "The Garden of Ireland", has been a popular tourist destination for many years, due to its scenery, beaches, walking, hiking and climbing options, and attractions including the ruins of the monastic city of Glendalough, Wicklow Gaol and water-based activities on reservoirs and the coast.

The [Wicklow Way](/wiki/Wicklow_Way) is the oldest [waymarked](/wiki/Waymarking) [long-distance walking trail](/wiki/Long-distance_trail) in Ireland.

The popular annual mass participation bike ride [Wicklow 200](/wiki/Wicklow_200) has taken place in the county every year since 1982.

### Former districts

County Wicklow was formerly divided into the [rural districts](/wiki/Rural_district_(Ireland)) of Baltinglass No. 1, Rathdown No. 2, Rathdrum, and Shillelagh, and the [urban districts](/wiki/Urban_and_rural_districts_(Ireland)) of [Arklow](/wiki/Arklow), [Bray](/wiki/Bray,_County_Wicklow) and [Wicklow](/wiki/Wicklow). The rural districts were abolished in 1925. [Greystones](/wiki/Greystones), in the former rural district of Rathdown No. 2, was given [town commissioners](/wiki/Town_commissioners) in 1984. The urban districts of Arklow, Bray and Wicklow and the town commissioners of Greystones became [town councils](/wiki/Town_council_(Ireland)) in 2002. All town councils in Ireland were abolished in 2014.

## Transportation

### Road

[M11](/wiki/N11_road_(Ireland)) motorway south of Wicklow town

View from the Sally Gap along the [R759](/wiki/R759_road_(Ireland))

Wicklow has a well-maintained network of [regional roads](/wiki/Regional_road_(Ireland)) which connect the county's western portion to its eastern portion, cross-cutting the Wicklow Mountains from east to west. Several north-south regional roads also traverse the mountains, providing amenity access to the county's interior.

The county is also serviced by a [National secondary road](/wiki/National_secondary_road) (N81) in western Wicklow, and a [National primary road](/wiki/National_primary_road) (N11) in eastern Wicklow which becomes a [motorway](/wiki/Motorways_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) at Junction 14 north of Ashford. The N11/M11 is the main route used by Wicklow commuters heading northbound to Dublin. Southbound traffic can also be heavy on weekends due to the influx of Dublin visitors into Wicklow and [Wexford](/wiki/County_Wexford).

Major routes include:

- [N11/M11](/wiki/N11_road_(Ireland)) – Runs near the east coast for 129 km (80 mi), linking [Dublin](/wiki/Dublin) and [Wexford](/wiki/Wexford). Becomes the M11 at Junction 14 north of [Ashford](/wiki/Ashford,_County_Wicklow).
- [N81](/wiki/N81_road_(Ireland)) – National secondary road that runs from Dublin to [Tullow](/wiki/Tullow), crossing through western Wicklow from [Blessington](/wiki/Blessington) to [Baltinglass](/wiki/Baltinglass).
- [R115](/wiki/R115_road_(Ireland)) – Ireland's highest paved mountain pass. Follows the route of the old Military Road, constructed by the [British Army](/wiki/British_Army) from 1800 to 1809 to put down insurgents in the Wicklow mountains. Terminates at [Laragh](/wiki/Laragh,_County_Wicklow).
- [R412](/wiki/R412_road_(Ireland)) – Regional road that runs north–south from the outskirts of [Naas](/wiki/Naas), crossing through western Wicklow for 8 km (5.0 mi), terminating at the N81 near Loughmogue.
- [R725](/wiki/R725_road_(Ireland)) – 53 km (33 mi) long regional road that crosses the Wicklow Mountains from east to west, connecting [Gorey](/wiki/Gorey) to [Carlow](/wiki/Carlow).
- [R747](/wiki/R747_road_(Ireland)) – Crosses the southern foothills of the Wicklow Mountains through [Tinahely](/wiki/Tinahely), connecting [Arklow](/wiki/Arklow) to [Ballitore](/wiki/Ballitore).
- [R750](/wiki/R750_road_(Ireland)) – Runs for 30 km (19 mi) in eastern Wicklow from [Rathnew](/wiki/Rathnew) to the northern outskirts of Arklow.
- [R755](/wiki/R755_road_(Ireland)) – North-south regional road that runs for 36 km (22 mi) from the N11 at [Kilmacanogue](/wiki/Kilmacanogue) to [Rathdrum](/wiki/Rathdrum,_County_Wicklow).
- [R756](/wiki/R756_road_(Ireland)) – Runs west-east through the [Wicklow Gap](/wiki/Wicklow_Gap) for 32 km (20 mi) from [Dunlavin](/wiki/Dunlavin) to Laragh, intersecting the N81 at the village of [Hollywood](/wiki/Hollywood,_County_Wicklow). The section from Hollywood to Laragh is known as the *Wicklow Gap Road*.
- [R759](/wiki/R759_road_(Ireland)) – Runs south-east to north-west through the Sally Gap. The road is 27 km (17 mi) in length, and in winter can be dangerous or impassable as it is not treated by the Local Authority.
- [R761](/wiki/R761_road_(Ireland)) – 25 km (16 mi) long regional road that runs through several settlements in northeastern Wicklow (Bray, Greystones, Kilcoole, Newcastle) south to Rathnew.

## Culture

### Architecture

A [thatched](/wiki/Thatched) cottage on the Dublin-Wicklow border circa 1895

The [vernacular architecture](/wiki/Architecture_of_Ireland) of County Wicklow conformed to that of Ireland as a whole. Unlike neighbouring [County Wexford](/wiki/County_Wexford) however, Wicklow was not a county generally associated with [thatched roofing](/wiki/Thatching). Historian Christiaan Corlett contends that thatch was "formerly very common in Wicklow until about the 1930s", when afterwards "during the 1930s, 40s and 50s the fashion in Wicklow was to replace thatch roofs with [galvanised](/wiki/Corrugated_galvanised_iron) ones". Sometimes the old thatched roof was kept in place underneath the sheets of galvanising to act as a layer of insulation and dampen the noise of heavy rain.

### Media

Lowlands in east Wicklow, west of [Greystones](/wiki/Greystones).

Mermaid, County Wicklow Arts Centre is based in Bray. Mermaid is the county's hub of artistic activity and creation, offering a programme in many art forms: visual arts, theatre productions, opera, dance performances, arthouse cinema, comedy and a music programme. Two of the county's festivals take place in Arklow, the Arklow Music Festival and the Arklow Seabreeze Festival.

The county is a popular film-making location in Ireland. [Bray](/wiki/Bray,_County_Wicklow) is home to [Ardmore Studios](/wiki/Ardmore_Studios), where many of Ireland's best known feature films, including [John Boorman](/wiki/John_Boorman)'s *[Excalibur](/wiki/Excalibur_(film))* and *[Zardoz](/wiki/Zardoz)*, [Jim Sheridan](/wiki/Jim_Sheridan)'s [Oscar](/wiki/Academy_Award)-winning *[In the Name of the Father](/wiki/In_the_Name_of_the_Father)*, *[Rawhead Rex](/wiki/Rawhead_Rex_(film))*, and several [Neil Jordan](/wiki/Neil_Jordan) films, have been shot. The [BBC](/wiki/BBC) series *[Ballykissangel](/wiki/Ballykissangel)* was also filmed in County Wicklow. Scenes from the movie *[P.S. I Love You](/wiki/P.S._I_Love_You_(film))* were shot in the [Wicklow Mountains National Park](/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains_National_Park) while several scenes from other movies, from *[Barry Lyndon](/wiki/Barry_Lyndon)* to *[Haywire](/wiki/Haywire_(2011_film))*, have been filmed in the county.

The set for Kattegat for the *Vikings* TV series, Ashford Studios
