**James Ray Hines** (September 10, 1946 – June 3, 2023) was an American [track and field](/wiki/Track_and_field) athlete and [National Football League](/wiki/National_Football_League) (NFL) player, who held the [100-meter world record](/wiki/Men%27s_100_metres_world_record_progression) for 15 years. In 1968, he became the first man to officially break the [10-second barrier](/wiki/10-second_barrier) in the [100 meters](/wiki/100_metres), and won individual and relay gold at the [Mexico City Olympics](/wiki/Athletics_at_the_1968_Summer_Olympics).

## Track career

Born in [Dumas, Arkansas](/wiki/Dumas,_Arkansas), Hines was raised in [Oakland, California](/wiki/Oakland,_California), and graduated from [McClymonds High School](/wiki/McClymonds_High_School) in 1964. He was a [baseball](/wiki/Baseball) player in his younger years until he was spotted by track coach Jim Coleman as a running talent, and Hines became a sprinter. At the 1968 U.S. national championships in [Sacramento, California](/wiki/Sacramento,_California), Hines became the first man to break the ten second barrier in the 100-metre race, setting 9.9 (manual timing), with an electronic time of 10.03 – two other athletes, [Ronnie Ray Smith](/wiki/Ronnie_Ray_Smith) behind him (electronic time 10.13) and [Charles Greene](/wiki/Charles_Greene_(athlete)) on the other semi-final (electronic time 10.09) having the same official clocking. That evening of June 20, 1968, at [Hughes Stadium](/wiki/Charles_C._Hughes_Stadium) has been dubbed by track and field historians as the "[Night of Speed](/wiki/Night_of_Speed)". Hines attended [Texas Southern University](/wiki/Texas_Southern_University) in [Houston, Texas](/wiki/Houston,_Texas). He was a member of the Texas Southern University Tigers track team.

A few months later, at the [1968 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics), Hines – a [black](/wiki/Black_people) athlete – found himself in a tense situation, with [racial riots](/wiki/Race_riot) going on in his home country and a threat of a boycott by the black athletes of the U.S. team, who were disturbed by the controversial idea of admitting [apartheid](/wiki/Apartheid) [South Africa](/wiki/South_Africa_at_the_Olympics) to the Games and revelations linking the head of the [International Olympic Committee](/wiki/International_Olympic_Committee), [Avery Brundage](/wiki/Avery_Brundage), to a racist and [antisemitic](/wiki/Antisemitism) country club. Hines reached the 100 m final, and won it with the time 9.89 appearing on the screen, later corrected to 9.95. The 9.89 was taken from a light beam across the finish line, while the official photographic process used [Polaroid film](/wiki/Polaroid_film) and took a couple of minutes to process and read. There was some controversy over how his (slower appearing) automatic time of 9.95 should compare to the hand timed 9.9 world record (Hines was again recorded at 9.9 in his 9.95 race). Automatic times start instantly with the sound of the gun, while hand times include human reaction time to start the watch. It took until 1977 before [fully automatic timing](/wiki/Fully_automatic_timing) was required of world records. As the fastest electronic time to that point, Hines' mark was recognized exclusively as a new world record. The race was also significant for being the third all-black podium in Olympic history. Hines helped break another world record, when he and his teammates sprinted to the [4 × 100 m relay](/wiki/4_%C3%97_100_metres_relay) [gold](/wiki/Gold_medal) at the same Games.

## Professional football career

Hines was drafted by the [Miami Dolphins](/wiki/Miami_Dolphins) of the NFL in the sixth round of the [1968 NFL/AFL draft](/wiki/1968_NFL/AFL_draft). Hines spent the [1968 season](/wiki/1968_NFL_season) on the Dolphins' [practice squad](/wiki/Practice_squad). He was given the nickname "Oops". He appeared in ten games with Miami in 1969, accumulating a total of 52 all-purpose yards. Hines' final professional game was his first and only game with the [Kansas City Chiefs](/wiki/Kansas_City_Chiefs) in 1970. Hines was ranked as the 10th-worst NFL player of all time by [Deadspin](/wiki/Deadspin) writer Jeff Pearlman.

In 1977, Hines played football for the Austin Texans of the semi-pro [American Football Association](/wiki/American_Football_Association_(1977%E2%80%931983)).

## Later years

For years Hines worked with inner-city youth in [Houston](/wiki/Houston), as well as on oil rigs outside the city.

Hines's world record remained unsurpassed until [Calvin Smith](/wiki/Calvin_Smith) ran a time of 9.93 in 1983.

Hines competed in the 100 at a 1984 Masters Track and Field Competition at UCLA.

Hines was inducted into the Texas Sports Hall of Fame, class of 1994. Hines was also inducted into the Texas Track and Field coaches Hall of Fame, class of 2016.

Hines died in [Hayward, California](/wiki/Hayward,_California), on June 3, 2023, at the age of 76.
