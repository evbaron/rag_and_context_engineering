# Gothic Revival architecture

"Neo-Gothic" redirects here. For the contemporary art movement, see [New Gothic](/wiki/New_Gothic).

[Sint-Petrus-en-Pauluskerk](/wiki/Sint-Petrus-en-Pauluskerk) in [Ostend](/wiki/Ostend) (Belgium), built between 1899 and 1908

The [Cathedral of St. John the Baptist](/wiki/Cathedral_of_St._John_the_Baptist_(Savannah,_Georgia)), [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia) ([Georgia](/wiki/Georgia_(U.S._state)), United States)

**Gothic Revival** (also referred to as **Victorian Gothic** or **Neo-Gothic**) is an [architectural movement](/wiki/Architectural_style) that after a gradual build-up beginning in the second half of the 17th century became a widespread movement in the first half of the 19th century, mostly in England. Increasingly serious and learned admirers sought to revive medieval [Gothic architecture](/wiki/Gothic_architecture), intending to complement or even supersede the [neoclassical](/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture) styles prevalent at the time. Gothic Revival draws upon features of medieval examples, including decorative patterns, [finials](/wiki/Finial), [lancet windows](/wiki/Lancet_window), and [hood moulds](/wiki/Hood_mould). By the middle of the 19th century, Gothic Revival had become the pre-eminent architectural style in the [Western world](/wiki/Western_world), only to begin to fall out of fashion in the 1880s and early 1890s.

For some in England, the Gothic Revival movement had roots that were intertwined with philosophical movements associated with [Catholicism](/wiki/Catholicism) and a re-awakening of [high church](/wiki/High_church) or [Anglo-Catholic](/wiki/Anglo-Catholic) belief concerned by the growth of religious nonconformism. The "Anglo-Catholic" tradition of religious belief and style became known for its intrinsic appeal in the third quarter of the 19th century. Gothic Revival architecture varied considerably in its faithfulness to both the ornamental styles and construction principles of its medieval ideal, sometimes amounting to little more than pointed window frames and touches of neo-Gothic decoration on buildings otherwise created on wholly 19th-century plans, using contemporary materials and construction methods; most notably, this involved the use of iron and, after the 1880s, steel in ways never seen in medieval exemplars.

In parallel with the ascendancy of neo-Gothic styles in 19th century England, interest spread to the rest of Europe, Australia, Asia and the Americas; the 19th and early 20th centuries saw the construction of very large numbers of Gothic Revival structures worldwide. The influence of [Revivalism](/wiki/Revivalism_(architecture)) had nevertheless peaked by the 1870s. New architectural movements, sometimes related, as in the [Arts and Crafts movement](/wiki/Arts_and_Crafts_movement), and sometimes in outright opposition, such as [Modernism](/wiki/Modern_architecture), gained ground, and by the 1930s the architecture of the [Victorian era](/wiki/Victorian_era) was generally condemned or ignored. The later 20th century saw a revival of interest, manifested in the United Kingdom by the establishment of the [Victorian Society](/wiki/Victorian_Society) in 1958.

## Roots

The rise of [evangelicalism](/wiki/Evangelicalism) in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries saw in England a reaction in the [high church](/wiki/High_church) movement which sought to emphasise the continuity between the established church and the pre-[Reformation](/wiki/Reformation) Catholic church. Architecture, in the form of the Gothic Revival, became one of the main weapons in the high church's armoury. The Gothic Revival was also paralleled and supported by "[medievalism](/wiki/Medievalism)", which had its roots in [antiquarian](/wiki/Antiquarian) concerns with survivals and curiosities. As "[industrialisation](/wiki/Industrialisation)" progressed, a reaction against machine production and the appearance of factories also grew. Proponents of the picturesque such as [Thomas Carlyle](/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle) and [Augustus Pugin](/wiki/Augustus_Pugin) took a critical view of industrial society and portrayed pre-industrial medieval society as a golden age. To Pugin, Gothic architecture was infused with the Christian values that had been supplanted by [classicism](/wiki/Classicism) and were being destroyed by [industrialisation](/wiki/Industrialisation).

Gothic Revival also took on political connotations, with the "rational" and "radical" Neoclassical style being seen as associated with [republicanism](/wiki/Republicanism) and [liberalism](/wiki/Liberalism) (as evidenced by its use in the United States and to a lesser extent in [Republican](/wiki/French_Revolution) France). In contrast, the more spiritual and traditional Gothic Revival became associated with [monarchism](/wiki/Monarchism) and [conservatism](/wiki/Conservatism), which was reflected by the choice of styles for the rebuilt government centres of the British Parliament's [Palace of Westminster](/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster) in London, the Canadian [Parliament Buildings](/wiki/Parliament_Hill) in [Ottawa](/wiki/Ottawa) and the [Hungarian Parliament Building](/wiki/Hungarian_Parliament_Building) in Budapest.

In English literature, the architectural Gothic Revival and classical [Romanticism](/wiki/Romanticism) gave rise to the [Gothic novel](/wiki/Gothic_novel) genre, beginning with *[The Castle of Otranto](/wiki/The_Castle_of_Otranto)* (1764) by [Horace Walpole](/wiki/Horace_Walpole), and inspired a 19th-century genre of medieval poetry that stems from the pseudo-[bardic poetry](/wiki/Bardic_poetry) of "[Ossian](/wiki/Ossian)". Poems such as "[Idylls of the King](/wiki/Idylls_of_the_King)" by [Alfred, Lord Tennyson](/wiki/Alfred,_Lord_Tennyson) recast specifically modern themes in medieval settings of [Arthurian](/wiki/King_Arthur) romance. In [German literature](/wiki/German_literature), the Gothic Revival also had a grounding in literary fashions.

## Survival and revival

[Tom Tower](/wiki/Tom_Tower), Oxford, by [Sir Christopher Wren](/wiki/Christopher_Wren) 1681–1682, to match the Tudor surroundings

[Gothic architecture](/wiki/Gothic_architecture) began at the [Basilica of Saint Denis](/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint_Denis) near Paris, and the [Cathedral of Sens](/wiki/Cathedral_of_Sens) in 1140 and ended with a last flourish in the early 16th century with buildings like [Henry VII's Chapel](/wiki/Henry_VII_Lady_Chapel) at Westminster. However, Gothic architecture did not die out completely in the 16th century but instead lingered in on-going cathedral-building projects; at [Oxford](/wiki/Oxford_University) and [Cambridge](/wiki/Cambridge_University) Universities, and in the construction of churches in increasingly isolated rural districts of England, France, Germany, the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth) and in Spain.

### Britain and Ireland

[St Columb's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Columb%27s_Cathedral), in [Derry](/wiki/Derry), may be considered 'Gothic Survival', as it was completed in 1633 in a [Perpendicular Gothic](/wiki/Perpendicular_Gothic) style. Similarly, Gothic architecture survived in some urban settings during the later 17th century, as shown in [Oxford](/wiki/Oxford) and [Cambridge](/wiki/Cambridge), where some additions and repairs to Gothic buildings were considered to be more in keeping with the style of the original structures than contemporary [Baroque](/wiki/Baroque_architecture).

In contrast, [Dromore Cathedral](/wiki/Dromore_Cathedral), built in 1660/1661, immediately after the end of [the Protectorate](/wiki/The_Protectorate), revived [Early English](/wiki/Early_English_architecture) forms, demonstrating the restitution of the monarchy and claiming Ireland for the English crown. At the same time, the Great Hall of [Lambeth Palace](/wiki/Lambeth_Palace), that had been despoiled by the [Puritans](/wiki/Puritans), was rebuilt in a mixture of Baroque and older Gothic forms, demonstrating the restitution of the Anglican Church. These two buildings can be said to herald the onset of Gothic Revival architecture, several decades before it became mainstream. [Sir Christopher Wren](/wiki/Christopher_Wren)'s [Tom Tower](/wiki/Tom_Tower) for [Christ Church](/wiki/Christ_Church,_Oxford), [University of Oxford](/wiki/University_of_Oxford), consciously set out to imitate [Cardinal Wolsey's](/wiki/Thomas_Wolsey) architectural style. Writing to [Dean Fell](/wiki/John_Fell_(bishop)) in 1681, he noted; "I resolved it ought to be Gothic to agree with the Founder's work", adding that to do otherwise would lead to "an unhandsome medley". [Pevsner](/wiki/Pevsner_Architectural_Guides) suggests that he succeeded "to the extent that innocent visitors never notice the difference". It was followed in 1697–1704 by the rebuilding of [Collegiate Church of St Mary](/wiki/Collegiate_Church_of_St_Mary,_Warwick) in [Warwick](/wiki/Warwick) as a stone-vaulted [hall church](/wiki/Hall_church), whereas the burnt church had been a [basilica](/wiki/Basilica) with timbered roofs. Also in [Warwickshire](/wiki/Warwickshire), in 1729/30, the nave and aisles of the church of St Nicholas at [Alcester](/wiki/Alcester) were rebuilt by Edward and Thomas Woodward, the exterior in Gothic forms but with a [Neoclassical](/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture) interior. At the same time, 1722–1746, [Nicholas Hawksmoor](/wiki/Nicholas_Hawksmoor) added the west towers to [Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey), which made him a pioneer of Gothic Revival completions of medieval buildings, which from the late 19th century were increasingly disapproved of, although work in this style continued into the 20th century. Back in Oxford, the redecoration of the dining hall at [University College](/wiki/University_College,_Oxford) between 1766 and 1768 has been described as "the first major example of the Gothic Revival style in Oxford".

### Continental Europe

[Pilgrimage Church of Saint John of Nepomuk](/wiki/Pilgrimage_Church_of_Saint_John_of_Nepomuk) by [Johann Santini Aichel](/wiki/Jan_Santini_Aichel) (around 1720), [Czech Republic](/wiki/Czech_Republic), historic [Moravia](/wiki/Moravia)

Throughout France in the 16th and 17th centuries, churches continued to be built following Gothic principles (structure of the buildings, application of tracery) such as [St-Eustache](/wiki/Saint-Eustache,_Paris) (1532–1640, façade 1754) in Paris and [Orléans Cathedral](/wiki/Orl%C3%A9ans_Cathedral) (1601–1829). This newer construction incorporated some little changes like the use of round arches instead of pointed arches and the application of some Classical details, until largely being replaced in new construction with the arrival of Baroque architecture.

In [Bologna](/wiki/Bologna), in 1646, the [Baroque](/wiki/Baroque) architect [Carlo Rainaldi](/wiki/Carlo_Rainaldi) constructed [Gothic vaults](/wiki/Vault_(architecture)) (completed 1658) for the [Basilica of San Petronio](/wiki/Basilica_of_San_Petronio) in Bologna, which had been under construction since 1390; there, the Gothic context of the structure overrode considerations of the current architectural mode. Similarly, in [St. Salvator's Cathedral](/wiki/St._Salvator%27s_Cathedral) of [Bruges](/wiki/Bruges), the timbered medieval vaults of nave and choir were replaced by "Gothic" stone vaults in 1635 resp. 1738/39. [Guarino Guarini](/wiki/Guarino_Guarini), a 17th-century Theatine monk active primarily in [Turin](/wiki/Turin), recognized the "Gothic order" as one of the primary systems of architecture and made use of it in his practice.

Even in [Central Europe](/wiki/Central_Europe) of the late 17th and 18th centuries, where Baroque dominated, some architects continued to use elements of the Gothic style. The most important example is [Johann Santini Aichel](/wiki/Jan_Santini_Aichel), whose [Pilgrimage Church of Saint John of Nepomuk](/wiki/Pilgrimage_Church_of_Saint_John_of_Nepomuk) in [Žďár nad Sázavou](/wiki/%C5%BD%C4%8F%C3%A1r_nad_S%C3%A1zavou), Czech Republic, represents a peculiar and creative synthesis of Baroque and Gothic. An example of another and less striking use of the Gothic style in the time is the [Basilica of Our Lady of Hungary](/wiki/Basilica_of_Our_Lady_of_Hungary) in [Márianosztra](/wiki/M%C3%A1rianosztra), Hungary, whose choir (adjacent to a Baroque nave) was long considered authentically Gothic, because the 18th-century architect used medieval shapes to emphasize the continuity of the monastic community with its 14th-century founders.

### Romantic challenges

During the mid-18th century rise of [Romanticism](/wiki/Romanticism), an increased interest and awareness of the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages_in_history) among influential connoisseurs created a more appreciative approach to selected [medieval](/wiki/Medieval) arts, beginning with church architecture, the tomb monuments of royal and noble personages, stained glass, and late Gothic illuminated manuscripts. Other Gothic arts, such as tapestries and metalwork, continued to be disregarded as barbaric and crude, however sentimental and nationalist associations with historical figures were as strong in this early revival as purely aesthetic concerns.

[Strawberry Hill House](/wiki/Strawberry_Hill_House), [Twickenham](/wiki/Twickenham), London; 1749 by [Horace Walpole](/wiki/Horace_Walpole) (1717–1797). "The seminal house of the Gothic Revival in England", it established the "Strawberry Hill Gothic" style

[German Romanticists](/wiki/German_Romanticism) (including philosopher and writer [Goethe](/wiki/Goethe) and architect [Karl Friedrich Schinkel](/wiki/Karl_Friedrich_Schinkel)), began to appreciate the [picturesque](/wiki/Picturesque) character of ruins—"picturesque" becoming a new aesthetic quality—and those mellowing effects of time that the Japanese call *[wabi-sabi](/wiki/Wabi-sabi)* and that [Horace Walpole](/wiki/Horace_Walpole) independently admired, mildly tongue-in-cheek, as "the true rust of the Barons' wars". The "Gothick" details of Walpole's Twickenham villa, [Strawberry Hill House](/wiki/Strawberry_Hill_House) begun in 1749, appealed to the [rococo](/wiki/Rococo) tastes of the time, and were fairly quickly followed by James Talbot at [Lacock Abbey](/wiki/Lacock_Abbey), Wiltshire. By the 1770s, thoroughly neoclassical architects such as [Robert Adam](/wiki/Robert_Adam) and [James Wyatt](/wiki/James_Wyatt) were prepared to provide Gothic details in drawing-rooms, libraries and chapels and, for William Beckford at [Fonthill](/wiki/Fonthill_Abbey) in Wiltshire, a complete romantic vision of a Gothic abbey.

Some of the earliest architectural examples of the revived are found in Scotland. [Inveraray Castle](/wiki/Inveraray_Castle), constructed from 1746 for the [Duke of Argyll](/wiki/Archibald_Campbell,_3rd_Duke_of_Argyll), with design input from [William Adam](/wiki/William_Adam_(architect)), displays the incorporation of turrets. The architectural historian John Gifford writes that the castellations were the "symbolic assertion of the still quasi-feudal power [the duke] exercised over the inhabitants within his heritable jurisdictions". Most buildings were still largely in the established [Palladian](/wiki/Palladian) style, but some houses incorporated external features of the Scots baronial style. Robert Adam's houses in this style include [Mellerstain](/wiki/Mellerstain) and [Wedderburn](/wiki/Wedderburn_Castle) in Berwickshire and [Seton Castle](/wiki/Seton_Castle) in East Lothian, but it is most clearly seen at [Culzean Castle](/wiki/Culzean_Castle), Ayrshire, remodelled by Adam from 1777. The eccentric landscape designer [Batty Langley](/wiki/Batty_Langley) even attempted to "improve" Gothic forms by giving them classical proportions.

Basilica of Sainte Clotilde Sanctuary, Paris, France

A younger generation, taking Gothic architecture more seriously, provided the readership for John Britton's series *Architectural Antiquities of Great Britain*, which began appearing in 1807. In 1817, [Thomas Rickman](/wiki/Thomas_Rickman) wrote an *Attempt...* to name and define the sequence of Gothic styles in English ecclesiastical architecture, "a text-book for the architectural student". Its long antique title is descriptive: *Attempt to discriminate the styles of English architecture from the Conquest to the Reformation; preceded by a sketch of the Grecian and Roman orders, with notices of nearly five hundred English buildings*. The categories he used were [Norman](/wiki/Norman_architecture), [Early English](/wiki/Early_English_Period), [Decorated](/wiki/Decorated_Gothic), and [Perpendicular](/wiki/Perpendicular_Period). It went through numerous editions, was still being republished by 1881, and has been reissued in the 21st century.

The most common use for Gothic Revival architecture was in the building of churches. Major examples of Gothic cathedrals in the U.S. include the cathedrals of [St. John the Divine](/wiki/Cathedral_of_St._John_the_Divine) and [St. Patrick](/wiki/St._Patrick%27s_Cathedral_(Manhattan)) in New York City and the [Washington National Cathedral](/wiki/Washington_National_Cathedral) on Mount St. Alban in northwest [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.) One of the biggest churches in Gothic Revival style in Canada is the [Basilica of Our Lady Immaculate](/wiki/Basilica_of_Our_Lady_Immaculate) in [Guelph, Ontario](/wiki/Guelph).

Gothic Revival architecture remained one of the most popular and long-lived of the many [revival styles of architecture](/wiki/Revivalism_(architecture)). Although it began to lose force and popularity after the third quarter of the 19th century in commercial, residential and industrial fields, some buildings such as churches, schools, colleges and universities were still constructed in the Gothic style, often known as "Collegiate Gothic", which remained popular in England, Canada and in the United States until well into the early to mid-20th century. Only when new materials, like steel and glass along with concern for function in everyday working life and saving space in the cities, meaning the need to build up instead of out, began to take hold did the Gothic Revival start to disappear from popular building requests.

## Gothic Revival in the other decorative arts

Main article: [Gothic Revival decorative arts](/wiki/Gothic_Revival_decorative_arts)

The study at [Abbotsford](/wiki/Abbotsford,_Scottish_Borders), created for [Sir Walter Scott](/wiki/Walter_Scott), whose novels popularised the [Medieval](/wiki/Medieval) period from which the Gothic Revival drew its inspiration

The revived Gothic style was not limited to architecture. Classical Gothic buildings of the 12th to 16th Centuries were a source of inspiration to 19th-century designers in numerous fields of work. Architectural elements such as pointed arches, steep-sloping roofs and fancy carvings like lace and lattice work were applied to a wide range of Gothic Revival objects. Some examples of Gothic Revival influence can be found in heraldic motifs in coats of arms, furniture with elaborate painted scenes like the whimsical Gothic detailing in English furniture is traceable as far back as [Lady Pomfret](/wiki/Henrietta_Louisa_Fermor)'s house in Arlington Street, London (1740s), and Gothic fretwork in chairbacks and glazing patterns of bookcases is a familiar feature of [Chippendale](/wiki/Thomas_Chippendale)'s *Director* (1754, 1762), where, for example, the three-part bookcase employs Gothic details with Rococo profusion, on a symmetrical form. [Abbotsford](/wiki/Abbotsford,_Scottish_Borders) in the [Scottish Borders](/wiki/Scottish_Borders), rebuilt from 1816 by [Sir Walter Scott](/wiki/Walter_Scott) and paid for by the profits from his hugely successful, historical novels, exemplifies the "Regency Gothic" style. Gothic Revival also includes the reintroduction of medieval clothes and dances in historical re-enactments staged especially in the second part of the 19th century, although one of the first, the [Eglinton Tournament](/wiki/Eglinton_Tournament) of 1839, remains the most famous.

During the [Bourbon Restoration in France](/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France) (1814–1830) and the [Louis-Philippe](/wiki/Louis-Philippe) period (1830–1848), Gothic Revival motifs start to appear, together with revivals of the [Renaissance](/wiki/Renaissance) and of [Rococo](/wiki/Rococo). During these two periods, the vogue for medieval things led craftsmen to adopt Gothic decorative motifs in their work, such as bell [turrets](/wiki/Turret_(architecture)), lancet arches, [trefoils](/wiki/Trefoil), Gothic tracery and [rose windows](/wiki/Rose_window). This style was also known as "Cathedral style" ("À la catédrale").

By the mid-19th century, Gothic traceries and niches could be inexpensively re-created in [wallpaper](/wiki/Wallpaper), and Gothic blind arcading could decorate a ceramic pitcher. Writing in 1857, [J. G. Crace](/wiki/John_Gregory_Crace_(designer)), an influential decorator from a family of influential interior designers, expressed his preference for the Gothic style: "In my opinion there is no quality of lightness, elegance, richness or beauty possessed by any other style... [or] in which the principles of sound construction can be so well carried out". The illustrated catalogue for the [Great Exhibition](/wiki/Great_Exhibition) of 1851 is replete with Gothic detail, from lacemaking and carpet designs to heavy machinery. [Nikolaus Pevsner's](/wiki/Nikolaus_Pevsner) volume on the exhibits at the Great Exhibition, *High Victorian Design* published in 1951, was an important contribution to the academic study of [Victorian](/wiki/Victorian_era) taste and an early indicator of the later 20th century rehabilitation of Victorian architecture and the objects with which they decorated their buildings.

In 1847, eight thousand British [crown](/wiki/Crown_(British_coin)) coins were minted in [proof](/wiki/Proof_coinage) condition with the design using an ornate reverse in keeping with the revived style. Considered by collectors to be particularly beautiful, they are known as 'Gothic crowns'. The design was repeated in 1853, again in proof. A similar two shilling coin, the 'Gothic [florin](/wiki/Two_shillings_(British_coin))' was minted for circulation from 1851 to 1887.

## Romanticism and nationalism

Gothic façade of the [Parlement de Rouen](/wiki/Parlement_de_Rouen) in France, built between 1499 and 1508, which inspired neo-Gothic revival in the 19th century

French neo-Gothic had its roots in the French [medieval Gothic architecture](/wiki/Gothic_architecture), where it was created in the 12th century. Gothic architecture was sometimes known during the medieval period as the "Opus Francigenum", (the "French Art"). French scholar [Alexandre de Laborde](/wiki/Alexandre_de_Laborde) wrote in 1816 that "Gothic architecture has beauties of its own", which marked the beginning of the Gothic Revival in France. Starting in 1828, Alexandre Brogniart, the director of the [Sèvres porcelain manufactory](/wiki/S%C3%A8vres_porcelain), produced fired enamel paintings on large panes of plate glass, for [King Louis-Philippe](/wiki/Louis-Philippe_of_France)'s [Chapelle royale de Dreux](/wiki/Chapelle_royale_de_Dreux), an important early French commission in Gothic taste, preceded mainly by some Gothic features in a few *[jardins paysagers](/wiki/Jardin_paysager)*.

[Sainte-Clotilde Basilica](/wiki/Sainte-Clotilde,_Paris) completed in 1857, Paris

The French Gothic Revival was set on more sound intellectual footings by a pioneer, [Arcisse de Caumont](/wiki/Arcisse_de_Caumont), who founded the Societé des Antiquaires de Normandie at a time when *antiquaire* still meant a connoisseur of antiquities, and who published his great work on architecture in French Normandy in 1830. The following year [Victor Hugo](/wiki/Victor_Hugo)'s historical romance novel *[The Hunchback of Notre-Dame](/wiki/The_Hunchback_of_Notre-Dame)* appeared, in which the great [Gothic cathedral of Paris](/wiki/Notre-Dame_de_Paris) was at once a setting and a protagonist in a hugely popular work of fiction. Hugo intended his book to awaken a concern for the surviving Gothic architecture left in Europe, however, rather than to initiate a craze for neo-Gothic in contemporary life. In the same year that *Notre-Dame de Paris* appeared, the new restored [Bourbon](/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France) monarchy established an office in the Royal French Government of Inspector-General of Ancient Monuments, a post which was filled in 1833 by [Prosper Mérimée](/wiki/Prosper_M%C3%A9rim%C3%A9e), who became the secretary of a new Commission des Monuments Historiques in 1837. This was the Commission that instructed [Eugène Viollet-le-Duc](/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Viollet-le-Duc) to report on the condition of the [Abbey of Vézelay](/wiki/Abbey_of_V%C3%A9zelay) in 1840. Following this, Viollet le Duc set out to restore most of the symbolic buildings in France including Notre Dame de Paris, Vézelay, [Carcassonne](/wiki/Cit%C3%A9_de_Carcassonne), [Roquetaillade castle](/wiki/Roquetaillade_castle), [Mont-Saint-Michel Abbey](/wiki/Mont-Saint-Michel_Abbey) on its peaked coastal island, [Pierrefonds](/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau_de_Pierrefonds), and the [Palais des Papes](/wiki/Palais_des_Papes) in [Avignon](/wiki/Avignon). When France's first prominent neo-Gothic church was built, the [Basilica of Saint-Clotilde](/wiki/Saint_Clotilde_Basilica), Paris, begun in 1846 and consecrated in 1857, the architect chosen was of German extraction, [Franz Christian Gau](/wiki/Franz_Christian_Gau), (1790–1853); the design was significantly modified by Gau's assistant, [Théodore Ballu](/wiki/Th%C3%A9odore_Ballu), in the later stages, to produce the pair of *flèches* that crown the west end.

The lifting of a ban on the [construction of new Catholic churches](/wiki/Russification#Lithuania_and_Poland) around 1900 saw a resurgence of church building across Lithuania, such as St John the Apostle in [Švėkšna](/wiki/%C5%A0v%C4%97k%C5%A1na).

In Germany, there was a renewal of interest in the completion of [Cologne Cathedral](/wiki/Cologne_Cathedral). Begun in 1248, it was still unfinished at the time of the revival. The 1820s "Romantic" movement brought a new appreciation of the building, and construction work began once more in 1842, marking a German return for Gothic architecture. [St. Vitus Cathedral](/wiki/St._Vitus_Cathedral) in [Prague](/wiki/Prague), begun in 1344, was also completed in the mid-19th and early 20th centuries. The importance of the Cologne completion project in German-speaking lands has been explored by Michael J. Lewis, *"The Politics of the German Gothic Revival: August Reichensperger"*. Reichensperger was himself in no doubt as to the cathedral's central position in Germanic culture; "Cologne Cathedral is German to the core, it is a national monument in the fullest sense of the word, and probably the most splendid monument to be handed down to us from the past".

Because of [Romantic nationalism](/wiki/Romantic_nationalism) in the early 19th century, the Germans, French and English all claimed the original Gothic architecture of the 12th century era as originating in their own country. The English boldly coined the term "Early English" for "Gothic", a term that implied Gothic architecture was an English creation. In his 1832 edition of *Notre Dame de Paris*, author Victor Hugo said "Let us inspire in the nation, if it is possible, love for the national architecture", implying that "Gothic" is France's national heritage. In Germany, with the completion of Cologne Cathedral in the 1880s, at the time its summit was the world's tallest building, the cathedral was seen as the height of Gothic architecture. Other major completions of Gothic cathedrals were of [Regensburger Dom](/wiki/Regensburger_Dom) (with twin [spires](/wiki/Spire) completed from 1869 to 1872), [Ulm Münster](/wiki/Ulm_M%C3%BCnster) (with a 161-meter tower from 1890) and [St. Vitus Cathedral](/wiki/St._Vitus_Cathedral) in Prague (1844–1929).

[Cologne Cathedral](/wiki/Cologne_Cathedral), finally completed in 1880 although construction began in 1248

In Belgium, a 15th-century church in [Ostend](/wiki/Ostend) burned down in 1896. King [Leopold II](/wiki/Leopold_II_of_Belgium) funded its replacement, the [Saint Peter's and Saint Paul's Church](/wiki/Sint-Petrus-en-Pauluskerk), a cathedral-scale design which drew inspiration from the neo-Gothic [Votive Church](/wiki/Votive_Church,_Vienna) in [Vienna](/wiki/Vienna) and Cologne Cathedral. In [Mechelen](/wiki/Mechelen), the largely unfinished building drawn in 1526 as the seat of the [Great Council of The Netherlands](/wiki/Great_Council_of_Mechelen), was not actually built until the early 20th century, although it closely followed [Rombout II Keldermans](/wiki/Rombout_II_Keldermans)'s [Brabantine Gothic](/wiki/Brabantine_Gothic) design, and became the 'new' north wing of the City Hall. In [Florence](/wiki/Florence), the [Duomo](/wiki/Florence_Cathedral)'s temporary façade erected for the Medici-House of Lorraine nuptials in 1588–1589, was dismantled, and the west end of the cathedral stood bare again until 1864, when a competition was held to design a new façade suitable to [Arnolfo di Cambio](/wiki/Arnolfo_di_Cambio)'s original structure and the fine [campanile](/wiki/Campanile) next to it. This competition was won by [Emilio De Fabris](/wiki/Emilio_De_Fabris), and so work on his polychrome design and panels of [mosaic](/wiki/Mosaic) was begun in 1876 and completed by 1887, creating the Neo-Gothic western façade.

Eastern Europe also saw much Revival construction; in addition to the [Hungarian Parliament Building](/wiki/Hungarian_Parliament_Building) in Budapest, the [Bulgarian National Revival](/wiki/Bulgarian_National_Revival) saw the introduction of Gothic Revival elements into its vernacular ecclesiastical and residential architecture. The largest project of the Slavine School is the [Lopushna Monastery](/wiki/Lopushna_Monastery) cathedral (1850–1853), though later churches such as [Saint George's Church, Gavril Genovo](/wiki/Saint_George%27s_Church,_Gavril_Genovo) display more prominent vernacular Gothic Revival features.

The [Canadian Parliament Buildings](/wiki/Canadian_Parliament_Buildings) from the [Ottawa River](/wiki/Ottawa_River), including [Gothic Revival library](/wiki/Library_of_Parliament) at rear, built between 1859 and 1876

In Scotland, while a similar Gothic style to that used further south in England was adopted by figures including [Frederick Thomas Pilkington](/wiki/Frederick_Thomas_Pilkington) (1832–1898) in secular architecture it was marked by the re-adoption of the [Scots baronial](/wiki/Scots_baronial) style. Important for the adoption of the style in the early 19th century was Abbotsford, which became a model for the modern revival of the baronial style. Common features borrowed from 16th- and 17th-century houses included [battlemented](/wiki/Battlements) gateways, [crow-stepped gables](/wiki/Crow-stepped_gable), pointed [turrets](/wiki/Turret_(architecture)) and [machicolations](/wiki/Machicolations). The style was popular across Scotland and was applied to many relatively modest dwellings by architects such as [William Burn](/wiki/William_Burn) (1789–1870), [David Bryce](/wiki/David_Bryce) (1803–1876), [Edward Blore](/wiki/Edward_Blore) (1787–1879), [Edward Calvert](/wiki/Edward_Calvert_(architect)) (c. 1847–1914) and [Robert Stodart Lorimer](/wiki/Robert_Stodart_Lorimer) (1864–1929) and in urban contexts, including the building of [Cockburn Street](/wiki/Cockburn_Street,_Edinburgh) in Edinburgh (from the 1850s) as well as the National [Wallace Monument](/wiki/Wallace_Monument) at Stirling (1859–1869). The reconstruction of [Balmoral Castle](/wiki/Balmoral_Castle) as a baronial palace and its adoption as a royal retreat from 1855 to 1858 confirmed the popularity of the style.

In the United States, the first "Gothic stile" church (as opposed to churches with Gothic elements) was [Trinity Church on the Green](/wiki/Trinity_Church_on_the_Green), New Haven, Connecticut. It was designed by [Ithiel Town](/wiki/Ithiel_Town) between 1812 and 1814, while he was building his [Federalist-style](/wiki/Federal_architecture) Center Church, New Haven next to this radical new "Gothic-style" church. Its cornerstone was laid in 1814, and it was consecrated in 1816. It predates [St Luke's Church, Chelsea](/wiki/St_Luke%27s_Church,_Chelsea), often said to be the first Gothic-revival church in London. Though built of [trap rock](/wiki/Trap_rock) stone with arched windows and doors, parts of its tower and its battlements were wood. Gothic buildings were subsequently erected by Episcopal congregations in Connecticut at St John's in Salisbury (1823), St John's in Kent (1823–1826) and St Andrew's in Marble Dale (1821–1823). These were followed by Town's design for [Christ Church Cathedral (Hartford, Connecticut)](/wiki/Christ_Church_Cathedral_(Hartford,_Connecticut)) (1827), which incorporated Gothic elements such as buttresses into the fabric of the church. [St. Paul's Episcopal Church](/wiki/St._Paul%27s_Episcopal_Church_(Troy,_New_York)) in Troy, New York, was constructed in 1827–1828 as an exact copy of Town's design for Trinity Church, New Haven, but using local stone; due to changes in the original, St. Paul's is closer to Town's original design than Trinity itself. In the 1830s, architects began to copy specific English Gothic and Gothic Revival Churches, and these "'mature Gothic Revival' buildings made the domestic Gothic style architecture which preceded it seem primitive and old-fashioned".

There are many examples of [Gothic Revival architecture in Canada](/wiki/Gothic_Revival_architecture_in_Canada). The first major structure was [Notre-Dame Basilica](/wiki/Notre-Dame_Basilica_(Montreal)) in [Montreal, Quebec](/wiki/Montreal), which was designed in 1824. The capital, [Ottawa, Ontario](/wiki/Ottawa), was predominantly a 19th-century creation in the Gothic Revival style. The [Parliament Hill](/wiki/Parliament_Hill) buildings were the preeminent example, of which the original library survives today (after the rest was destroyed by fire in 1916). Their example was followed elsewhere in the city and outlying areas, showing how popular the Gothic Revival movement had become. Other examples of Canadian Gothic Revival architecture in Ottawa are the [Victoria Memorial Museum](/wiki/Victoria_Memorial_Museum), (1905–1908), the [Royal Canadian Mint](/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Mint), (1905–1908), and the [Connaught Building](/wiki/Connaught_Building), (1913–1916), all by [David Ewart](/wiki/David_Ewart).

## Gothic as a moral force

### Pugin and "truth" in architecture

The [Palace of Westminster](/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster) (1840–1876), designed by [Charles Barry](/wiki/Charles_Barry) & [Augustus Pugin](/wiki/Augustus_Pugin)

In the late 1820s, [A. W. N. Pugin](/wiki/Augustus_Pugin), still a teenager, was working for two highly visible employers, providing Gothic detailing for luxury goods. For the Royal furniture makers Morel and Seddon he provided designs for redecorations for the elderly [George IV](/wiki/George_IV_of_the_United_Kingdom) at [Windsor Castle](/wiki/Windsor_Castle) in a Gothic taste suited to the setting. For the royal silversmiths [Rundell Bridge and Co.](/wiki/Rundell_and_Bridge), Pugin provided designs for silver from 1828, using the 14th-century Anglo-French Gothic vocabulary that he would continue to favour later in designs for the new Palace of Westminster. Between 1821 and 1838 Pugin and his father published a series of volumes of [architectural drawings](/wiki/Architectural_drawing), the first two entitled, *Specimens of Gothic Architecture*, and the following three, *Examples of Gothic Architecture*, that were to remain both in print and the standard references for Gothic Revivalists for at least the next century.

In *Contrasts: or, a Parallel between the Noble Edifices of the Middle Ages, and similar Buildings of the Present Day* (1836), Pugin expressed his admiration not only for medieval art but for the whole medieval ethos, suggesting that Gothic architecture was the product of a purer society. In *The True Principles of Pointed or Christian Architecture* (1841), he set out his "two great rules of design: 1st, that there should be no features about a building which are not necessary for convenience, construction or propriety; 2nd, that all ornament should consist of enrichment of the essential construction of the building". Urging modern craftsmen to seek to emulate the style of medieval workmanship as well as reproduce its methods, Pugin sought to reinstate Gothic as the true Christian architectural style.

Pugin's most notable project was the [Houses of Parliament](/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster) in London, after its predecessor was largely destroyed in a fire in 1834. His part in the design consisted of two campaigns, 1836–1837 and again in 1844 and 1852, with the classicist [Charles Barry](/wiki/Charles_Barry) as his nominal superior. Pugin provided the external decoration and the interiors, while Barry designed the symmetrical layout of the building, causing Pugin to remark, "All Grecian, Sir; Tudor details on a classic body".

### Ruskin and Venetian Gothic

Main articles: [Venetian Gothic architecture](/wiki/Venetian_Gothic_architecture) and [High Victorian Gothic](/wiki/High_Victorian_Gothic)

Venetian Gothic in [Baku](/wiki/Baku), [Azerbaijan](/wiki/Azerbaijan)

[John Ruskin](/wiki/John_Ruskin) supplemented Pugin's ideas in his two influential theoretical works, *[The Seven Lamps of Architecture](/wiki/The_Seven_Lamps_of_Architecture)* (1849) and [*The Stones of Venice*](/wiki/The_Stones_of_Venice_(book)) (1853). Finding his architectural ideal in [Venice](/wiki/Venice), Ruskin proposed that Gothic buildings excelled above all other architecture because of the "sacrifice" of the stone-carvers in intricately decorating every stone. In this, he drew a contrast between the physical and spiritual satisfaction which a medieval craftsman derived from his work, and the lack of these satisfactions afforded to modern, [industrialised](/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) labour.

By declaring the [Doge's Palace](/wiki/Doge%27s_Palace,_Venice) to be "the central building of the world", Ruskin argued the case for Gothic government buildings as Pugin had done for churches, though mostly only in theory. When his ideas were put into practice, Ruskin often disliked the result, although he supported many architects, such as [Thomas Newenham Deane](/wiki/Thomas_Newenham_Deane) and [Benjamin Woodward](/wiki/Benjamin_Woodward), and was reputed to have designed some of the [corbel](/wiki/Corbel) decorations for that pair's [Oxford University Museum of Natural History](/wiki/Oxford_University_Museum_of_Natural_History). A major clash between the Gothic and Classical styles in relation to governmental offices occurred less than a decade after the publication of *The Stones of Venice*. A public competition for the construction of a new [Foreign Office](/wiki/Foreign_and_Commonwealth_Office) in [Whitehall](/wiki/Whitehall) saw the decision to award first place to a Gothic design by [George Gilbert Scott](/wiki/George_Gilbert_Scott) overturned by the Prime Minister, [Lord Palmerston](/wiki/Henry_John_Temple,_3rd_Viscount_Palmerston), who successfully demanded a building in the [Italianate](/wiki/Italianate_architecture) style.

### Ecclesiology and funerary style

In England, the [Church of England](/wiki/Anglicanism) was undergoing a revival of [Anglo-Catholic](/wiki/Anglo-Catholic) and [ritualist](/wiki/Ritualism) ideology in the form of the [Oxford Movement](/wiki/Oxford_Movement), and it became desirable to build large numbers of new churches to cater for the growing population, and cemeteries for their hygienic burials. This found ready exponents in the universities, where the [ecclesiological movement](/wiki/Ecclesiological_movement) was forming. Its proponents believed that Gothic was the *only* style appropriate for a parish church, and favoured a particular era of Gothic architecture – the "[decorated](/wiki/Decorated_Period)". The [Cambridge Camden Society](/wiki/Cambridge_Camden_Society), through its journal *The Ecclesiologist*, was so savagely critical of new church buildings that were below its exacting standards and its pronouncements were followed so avidly that it became the epicentre of the flood of [Victorian restoration](/wiki/Victorian_restoration) that affected most of the Anglican cathedrals and parish churches in England and Wales.

[Exeter College, Oxford](/wiki/Exeter_College,_Oxford) Chapel

[St Luke's Church, Chelsea](/wiki/St_Luke%27s_Church,_Chelsea), was a new-built [Commissioner's Church](/wiki/Commissioner%27s_Church) of 1820–1824, partly built using a grant of £8,333 towards its construction with money voted by [Parliament](/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom) as a result of the [Church Building Act 1818](/wiki/Church_Building_Act_1818). It is often said to be the first Gothic Revival church in London, and, as [Charles Locke Eastlake](/wiki/Charles_Locke_Eastlake) put it: "probably the only church of its time in which the main roof was groined throughout in stone". Nonetheless, the parish was firmly [low church](/wiki/Low_church), and the original arrangement, modified in the 1860s, was as a "preaching church" dominated by the pulpit, with a small altar and wooden galleries over the nave aisle.

The development of the private [major metropolitan cemeteries](/wiki/Magnificent_Seven,_London) was occurring at the same time as the movement; [Sir William Tite](/wiki/William_Tite) pioneered the first cemetery in the Gothic style at [West Norwood](/wiki/West_Norwood_Cemetery) in 1837, with chapels, gates, and decorative features in the Gothic manner, attracting the interest of contemporary architects such as [George Edmund Street](/wiki/George_Edmund_Street), Barry, and [William Burges](/wiki/William_Burges). The style was immediately hailed a success and universally replaced the previous preference for classical design.

Not every architect or client was swept away by this tide. Although Gothic Revival succeeded in becoming an increasingly familiar style of architecture, the attempt to associate it with the notion of high church superiority, as advocated by Pugin and the ecclesiological movement, was anathema to those with ecumenical or nonconformist principles. [Alexander "Greek" Thomson](/wiki/Alexander_Thomson) launched a famous attack; "We are told we should adopt [Gothic] because it is the Christian style, and this most impudent assertion has been accepted as sound doctrine even by earnest and intelligent Protestants; whereas it ought only to have force with those who believe that Christian truth attained its purest and most spiritual development at the period when this style of architecture constituted its corporeal form". Those rejecting the link between Gothic and Catholicism looked to adopt it solely for its aesthetic romantic qualities, to combine it with other styles, or look to northern European [Brick Gothic](/wiki/Brick_Gothic) for a more plain appearance; or in some instances all three of these, as at the non-denominational [Abney Park Cemetery](/wiki/Abney_Park_Cemetery) in east London, designed by [William Hosking FSA](/wiki/William_Hosking) in 1840.

## Viollet-le-Duc and Iron Gothic

View of [Carcassonne](/wiki/Carcassonne)

France had lagged slightly in entering the neo-Gothic scene, but produced a major figure in the revival in [Eugène Viollet-le-Duc](/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Viollet-le-Duc). As well as a powerful and influential theorist, Viollet-le-Duc was a leading architect whose genius lay in restoration. He believed in restoring buildings to a state of completion that they would not have known even when they were first built, theories he applied to his restorations of the walled city of [Carcassonne](/wiki/Carcassonne), and to [Notre-Dame](/wiki/Notre-Dame_de_Paris) and [Sainte Chapelle](/wiki/Sainte_Chapelle) in Paris. In this respect he differed from his English counterpart Ruskin, as he often replaced the work of mediaeval stonemasons. His rational approach to Gothic stood in stark contrast to the revival's romanticist origins. Throughout his career he remained in a quandary as to whether iron and masonry should be combined in a building. Iron, in the form of iron anchors, had been used in the most ambitious buildings of medieval Gothic, especially but not only for tracery.

It had in fact been used in "Gothic" buildings since the earliest days of the revival. In some cases, cast iron enabled something like a perfection of medieval design. It was only with Ruskin and the archaeological Gothic's demand for historical truth that iron, whether it was visible or not, was deemed improper for a Gothic building. Ultimately, the utility of iron won out: "substituting a cast iron shaft for a granite, marble or stone column is not bad, but one must agree that it cannot be considered as an innovation, as the introduction of a new principle. Replacing a stone or wooden [lintel](/wiki/Lintel) by an iron [breastsummer](/wiki/Bressummer) is very good". He strongly opposed illusion, however: reacting against the casing of a cast iron pillar in stone, he wrote; "il faut que la pierre paraisse bien être de la pierre; le fer, du fer; le bois, du bois" (stone must appear to be stone; iron, iron; wood, wood).

Cast-iron Gothic tracery supports a bridge by [Calvert Vaux](/wiki/Calvert_Vaux), in [Central Park](/wiki/Central_Park), New York City

The arguments against modern construction materials began to collapse in the mid-19th century as great prefabricated structures such as the glass and iron [Crystal Palace](/wiki/The_Crystal_Palace) and the glazed courtyard of the [Oxford University Museum of Natural History](/wiki/Oxford_University_Museum_of_Natural_History) were erected, which appeared to embody Gothic principles. Between 1863 and 1872 Viollet-le-Duc published his *Entretiens sur l'architecture*, a set of daring designs for buildings that combined iron and masonry. Though these projects were never realised, they influenced several generations of designers and architects, notably [Antoni Gaudí](/wiki/Antoni_Gaud%C3%AD) in Spain and, in England, [Benjamin Bucknall](/wiki/Benjamin_Bucknall), Viollet's foremost English follower and translator, whose masterpiece was [Woodchester Mansion](/wiki/Woodchester_Mansion). The flexibility and strength of cast-iron freed neo-Gothic designers to create new structural Gothic forms impossible in stone, as in [Calvert Vaux](/wiki/Calvert_Vaux)'s cast-iron Gothic bridge in [Central Park](/wiki/Central_Park), New York dating from the 1860. Vaux enlisted openwork forms derived from Gothic blind-arcading and window tracery to express the spring and support of the arching bridge, in flexing forms that presage [Art Nouveau](/wiki/Art_Nouveau).

## Collegiate Gothic

[Trinity College, Hartford](/wiki/Trinity_College,_Hartford): Burges's revised, three-quadrangle, masterplan

Church of St Avila, Bodega, California

Main article: [Collegiate Gothic](/wiki/Collegiate_Gothic)

In the United States, Collegiate Gothic was a late and literal resurgence of the English Gothic Revival, adapted for American college and university campuses. The term "Collegiate Gothic" originated from American architect [Alexander Jackson Davis](/wiki/Alexander_Jackson_Davis)'s handwritten description of his own "English Collegiate Gothic Mansion" of 1853 for the Harrals of Bridgeport. By the 1890s, the movement was known as "Collegiate Gothic".

The firm of [Cope & Stewardson](/wiki/Cope_%26_Stewardson) was an early and important exponent, transforming the campuses of [Bryn Mawr College](/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College), [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University) and the [University of Pennsylvania](/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania) in the 1890s. In 1872, [Abner Jackson](/wiki/Abner_Jackson), the President of [Trinity College, Connecticut](/wiki/Trinity_College_(Connecticut)), visited Britain, seeking models and an architect for a planned new campus for the college. William Burges was chosen and he drew up a four-quadrangled masterplan, in his [Early French](/wiki/French_Gothic#Early_Gothic) style. Lavish illustrations were produced by [Axel Haig](/wiki/Axel_Haig). However, the estimated cost, at just under one million dollars, together with the sheer scale of the plans, thoroughly alarmed the College Trustees and only one-sixth of the plan was executed, the present [Long Walk](/wiki/Trinity_College_Long_Walk), with [Francis H. Kimball](/wiki/Francis_H._Kimball) acting as local, supervising, architect, and [Frederick Law Olmsted](/wiki/Frederick_Law_Olmsted) laying out the grounds. Hitchcock considers the result, "perhaps the most satisfactory of all of [Burges's] works and the best example anywhere of Victorian Gothic collegiate architecture".

The movement continued into the 20th century, with Cope & Stewardson's campus for [Washington University in St. Louis](/wiki/Washington_University_in_St._Louis) (1900–1909), [Charles Donagh Maginnis](/wiki/Charles_Donagh_Maginnis)'s buildings at [Boston College](/wiki/Boston_College) (1910s) (including [Gasson Hall](/wiki/Gasson_Hall)), [Ralph Adams Cram](/wiki/Ralph_Adams_Cram)'s design for the [Princeton University Graduate College](/wiki/Princeton_University_Graduate_College) (1913), and [James Gamble Rogers](/wiki/James_Gamble_Rogers)' reconstruction of the campus of [Yale University](/wiki/Yale_University) (1920s). [Charles Klauder](/wiki/Charles_Klauder)'s Gothic Revival skyscraper on the [University of Pittsburgh](/wiki/University_of_Pittsburgh)'s campus, the [Cathedral of Learning](/wiki/Cathedral_of_Learning) (1926) exhibited Gothic stylings both inside and out, while using modern technologies to make the building taller.

## Vernacular adaptations and the revival in Oceania

Main article: [Carpenter Gothic](/wiki/Carpenter_Gothic)

[Carpenter Gothic](/wiki/Carpenter_Gothic) houses and small churches became common in North America and other places in the late 19th century. These structures adapted Gothic elements such as pointed arches, steep gables, and towers to traditional American [light-frame construction](/wiki/Light-frame_construction). The invention of the [scroll saw](/wiki/Scroll_saw) and mass-produced wood moldings allowed a few of these structures to mimic the florid [fenestration](/wiki/Window) of the High Gothic. But, in most cases, Carpenter Gothic buildings were relatively unadorned, retaining only the basic elements of pointed-arch windows and steep gables. A well-known example of Carpenter Gothic is a house in [Eldon, Iowa](/wiki/Eldon,_Iowa), that [Grant Wood](/wiki/Grant_Wood) used for the background of his painting [American Gothic](/wiki/American_Gothic).

### Australia

Australia, in particular in Melbourne and Sydney, saw the construction of large numbers of Gothic Revival buildings. [William Wardell](/wiki/William_Wardell) (1823–1899) was among the country's most prolific architects; born and trained in England, after emigrating his most notable Australian designs include [St Patrick's Cathedral, Melbourne](/wiki/St_Patrick%27s_Cathedral,_Melbourne) and [St John's College](/wiki/St_John%27s_College,_University_of_Sydney) and [St Mary's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Mary%27s_Cathedral,_Sydney) in Sydney. In common with many other 19th century architects, Wardell could deploy different styles at the command of his clients; [Government House, Melbourne](/wiki/Government_House,_Melbourne) is [Italianate](/wiki/Italianate_architecture). His banking house for the [English, Scottish and Australian Bank](/wiki/English,_Scottish_and_Australian_Bank) in Melbourne has been described as "the Australian masterpiece of neo-Gothic". This claim has also been made for [Edmund Blacket](/wiki/Edmund_Blacket)'s [MacLaurin Hall](/wiki/Great_Hall_of_the_University_of_Sydney) at the [University of Sydney](/wiki/University_of_Sydney), which sits in the [quadrangle complex](/wiki/University_of_Sydney_Quadrangle) described as "arguably the most important group of Gothic and Tudor Revival style architecture in Australia".

### New Zealand

Old St Paul's in [Wellington, New Zealand](/wiki/Wellington,_New_Zealand)

[Benjamin Mountfort](/wiki/Benjamin_Mountfort), born in Britain, trained in Birmingham, and subsequently resident in [Canterbury, New Zealand](/wiki/Canterbury,_New_Zealand) imported the Gothic Revival style to his adopted country and designed Gothic Revival churches in both wood and stone, notably in the city of [Christchurch](/wiki/Christchurch).

[Frederick Thatcher](/wiki/Frederick_Thatcher) designed wooden churches in the Gothic Revival style, for example [Old St. Paul's, Wellington](/wiki/Old_St._Paul%27s,_Wellington), contributing to what has been described as New Zealand's "one memorable contribution to world architecture". [St Mary of the Angels, Wellington](/wiki/St_Mary_of_the_Angels,_Wellington) by [Frederick de Jersey Clere](/wiki/Frederick_de_Jersey_Clere) is in the French Gothic style, and was the first Gothic design church built in ferro-concrete. Other examples in [Wellington](/wiki/Wellington) include [John Sydney Swan](/wiki/John_Sydney_Swan)'s now-demolished [Erskine College](/wiki/Erskine_College,_Wellington).

The style also found favour in the southern New Zealand city of [Dunedin](/wiki/Dunedin), where the wealth brought in by the [Otago gold rush](/wiki/Otago_gold_rush) of the 1860s allowed for substantial stone edifices to be constructed, using hard, dark [breccia](/wiki/Breccia) stone and a local white limestone, [Oamaru stone](/wiki/Oamaru_stone). Among them were [Maxwell Bury](/wiki/Maxwell_Bury)'s [University of Otago Registry Building](/wiki/University_of_Otago_Registry_Building) and the [Dunedin Law Courts](/wiki/Dunedin_Law_Courts) by [John Campbell](/wiki/John_Campbell_(architect)).

Welsh-born [William Skinner](/wiki/William_Henry_Skinner) designed the third and current building for [St Paul's](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Church,_Auckland) in [Auckland](/wiki/Auckland) in the Gothic Revival style. Known as the ['Mother Church'](/wiki/Mother_church), due to its foundation within a year of the city’s establishment in 1841, the dark [Rangitoto](/wiki/Rangitoto_Island) [basalt](/wiki/Basalt) and light Oamaru limestone church was consecrated in 1895. Skinner also designed the [kauri](/wiki/Agathis) timber St James' Union Church in [Thames](/wiki/Thames,_New_Zealand) in the Gothic style.

### Pacific islands

Several examples of Gothic Revival architecture can be found among the buildings of the Pacific islands. Notable among these is [Sacred Heart Church](/wiki/Sacred_Heart_Church,_Levuka) in [Levuka](/wiki/Levuka), [Fiji](/wiki/Fiji), built by Father Louyot in 1858. This unusual structure consists of a church and presbytery in a local version of [Carpenter Gothic](/wiki/Carpenter_Gothic) alongside a stone church tower.

## Global Gothic

[Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus](/wiki/Chhatrapati_Shivaji_Terminus) in [Mumbai](/wiki/Mumbai), India. A mixture of Romanesque, Gothic and [Indian elements](/wiki/Indo-Saracenic_architecture).

[Henry-Russell Hitchcock](/wiki/Henry-Russell_Hitchcock), the architectural historian, noted the spread of the Gothic Revival in the 19th and early 20th centuries, "wherever English culture extended – as far as the West Coast of the United States and to the remotest Antipodes". The [British Empire](/wiki/British_Empire), almost at its geographic peak at the height of the Gothic Revival, assisted or compelled this spread. The English-speaking [dominions](/wiki/Dominions), Canada, Australia particularly the state of Victoria and New Zealand generally adopted British styles in toto (see above); other parts of the empire saw regional adaptations. India saw the construction of many such buildings, in styles termed [Indo-Saracenic](/wiki/Indo-Saracenic_architecture) or Hindu-Gothic. Notable examples include [Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus](/wiki/Chhatrapati_Shivaji_Terminus) (formerly Victoria Terminus) and the [Taj Mahal Palace Hotel](/wiki/Taj_Mahal_Palace_Hotel), both in [Mumbai](/wiki/Mumbai). At the hill station of [Shimla](/wiki/Shimla), the [summer capital](/wiki/Summer_capital) of [British India](/wiki/British_Raj), an attempt was made to recreate the [Home counties](/wiki/Home_counties) in the foothills of the [Himalayas](/wiki/Himalayas). Although Gothic Revival was the predominant architectural style, alternatives were also deployed; [Rashtrapati Niwas](/wiki/Rashtrapati_Niwas), the former Viceregal Lodge, has been variously described as [Scottish Baronial Revival](/wiki/Scottish_baronial_architecture#Scottish_Baronial_Revival), [Tudor Revival](/wiki/Tudor_Revival_architecture) and [Jacobethan](/wiki/Jacobethan).

Other examples in the east include the late 19th century [Church of the Saviour, Beijing](/wiki/Church_of_the_Saviour,_Beijing), constructed on the orders of the [Guangxu Emperor](/wiki/Guangxu_Emperor) and designed by the Catholic missionary and architect [Alphonse Favier](/wiki/Alphonse_Favier); and the [Wat Niwet Thammaprawat](/wiki/Wat_Niwet_Thammaprawat) in the [Bang Pa-In Royal Palace](/wiki/Bang_Pa-In_Royal_Palace) in [Bangkok](/wiki/Bangkok), by the Italian [Joachim Grassi](/wiki/Joachim_Grassi). In Indonesia, (the former colony of the [Dutch East Indies](/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies)), the [Jakarta Cathedral](/wiki/Jakarta_Cathedral) was begun in 1891 and completed in 1901 by Dutch architect Antonius Dijkmans; while further north in the islands of the Philippines, the [San Sebastian Church](/wiki/San_Sebastian_Church_(Manila)), designed by architects Genaro Palacios and [Gustave Eiffel](/wiki/Gustave_Eiffel), was consecrated in 1891 in the still Spanish colony. Church building in [South Africa](/wiki/South_Africa) was extensive, with little or no effort to adopt vernacular forms. [Robert Gray](/wiki/Robert_Gray_(bishop_of_Cape_Town)), the first [bishop](/wiki/Anglican_Diocese_of_Cape_Town) of [Cape Town](/wiki/Cape_Town), wrote; "I am sure we do not overestimate the importance of real Churches built after the fashion of our English churches". He oversaw the construction of some fifty such buildings between 1848 and his death in 1872. [South America](/wiki/South_America) saw a later flourishing of the Revival, particularly in church architecture, for example the [Metropolitan Cathedral of São Paulo](/wiki/S%C3%A3o_Paulo_Cathedral) in Brazil by the German [Maximilian Emil Hehl](/wiki/Maximilian_Emil_Hehl), the [Basilica of the National Vow](/wiki/Bas%C3%ADlica_del_Voto_Nacional) in Ecuador, the [Sanctuary of Las Lajas](/wiki/Sanctuary_of_Las_Lajas) in Colombia, the [Basílica de Nuestra Señora del Perpetuo Socorro](/wiki/Bas%C3%ADlica_de_Nuestra_Se%C3%B1ora_del_Perpetuo_Socorro) in Chile, the [Church of the Virgin of Mount Carmel and St. Thérèse of Lisieux](/wiki/Virgen_del_Carmen_y_Santa_Teresita,_Montevideo) in Uruguay, or the [Cathedral of La Plata](/wiki/Cathedral_of_La_Plata) in Argentina.

## 20th and 21st centuries

Construction of [Washington National Cathedral](/wiki/Washington_National_Cathedral) began in 1907 and was completed in 1990.

The Gothic style dictated the use of structural members in [compression](/wiki/Compression_member), leading to tall, buttressed buildings with interior columns of [load-bearing](/wiki/Load-bearing_wall) masonry and tall, narrow windows. But, by the start of the 20th century, technological developments such as the [steel frame](/wiki/Steel_frame), the [incandescent light bulb](/wiki/Incandescent_light_bulb) and the [elevator](/wiki/Elevator) made this approach obsolete. Steel framing supplanted the non-ornamental functions of [rib vaults](/wiki/Rib_vault) and [flying buttresses](/wiki/Flying_buttresses), providing wider open interiors with fewer columns interrupting the view.

Some architects persisted in using Neo-Gothic tracery as applied ornamentation to an iron skeleton underneath, for example in [Cass Gilbert](/wiki/Cass_Gilbert)'s 1913 [Woolworth Building](/wiki/Woolworth_Building) skyscraper in New York and [Raymond Hood](/wiki/Raymond_Hood)'s 1922 [Tribune Tower](/wiki/Tribune_Tower) in Chicago. The [Tower Life Building](/wiki/Tower_Life_Building) in San Antonio, completed in 1929, is noted for the arrays of decorative [gargoyles](/wiki/Gargoyle) on its upper floors. But, over the first half of the century, Neo-Gothic was supplanted by [Modernism](/wiki/Modernism), although some modernist architects saw the Gothic tradition of architectural form entirely in terms of the "honest expression" of the technology of the day, and saw themselves as heirs to that tradition, with their use of rectangular frames and exposed iron girders.

[Liverpool Cathedral](/wiki/Liverpool_Cathedral), whose construction ran from 1903 to 1978

In spite of this, the Gothic Revival continued to exert its influence, simply because many of its more massive projects were still being built well into the second half of the 20th century, such as [Giles Gilbert Scott](/wiki/Giles_Gilbert_Scott)'s [Liverpool Cathedral](/wiki/Liverpool_Cathedral) and the [Washington National Cathedral](/wiki/Washington_National_Cathedral) (1907–1990). [Ralph Adams Cram](/wiki/Ralph_Adams_Cram) became a leading force in American Gothic, his most ambitious project being the [Cathedral of St. John the Divine](/wiki/Cathedral_of_St._John_the_Divine) in New York (claimed to be the largest cathedral in the world); another was the Collegiate Gothic buildings at [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University). Cram said "the style hewn out and perfected by our ancestors [has] become ours by uncontested inheritance".

Though the number of new Gothic Revival buildings declined sharply after the 1930s, they continue to be built. [St Edmundsbury Cathedral](/wiki/St_Edmundsbury_Cathedral), the cathedral of [Bury St Edmunds](/wiki/Bury_St_Edmunds) in [Suffolk](/wiki/Suffolk), was expanded and reconstructed in a neo-Gothic style between the late 1950s and 2005, and a commanding stone central tower was added. A new church in the Gothic style is planned for St. John Vianney Parish in [Fishers, Indiana](/wiki/Fishers,_Indiana). The Whittle Building at [Peterhouse](/wiki/Peterhouse,_Cambridge), [University of Cambridge](/wiki/University_of_Cambridge), opened in 2016, matches the neo-Gothic style of the rest of the courtyard in which it is situated.

## Appreciation

By 1872, the Gothic Revival was mature enough in the United Kingdom that [Charles Locke Eastlake](/wiki/Charles_Locke_Eastlake), an influential professor of design, could produce *A History of the Gothic Revival*. [Kenneth Clark's](/wiki/Kenneth_Clark), *The Gothic Revival. An Essay*, followed in 1928, in which he described the Revival as "the most widespread and influential artistic movement which England has ever produced." The architect and writer [Harry Stuart Goodhart-Rendel](/wiki/Harry_Stuart_Goodhart-Rendel) covered the subject of the Revival in an appreciative way in his [Slade Lectures](/wiki/Slade_Professor_of_Fine_Art) in 1934. But the conventional early 20th century view of the architecture of the Gothic Revival was strongly dismissive, critics writing of "the nineteenth century architectural tragedy", ridiculing "the uncompromising ugliness" of the era's buildings and attacking the "sadistic hatred of beauty" of its architects. The 1950s saw further signs of a recovery in the reputation of Revival architecture. John Steegman's study, *Consort of Taste* (re-issued in 1970 as *Victorian Taste*, with a foreword by [Nikolaus Pevsner](/wiki/Nikolaus_Pevsner)), was published in 1950 and began a slow turn in the tide of opinion "towards a more serious and sympathetic assessment." In 1958, Henry-Russell Hitchcock published his *Architecture: Nineteenth and Twentieth Centuries*, as part of the [Pelican History of Art](/wiki/Penguin_Books) series edited by Nikolaus Pevsner. Hitchcock devoted substantial chapters to the Gothic Revival, noting that, while "there is no more typical nineteenth-century product than a Victorian Gothic church", the success of the Victorian Gothic saw its practitioners design mansions, castles, colleges, and parliaments. The same year saw the foundation of the [Victorian Society](/wiki/Victorian_Society) in England and, in 1963, the publication of *Victorian Architecture*, an influential collection of essays edited by Peter Ferriday. By 2008, the fiftieth anniversary of the founding of the Victorian Society, the architecture of the Gothic Revival was more fully appreciated with some of its leading architects receiving scholarly attention and some of its best buildings, such as [George Gilbert Scott](/wiki/George_Gilbert_Scott)'s [St Pancras Station Hotel](/wiki/St._Pancras_Renaissance_London_Hotel), being magnificently restored. The Society's 50th anniversary publication, *Saving A Century*, surveyed a half-century of losses and successes, reflected on the changing perceptions toward Victorian architecture and concluded with a chapter entitled "The Victorians Victorious".
