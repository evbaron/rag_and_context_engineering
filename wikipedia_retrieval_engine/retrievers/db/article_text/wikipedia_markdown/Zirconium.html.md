**Zirconium** is a [chemical element](/wiki/Chemical_element); it has [symbol](/wiki/Symbol_(chemistry)) **Zr** and [atomic number](/wiki/Atomic_number) 40. First identified in 1789, isolated in impure form in 1824, and manufactured at scale by 1925, pure zirconium is a lustrous [transition metal](/wiki/Transition_metal) with a greyish-white color that closely resembles [hafnium](/wiki/Hafnium) and, to a lesser extent, [titanium](/wiki/Titanium). It is solid at room temperature, [ductile](/wiki/Ductility), [malleable](/wiki/Malleable) and [corrosion](/wiki/Corrosion)-resistant. The name *zirconium* is derived from the name of the mineral [zircon](/wiki/Zircon), the most important source of zirconium. The word is related to [Persian](/wiki/Persian_Language) *[zargun](/wiki/Jargoon)* (zircon; *zar-gun*, "gold-like" or "as gold"). Besides zircon, zirconium occurs in over 140 other minerals, including [baddeleyite](/wiki/Baddeleyite) and [eudialyte](/wiki/Eudialyte); most zirconium is produced as a byproduct of minerals mined for titanium and [tin](/wiki/Tin).

Zirconium forms a variety of [inorganic](/wiki/Inorganic_chemistry) compounds, such as [zirconium dioxide](/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide), and [organometallic compounds](/wiki/Organometallic_compounds), such as [zirconocene dichloride](/wiki/Zirconocene_dichloride). Five [isotopes](/wiki/Isotopes_of_zirconium) occur naturally, four of which are stable. The metal and its alloys are mainly used as a [refractory](/wiki/Refractory) and [opacifier](/wiki/Opacifier); zirconium alloys are used to clad [nuclear fuel rods](/wiki/Nuclear_fuel_rod) due to their low neutron absorption and strong resistance to corrosion, and in space vehicles and turbine blades where high heat resistance is necessary. Zirconium also finds uses in [flashbulbs](/wiki/Flashbulb_(photography)), biomedical applications such as [dental implants](/wiki/Dental_implant) and [prosthetics](/wiki/Prosthesis), [deodorant](/wiki/Deodorant), and [water purification](/wiki/Water_purification) systems.

Zirconium compounds have no known biological role, though the element is widely distributed in nature and appears in small quantities in biological systems without adverse effects. There is no indication of zirconium as a carcinogen. The main hazards posed by zirconium are flammability in powder form and irritation of the eyes.

## Characteristics

Zirconium rod

Zirconium is a [lustrous](/wiki/Luster_(mineralogy)), greyish-white, soft, ductile, malleable metal that is solid at room temperature, though it is hard and [brittle](/wiki/Brittle) at lesser purities. In powder form, zirconium is highly flammable, but the solid form is much less prone to ignition. Zirconium is highly resistant to corrosion by alkalis, acids, salt water, and other agents. However, it will dissolve in [hydrochloric](/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid) and [sulfuric acid](/wiki/Sulfuric_acid), especially when [fluorine](/wiki/Fluorine) is present. [Alloys](/wiki/Alloy) with [zinc](/wiki/Zinc) are [magnetic](/wiki/Magnetism) at less than 35 K.

The [melting point](/wiki/Melting_point) of zirconium is 1855 °C (3371 °F), and the [boiling point](/wiki/Boiling_point) is 4409 °C (7968 °F). Zirconium has an [electronegativity](/wiki/Electronegativity) of 1.33 on the Pauling scale. Of the elements within the [d-block](/wiki/D-block) with known electronegativities, zirconium has the fourth lowest electronegativity after [hafnium](/wiki/Hafnium), [yttrium](/wiki/Yttrium), and [lutetium](/wiki/Lutetium).

At room temperature zirconium exhibits a hexagonally close-packed crystal structure, α-Zr, which changes to β-Zr, a body-centered cubic crystal structure, at 863 °C. Zirconium exists in the β-phase until the melting point.

### Isotopes

Main article: [Isotopes of zirconium](/wiki/Isotopes_of_zirconium)

Naturally occurring zirconium is composed of five isotopes. 90Zr, 91Zr, 92Zr, 94Zr, and 96Zr. The first four are stable, while 96 is observed to decay with a half-life of 2.34×1019 years by [double beta](/wiki/Double_beta_decay) emission; this is theoretically possible also for 94Zr. Of these natural isotopes, 90Zr is the most common, making up 51.45% of all zirconium, and 96Zr is the least common, comprising only 2.80%.

The artificial [radioisotopes](/wiki/Radioisotope) of zirconium known range from 77Zr to 114Zr, and 13 [nuclear isomers](/wiki/Nuclear_isomer) are also listed. The most stable among them is [93Zr](/wiki/Zirconium-93), a [long-lived fission product](/wiki/Long-lived_fission_product), with a half-life of 1.61 million years. Radioactive isotopes at or above mass number 93 decay by [electron emission](/wiki/Beta_decay) resulting in [niobium](/wiki/Isotopes_of_niobium) isotopes, whereas those at or below 89 decay by [positron emission](/wiki/Beta_decay) or [electron capture](/wiki/Electron_capture), resulting in [yttrium](/wiki/Isotopes_of_yttrium) isotopes.

### Occurrence

See also: [Category:Zirconium minerals](/wiki/Category:Zirconium_minerals)

World production trend of zirconium mineral concentrates

Zirconium has a concentration of about 130 mg/kg within the [Earth's crust](/wiki/Abundance_of_elements_in_Earth%27s_crust) and about 0.026 μg/L in [sea water](/wiki/Sea_water). It is the 18th most abundant element in the crust. It is not found in nature as a [native metal](/wiki/Native_metal), reflecting its intrinsic instability with respect to water. The principal commercial source of zirconium is [zircon](/wiki/Zircon) (ZrSiO4), a [silicate mineral](/wiki/Silicate_mineral), which is found primarily in Australia, Brazil, India, Russia, South Africa and the United States, as well as in smaller deposits around the world. As of 2023, Australia and South Africa are the leading producers, together accounting for approximately half of global zircon production. Zircon resources exceed 60 million [tonnes](/wiki/Tonne) worldwide and annual worldwide zirconium production is approximately 900,000 tonnes. Zirconium also occurs in more than 140 other minerals, including the commercially useful ores [baddeleyite](/wiki/Baddeleyite) and [eudialyte](/wiki/Eudialyte).

Zirconium is relatively abundant in [S-type stars](/wiki/Stellar_classification#Class_S), and has been detected in the sun and in meteorites. Lunar rock samples brought back from several [Apollo](/wiki/Apollo_program) missions to the moon have a high zirconium oxide content relative to terrestrial rocks.

[EPR spectroscopy](/wiki/EPR_spectroscopy) has been used in investigations of the unusual 3+ valence state of zirconium. The EPR spectrum of Zr3+, which has been initially observed as a parasitic signal in Fe‐doped single crystals of ScPO4, was definitively identified by preparing single crystals of ScPO4 doped with isotopically enriched (94.6%)91Zr. Single crystals of LuPO4 and YPO4 doped with both naturally abundant and isotopically enriched Zr have also been grown and investigated.

## Production

### Occurrence

Zirconium output in 2005

Zirconium is a by-product formed after mining and processing of the [titanium](/wiki/Titanium) minerals [ilmenite](/wiki/Ilmenite) and [rutile](/wiki/Rutile), as well as [tin](/wiki/Tin) mining. From 2003 to 2007, while prices for the mineral zircon steadily increased from $360 to $840 per tonne, the price for unwrought zirconium metal decreased from $39,900 to $22,700 per ton. Zirconium metal is much more expensive than [zircon](/wiki/Zircon) because the reduction processes are costly.

Collected from coastal waters, zircon-bearing sand is purified by [spiral concentrators](/wiki/Spiral_separator#Wet_Spiral_Separators) to separate lighter materials, which are then returned to the water because they are natural components of beach sand. Using [magnetic separation](/wiki/Magnetic_separation), the titanium ores [ilmenite](/wiki/Ilmenite) and [rutile](/wiki/Rutile) are removed.

Most zircon is used directly in commercial applications, but a small percentage is converted to the metal. Most Zr metal is produced by the reduction of the [zirconium(IV) chloride](/wiki/Zirconium(IV)_chloride) with [magnesium](/wiki/Magnesium) metal in the [Kroll process](/wiki/Kroll_process). The resulting metal is [sintered](/wiki/Sintering) until sufficiently ductile for metalworking.

### Separation of zirconium and hafnium

Commercial zirconium metal typically contains 1–3% of [hafnium](/wiki/Hafnium), which is usually not problematic because the chemical properties of hafnium and zirconium are very similar. Their neutron-absorbing properties differ strongly, however, necessitating the separation of hafnium from zirconium for nuclear reactors. Several separation schemes are in use. The [liquid–liquid extraction](/wiki/Liquid%E2%80%93liquid_extraction) of the [thiocyanate](/wiki/Thiocyanate)-oxide derivatives exploits the fact that the hafnium derivative is slightly more soluble in [methyl isobutyl ketone](/wiki/Methyl_isobutyl_ketone) than in water. This method accounts for roughly two-thirds of pure zirconium production, though other methods are being researched; for instance, in India, a TBP-nitrate solvent extraction process is used for the separation of zirconium from other metals. Zr and Hf can also be separated by [fractional crystallization](/wiki/Fractional_crystallization_(chemistry)) of [potassium hexafluorozirconate](/wiki/Potassium_hexafluorozirconate) (K2ZrF6), which is less soluble in water than the analogous hafnium derivative. [Fractional distillation](/wiki/Fractional_distillation) of the tetrachlorides, also called [extractive distillation](/wiki/Extractive_distillation), is also used.

Vacuum [arc melting](/wiki/Electric_arc_furnace), combined with the use of hot extruding techniques and [supercooled](/wiki/Supercooling) copper hearths, is capable of producing zirconium that has been purified of oxygen, nitrogen, and carbon.

Hafnium must be removed from zirconium for nuclear applications because hafnium has a neutron absorption cross-section 600 times greater than zirconium. The separated hafnium can be used for reactor [control rods](/wiki/Control_rods).

## Compounds

See also the categories [Zirconium compounds](/wiki/Category:Zirconium_compounds) and [Zirconium minerals](/wiki/Category:Zirconium_minerals)

Like other [transition metals](/wiki/Transition_metal), zirconium forms a wide range of [inorganic compounds](/wiki/Inorganic_chemistry) and [coordination complexes](/wiki/Coordination_complex). In general, these compounds are colourless diamagnetic solids wherein zirconium has the [oxidation state](/wiki/Oxidation_state) +4. Some organometallic compounds are considered to have Zr(II) oxidation state. Non-equilibrium oxidation states between 0 and 4 have been detected during zirconium oxidation.

### Oxides, nitrides, and carbides

"ZrO" redirects here. For other uses, see [ZRO](/wiki/ZRO_(disambiguation)).

The most common oxide is [zirconium dioxide](/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide), ZrO2, also known as *zirconia*. This clear to white-coloured solid has exceptional [fracture toughness](/wiki/Fracture_toughness) (for a ceramic) and chemical resistance, especially in its [cubic](/wiki/Cubic_zirconia) form. These properties make zirconia useful as a [thermal barrier](/wiki/Thermal_barrier) coating, although it is also a common [diamond](/wiki/Diamond) substitute. Zirconium monoxide, ZrO, is also known and [S-type stars](/wiki/S-type_star) are recognised by detection of its emission lines.

[Zirconium tungstate](/wiki/Zirconium_tungstate) has the unusual property of shrinking in all dimensions when heated, whereas most other substances expand when heated. [Zirconyl chloride](/wiki/Zirconyl_chloride) is one of the few water-soluble zirconium complexes, with the formula [Zr4(OH)12(H2O)16]Cl8.

[Zirconium carbide](/wiki/Zirconium_carbide) and [zirconium nitride](/wiki/Zirconium_nitride) are refractory solids. Both are highly [corrosion](/wiki/Corrosion)-resistant and find uses in high-temperature resistant coatings and cutting tools. Zirconium hydride phases are known to form when zirconium alloys are exposed to large quantities of [hydrogen](/wiki/Hydrogen) over time; due to the brittleness of zirconium hydrides relative to zirconium alloys, the mitigation of zirconium hydride formation was highly studied during the development of the first commercial [nuclear reactors](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor), in which zirconium carbide was a frequently used material.

[Lead zirconate titanate](/wiki/Lead_zirconate_titanate) (PZT) is the most commonly used [piezoelectric](/wiki/Piezoelectricity) material, being used as [transducers](/wiki/Transducer) and [actuators](/wiki/Actuator) in medical and [microelectromechanical systems](/wiki/MEMS) applications.

### Halides and pseudohalides

All four common halides are known, [ZrF4](/wiki/Zirconium(IV)_fluoride), [ZrCl4](/wiki/Zirconium(IV)_chloride), [ZrBr4](/wiki/Zirconium(IV)_bromide), and [ZrI4](/wiki/Zirconium(IV)_iodide). All have polymeric structures and are far less volatile than the corresponding titanium tetrahalides; they find applications in the formation of organic complexes such as [zirconocene dichloride](/wiki/Zirconocene_dichloride). All tend to [hydrolyse](/wiki/Hydrolyse) to give the so-called oxyhalides and dioxides.

Fusion of the tetrahalides with additional metal gives lower zirconium halides (e.g. [ZrCl3](/wiki/Zirconium(III)_chloride)). These adopt a layered structure, conducting within the layers but not perpendicular thereto.

The corresponding tetra[alkoxides](/wiki/Alkoxide) are also known. Unlike the halides, the alkoxides dissolve in nonpolar solvents. [Dihydrogen hexafluorozirconate](/wiki/Hexafluorozirconic_acid) is used in the metal finishing industry as an etching agent to promote paint adhesion.

### Organic derivatives

Main article: [Organozirconium chemistry](/wiki/Organozirconium_chemistry)

[Zirconocene dichloride](/wiki/Zirconocene_dichloride), a representative [organozirconium compound](/wiki/Organozirconium_compound)

[Organozirconium chemistry](/wiki/Organozirconium_chemistry) is key to [Ziegler–Natta catalysts](/wiki/Ziegler%E2%80%93Natta_catalyst), used to produce [polypropylene](/wiki/Polypropylene). This application exploits the ability of zirconium to reversibly form bonds to carbon. Zirconocene dibromide ((C5H5)2ZrBr2), reported in 1952 by Birmingham and [Wilkinson](/wiki/Geoffrey_Wilkinson), was the first organozirconium compound. [Schwartz's reagent](/wiki/Schwartz%27s_reagent), prepared in 1970 by P. C. Wailes and H. Weigold, is a [metallocene](/wiki/Metallocene) used in [organic synthesis](/wiki/Organic_synthesis) for transformations of [alkenes](/wiki/Alkenes) and [alkynes](/wiki/Alkyne).

Many complexes of Zr(II) are derivatives of zirconocene, one example being (C5Me5)2Zr(CO)2.

## History

The zirconium-containing mineral zircon and related minerals ([jargoon](/wiki/Jargoon), [jacinth](/wiki/Jacinth), or hyacinth, [ligure](/wiki/Priestly_breastplate#Third_row)) were mentioned in biblical writings. The mineral was not known to contain a new element until 1789, when [Klaproth](/wiki/Martin_Heinrich_Klaproth) analyzed a jargoon from the island of Ceylon (now [Sri Lanka](/wiki/Sri_Lanka)). He named the new element Zirkonerde (zirconia), related to the [Persian](/wiki/Persian_Language) *[zargun](/wiki/Jargoon)* (zircon; *zar-gun*, "gold-like" or "as gold"). [Humphry Davy](/wiki/Humphry_Davy) attempted to isolate this new element in 1808 through [electrolysis](/wiki/Electrolysis), but failed. Zirconium metal was first obtained in an impure form in 1824 by [Berzelius](/wiki/J%C3%B6ns_Jakob_Berzelius) by heating a mixture of potassium and potassium zirconium fluoride in an iron tube.

The *[crystal bar process](/wiki/Crystal_bar_process)* (also known as the *Iodide Process*), discovered by [Anton Eduard van Arkel](/wiki/Anton_Eduard_van_Arkel) and [Jan Hendrik de Boer](/wiki/Jan_Hendrik_de_Boer) in 1925, was the first industrial process for the commercial production of metallic zirconium. It involves the formation and subsequent [thermal decomposition](/wiki/Thermal_decomposition) of [zirconium tetraiodide](/wiki/Zirconium_tetraiodide) (ZrI4), and was superseded in 1945 by the much cheaper [Kroll process](/wiki/Kroll_process) developed by [William Justin Kroll](/wiki/William_Justin_Kroll), in which zirconium tetrachloride (ZrCl4) is reduced by magnesium:

:   ZrCl4 + 2 Mg → Zr + 2 MgCl2

## Applications

Approximately 900,000 tonnes of zirconium ores were mined in 1995, mostly as zircon.

Most zircon is used directly in high-temperature applications. Because it is refractory, hard, and resistant to chemical attack, zircon finds many applications. Its main use is as an opacifier, conferring a white, opaque appearance to ceramic materials. Because of its chemical resistance, zircon is also used in aggressive environments, such as moulds for molten metals.

[Zirconium dioxide](/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide) (ZrO2) is used in laboratory crucibles, in metallurgical furnaces, and as a refractory material Because it is mechanically strong and flexible, it can be [sintered](/wiki/Sintered) into [ceramic knives](/wiki/Ceramic_knife) and other blades. Zircon (ZrSiO4) and [cubic zirconia](/wiki/Cubic_zirconia) (ZrO2) are cut into gemstones for use in jewelry. Zirconium dioxide is a component in some [abrasives](/wiki/Abrasive), such as grinding wheels and [sandpaper](/wiki/Sandpaper). Zircon is also used in [dating of rocks](/wiki/Detrital_zircon_geochronology) from about the time of the Earth's formation through the measurement of its inherent [radioisotopes](/wiki/Radionuclide), most often [uranium](/wiki/Uranium) and [lead](/wiki/Lead).

Further information: [Zirconium alloys](/wiki/Zirconium_alloys)

A small fraction of the zircon is converted to the metal, which finds various niche applications. Because of zirconium's excellent resistance to corrosion, it is often used as an alloying agent in materials that are exposed to aggressive environments, such as surgical appliances, light filaments, and watch cases. The high reactivity of zirconium with oxygen at high temperatures is exploited in some specialised applications such as explosive primers and as [getters](/wiki/Getter) in [vacuum tubes](/wiki/Vacuum_tube). Zirconium powder is used as a degassing agent in electron tubes, while zirconium wire and sheets are utilized for grid and [anode](/wiki/Anode) supports. Burning zirconium was used as a light source in some [photographic flashbulbs](/wiki/Flash_(photography)#Flashbulbs). Zirconium powder with a [mesh size](/wiki/Mesh_(scale)) from 10 to 80 is occasionally used in pyrotechnic compositions to generate [sparks](/wiki/Spark_(fire)). The high reactivity of zirconium leads to bright white sparks.

### Nuclear applications

Cladding for nuclear reactor fuels consumes about 1% of the zirconium supply, mainly in the form of [zircaloys](/wiki/Zircaloy). The desired properties of these alloys are a low neutron-capture [cross-section](/wiki/Neutron_cross-section) and resistance to corrosion under normal service conditions. Efficient methods for removing the hafnium impurities were developed to serve this purpose.

One disadvantage of zirconium alloys is the reactivity with water, producing [hydrogen](/wiki/Hydrogen), leading to degradation of the [fuel rod cladding](/wiki/Nuclear_fuel#Common_physical_forms_of_nuclear_fuel):

:   Zr + 2 H2O → ZrO2 + 2 H2

Hydrolysis is very slow below 100 °C, but rapid at temperature above 900 °C. Most metals undergo similar reactions. The redox reaction is relevant to the instability of [fuel assemblies](/wiki/Nuclear_fuel) at high temperatures. This reaction occurred in the reactors 1, 2 and 3 of the [Fukushima I Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Fukushima_I_Nuclear_Power_Plant) (Japan) after the reactor cooling was interrupted by the [earthquake and tsunami](/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami) disaster of March 11, 2011, leading to the [Fukushima I nuclear accidents](/wiki/Fukushima_I_nuclear_accidents). After venting the hydrogen in the maintenance hall of those three reactors, the mixture of hydrogen with atmospheric [oxygen](/wiki/Oxygen) exploded, severely damaging the installations and at least one of the containment buildings.

Zirconium is a constituent of [uranium zirconium hydrides](/wiki/Uranium_zirconium_hydride), nuclear fuels used in [research reactors](/wiki/Research_reactor).

### Space and aeronautic industries

Materials fabricated from zirconium metal and ZrO2 are used in space vehicles where resistance to heat is needed.

High temperature parts such as combustors, blades, and vanes in [jet engines](/wiki/Jet_engine) and stationary [gas turbines](/wiki/Gas_turbine) are increasingly being protected by thin [ceramic](/wiki/Ceramic) layers and/or paintable coatings, usually composed of a mixture of zirconia and [yttria](/wiki/Yttria).

Zirconium is also used as a material of first choice for [hydrogen peroxide](/wiki/Hydrogen_peroxide) (H2O2) tanks, propellant lines, valves, and thrusters, in [propulsion space systems](/wiki/Spacecraft_propulsion) such as these equipping the [Sierra Space](/wiki/Sierra_Space)'s [Dream Chaser](/wiki/Dream_Chaser) [spaceplane](/wiki/Spaceplane) where the [thrust](/wiki/Thrust) is provided by the [combustion](/wiki/Combustion) of [kerosene](/wiki/Kerosene) and hydrogen peroxide, a powerful, but unstable, [oxidizer](/wiki/Oxidizer). The reason is that zirconium has an excellent [corrosion resistance](/wiki/Corrosion#Resistance_to_corrosion) to H2O2 and, above all, does not [catalyse](/wiki/Catalysis) its spontaneous self-decomposition as the [ions](/wiki/Ion) of many [transition metals](/wiki/Transition_metal) do.

### Medical uses

Zirconium-bearing compounds are used in many biomedical applications, including dental implants and [crowns](/wiki/Crown_(dentistry)), knee and hip replacements, middle-ear [ossicular](/wiki/Ossicles) chain reconstruction, and other restorative and [prosthetic](/wiki/Prosthesis) devices.

Zirconium binds [urea](/wiki/Urea), a property that has been utilized extensively to the benefit of patients with [chronic kidney disease](/wiki/Chronic_kidney_disease). For example, zirconium is a primary component of the [sorbent](/wiki/Sorbent) column dependent dialysate regeneration and recirculation system known as the REDY system, which was first introduced in 1973. More than 2,000,000 [dialysis](/wiki/Kidney_dialysis) treatments have been performed using the sorbent column in the REDY system. Although the REDY system was superseded in the 1990s by less expensive alternatives, new sorbent-based dialysis systems are being evaluated and approved by the U.S. [Food and Drug Administration](/wiki/Food_and_Drug_Administration) (FDA). Renal Solutions developed the DIALISORB technology, a portable, low water dialysis system. Also, developmental versions of a Wearable Artificial Kidney have incorporated sorbent-based technologies.

[Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate](/wiki/Sodium_zirconium_cyclosilicate) is used by mouth in the treatment of [hyperkalemia](/wiki/Hyperkalemia). It is a selective sorbent designed to trap [potassium](/wiki/Potassium) ions in preference to other [ions](/wiki/Ions) throughout the gastrointestinal tract.

Mixtures of monomeric and polymeric Zr4+ and Al3+ complexes with [hydroxide](/wiki/Hydroxide), [chloride](/wiki/Chloride) and [glycine](/wiki/Glycine), called [aluminium zirconium glycine](/wiki/Aluminium_zirconium_tetrachlorohydrex_gly) salts, are used in a preparation as an [antiperspirant](/wiki/Antiperspirant) in many [deodorant](/wiki/Deodorant) products. It has been used since the early 1960s, as it was determined more efficacious as an antiperspirant than contemporary active ingredients such as [aluminium chlorohydrate](/wiki/Aluminium_chlorohydrate).

### Defunct applications

Zirconium carbonate (3ZrO2·CO2·H2O) was used in lotions to treat [poison ivy](/wiki/Poison_ivy) but was discontinued because it occasionally caused skin reactions.

## Safety

Although zirconium has no known biological role, the human body contains, on average, 250 milligrams of zirconium, and daily intake is approximately 4.15 milligrams (3.5 milligrams from food and 0.65 milligrams from water), depending on dietary habits. Zirconium is widely distributed in nature and is found in all biological systems, for example: 2.86 μg/g in whole wheat, 3.09 μg/g in brown rice, 0.55 μg/g in [spinach](/wiki/Spinach), 1.23 μg/g in eggs, and 0.86 μg/g in ground beef. Further, zirconium is commonly used in commercial products (e.g. [deodorant](/wiki/Deodorant) sticks, aerosol [antiperspirants](/wiki/Antiperspirants)) and also in water purification (e.g. control of [phosphorus](/wiki/Phosphorus) pollution, bacteria- and pyrogen-contaminated water).

Short-term exposure to zirconium powder can cause irritation, but only contact with the eyes requires medical attention. Persistent exposure to [zirconium tetrachloride](/wiki/Zirconium_tetrachloride) results in increased mortality in rats and guinea pigs and a decrease of blood [hemoglobin](/wiki/Hemoglobin) and [red blood cells](/wiki/Red_blood_cell) in dogs. However, in a study of 20 rats given a standard diet containing ~4% zirconium oxide, there were no adverse effects on growth rate, blood and urine parameters, or mortality. The U.S. [Occupational Safety and Health Administration](/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration) (OSHA) legal limit ([permissible exposure limit](/wiki/Permissible_exposure_limit)) for zirconium exposure is 5 mg/m3 over an 8-hour workday. The [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health) (NIOSH) [recommended exposure limit](/wiki/Recommended_exposure_limit) (REL) is 5 mg/m3 over an 8-hour workday and a short term limit of 10 mg/m3. At levels of 25 mg/m3, zirconium is [immediately dangerous to life and health](/wiki/IDLH). However, zirconium is not considered an industrial health hazard. Furthermore, reports of zirconium-related adverse reactions are rare and, in general, rigorous cause-and-effect relationships have not been established. No evidence has been validated that zirconium is carcinogenic or genotoxic.

Among the numerous radioactive isotopes of zirconium, 93Zr is among the most common. It is released as a [product of nuclear fission](/wiki/Fission_products_(by_element)#Zirconium-90_to_96) of 235U and 239Pu, mainly in nuclear power plants and during nuclear weapons tests in the 1950s and 1960s. It has a very long half-life (1.53 million years), its decay emits only low energy radiations, and it is not considered particularly hazardous.
