**Richard Powers** (born June 18, 1957) is an American [novelist](/wiki/Novelist) whose works explore the effects of modern science and technology. His novel *[The Echo Maker](/wiki/The_Echo_Maker)* won the 2006 [National Book Award for Fiction](/wiki/National_Book_Award_for_Fiction). He has also won many other awards over the course of his career, including a [MacArthur Fellowship](/wiki/MacArthur_Fellowship). As of 2024, Powers has published fourteen novels and has taught at the [University of Illinois](/wiki/University_of_Illinois_System) and [Stanford University](/wiki/Stanford_University). He won the 2019 [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction) for *[The Overstory](/wiki/The_Overstory)*.

## Life and work

### Early life

One of five children, Powers was born in [Evanston](/wiki/Evanston,_Illinois), Illinois, the son of Richard Franklin Powers and his wife Donna Powers (née Belik). His family later moved a few miles west to [Lincolnwood](/wiki/Lincolnwood,_Illinois), where his father was a local school [principal](/wiki/Principal_(school)). When Powers was 11, they moved to [Bangkok](/wiki/Bangkok), [Thailand](/wiki/Thailand), where his father had accepted a position at [International School Bangkok](/wiki/International_School_Bangkok). Powers attended this through his freshman year, ending in 1972. During that time outside the U.S., he developed skills in vocal music and proficiency in cello, guitar, saxophone, and clarinet. He also became an avid reader, enjoying nonfiction primarily and classics such as the *[Iliad](/wiki/Iliad)* and the *[Odyssey](/wiki/Odyssey)*.

The family returned to the U.S. when Powers was 16. Following graduation in 1975 from [DeKalb High School](/wiki/DeKalb_High_School_(Illinois)) in [DeKalb](/wiki/DeKalb,_Illinois), Illinois, he enrolled at the [University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign](/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana%E2%80%93Champaign) (UIUC) with a [major](/wiki/Academic_major) in [physics](/wiki/Physics), which he switched to [English literature](/wiki/English_literature) during his first semester. He earned a BA in 1978 and an [MA](/wiki/Master_of_Arts) in Literature in 1980.

### Professorships and awards

In 2010 and 2013, Powers was a Stein Visiting Writer at Stanford University, during which time he partly assisted in the lab of biochemist Aaron Straight.

Powers was named a [MacArthur Fellow](/wiki/MacArthur_Fellowship) in 1989. He received a Lannan Literary Award in 1999.

Powers was appointed the Swanlund Professor of English at [UIUC](/wiki/UIUC) in 1996.

On August 22, 2013, Stanford University announced that Powers had been named the Phil and Penny Knight Professor of Creative Writing in the Department of English.

As of 2018, he has retired and is now professor emeritus of English at UIUC.

### Novels

Powers learned [computer programming](/wiki/Computer_programming) at Illinois as a user of [PLATO](/wiki/PLATO_(computer_system)) and moved to Boston to work as a programmer. One Saturday in 1980, Powers saw the 1914 photograph "[Young Farmers](/wiki/Young_Farmers_(photograph))" by [August Sander](/wiki/August_Sander) at the [Museum of Fine Arts, Boston](/wiki/Museum_of_Fine_Arts,_Boston). He was so inspired that he quit his job two days later to write a novel about the people in the photograph.

Powers worked for two years on his debut novel, *[Three Farmers on Their Way to a Dance](/wiki/Three_Farmers_on_Their_Way_to_a_Dance)*, which was published by [William Morrow](/wiki/William_Morrow_and_Company) in 1985. It comprises three alternating threads: a novella featuring the three young men in the photo during [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I), a technology magazine editor who is obsessed with the photo, and the author's critical and historical musings about the mechanics of photography and the life of [Henry Ford](/wiki/Henry_Ford). It was a [National Book Critics Circle Award](/wiki/National_Book_Critics_Circle_Award) finalist, and received the Rosenthal Award from the [American Academy and Institute of Arts and Letters](/wiki/American_Academy_and_Institute_of_Arts_and_Letters). It also received a Special Citation from the [PEN/Hemingway Awards](/wiki/PEN/Hemingway_Award_for_Debut_Novel).

Powers moved to the Netherlands, where he wrote *[Prisoner's Dilemma](/wiki/Prisoner%27s_Dilemma_(novel))* about [The Walt Disney Company](/wiki/The_Walt_Disney_Company) and nuclear warfare.

He followed with *[The Gold Bug Variations](/wiki/The_Gold_Bug_Variations)* about genetics, music, and computer science. It was a National Book Critics Circle Award finalist.

In 1993, Powers wrote *[Operation Wandering Soul](/wiki/Operation_Wandering_Soul_(novel))* about, among other things, a genetic condition that causes accelerated aging, and an agonized young surgical trainee. It was a finalist for the [National Book Award](/wiki/National_Book_Award).

In 1995, Powers published the [Pygmalion](/wiki/Pygmalion_(mythology)) story *[Galatea 2.2](/wiki/Galatea_2.2)* about an [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) experiment gone awry. It was a National Book Critics Circle Award finalist.

In 1998, Powers wrote *[Gain](/wiki/Gain_(novel))* about a 150-year-old chemical company and a woman who lives near one of its plants and succumbs to [ovarian cancer](/wiki/Ovarian_cancer). It won the [James Fenimore Cooper Prize for Best Historical Fiction](/wiki/James_Fenimore_Cooper_Prize_for_Best_Historical_Fiction) in 1999.

His *[Plowing the Dark](/wiki/Plowing_the_Dark)* (2000) tells of a [Seattle](/wiki/Seattle,_Washington) research team building a groundbreaking [virtual reality](/wiki/Virtual_reality) while an American teacher is held hostage in [Beirut](/wiki/Beirut). It received [Harold D. Vursell Memorial Award](/wiki/Harold_D._Vursell_Memorial_Award) from the American Academy and Institute of Arts and Letters.

Powers wrote *[The Time of Our Singing](/wiki/The_Time_of_Our_Singing)* in 2003. It is about the musician children of an interracial couple who met at [Marian Anderson](/wiki/Marian_Anderson)'s famed 1939 concert on the [Lincoln Memorial](/wiki/Lincoln_Memorial) steps.

Powers's ninth novel, *[The Echo Maker](/wiki/The_Echo_Maker)* (2006), is about a Nebraska man who suffers head trauma in a truck accident and believes his caregiver sister is an impostor. It won a National Book Award and was a finalist for the [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction).

Powers's tenth novel, *[Generosity: An Enhancement](/wiki/Generosity:_An_Enhancement)* (2009) has writing professor Russell Stone encountering his former student, Thassa, an Algerian woman whose constant happiness is exploited by journalists and scientists.

In 2014, Powers wrote *[Orfeo](/wiki/Orfeo_(novel))*, about Peter Els, a retired music composition instructor and avant-garde composer who is mistaken for a bio-terrorist after being discovered with a makeshift genetics lab in his house.

*[The Overstory](/wiki/The_Overstory)*, published in April 2018, is about nine Americans whose unique life experiences with trees bring them together to address the destruction of forests. It won the 2019 [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction), was shortlisted for the [Booker Prize](/wiki/Booker_Prize) and the $75,000 [2019 PEN/Jean Stein Book Award](/wiki/PEN/Jean_Stein_Book_Award), and was runner-up for the Dayton Literary Peace Prize.

*[Bewilderment](/wiki/Bewilderment)*, published in September 2021, was shortlisted for the 2021 [Booker Prize](/wiki/Booker_Prize) and longlisted for the National Book Award and [Andrew Carnegie Medal for Excellence in Fiction](/wiki/Andrew_Carnegie_Medals_for_Excellence_in_Fiction_and_Nonfiction). It is described as "an astrobiologist thinks of a creative way to help his rare and troubled son in Richard Powers’ deeply moving and brilliantly original novel."

*[Playground](/wiki/Playground_(novel))* (2024), the 14th novel by Powers, was longlisted for the [2024 Booker Prize](/wiki/2024_Booker_Prize).
