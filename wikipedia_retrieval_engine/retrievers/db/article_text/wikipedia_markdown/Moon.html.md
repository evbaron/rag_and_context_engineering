This article is about Earth's natural satellite. For moons in general, see [Natural satellite](/wiki/Natural_satellite). For other uses, see [Moon (disambiguation)](/wiki/Moon_(disambiguation)).

The **Moon** is the only [natural satellite](/wiki/Natural_satellite) of [Earth](/wiki/Earth). It [orbits](/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon) around Earth at an average [distance of](/wiki/Lunar_distance) 384,399 kilometres (238,854 mi), a distance roughly 30 times the [width of Earth](/wiki/Earth_diameter). It [completes an orbit](/wiki/Orbital_period) ([lunar month](/wiki/Lunar_month)) in relation to Earth and the [Sun](/wiki/Sun) ([synodically](/wiki/Lunation)) every 29.5 days. The Moon and Earth are bound by [gravitational](/wiki/Gravitational) attraction, which is stronger on the sides facing each other. The resulting [tidal forces](/wiki/Tidal_force) are the main driver of Earth's [tides](/wiki/Tide), and have pulled the Moon to always face Earth with the same [near side](/wiki/Near_side_of_the_Moon). This [tidal locking](/wiki/Tidal_locking) effectively synchronizes the Moon's [rotation period](/wiki/Rotation_period_(astronomy)) ([lunar day](/wiki/Lunar_day)) to its orbital period (lunar month).

In [geophysical terms](/wiki/Geophysical_definition_of_planet), the Moon is a [planetary-mass object](/wiki/Planetary-mass_object) or [satellite planet](/wiki/Satellite_planet). Its mass is 1.2% that of the Earth, and its diameter is 3,474 km (2,159 mi), roughly one-quarter of Earth's (about as wide as the [contiguous United States](/wiki/Contiguous_United_States)). Within the [Solar System](/wiki/Solar_System), it is [larger and more massive](/wiki/List_of_Solar_System_objects_by_size) than any known [dwarf planet](/wiki/Dwarf_planet), and the fifth-largest and fifth–most massive moon, as well as the largest and most massive in relation to its [parent planet](/wiki/Primary_body). Its [surface gravity](/wiki/Surface_gravity) is about one-sixth of Earth's, about half that of [Mars](/wiki/Mars), and the second-highest among all moons in the Solar System after [Jupiter](/wiki/Jupiter)'s moon [Io](/wiki/Io_(moon)). The body of the Moon is [differentiated](/wiki/Planetary_differentiation) and [terrestrial](/wiki/Terrestrial_planet), with only a minuscule [hydrosphere](/wiki/Hydrosphere), [atmosphere](/wiki/Atmosphere_of_the_Moon), and [magnetic field](/wiki/Magnetic_field_of_the_Moon). The [lunar surface](/wiki/Lunar_surface) is covered in [regolith dust](/wiki/Lunar_regolith), which mainly consists of the fine [material ejected](/wiki/Ejecta_blanket) from the [lunar crust](/wiki/Lunar_crust) by [impact events](/wiki/Impact_event). The lunar crust is marked by [impact craters](/wiki/Impact_crater); some younger ones feature bright [ray-like streaks](/wiki/Ray_system). The Moon was volcanically active until 1.2 billion years ago, surfacing lava mostly on the thinner near side of the Moon, filling ancient craters, which through cooling formed the today prominently visible dark plains of [basalt](/wiki/Basalt) called [maria](/wiki/Lunar_mare) ('seas'). The [origin of the Moon](/wiki/Origin_of_the_Moon) is not clear, although it is thought to have been formed out of material from Earth that was ejected by [a giant impact](/wiki/Giant-impact_hypothesis) of a [Mars-sized body](/wiki/Theia_(planet)) 4.51 billion years ago, not long after [Earth's formation](/wiki/Age_of_Earth).

From a distance, the day and night phases of the lunar day are visible as the [lunar phases](/wiki/Lunar_phases), and when the Moon passes through [Earth's shadow](/wiki/Earth%27s_shadow) a [lunar eclipse](/wiki/Lunar_eclipse) is observable. The Moon's [apparent size](/wiki/Apparent_size) in Earth's sky is about the same as that of the Sun, which causes it to cover the Sun completely during a total [solar eclipse](/wiki/Solar_eclipse). The Moon is the [brightest celestial object](/wiki/List_of_brightest_natural_objects_in_the_sky) in Earth's [night sky](/wiki/Night_sky) because of its large apparent size, while the [reflectance](/wiki/Reflectance) (albedo) of its surface is comparable to that of [asphalt](/wiki/Asphalt_concrete). About 59% of the surface of the Moon is visible from Earth owing to the different angles at which the Moon can appear in Earth's sky ([libration](/wiki/Libration)), making parts of the [far side of the Moon](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon) visible.

The Moon has been an important source of inspiration and knowledge in human history, having been crucial to [cosmography](/wiki/Cosmography), [mythology](/wiki/Mythology), [religion](/wiki/Lunar_deity), art, [time keeping](/wiki/Lunar_calendar), [natural science](/wiki/Natural_science) and [spaceflight](/wiki/Spaceflight). The [first spaceflights](/wiki/Timeline_of_Solar_System_exploration) to an extraterrestrial body were to the Moon, starting in 1959 with the [flyby](/wiki/Flyby_(spaceflight)) of *[Luna 1](/wiki/Luna_1)* (sent by the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union)), and the [intentional impact](/wiki/Impactor_(spacecraft)) of *[Luna 2](/wiki/Luna_2)*, followed in 1966 by the first [soft landing](/wiki/Soft_landing) (by *[Luna 9](/wiki/Luna_9)*) and [orbital insertion](/wiki/Orbital_insertion) (by *[Luna 10](/wiki/Luna_10)*). Humans first arrived in orbit with [Apollo 8](/wiki/Apollo_8) (sent by the [United States](/wiki/United_States)) on December 24, 1968, and then on the surface with [Apollo 11](/wiki/Apollo_11) on July 20, 1969, making the Moon the only celestial body beyond Earth that humans have visited. By 1972, six Apollo missions had landed [twelve people](/wiki/List_of_people_who_have_flown_to_the_Moon#People_who_walked_on_the_Moon) on the Moon and stayed up to three days. Renewed robotic [exploration of the Moon](/wiki/Exploration_of_the_Moon), in particular to confirm the presence of [water on the Moon](/wiki/Lunar_water), has fueled plans to return humans to the Moon, starting with the [Artemis program](/wiki/Artemis_program) scheduled for the late 2020s.

## Names and etymology

The English proper name for Earth's natural satellite is typically written as *Moon*, with a capital *M*. The noun *moon* is derived from [Old English](/wiki/Old_English) *mōna*, which stems from [Proto-Germanic](/wiki/Proto-Germanic) \**mēnōn*, which in turn comes from [Proto-Indo-European](/wiki/Proto-Indo-European) \**mēnsis* ('month') – from earlier \**mēnōt* (genitive \**mēneses*), which may be related to a verb meaning 'to measure [time]'.

The Latin name for the Moon is *lūna*. The English adjective *lunar* was ultimately borrowed from Latin, likely through French. In scientific writing and science fiction, the Moon is sometimes referred to as *Luna* [/ˈluːnə/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) to distinguish it from other moons. In poetry, *Luna* may also refer to the [personification](/wiki/Personification) of the Moon as a woman.

The Ancient Greek word *selḗnē* referred to the Moon as a celestial body, and also to the moon goddess [Selene](/wiki/Selene) [/səˈliːniː/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English). The rare English adjective *selenian* [/səliːniən/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) is used to describe the Moon as a world, as opposed to a celestial object. Its cognate *selenic*, originally a rare synonym, now almost always refers to the chemical element [selenium](/wiki/Selenium). The corresponding prefix *seleno-* appears in terms including *[selenography](/wiki/Selenography)* (the study of the lunar surface).

[Artemis](/wiki/Artemis), the Greek goddess of the wilderness and the hunt, also came to be identified with Selene, and was sometimes called [Cynthia](/wiki/Cynthia) after her birthplace on [Mount Cynthus](/wiki/Mount_Cynthus). Her Roman equivalent is [Diana](/wiki/Diana_(mythology)).

The [astronomical symbols](/wiki/Astronomical_symbols) for the Moon are the crescent  and decrescent , for example in *M*☾ 'lunar mass'.

### Classification

The International Astronomical Union (IAU) calls Earth's satellite "the Moon", with a capital "M". Other natural satellites of planets are called 'moons', with a lower-case "m".
The Moon has historically been identified as a [classical planet](/wiki/Classical_planet), in the original sense of a wandering object in the sky. When Galileo discovered satellites orbiting Jupiter, he named them 'moons' because, like the Moon, they can be considered planets orbiting another planet. Some experts hold to a [geophysical definition](/wiki/Geophysical_definition_of_planet) that classifies moons as planets that orbit the Sun while incidentally orbiting another planet. Some have suggested that the Earth and Moon form a [double planet](/wiki/Double_planet) system, but most scientists agree that this would require the orbital [barycenter](/wiki/Barycenter) to lie outside of Earth, which it does not.

## Natural history

### Formation

Main articles: [Origin of the Moon](/wiki/Origin_of_the_Moon) and [Giant-impact hypothesis](/wiki/Giant-impact_hypothesis)

This not-to-scale animation shows (in an Earth and Sun stationary rotating frame of reference) the formation of the Moon through the collision of the hypothetical Mars-sized planet named [Theia](/wiki/Theia_(hypothetical_planet)) with Earth. After orbiting for tens of millions of years at the L4 [Lagrange point](/wiki/Lagrange_point) as an Earth-[trojan](/wiki/Trojan_(astronomy)), Theia's orbit comes in "loops" finally too close to Earth.

[Isotope dating](/wiki/Isotope_dating) of lunar samples suggests the Moon formed around 50 million years after the [origin of the Solar System](/wiki/Origin_of_the_Solar_System). Historically, several formation mechanisms have been proposed, but none satisfactorily explains the features of the Earth–Moon system. A fission of the Moon from Earth's crust through [centrifugal force](/wiki/Centrifugal_force) would require too great an initial rotation rate of Earth. Gravitational capture of a pre-formed Moon depends on an unfeasibly extended [atmosphere of Earth](/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) to [dissipate](/wiki/Dissipate) the energy of the passing Moon. A co-formation of Earth and the Moon together in the [primordial](/wiki/Circumplanetary_disk) [accretion disk](/wiki/Accretion_disk) does not explain the depletion of metals in the Moon. None of these hypotheses can account for the high [angular momentum](/wiki/Angular_momentum) of the Earth–Moon system.

The prevailing theory is that the Earth–Moon system formed after a [giant impact](/wiki/Giant_impact) of a [Mars](/wiki/Mars)-sized body (named *[Theia](/wiki/Theia_(planet))*) with the [proto-Earth](/wiki/Proto-Earth). The oblique impact blasted material into orbit about the Earth and the material accreted and formed the Moon just beyond the Earth's [Roche limit](/wiki/Roche_limit) of ~2.56 [*R*🜨](/wiki/Earth_radius).

Giant impacts are thought to have been common in the early Solar System. Computer simulations of giant impacts have produced results that are consistent with the mass of the lunar core and the angular momentum of the Earth–Moon system. These simulations show that most of the Moon derived from the impactor, rather than the proto-Earth. However, models from 2007 and later suggest a larger fraction of the Moon derived from the proto-Earth. Other bodies of the inner Solar System such as Mars and [Vesta](/wiki/4_Vesta) have, according to meteorites from them, very different oxygen and tungsten [isotopic](/wiki/Isotope) compositions compared to Earth. However, Earth and the Moon have nearly identical isotopic compositions. The isotopic equalization of the Earth–Moon system might be explained by the post-impact mixing of the vaporized material that formed the two, although this is debated.

The impact would have released enough energy to liquefy both the ejecta and the Earth's crust, forming a magma ocean. The liquefied ejecta could have then re-accreted into the Earth–Moon system. The newly formed Moon would have had [its own magma ocean](/wiki/Lunar_magma_ocean); its depth is estimated from about 500 km (300 miles) to 1,737 km (1,079 miles).

While the giant-impact theory explains many lines of evidence, some questions are still unresolved, most of which involve the Moon's composition. Models that have the Moon acquiring a significant amount of the proto-Earth are more difficult to reconcile with geochemical data for the isotopes of zirconium, oxygen, silicon, and other elements. A study published in 2022, using high-resolution simulations (up to 108 particles), found that giant impacts can immediately place a satellite with similar mass and iron content to the Moon into orbit far outside Earth's [Roche limit](/wiki/Roche_limit). Even satellites that initially pass within the Roche limit can reliably and predictably survive, by being partially stripped and then torqued onto wider, stable orbits.

On November 1, 2023, scientists reported that, according to computer simulations, remnants of Theia could still be present inside the Earth.

### Natural development

Artist's depiction of the Moon in Earth's sky as it might have appeared around 4 billion years ago, after the [Late Heavy Bombardment](/wiki/Late_Heavy_Bombardment). At that time the Moon orbited the Earth at a third of its current distance, making it appear 2.8 times larger in [apparent size](/wiki/Apparent_size) than it does today.

The Moon was volcanically active for billions of years after its formation. Lava flooding the lunar low lying terrane over time formed today's basalt maria, with their ages marked in this map (yellow to red: youngest; blue to purple: oldest).

The newly formed Moon settled into a much closer Earth orbit than it has today. Each body therefore appeared much larger in the sky of the other, [eclipses](/wiki/Eclipse) were more frequent, and [tidal effects](/wiki/Tidal_effect) were stronger. Due to [tidal acceleration](/wiki/Tidal_acceleration), the Moon's orbit around Earth has become significantly larger, with a longer period.

Following formation, the Moon has cooled and most of [its atmosphere](/wiki/Atmosphere_of_the_Moon) has been stripped. The [lunar surface](/wiki/Surface_of_the_Moon) has since been shaped by large [impact events](/wiki/Impact_event) and many small ones, forming a landscape featuring [craters](/wiki/Lunar_crater) of all ages.

The Moon was [volcanically active](/wiki/Volcanism) until 1.2 billion years ago, which laid down the prominent [lunar maria](/wiki/Lunar_maria). Most of the [mare basalts](/wiki/Lunar_mare) erupted during the [Imbrian period](/wiki/Imbrian), 3.3–3.7 billion years ago, though some are as young as 1.2 billion years and some as old as 4.2 billion years. The distribution of the mare basalts is uneven, with the basalts predominantly appearing on the Moon's near-side hemisphere. The reasons for this are not yet known, although the relative thinness of the crust on the near side of the Moon is hypothesized to be a factor. Causes of the distribution of the [lunar highlands](/wiki/Lunar_highlands) on the [far side](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon) are also not well understood. Topological measurements show the crust on the near side to be thinner than on the far side. One possible explanation for this is that large impacts on the near side may have made it easier for lava to flow onto the surface.

### Lunar geologic timescale

Main article: [Lunar geologic timescale](/wiki/Lunar_geologic_timescale)

Millions of years before present

The lunar geological periods are named after their characteristic features, from most [impact craters](/wiki/Impact_crater) outside the dark [mare](/wiki/Lunar_mare), to the mare and later craters, and finally the young, still bright and therefore readily visible craters with [ray systems](/wiki/Ray_system) like [Copernicus](/wiki/Copernicus_(lunar_crater)) or [Tycho](/wiki/Tycho_(lunar_crater)).

### Future

See also: [Destruction of the Moon](/wiki/Destruction_of_the_Moon)

In five billion years the Moon will have wandered 40% further away from Earth than it is now. However, about two to three billion years after that, the Sun will have become a [red giant](/wiki/Red_giant). Assuming the Sun envelopes the Earth-Moon system, the consequent drag from the Sun's atmosphere may cause the orbital distance between the Earth and the Moon to decay to the point where the Moon comes within the Earth's [Roche limit](/wiki/Roche_limit), leading it to disintegrate.

## Physical characteristics

The Moon is a very slightly scalene [ellipsoid](/wiki/Ellipsoid) due to tidal stretching, with its long axis displaced 30° from facing the Earth, due to gravitational anomalies from impact basins. Its shape is more elongated than current tidal forces can account for. This 'fossil bulge' indicates that the Moon solidified when it orbited at half its current distance to the Earth, and that it is now too cold for its shape to restore [hydrostatic equilibrium](/wiki/Hydrostatic_equilibrium) at its current orbital distance. Today tidal crust deformation is limited to lobate thrust fault scarps formation.

### Size and mass

Further information: [List of natural satellites](/wiki/List_of_natural_satellites)

Size comparison of the main moons of the Solar System with Earth to scale. Nineteen moons are large enough [to be round](/wiki/Hydrostatic_equilibrium), several having [subsurface oceans](/wiki/Subsurface_ocean) and one, [Titan](/wiki/Titan_(moon)), having a considerable atmosphere.

The Moon is the fifth largest (by size and mass) natural satellite of the Solar System. It is categorizable as a [planetary-mass moon](/wiki/Planetary-mass_moon), making it a satellite planet under the [geophysical definitions of the term](/wiki/Geophysical_definition_of_planet). It is smaller than [Mercury](/wiki/Mercury_(planet)) but considerably larger than the largest [dwarf planet](/wiki/Dwarf_planet) of the Solar System, [Pluto](/wiki/Pluto). The Moon is the largest natural satellite in the Solar System relative to its [primary](/wiki/Primary_(astronomy)) planet.

The Moon's diameter is about 3,500 km, more than one-quarter of Earth's, with the face of the Moon comparable to the width of [mainland Australia](/wiki/Mainland_Australia), Europe or the [contiguous United States](/wiki/Contiguous_United_States). The whole surface area of the Moon is about 38 million square kilometers, comparable to that of the whole [Americas](/wiki/Americas), the areas of the lunar hemispheres being comparable to the areas of [North America](/wiki/North_America) and [South America](/wiki/South_America).

The Moon's mass is 1⁄81 of Earth's, being the second densest among the planetary moons, and having the second highest [surface gravity](/wiki/Surface_gravity), after [Io](/wiki/Io_(moon)), at 0.1654 [g](/wiki/G-force) and an escape velocity of 2.38 km/s (8600 km/h; 5300 mph).

### Structure

Main articles: [Internal structure of the Moon](/wiki/Internal_structure_of_the_Moon) and [Geology of the Moon](/wiki/Geology_of_the_Moon)

Moon's internal structure: solid inner core (iron-metallic), molten outer core, hardened mantle and crust. The crust on the Moon's near side permanently facing Earth is thinner, featuring larger areas flooded by material of the once molten mantle forming today's [lunar mare](/wiki/Lunar_mare).

The Moon is a [differentiated](/wiki/Planetary_differentiation) body that was initially in [hydrostatic equilibrium](/wiki/Hydrostatic_equilibrium) but has since departed from this condition. It has a [geochemically](/wiki/Geochemically) distinct [crust](/wiki/Crust_(geology)), [mantle](/wiki/Mantle_(geology)), and [core](/wiki/Planetary_core). The Moon has a solid iron-rich inner core with a radius possibly as small as 240 kilometres (150 mi) and a fluid outer core primarily made of liquid iron with a radius of roughly 300 kilometres (190 mi). Around the core is a partially molten boundary layer with a radius of about 500 kilometres (310 mi). This structure is thought to have developed through the [fractional crystallization](/wiki/Fractional_crystallization_(geology)) of a global magma ocean shortly after the Moon's formation 4.5 billion years ago.

Crystallization of this magma ocean would have created a [mafic](/wiki/Mafic) mantle from the [precipitation](/wiki/Precipitation_(chemistry)) and sinking of the minerals [olivine](/wiki/Olivine), [clinopyroxene](/wiki/Clinopyroxene), and [orthopyroxene](/wiki/Orthopyroxene); after about three-quarters of the magma ocean had crystallized, lower-density [plagioclase](/wiki/Plagioclase) minerals could form and float into a crust atop. The final liquids to crystallize would have been initially sandwiched between the crust and mantle, with a high abundance of [incompatible](/wiki/Compatibility_(geochemistry)) and heat-producing elements. Consistent with this perspective, geochemical mapping made from orbit suggests a crust of mostly [anorthosite](/wiki/Anorthosite). The [Moon rock](/wiki/Moon_rock) samples of the flood lavas that erupted onto the surface from partial melting in the mantle confirm the mafic mantle composition, which is more iron-rich than that of Earth. The crust is on average about 50 kilometres (31 mi) thick.

The Moon is the second-densest satellite in the Solar System, after [Io](/wiki/Io_(moon)). However, the inner core of the Moon is small, with a radius of about 350 kilometres (220 mi) or less, around 20% of the radius of the Moon. Its composition is not well understood but is probably metallic iron alloyed with a small amount of sulfur and nickel. Analyses of the Moon's time-variable rotation suggest that it is at least partly molten. The pressure at the lunar core is estimated to be 5 GPa (49,000 atm).

### Gravitational field

See also: [Lunar orbit](/wiki/Lunar_orbit)

Astronaut [John Young](/wiki/John_Young_(astronaut)) jumping on the Moon, illustrating that the [gravitational pull](/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration) of the Moon is approximately 1/6 of Earth's. The jumping height is limited by the EVA space suit's weight on the Moon of about 13.6 kg (30 lb) and by the suit's pressurization resisting the bending of the suit, as needed for jumping.

On average the Moon's [surface gravity](/wiki/Surface_gravity) is 1.62 [m/s2](/wiki/Metre_per_second_squared) (0.1654 [g](/wiki/G-force); 5.318 [ft/s2](/wiki/Foot_per_second_squared)), about half of the surface gravity of [Mars](/wiki/Mars) and about a sixth of Earth's.

The [Moon's gravitational field](/wiki/Gravitation_of_the_Moon) is not uniform. The details of the gravitational field have been measured through tracking the [Doppler shift](/wiki/Doppler_shift) of radio signals emitted by [orbiting spacecraft](/wiki/Lunar_orbiter). The main lunar gravity features are [mascons](/wiki/Mass_concentration_(astronomy)), large positive gravitational anomalies associated with some of the giant impact basins, partly caused by the dense mare basaltic lava flows that fill those basins. The anomalies greatly influence the [orbit of spacecraft about the Moon](/wiki/Lunar_orbit). There are some puzzles: lava flows by themselves cannot explain all of the gravitational signature, and some mascons exist that are not linked to mare volcanism.

The [sphere of influence](/wiki/Sphere_of_influence_(astronomy)), of the Moon's gravity field, in which it dominates over Earth's has a [Hill radius](/wiki/Hill_sphere) of 60,000 km (i.e., extending less than one-sixth the distance of the 378,000 km between the Moon and the Earth), extending to the Earth-Moon [lagrange points](/wiki/Lagrange_points). This space is called [cislunar space](/wiki/Cislunar_space).

### Magnetic field

The Moon has [an external magnetic field](/wiki/Magnetic_field_of_the_Moon) of less than 0.2 [nanoteslas](/wiki/Tesla_(unit)), or less than one hundred thousandth [that of Earth](/wiki/Earth%27s_magnetic_field). The Moon does not have a global [dipolar](/wiki/Dipolar) magnetic field and only has crustal magnetization likely acquired early in its history when a dynamo was still operating. Early in its history, 4 billion years ago, its magnetic field strength was likely close to that of Earth today. This early dynamo field apparently expired by about one billion years ago, after the lunar core had crystallized. Theoretically, some of the remnant magnetization may originate from transient magnetic fields generated during large impacts through the expansion of plasma clouds. These clouds are generated during large impacts in an ambient magnetic field. This is supported by the location of the largest crustal magnetizations situated near the [antipodes](/wiki/Antipodes) of the giant impact basins.

Additionally the Moon moves ~27% of the time, or 5–6 days per lunar month in Earth's [magnetotail](/wiki/Magnetotail), replacing [solar wind](/wiki/Solar_wind) with Earth wind.

### Atmosphere

Main article: [Atmosphere of the Moon](/wiki/Atmosphere_of_the_Moon)

[Surveyor 7](/wiki/Surveyor_7) observes levitating dust, a phenomenon named [Lunar horizon glow](/wiki/Lunar_horizon_glow) can be seen

Observations and modelling of the [Moon's sodium exosphere, tail](/wiki/Sodium_tail_of_the_Moon) and "spot".

The Moon has an [atmosphere](/wiki/Atmosphere) consisting of only an [exosphere](/wiki/Exosphere), which is so tenuous as to be nearly [vacuum](/wiki/Vacuum), with a total mass of less than 10 tonnes (9.8 long tons; 11 short tons). The surface pressure of this small mass is around 3 × 10−15 [atm](/wiki/Atmosphere_(unit)) (0.3 [nPa](/wiki/Nanopascal)); it varies with the lunar day. Its sources include [outgassing](/wiki/Outgassing) and [sputtering](/wiki/Sputtering), a product of the bombardment of lunar soil by solar wind ions. Elements that have been detected include [sodium](/wiki/Sodium) and [potassium](/wiki/Potassium), produced by sputtering (also found in the atmospheres of Mercury and [Io](/wiki/Io_(moon))); [helium-4](/wiki/Helium-4) and [neon](/wiki/Neon) from the solar wind; and [argon-40](/wiki/Argon), [radon-222](/wiki/Radon), and [polonium-210](/wiki/Polonium-210), outgassed after their creation by [radioactive decay](/wiki/Radioactive_decay) within the crust and mantle. The absence of such neutral species (atoms or molecules) as [oxygen](/wiki/Oxygen), [nitrogen](/wiki/Nitrogen), [carbon](/wiki/Carbon), [hydrogen](/wiki/Hydrogen) and [magnesium](/wiki/Magnesium), which are present in the [regolith](/wiki/Regolith), is not understood. Water vapor has been detected by *[Chandrayaan-1](/wiki/Chandrayaan-1)* and found to vary with latitude, with a maximum at ~60–70 degrees; it is possibly generated from the [sublimation](/wiki/Sublimation_(chemistry)) of water ice in the regolith. These gases either return into the regolith because of the Moon's gravity or are lost to space, either through solar radiation pressure or, if they are ionized, by being swept away by the solar wind's magnetic field.

A permanent [Moon dust](/wiki/Moon_dust) cloud exists around the Moon, generated by small particles from comets. 5 tons of comet particles are estimated to strike the Moon's surface every 24 hours, resulting in the ejection of dust particles. The dust stays above the Moon for approximately 10 minutes, taking 5 minutes to rise, and 5 minutes to fall. On average, 120 kilograms of dust are present above the Moon, rising up to 100 kilometers above the surface. Dust counts made by [LADEE](/wiki/LADEE)'s Lunar Dust EXperiment (LDEX) found particle counts peaked during the [Geminid](/wiki/Geminid), [Quadrantid](/wiki/Quadrantid), [Northern Taurid](/wiki/Taurids), and [Omicron Centaurid](/wiki/Omicron_Centaurids) [meteor showers](/wiki/Meteor_shower), when the Earth, and Moon pass through comet debris. The lunar dust cloud is asymmetric, being denser near the boundary between the Moon's dayside and nightside.

Studies of Moon magma samples retrieved by the [Apollo](/wiki/Apollo_program) missions demonstrate that the Moon had once possessed a relatively thick atmosphere for a period of 70 million years between 3 and 4 billion years ago. This atmosphere, sourced from gases ejected from lunar volcanic eruptions, was twice the thickness of that of present-day [Mars](/wiki/Mars). The ancient lunar atmosphere was eventually stripped away by solar winds and dissipated into space.

### Surface conditions

Astronaut [Charles Duke](/wiki/Charles_Duke) working on the Moon with his suit covered in [lunar dust](/wiki/Lunar_dust). Lunar dust is highly abrasive and can cause damage to human lungs and nervous and cardiovascular systems.

[Ionizing radiation](/wiki/Ionizing_radiation) from [cosmic rays](/wiki/Cosmic_ray), their resulting [neutron radiation](/wiki/Neutron_radiation), and the Sun results in an average radiation level of 1.369 [millisieverts](/wiki/Millisievert) per day during lunar [daytime](/wiki/Daytime), which is about 2.6 times more than the level on the [International Space Station](/wiki/International_Space_Station), 5–10 times more than the level during a trans-Atlantic flight, and 200 times more than the level on Earth's surface. For further comparison, radiation levels average about 1.84 millisieverts per day on a [flight to Mars](/wiki/Human_mission_to_Mars) and about 0.64 millisieverts per day on Mars itself, with some locations on Mars possibly having levels as low as 0.342 millisieverts per day.
Solar radiation also [electrically charges](/wiki/Static_electricity) the highly abrasive [lunar dust](/wiki/Lunar_dust) and makes it levitate. This effect contributes to the easy spread of the sticky, lung- and gear-damaging lunar dust.

The Moon's [axial tilt](/wiki/Axial_tilt) with respect to the [ecliptic](/wiki/Ecliptic) is only 1.5427°, much less than the 23.44° of Earth. This small axial tilt means that the Moon's solar illumination varies much less with [season](/wiki/Season) than Earth's, and it also allows for the existence of some [peaks of eternal light](/wiki/Peaks_of_eternal_light) at the [Moon's north pole](/wiki/Lunar_north_pole), at the rim of the crater [Peary](/wiki/Peary_(crater)).

The lunar surface is exposed to temperature differences ranging from 120 °C to −171 °C depending on the [solar irradiance](/wiki/Solar_irradiance).
Because of the lack of atmosphere, temperatures of different areas vary particularly upon whether they are in sunlight or shadow, making topographical details play a decisive role on local [surface temperatures](/wiki/Global_surface_temperature).
Parts of many craters, particularly the bottoms of many polar craters, are permanently shadowed. These [craters of eternal darkness](/wiki/Crater_of_eternal_darkness) have extremely low temperatures. The *Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter* measured the lowest summer temperatures in craters at the southern pole at 35 K (−238 °C; −397 °F) and just 26 K (−247 °C; −413 °F) close to the winter solstice in the north polar crater [Hermite](/wiki/Hermite_(crater)). This is the coldest temperature in the Solar System ever measured by a spacecraft, colder even than the surface of [Pluto](/wiki/Pluto).

Blanketed on top of the Moon's crust is a highly [comminuted](/wiki/Comminution) (broken into ever smaller particles) and [impact gardened](/wiki/Impact_gardening) mostly gray surface layer called [regolith](/wiki/Regolith), formed by impact processes. The finer regolith, the [lunar soil](/wiki/Lunar_soil) of [silicon dioxide](/wiki/Silicon_dioxide) glass, has a texture resembling snow and a scent resembling spent [gunpowder](/wiki/Gunpowder). The regolith of older surfaces is generally thicker than for younger surfaces: it varies in thickness from 10–15 m (33–49 ft) in the highlands and 4–5 m (13–16 ft) in the maria. Beneath the finely comminuted regolith layer is the megaregolith, a layer of highly fractured bedrock many kilometers thick.

These extreme conditions are considered to make it unlikely for spacecraft to harbor bacterial spores at the Moon for longer than just one lunar orbit.

The [topography of the Moon](/wiki/Topography_of_the_Moon) has been measured with [laser altimetry](/wiki/Laser_altimetry) and [stereo image analysis](/wiki/Stereoscopy). Its most extensive [topographic feature](/wiki/List_of_features_on_the_Moon) is the giant far-side [South Pole–Aitken basin](/wiki/South_Pole%E2%80%93Aitken_basin), some 2,240 km (1,390 mi) in diameter, the largest crater on the Moon and the second-largest confirmed impact [crater in the Solar System](/wiki/List_of_largest_craters_in_the_Solar_System). At 13 km (8.1 mi) deep, its floor is the lowest point on the surface of the Moon, reaching −9.178 kilometres (−5.703 mi) at [70°22′05″S 172°24′47″W / 70.368°S 172.413°W / -70.368; -172.413](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Moon&params=70.368_S_172.413_W_globe:moon_type:landmark) in a crater within [Antoniadi crater](/wiki/Antoniadi_(lunar_crater)). The [highest elevations](/wiki/List_of_tallest_mountains_in_the_Solar_System) of the Moon's surface, with the so-called [Selenean summit](/wiki/Selenean_summit) at 10.629 kilometres (6.605 mi) , are located directly to the northeast ([5°26′28″N 158°39′22″W / 5.441°N 158.656°W / 5.441; -158.656](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Moon&params=5.441_N_158.656_W_globe:moon_type:landmark)), which might have been thickened by the oblique formation impact of the South Pole–Aitken basin. Other large impact basins such as [Imbrium](/wiki/Mare_Imbrium), [Serenitatis](/wiki/Mare_Serenitatis), [Crisium](/wiki/Mare_Crisium), [Smythii](/wiki/Mare_Smythii), and [Orientale](/wiki/Mare_Orientale) possess regionally low elevations and elevated rims. The far side of the lunar surface is on average about 1.9 km (1.2 mi) higher than that of the near side.

The discovery of [fault scarp](/wiki/Fault_scarp) cliffs suggest that the Moon has shrunk by about 90 metres (300 ft) within the past billion years. Similar shrinkage features exist on [Mercury](/wiki/Geology_of_Mercury). Mare Frigoris, a basin near the north pole long assumed to be geologically dead, has cracked and shifted. Since the Moon does not have tectonic plates, its tectonic activity is slow, and cracks develop as it loses heat.

Scientists have confirmed the presence of a cave on the Moon near the [Sea of Tranquility](/wiki/Sea_of_Tranquility), not far from the 1969 [Apollo 11](/wiki/Apollo_11) landing site. The cave, identified as an entry point to a collapsed [lava tube](/wiki/Lunar_lava_tube), is roughly 45 meters wide and up to 80 m long. This discovery marks the first confirmed entry point to a lunar cave. The analysis was based on photos taken in 2010 by NASA's [Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter](/wiki/Lunar_Reconnaissance_Orbiter). The cave's stable temperature of around 17 °C could provide a hospitable environment for future astronauts, protecting them from extreme temperatures, solar radiation, and micrometeorites. However, challenges include accessibility and risks of avalanches and cave-ins. This discovery offers potential for future lunar bases or emergency shelters.

#### Volcanic features

Main article: [Volcanism on the Moon](/wiki/Volcanism_on_the_Moon)

Map of the near side of the Moon, with the dark [lunar maria](/wiki/Lunar_mare), the most prominent volcanic features of the Moon, labeled in blue (brown labels are prominent [lunar craters](/wiki/Lunar_craters))

The main features visible from Earth by the naked eye are dark and relatively featureless lunar plains called *[maria](/wiki/Lunar_mare)* (sing. *mare*; from Latin meaning 'seas', as they were once believed to be filled with water) which are vast solidified pools of ancient [basaltic](/wiki/Basalt) lava. Although similar to terrestrial basalts, lunar basalts have more iron and no minerals altered by water. The majority of these lava deposits erupted or flowed into the depressions associated with [impact basins](/wiki/Impact_basins), though the Moon's largest expanse of basalt flooding, [Oceanus Procellarum](/wiki/Oceanus_Procellarum), does not correspond to an obvious impact basin. Different episodes of lava flow in maria can often be recognized by variations in surface albedo and distinct flow margins.

Old [hardened](/wiki/Basalt) lava flows of [Mare Imbrium](/wiki/Mare_Imbrium) forming [wrinkle ridges](/wiki/Wrinkle_ridge)

As the maria formed, cooling and contraction of the basaltic lava created [wrinkle ridges](/wiki/Wrinkle_ridge) in some areas. These low, sinuous ridges can extend for hundreds of kilometers and often outline buried structures within the mare. Another result of maria formation is the creation of concentric depressions along the edges, known as [arcuate rilles](/wiki/Rille). These features occur as the mare basalts sink inward under their own weight, causing the edges to fracture and separate.

In addition to the visible maria, the Moon has mare deposits covered by ejecta from impacts. Called cryptomares, these hidden mares are likely older than the exposed ones. Conversely, mare lava has obscured many impact melt sheets and pools. Impact melts are formed when intense shock pressures from collisions vaporize and melt zones around the impact site. Where still exposed, impact melt can be distinguished from mare lava by its distribution, albedo, and texture.

[Sinuous rilles](/wiki/Sinuous_rilles), found in and around maria, are likely extinct [lava channels](/wiki/Lava_channels) or collapsed [lava tubes](/wiki/Lava_tubes). They typically originate from volcanic [vents](/wiki/Volcanism_on_the_Moon), meandering and sometimes branching as they progress. The largest examples, such as [Schroter's Valley](/wiki/Schroter%27s_Valley) and [Rima Hadley](/wiki/Hadley%E2%80%93Apennine), are significantly longer, wider, and deeper than terrestrial lava channels, sometimes featuring bends and sharp turns that again, are uncommon on Earth.

[Sinuous rilles](/wiki/Sinuous_rilles) features and [lunar lava tube](/wiki/Lunar_lava_tube) cave *Marius Hills pit* as observed under different solar illumination conditions.

Mare volcanism has altered impact craters in various ways, including filling them to varying degrees, and raising and fracturing their floors from uplift of mare material beneath their interiors. Examples of such craters include [Taruntius](/wiki/Taruntius_(crater)) and [Gassendi](/wiki/Gassendi_(crater)). Some craters, such as [Hyginus](/wiki/Hyginus_(crater)), are of wholly volcanic origin, forming as [calderas](/wiki/Caldera) or [collapse pits](/wiki/Pit_crater). Such craters are relatively rare and tend to be smaller (typically a few kilometers wide), shallower, and more irregularly shaped than impact craters. They also lack the upturned rims characteristic of impact craters.

Several [geologic provinces](/wiki/Geologic_province) containing [shield volcanoes](/wiki/Shield_volcano) and volcanic [domes](/wiki/Lunar_dome) are found within the near side maria. There are also some regions of [pyroclastic deposits](/wiki/Pyroclastic_rock), [scoria cones](/wiki/Scoria_cones) and [non-basaltic domes](/wiki/Volcanism_on_the_Moon) made of particularly high viscosity lava.

Almost all maria are on the near side of the Moon, and cover 31% of the surface of the near side compared with 2% of the far side. This is likely due to a [concentration of heat-producing elements](/wiki/KREEP) under the crust on the near side, which would have caused the underlying mantle to heat up, partially melt, rise to the surface and erupt. Most of the Moon's [mare basalts](/wiki/Lunar_mare) erupted during the [Imbrian period](/wiki/Imbrian), 3.3–3.7 billion years ago, though some are as young as 1.2 billion years and as old as 4.2 billion years.

In 2006, a study of [Ina](/wiki/Ina_(crater)), a tiny depression in [Lacus Felicitatis](/wiki/Lacus_Felicitatis), found jagged, relatively dust-free features that, because of the lack of erosion by infalling debris, appeared to be only 2 million years old. [Moonquakes](/wiki/Moonquake) and releases of gas indicate continued lunar activity. Evidence of recent lunar volcanism has been identified at 70 [irregular mare patches](/wiki/Irregular_mare_patch), some less than 50 million years old. This raises the possibility of a much warmer lunar mantle than previously believed, at least on the near side where the deep crust is substantially warmer because of the greater concentration of radioactive elements. Evidence has been found for 2–10 million years old basaltic volcanism within the crater Lowell, inside the Orientale basin. Some combination of an initially hotter mantle and local enrichment of heat-producing elements in the mantle could be responsible for prolonged activities on the far side in the Orientale basin.

The lighter-colored regions of the Moon are called *terrae*, or more commonly *highlands*, because they are higher than most maria. They have been radiometrically dated to having formed 4.4 billion years ago and may represent [plagioclase](/wiki/Plagioclase) [cumulates](/wiki/Cumulates) of the lunar magma ocean. In contrast to Earth, no major lunar mountains are believed to have formed as a result of tectonic events.

The concentration of maria on the near side likely reflects the substantially thicker crust of the highlands of the Far Side, which may have formed in a slow-velocity impact of a second moon of Earth a few tens of millions of years after the Moon's formation. Alternatively, it may be a consequence of asymmetrical [tidal heating](/wiki/Tidal_heating) when the Moon was much closer to the Earth.

#### Impact craters

Further information: [List of craters on the Moon](/wiki/List_of_craters_on_the_Moon)

The heavily cratered surface of the Moon, particularly where no mare formed, is discernable when viewed close up, with crater ridges highlighted at the [terminator](/wiki/Terminator_(solar)) and the two most prominent craters on the near side, [Tycho](/wiki/Tycho_(lunar_crater)) (left) and [Copernicus](/wiki/Copernicus_(lunar_crater)) (top) visible, featuring long bright impact-streaks.

A view of a larger crater, the three-kilometer-deep [Daedalus](/wiki/Daedalus_(crater)) on the [Moon's far side](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon)

[Apollo 17](/wiki/Apollo_17) astronaut [Harrison H. Schmitt](/wiki/Harrison_H._Schmitt) next to the large Moon boulder nicknamed "[Tracy's Rock](/wiki/Tracy%27s_Rock)" in the [Taurus–Littrow](/wiki/Taurus%E2%80%93Littrow) of the [Mare Serenitatis](/wiki/Mare_Serenitatis) on the near side of the Moon

A major geologic process that has affected the Moon's surface is [impact cratering](/wiki/Impact_crater), with craters formed when asteroids and comets collide with the lunar surface. There are estimated to be roughly 300,000 craters wider than 1 km (0.6 mi) on the Moon's near side. Lunar craters exhibit a variety of forms, depending on their size. In order of increasing diameter, the basic types are simple craters with smooth bowl shaped interiors and upturned rims, [complex craters](/wiki/Complex_crater) with flat floors, terraced walls and central peaks, [peak ring](/wiki/Peak_ring) basins, and [multi-ring basins](/wiki/Multi-ring_basin) with two or more concentric rings of peaks. The vast majority of impact craters are circular, but some, like [Cantor](/wiki/Cantor_(crater)) and [Janssen](/wiki/Janssen_(lunar_crater)), have more polygonal outlines, possibly guided by underlying faults and joints. Others, such as the [Messier](/wiki/Messier_(crater)) pair, [Schiller](/wiki/Schiller_(crater)), and [Daniell](/wiki/Daniell_(crater)), are elongated. Such elongation can result from highly oblique impacts, [binary asteroid](/wiki/Binary_asteroid) impacts, fragmentation of impactors before surface strike, or closely spaced [secondary](/wiki/Secondary_crater) impacts.

The [lunar geologic timescale](/wiki/Lunar_geologic_timescale) is based on the most prominent impact events, such as multi-ring formations like [Nectaris](/wiki/Nectarian), [Imbrium](/wiki/Lower_Imbrian), and [Orientale](/wiki/Mare_Orientale) that are between hundreds and thousands of kilometers in diameter and associated with a broad apron of ejecta deposits that form a regional [stratigraphic horizon](/wiki/Stratigraphy). The lack of an atmosphere, weather, and recent geological processes mean that many of these craters are well-preserved. Although only a few [multi-ring basins](/wiki/Multi-ring_basins) have been definitively dated, they are useful for assigning relative ages. Because impact craters accumulate at a nearly constant rate, counting the number of craters per unit area can be used to estimate the age of the surface.
However care needs to be exercised with the [crater counting](/wiki/Crater_counting) technique due to the potential presence of [secondary craters](/wiki/Secondary_crater). Ejecta from impacts can create secondary craters that often appear in clusters or chains but can also occur as isolated formations at a considerable distance from the impact. These can resemble primary craters, and may even dominate small crater populations, so their unidentified presence can distort age estimates.

The radiometric ages of impact-melted rocks collected during the [Apollo missions](/wiki/Apollo_missions) cluster between 3.8 and 4.1 billion years old: this has been used to propose a [Late Heavy Bombardment](/wiki/Late_Heavy_Bombardment) period of increased impacts.

High-resolution images from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter in the 2010s show a contemporary crater-production rate significantly higher than was previously estimated. A secondary cratering process caused by [distal ejecta](/wiki/Distal_ejecta) is thought to churn the top two centimeters of regolith on a timescale of 81,000 years. This rate is 100 times faster than the rate computed from models based solely on direct micrometeorite impacts.

#### Lunar swirls

Main article: [Lunar swirls](/wiki/Lunar_swirls)

Wide-angle image of a lunar swirl, the 70-kilometer-long [Reiner Gamma](/wiki/Reiner_Gamma)

Lunar swirls are enigmatic features found across the Moon's surface. They are characterized by a high albedo, appear optically immature (i.e. have the optical characteristics of a relatively young [regolith](/wiki/Regolith)), and often have a sinuous shape. Their shape is often accentuated by low [albedo](/wiki/Albedo) regions that wind between the bright swirls. They are located in places with enhanced surface [magnetic fields](/wiki/Magnetic_field) and many are located at the [antipodal point](/wiki/Antipodal_point) of major impacts. Well known swirls include the [Reiner Gamma](/wiki/Reiner_Gamma) feature and [Mare Ingenii](/wiki/Mare_Ingenii). They are hypothesized to be areas that have been partially shielded from the [solar wind](/wiki/Solar_wind), resulting in slower [space weathering](/wiki/Space_weathering).

### Presence of water

Main article: [Lunar water](/wiki/Lunar_water)

Distribution of surface ice at the Moon's South (left) and North poles (right), as viewed by NASA's [Moon Mineralogy Mapper](/wiki/Moon_Mineralogy_Mapper) (M3) spectrometer onboard India's [Chandrayaan-1](/wiki/Chandrayaan-1) orbiter

Liquid water cannot persist on the lunar surface. When exposed to solar radiation, water quickly decomposes through a process known as [photodissociation](/wiki/Photodissociation) and is lost to space. However, since the 1960s, scientists have hypothesized that water ice may be deposited by impacting [comets](/wiki/Comets) or possibly produced by the reaction of oxygen-rich lunar rocks, and hydrogen from [solar wind](/wiki/Solar_wind), leaving traces of water which could possibly persist in cold, permanently shadowed craters at either pole on the Moon. Computer simulations suggest that up to 14,000 km2 (5,400 sq mi) of the surface may be in permanent shadow. The presence of usable quantities of water on the Moon is an important factor in rendering [lunar habitation](/wiki/Colonization_of_the_Moon) as a cost-effective plan; the alternative of transporting water from Earth would be prohibitively expensive.

In years since, signatures of water have been found to exist on the lunar surface. In 1994, the [bistatic radar experiment](/wiki/Clementine_mission#Bistatic_Radar_Experiment) located on the *[Clementine](/wiki/Clementine_(spacecraft))* spacecraft, indicated the existence of small, frozen pockets of water close to the surface. However, later radar observations by [Arecibo](/wiki/Arecibo_Telescope), suggest these findings may rather be rocks ejected from young impact craters. In 1998, the [neutron spectrometer](/wiki/Lunar_Prospector#Neutron_Spectrometer_(NS)) on the *Lunar Prospector* spacecraft showed that high concentrations of hydrogen are present in the first meter of depth in the regolith near the polar regions. Volcanic lava beads, brought back to Earth aboard Apollo 15, showed small amounts of water in their interior.

The 2008 *[Chandrayaan-1](/wiki/Chandrayaan-1)* spacecraft has since confirmed the existence of surface water ice, using the on-board [Moon Mineralogy Mapper](/wiki/Moon_Mineralogy_Mapper). The spectrometer observed absorption lines common to [hydroxyl](/wiki/Hydroxyl), in reflected sunlight, providing evidence of large quantities of water ice, on the lunar surface. The spacecraft showed that concentrations may possibly be as high as 1,000 [ppm](/wiki/Parts_per_million). Using the mapper's reflectance spectra, indirect lighting of areas in shadow confirmed water ice within 20° latitude of both poles in 2018. In 2009, *[LCROSS](/wiki/LCROSS)* sent a 2,300 kg (5,100 lb) impactor into a [permanently shadowed](/wiki/Permanently_shadowed_crater) polar crater, and detected at least 100 kg (220 lb) of water in a plume of ejected material. Another examination of the LCROSS data showed the amount of detected water to be closer to 155 ± 12 kg (342 ± 26 lb).

In May 2011, 615–1410 ppm water in [melt inclusions](/wiki/Melt_inclusions) in lunar sample 74220 was reported, the famous high-titanium "orange glass soil" of volcanic origin collected during the [Apollo 17](/wiki/Apollo_17) mission in 1972. The inclusions were formed during explosive eruptions on the Moon approximately 3.7 billion years ago. This concentration is comparable with that of magma in Earth's [upper mantle](/wiki/Upper_mantle). Although of considerable selenological interest, this insight does not mean that water is easily available since the sample originated many kilometers below the surface, and the inclusions are so difficult to access that it took 39 years to find them with a state-of-the-art ion microprobe instrument.

Analysis of the findings of the Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M3) revealed in August 2018 for the first time "definitive evidence" for water-ice on the lunar surface. The data revealed the distinct reflective signatures of water-ice, as opposed to dust and other reflective substances. The ice deposits were found on the North and South poles, although it is more abundant in the South, where water is trapped in permanently shadowed craters and crevices, allowing it to persist as ice on the surface since they are shielded from the sun.

In October 2020, astronomers reported detecting [molecular water](/wiki/Water) on the sunlit surface of the Moon by several independent spacecraft, including the [Stratospheric Observatory for Infrared Astronomy](/wiki/Stratospheric_Observatory_for_Infrared_Astronomy) (SOFIA).

## Earth–Moon system

See also: [Satellite system (astronomy)](/wiki/Satellite_system_(astronomy)) and [Claimed moons of Earth](/wiki/Claimed_moons_of_Earth)

### Orbit

Main articles: [Orbit of the Moon](/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon) and [Lunar theory](/wiki/Lunar_theory)

A [Sun-synchronous](/wiki/Sun-synchronous_orbit) view of the day-side of Earth from the Sun–Earth [Lagrange point](/wiki/Lagrange_point) L1, with Earth rotating and the Moon passing on its orbit in between the observing [DSCOVR satellite](/wiki/DSCOVR_satellite) and Earth, showing [the Moon's far side](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon) when it is illuminated and the Moon's day-side

The Moon's orbit is slightly elliptical, with an [orbital eccentricity](/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity) of 0.055.
The [semi-major axis](/wiki/Semi-major_axis) of the geocentric lunar orbit, called the [lunar distance](/wiki/Lunar_distance), is approximately 385,000 km (239,000 mi), or 1.3 [light-seconds](/wiki/Light-second), comparable to going [around Earth](/wiki/Circumference_of_Earth) approximately 9.6 times.

The Moon makes a complete orbit around Earth with respect to the fixed stars, its [sidereal period](/wiki/Sidereal_period), about once every 27.3 days. However, because the Earth–Moon system moves at the same time in its orbit around the Sun, it takes slightly longer, 29.5 days, to return to the same [lunar phase](/wiki/Lunar_phase), completing a full cycle, as seen from Earth. This [synodic period](/wiki/Synodic_period) or synodic month is commonly known as the [lunar month](/wiki/Lunar_month) and is equal to the length of the [solar day](/wiki/Solar_day) on the Moon.

Due to [tidal locking](/wiki/Tidal_locking), the Moon has a 1:1 [spin–orbit resonance](/wiki/Orbital_resonance). This [rotation](/wiki/Rotation)–[orbit](/wiki/Orbit) ratio makes the Moon's orbital periods around Earth equal to its corresponding [rotation periods](/wiki/Rotation_period). This is the reason for only one side of the Moon, its so-called [near side](/wiki/Near_side), being visible from Earth. That said, while the movement of the Moon is in resonance, it still is not without nuances such as [libration](/wiki/Libration), resulting in slightly changing perspectives, making over time and location on Earth about 59% of the Moon's surface visible from Earth.

Unlike most satellites of other planets, the Moon's orbital plane is closer to the [ecliptic plane](/wiki/Ecliptic_plane) than to the planet's [equatorial plane](/wiki/Equatorial_plane). The Moon's orbit is subtly [perturbed](/wiki/Perturbation_(astronomy)) by the Sun and Earth in many small, complex and interacting ways. For example, the plane of the Moon's orbit [gradually rotates](/wiki/Lunar_standstill) once every 18.61 years, which affects other aspects of lunar motion. These follow-on effects are mathematically described by [Cassini's laws](/wiki/Cassini%27s_laws).

Minimum, mean and maximum distances of the Moon from Earth with its angular diameter as seen from Earth's surface to scale

#### Orbital center

The Earth and the Moon form the Earth–Moon [satellite system](/wiki/Satellite_system_(astronomy)) with a shared center of mass, or [barycenter](/wiki/Barycenter). This barycenter is 5,000 km (3,100 mi) (about a 3/4 of Earth's radius) from the Earth's center. This barycenter is slowly moving outward as tidal friction drains energy from the rotating pair, and eventually the barycenter will be outside the Earth.

In a [heliocentric](/wiki/Heliocentrism#Modern_use_of_geocentric_and_heliocentric) [frame of reference](/wiki/Frame_of_reference) the Moon orbits the Sun, [perturbed](/wiki/Perturbation_(astronomy)) by Earth. This has led some scientists to argue that the Moon could be identified as a planet, both historically and qualitatively, adding that its mass would be enough to clear its orbit around the Sun if it were on its own. This would imply that the Earth-Moon system is a [double planet](/wiki/Double_planet). The [International Astronomical Union](/wiki/International_Astronomical_Union) standards organization has no definition for planets in a [binary system](/wiki/Binary_system), or for what constitutes a double planet system, but most scientists agree that this would require the Moon-Earth barycenter to be outside of Earth.

### Tidal effects

Main articles: [Tidal force](/wiki/Tidal_force), [Tidal acceleration](/wiki/Tidal_acceleration), [Tide](/wiki/Tide), and [Theory of tides](/wiki/Theory_of_tides)

Simplified diagram of Earth bulging, being pulled and stretched toward the Moon by its gravity, which is the main driver of the [tides](/wiki/Tide). The Ocean and Earth are being pulled more where it is closer to the Moon, causing [tidal forces](/wiki/Tidal_force) to be weaker at the far-side of Earth creating a second bulge and high-tide. The animation shows the change of the Moon's position on its inclined orbit.

The gravitational attraction that Earth and the Moon (as well as the Sun) exert on each other manifests in a slightly greater attraction on the sides closest to each other, resulting in [tidal forces](/wiki/Tidal_forces). [Ocean tides](/wiki/Ocean_tides) are the most widely experienced result of this, but tidal forces also considerably affect other mechanics of Earth, as well as the Moon and their system.

The lunar solid crust experiences tides of around 10 cm (4 in) amplitude over 27 days, with three components: a fixed one due to Earth, because they are in [synchronous rotation](/wiki/Synchronous_rotation), a variable tide due to orbital eccentricity and inclination, and a small varying component from the Sun. The Earth-induced variable component arises from changing distance and [libration](/wiki/Libration), a result of the Moon's orbital eccentricity and inclination (if the Moon's orbit were perfectly circular and un-inclined, there would only be solar tides). According to recent research, scientists suggest that the Moon's influence on the Earth may contribute to maintaining [Earth's magnetic field](/wiki/Earth%27s_magnetic_field).

The cumulative effects of stress built up by these tidal forces produces [moonquakes](/wiki/Moonquakes). Moonquakes are much less common and weaker than are earthquakes, although moonquakes can last for up to an hour – significantly longer than terrestrial quakes – because of scattering of the seismic vibrations in the dry fragmented upper crust. The existence of moonquakes was an unexpected discovery from [seismometers](/wiki/Seismometer) placed on the Moon by [Apollo](/wiki/Apollo_program) [astronauts](/wiki/Astronaut) from 1969 through 1972.

The most commonly known effect of tidal forces is elevated sea levels called ocean tides. While the Moon exerts most of the tidal forces, the Sun also exerts tidal forces and therefore contributes to the tides as much as 40% of the Moon's tidal force; producing in interplay the [spring and neap tides](/wiki/Spring_tide).

The tides are two bulges in the Earth's oceans, one on the side facing the Moon and the other on the side opposite. As the Earth rotates on its axis, one of the ocean bulges (high tide) is held in place "under" the Moon, while another such tide is opposite. The tide under the Moon is explained by the Moon's gravity being stronger on the water close to it. The tide on the opposite side can be explained either by the centrifugal force as the Earth orbits the [barycenter](/wiki/Barycenter) or by the water's inertia as the Moon's gravity is stronger on the solid Earth close to it and it is pulled away from the farther water.

Thus, there are two high tides, and two low tides in about 24 hours. Since the Moon is orbiting the Earth in the same direction of the Earth's rotation, the high tides occur about every 12 hours and 25 minutes; the 25 minutes is due to the Moon's time to orbit the Earth.

If the Earth were a water world (one with no continents) it would produce a tide of only one meter, and that tide would be very predictable, but the ocean tides are greatly modified by other effects:

- the frictional coupling of water to Earth's rotation through the ocean floors
- the [inertia](/wiki/Inertia) of water's movement
- ocean basins that grow shallower near land
- the sloshing of water between different ocean basins

As a result, the timing of the tides at most points on the Earth is a product of observations that are explained, incidentally, by theory.

### System evolution

Delays in the tidal peaks of both ocean and solid-body tides cause [torque](/wiki/Torque) in opposition to the Earth's rotation. This "drains" [angular momentum](/wiki/Angular_momentum) and rotational [kinetic energy](/wiki/Kinetic_energy) from Earth's rotation, slowing the Earth's rotation. That angular momentum, lost from the Earth, is transferred to the Moon in a process known as [tidal acceleration](/wiki/Tidal_acceleration), which lifts the Moon into a higher orbit while lowering orbital speed around the Earth. This has resulted in the length of a [anomalistic month](/wiki/Anomalistic_month) having increased from 20 days to today's 27.55 days over the course of 3.2 billion years.

Thus the distance between Earth and Moon is increasing, and the Earth's rotation is slowing in reaction. Measurements from laser reflectors left during the Apollo missions ([lunar ranging experiments](/wiki/Lunar_Laser_Ranging_experiments)) have found that the Moon's distance increases by 38 mm (1.5 in) per year (roughly the rate at which human fingernails grow).
[Atomic clocks](/wiki/Atomic_clock) show that Earth's Day lengthens by about 17 [microseconds](/wiki/Microsecond) every year, slowly increasing the rate at which [UTC](/wiki/UTC) is adjusted by [leap seconds](/wiki/Leap_second).

This tidal drag makes the rotation of the Earth, and the orbital period of the Moon very slowly match. This matching first results in [tidally locking](/wiki/Tidal_locking) the lighter body of the orbital system, as is already the case with the Moon. Theoretically, in 50 billion years, the Earth's rotation will have slowed to the point of matching the Moon's orbital period, causing the Earth to always present the same side to the Moon. However, the Sun will become a [red giant](/wiki/Red_giant#The_Sun_as_a_red_giant), most likely engulfing the Earth–Moon system long before then.

If the Earth–Moon system is not engulfed by the enlarged Sun, the drag from the solar atmosphere can cause the orbit of the Moon to decay. Once the orbit of the Moon closes to a distance of 18,470 km (11,480 mi), it will cross Earth's [Roche limit](/wiki/Roche_limit), meaning that tidal interaction with Earth would break apart the Moon, turning it into a [ring system](/wiki/Ring_system). Most of the orbiting rings will begin to decay, and the debris will impact Earth. Hence, even if the Sun does not swallow up Earth, the planet may be left moonless.

## Orientation and appearance

See also: [Lunar observation](/wiki/Lunar_observation)

[Libration](/wiki/Libration), the slight variation in the Moon's [apparent size](/wiki/Apparent_size) and viewing angle over a single lunar month as viewed from somewhere on the Earth's Northern Hemisphere.

The Moon appears in Earth's sky differently depending on the position of observation on Earth and the time of [lunar year](/wiki/Lunar_year), [lunar month](/wiki/Lunar_month) and day on Earth. During a year the Moon [culminates](/wiki/Culmination) during a day at different [altitudes](/wiki/Altitude_(astronomy)). The Moon appears highest in the sky unlike the Sun during winter and lowest during summer, which is true for the seasons of each of Earth's northern and southern hemispheres.

At the [North](/wiki/North_Pole) and [South Poles](/wiki/South_Pole) the Moon is 24 hours above the horizon for two weeks every [tropical month](/wiki/Tropical_month) (about 27.3 days), comparable to the [polar day](/wiki/Polar_day) of the [tropical year](/wiki/Tropical_year). [Zooplankton](/wiki/Zooplankton) in the [Arctic](/wiki/Arctic) use [moonlight](/wiki/Moonlight) when the Sun is [below the horizon](/wiki/Polar_night) for months on end.

The apparent orientation of the Moon depends on its position in the sky and the hemisphere of the Earth from which it is being viewed. In the [Northern Hemisphere](/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere) it appears upside down compared to the view from the [Southern Hemisphere](/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere). Sometimes the "horns" of a crescent moon appear to be pointing more upwards than sideways. This phenomenon is called a [wet moon](/wiki/Wet_moon) and occurs more frequently in the [tropics](/wiki/Tropics).

The [distance between the Moon and Earth](/wiki/Lunar_distance) varies from around 356,400 km (221,500 mi) ([perigee](/wiki/Perigee)) to 406,700 km (252,700 mi) (apogee), making the Moon's distance and apparent size fluctuate up to 14%. On average the Moon's [angular diameter](/wiki/Angular_diameter) is about 0.52°, roughly the same apparent size as the Sun (see [§ Eclipses](#Eclipses)). In addition, a purely psychological effect, known as the [Moon illusion](/wiki/Moon_illusion), makes the Moon appear larger when close to the horizon.

### Rotation

Comparison between the Moon on the left, rotating tidally locked (correct), and with the Moon on the right, without rotation (incorrect)

The [tidally locked synchronous rotation](/wiki/Synchronous_rotation) of the Moon as it orbits the Earth results in it always keeping nearly the same face turned towards the planet. The side of the Moon that faces Earth is called the [near side](/wiki/Near_side), and the opposite the [far side](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon). The far side is often inaccurately called the "dark side", but it is in fact illuminated as often as the near side: once every 29.5 Earth days. During [dark moon](/wiki/Dark_moon) to [new moon](/wiki/New_moon), the near side is dark.

The Moon originally rotated at a faster rate, but early in its history its rotation slowed and became [tidally locked](/wiki/Tidally_locked) in this orientation as a result of [frictional](/wiki/Friction) effects associated with [tidal](/wiki/Tidal_force) deformations caused by Earth. With time, the energy of rotation of the Moon on its axis was dissipated as heat, until there was no rotation of the Moon relative to Earth. In 2016, planetary scientists using data collected on the 1998–99 NASA *[Lunar Prospector](/wiki/Lunar_Prospector)* mission found two hydrogen-rich areas (most likely former water ice) on opposite sides of the Moon. It is speculated that these patches were the poles of the Moon billions of years ago before it was tidally locked to Earth.

### Illumination and phases

See also: [Lunar phase](/wiki/Lunar_phase), [Moonlight](/wiki/Moonlight), and [Halo (optical phenomenon)](/wiki/Halo_(optical_phenomenon))

The monthly changes in the angle between the direction of sunlight and view from Earth, and the [phases of the Moon](/wiki/Lunar_phase) that result, as viewed from the [Northern Hemisphere](/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere). The [Earth–Moon distance](/wiki/Earth%E2%80%93Moon_distance) is not to scale.

The Moon rotates, as it orbits Earth, changing orientation toward the Sun, experiencing a [lunar day](/wiki/Lunar_day). A lunar day is equal to one [lunar month](/wiki/Lunar_month) (one synodic orbit around Earth) due to it being [tidally locked](/wiki/Tidal_locking) to Earth. Since the Moon is not tidally locked to the Sun, lunar daylight and night times both occur around the Moon. The changing position of the illumination of the Moon by the Sun during a lunar day is observable from Earth as the changing [lunar phases](/wiki/Lunar_phase), waxing crescent being the sunrise and the waning crescent the sunset phase of a day observed from afar.

Lunar night is the darkest on the far side and during lunar eclipses on the near side (and darker than a moonless night on Earth). During its night, the near side is illuminated by [Earthlight](/wiki/Earthlight) to the extent that lunar surface features may be observable from Earth. Earthshine makes the night on the near side about 43 times brighter, and sometimes even 55 times brighter than a night on Earth illuminated by the light of the full moon.

In Earth's sky brightness and apparent size of the Moon changes also due to its elliptic [orbit around Earth](/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon). At [perigee](/wiki/Perigee) (closest), since the Moon is up to 14% closer to Earth than at [apogee](/wiki/Apogee) (most distant), it subtends a [solid angle](/wiki/Solid_angle) which is up to 30% larger. Consequently, given the same phase, the Moon's brightness also varies by up to 30% between apogee and perigee. A full (or new) moon at such a position is called a [supermoon](/wiki/Supermoon).

Each of the four intermediate phases of the Moon lasts, on average, about seven days (approximately 7.38 days), but this duration can vary by about ±11% due to the change in the distance between the Moon and Earth between apogee (farthest point) and perigee (closest point).

The number of days since the most recent new moon is called the Moon's age. A complete cycle of lunar phases is known as a lunation.

The approximate age and phase of the Moon for any date can be calculated by determining the number of days since a known new moon (e.g., January 1, 1900, or August 11, 1999) and dividing that value by the average length of a synodic month (29.53059 days). The remainder of the division represents the Moon's age. This method assumes a perfectly circular orbit and does not consider the exact time of the new moon, so results may be off by a few hours. Accuracy decreases as the date moves away from the reference date.

This simplified calculation is suitable for general or decorative purposes, such as moon phase clocks. More precise applications that take into account the Moon's apogee and perigee require more complex methods.

Due to lunar libration, it is sometimes possible to see slightly more than the full moon (up to about 101%) or a small part of its far side (up to 5%).

#### Observational phenomena

[Lunar impact flash](/wiki/Transient_lunar_phenomenon#Impact_flash) image shown superimposed on the Moon

There has been historical controversy over whether observed features on the Moon's surface change over time. Today, many of these claims are thought to be illusory, resulting from observation under different lighting conditions, poor [astronomical seeing](/wiki/Astronomical_seeing), or inadequate drawings. However, [outgassing](/wiki/Outgassing) does occasionally occur and could be responsible for a minor percentage of the reported [lunar transient phenomena](/wiki/Lunar_transient_phenomena). Recently, it has been suggested that a roughly 3 km (1.9 mi) diameter region of the lunar surface was modified by a gas release event about a million years ago.

### Albedo and true color of the surface

The Moon's appearance is shaped by its reflective properties and surface composition. Its [albedo](/wiki/Albedo) is about 0.12, comparable to worn [asphalt](/wiki/Asphalt_concrete), making it relatively dark despite its brightness in the night sky. This low albedo represents the reflectance of the lunar regolith, a layer of fine rock fragments created by meteorite impacts. Light scattering by the regolith causes the Moon to appear much brighter at full moon than at quarter phases.

The Moon also exhibits retro-reflection, scattering light back toward its source and creating a uniform brightness across its disc without significant limb darkening. Its apparent size and brightness vary due to its elliptical orbit, appearing up to 30% brighter and 14% larger at perigee compared to apogee, a phenomenon known as a "Supermoon".

The changing apparent color of the Moon, filtered by Earth's atmosphere

The Moon's true color, without atmospheric effects, is a muted brownish-gray. This results from silicate minerals in the regolith, with darker basaltic plains (maria) and lighter feldspar-rich highlands. The maria, formed by ancient volcanic flows, contain more iron and titanium, giving them a darker tone.

From Earth, the Moon's color can be altered by the atmosphere, at times appearing red or blue. It may appear red during a [lunar eclipse](/wiki/Lunar_eclipse), because of the red spectrum of the Sun's light being [refracted](/wiki/Refracted) onto the Moon by Earth's atmosphere. Because of this red color, lunar eclipses are also sometimes called [blood moons](/wiki/Blood_Moon_(eclipse)). The Moon can also seem red when it appears at low angles and through a thick atmosphere.

The Moon may appear blue depending on the presence of certain particles in the air, such as volcanic particles, in which case it can be called a [blue moon](/wiki/Blue_moon#Whole_blue_moon).

Because the words "red moon" and "blue moon" can also be used to refer to specific [full moons](/wiki/Full_moon) of the year, they do not always refer to the presence of red or blue [moonlight](/wiki/Moonlight).

### Eclipses

Main articles: [Solar eclipse](/wiki/Solar_eclipse), [Lunar eclipse](/wiki/Lunar_eclipse), [Solar eclipses on the Moon](/wiki/Solar_eclipses_on_the_Moon), and [Eclipse cycle](/wiki/Eclipse_cycle)

A [solar eclipse](/wiki/Solar_eclipse) causes the Sun to be covered, revealing the white [corona](/wiki/Stellar_corona).

The Moon, tinted reddish, during a [lunar eclipse](/wiki/Lunar_eclipse)

Eclipses only occur when the Sun, Earth, and Moon are all in a straight line (termed "[syzygy](/wiki/Syzygy_(astronomy))"). [Solar eclipses](/wiki/Solar_eclipse) occur at [new moon](/wiki/New_moon), when the Moon is between the Sun and Earth. In contrast, [lunar eclipses](/wiki/Lunar_eclipse) occur at full moon, when Earth is between the Sun and Moon. The apparent size of the Moon is roughly the same as that of the Sun, with both being viewed at close to one-half a degree wide. The Sun is much larger than the Moon, but it is the vastly greater distance that gives it the same apparent size as the much closer and much smaller Moon from the perspective of Earth. The variations in apparent size, due to the non-circular orbits, are nearly the same as well, though occurring in different cycles. This makes possible both [total](/wiki/Total_eclipse) (with the Moon appearing larger than the Sun) and [annular](/wiki/Annular_eclipse) (with the Moon appearing smaller than the Sun) solar eclipses. In a total eclipse, the Moon completely covers the disc of the Sun and the [solar corona](/wiki/Solar_corona) becomes visible to the [naked eye](/wiki/Naked_eye).

Because the distance between the Moon and Earth is very slowly increasing over time, the angular diameter of the Moon is decreasing. As it evolves toward becoming a [red giant](/wiki/Red_giant), the size of the Sun, and its apparent diameter in the sky, are slowly increasing. The combination of these two changes means that hundreds of millions of years ago, the Moon would always completely cover the Sun on solar eclipses, and no annular eclipses were possible. Likewise, hundreds of millions of years in the future, the Moon will no longer cover the Sun completely, and total solar eclipses will not occur.

As the Moon's orbit around Earth is inclined by about 5.145° (5° 9′) to the [orbit of Earth around the Sun](/wiki/Ecliptic), eclipses do not occur at every full and new moon. For an eclipse to occur, the Moon must be near the intersection of the two orbital planes. The periodicity and recurrence of eclipses of the Sun by the Moon, and of the Moon by Earth, is described by the [saros](/wiki/Saros_(astronomy)), which has a period of approximately 18 years.

Because the Moon continuously blocks the view of a half-degree-wide circular area of the sky, the related phenomenon of [occultation](/wiki/Occultation) occurs when a bright star or planet passes behind the Moon and is occulted: hidden from view. In this way, a solar eclipse is an occultation of the Sun. Because the Moon is comparatively close to Earth, occultations of individual stars are not visible everywhere on the planet, nor at the same time. Because of the [precession](/wiki/Precession) of the lunar orbit, each year different stars are occulted.

## History of scientific understanding and exploration

Main articles: [Exploration of the Moon](/wiki/Exploration_of_the_Moon), [List of spacecraft that orbited the Moon](/wiki/List_of_spacecraft_that_orbited_the_Moon), [List of missions to the Moon](/wiki/List_of_missions_to_the_Moon), and [List of lunar probes](/wiki/List_of_lunar_probes)

### Pre-telescopic observation (before 1609)

It is believed by some that the oldest [cave paintings](/wiki/Cave_painting) from up to 40,000 [BP](/wiki/Before_present) of bulls and geometric shapes, or 20–30,000 year old [tally sticks](/wiki/Tally_stick) were used to observe the phases of the Moon, keeping time using the waxing and waning of [the Moon's phases](/wiki/Lunar_phases).
Aspects of the Moon were identified and aggregated in [lunar deities](/wiki/Lunar_deities) from [prehistoric times](/wiki/Prehistoric_times) and were eventually documented and put into symbols from the very first instances of [writing](/wiki/Writing) in the [4th millennium BC](/wiki/4th_millennium_BC). One of the earliest-discovered possible depictions of the Moon is a 3,000 BCE rock carving *Orthostat 47* at [Knowth](/wiki/Knowth), Ireland. The [crescent](#Crescent) depicting the Moon as with the lunar deity [Nanna/Sin](/wiki/Sin_(mythology)) have been found from the 3rd millennium BCE.

The oldest named astronomer and poet [Enheduanna](/wiki/Enheduanna), [Akkadian](/wiki/Akkadian_Empire) high priestess to the lunar deity Nanna/Sin and princess, daughter of [Sargon the Great](/wiki/Sargon_the_Great) (c. 2334 – c. 2279 BCE), had the Moon tracked in her chambers. The oldest found and identified depiction of the Moon in an astronomical relation to other astronomical features is the [Nebra sky disc](/wiki/Nebra_sky_disc) from c. 1800–1600 BCE, depicting features like the [Pleiades](/wiki/Pleiades) next to the Moon.

The [Nebra sky disc](/wiki/Nebra_sky_disc) (c. 1800–1600 BCE), found near a possibly [astronomical complex](/wiki/Astronomical_complex), most likely depicting the Sun or full Moon, the Moon as a crescent, the [Pleiades](/wiki/Pleiades) and the summer and winter solstices as strips of gold on the side of the disc, with the top representing the [horizon](/wiki/Horizon) and [north](/wiki/North).

The [ancient Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greece) philosopher [Anaxagoras](/wiki/Anaxagoras) (d. 428 BC) reasoned that the Sun and Moon were both giant spherical rocks, and that the latter reflected the light of the former. Elsewhere in the 5th century BC to 4th century BC, [Babylonian astronomers](/wiki/Babylonian_astronomers) had recorded the 18-year [Saros cycle](/wiki/Saros_cycle) of [lunar eclipses](/wiki/Lunar_eclipse), and [Indian astronomers](/wiki/Indian_astronomy) had described the Moon's monthly elongation. The [Chinese astronomer](/wiki/Chinese_astronomer) [Shi Shen](/wiki/Shi_Shen) ([fl.](/wiki/Fl.) 4th century BC) gave instructions for predicting solar and lunar eclipses.

In [Aristotle](/wiki/Aristotle)'s (384–322 BC) [description of the universe](/wiki/On_the_Heavens), the Moon marked the boundary between the spheres of the mutable elements (earth, water, air and fire), and the imperishable stars of [aether](/wiki/Aether_(classical_element)), an [influential philosophy](/wiki/Aristotelian_physics) that would dominate for centuries. [Archimedes](/wiki/Archimedes) (287–212 BC) designed a planetarium that could calculate the motions of the Moon and other objects in the Solar System. In the 2nd century BC, [Seleucus of Seleucia](/wiki/Seleucus_of_Seleucia) correctly thought that [tides](/wiki/Tide) were due to the attraction of the Moon, and that their height depends on the Moon's position relative to the [Sun](/wiki/Sun). In the same century, [Aristarchus](/wiki/On_the_Sizes_and_Distances_(Aristarchus)) computed the size and distance of the Moon from Earth, obtaining a value of about twenty times the [radius of Earth](/wiki/Radius_of_Earth) for the distance.

The Chinese of the [Han dynasty](/wiki/Han_dynasty) believed the Moon to be energy equated to *[qi](/wiki/Qi)* and their 'radiating influence' theory recognized that the light of the Moon was merely a reflection of the Sun; [Jing Fang](/wiki/Jing_Fang) (78–37 BC) noted the sphericity of the Moon. [Ptolemy](/wiki/Ptolemy) (90–168 AD) greatly improved on the numbers of Aristarchus, calculating a mean distance of 59 times Earth's radius and a diameter of 0.292 Earth diameters, close to the correct values of about 60 and 0.273 respectively. In the 2nd century AD, [Lucian](/wiki/Lucian) wrote the novel *[A True Story](/wiki/A_True_Story)*, in which the heroes travel to the Moon and meet its inhabitants. In 510 AD, the Indian astronomer [Aryabhata](/wiki/Aryabhata) mentioned in his *[Aryabhatiya](/wiki/Aryabhatiya)* that reflected sunlight is the cause of the shining of the Moon. The astronomer and physicist [Ibn al-Haytham](/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham) (965–1039) found that [sunlight](/wiki/Sunlight) was not reflected from the Moon like a mirror, but that light was emitted from every part of the Moon's sunlit surface in all directions. [Shen Kuo](/wiki/Shen_Kuo) (1031–1095) of the [Song dynasty](/wiki/Song_dynasty) created an allegory equating the waxing and waning of the Moon to a round ball of reflective silver that, when doused with white powder and viewed from the side, would appear to be a crescent. During the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages), before the invention of the telescope, the Moon was increasingly recognized as a sphere, though many believed that it was "perfectly smooth".

### Telescopic exploration (1609–1959)

The first published sketches of one of the first telescopic observations of the Moon, from the paper *[Sidereus Nuncius](/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius)* (1610) by [Galileo Galilei](/wiki/Galileo_Galilei), providing among other findings the first descriptions of the Moon's topography

The [telescope](/wiki/Telescope) was developed and reported on in 1608. The first record of telescopic astronomy and rough mapping of the Moon's features is from early summer 1609 by [Thomas Harriot](/wiki/Thomas_Harriot), but which he did not publish. At the same time [Galileo Galilei](/wiki/Galileo_Galilei) too started to use telescopes to observe the sky and the Moon, recording later that year more detailed observations and crucial conclusions, such as that the Moon was not smooth, featuring mountains and craters, which he published in 1610 in his ground-breaking and soon widely discussed book *[Sidereus Nuncius](/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius)*.

Later in the 17th century, the efforts of [Giovanni Battista Riccioli](/wiki/Giovanni_Battista_Riccioli) and [Francesco Maria Grimaldi](/wiki/Francesco_Maria_Grimaldi) led to the system of naming of lunar features in use today. The more exact 1834–1836 *[Mappa Selenographica](/wiki/Mappa_Selenographica)* of [Wilhelm Beer](/wiki/Wilhelm_Beer) and [Johann Heinrich von Mädler](/wiki/Johann_Heinrich_von_M%C3%A4dler), and their associated 1837 book *Der Mond*, the first [trigonometrically](/wiki/Trigonometry) accurate study of lunar features, included the heights of more than a thousand mountains, and introduced the study of the Moon at accuracies possible in earthly geography. Lunar craters, first noted by Galileo, were thought to be [volcanic](/wiki/Volcanic) until the 1870s proposal of [Richard Proctor](/wiki/Richard_Proctor) that they were formed by collisions. This view gained support in 1892 from the experimentation of geologist [Grove Karl Gilbert](/wiki/Grove_Karl_Gilbert), and from comparative studies from 1920 to the 1940s, leading to the development of [lunar stratigraphy](/wiki/Lunar_geologic_timescale), which by the 1950s was becoming a new and growing branch of [astrogeology](/wiki/Astrogeology).

### First missions to the Moon (1959–1976)

See also: [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race) and [Moon landing](/wiki/Moon_landing)

After [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) the first [launch systems](/wiki/Launch_system) were developed and by the end of the 1950s they reached capabilities that allowed the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) and the [United States](/wiki/United_States) to launch [spacecraft](/wiki/Spacecraft) into space. The [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) fueled a closely followed development of launch systems by the two states, resulting in the so-called [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race) and its later phase the Moon Race, accelerating efforts and interest in [exploration of the Moon](/wiki/Exploration_of_the_Moon).

First view of the [far side of the Moon](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon), taken by [Luna 3](/wiki/Luna_3), October 7, 1959. Clearly visible is [Mare Moscoviense](/wiki/Mare_Moscoviense) (top right) and a mare triplet of [Mare Crisium](/wiki/Mare_Crisium), [Mare Marginis](/wiki/Mare_Marginis) and [Mare Smythii](/wiki/Mare_Smythii) (left center).

After the first spaceflight of [Sputnik 1](/wiki/Sputnik_1) in 1957 during [International Geophysical Year](/wiki/International_Geophysical_Year) the spacecraft of the Soviet Union's [*Luna* program](/wiki/Luna_programme) were the first to accomplish a number of goals. Following three unnamed failed missions in 1958, the first human-made object *[Luna 1](/wiki/Luna_1)* escaped Earth's gravity and passed the Moon on 4 January 1959. Later that year the first human-made object *[Luna 2](/wiki/Luna_2)* reached the Moon's surface by [intentionally impacting](/wiki/Lander_(spacecraft)#Impactors). By the end of the year *[Luna 3](/wiki/Luna_3)* reached as the first human-made object the normally occluded [far side of the Moon](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon), taking the first photographs of it.
The first spacecraft to perform a successful lunar [soft landing](/wiki/Lander_(spacecraft)) was *[Luna 9](/wiki/Luna_9)* and the first vehicle to orbit the Moon was *[Luna 10](/wiki/Luna_10)*, both in 1966.

*[Earthrise](/wiki/Earthrise)*, by NASA astronaut [William Anders](/wiki/William_Anders), the first color [image of Earth](/wiki/Timeline_of_first_images_of_Earth_from_space) taken by a human from the Moon, during [Apollo 8](/wiki/Apollo_8) (1968), the first time a crewed spacecraft left Earth orbit and reached another [astronomical body](/wiki/Astronomical_body)

Following President [John F. Kennedy](/wiki/John_F._Kennedy)'s 1961 commitment to a crewed Moon landing before the end of the decade, the United States, under NASA leadership, launched a series of uncrewed probes to develop an understanding of the lunar surface in preparation for human missions: the [Jet Propulsion Laboratory](/wiki/Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory)'s [Ranger program](/wiki/Ranger_program), the [Lunar Orbiter program](/wiki/Lunar_Orbiter_program) and the [Surveyor program](/wiki/Surveyor_program). The crewed [Apollo program](/wiki/Apollo_program) was developed in parallel; after a series of uncrewed and crewed tests of the Apollo spacecraft in Earth orbit, and spurred on by a potential [Soviet lunar human landing](/wiki/Soviet_human_lunar_programs), in 1968 [Apollo 8](/wiki/Apollo_8) made the first human mission to lunar orbit (the first Earthlings, two tortoises, had circled the Moon three months earlier on the Soviet Union's [Zond 5](/wiki/Zond_5), followed by turtles on [Zond 6](/wiki/Zond_6)).

The first time a person landed on the Moon and any extraterrestrial body was when [Neil Armstrong](/wiki/Neil_Armstrong), the commander of the American mission [Apollo 11](/wiki/Apollo_11), set foot on the Moon at 02:56 UTC on July 21, 1969. Considered the culmination of the [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race), an estimated 500 million people worldwide watched the transmission by the [Apollo TV camera](/wiki/Apollo_TV_camera), the largest television audience for a live broadcast at that time. While at the same time another mission, the robotic sample return mission [Luna 15](/wiki/Luna_15) by the Soviet Union had been in orbit around the Moon, becoming together with Apollo 11 the first ever case of two extraterrestrial missions being conducted at the same time.

The Apollo missions 11 to 17 (except [Apollo 13](/wiki/Apollo_13), which aborted its planned lunar landing) removed 380.05 kilograms (837.87 lb) of lunar rock and soil in 2,196 [separate samples](/wiki/Moon_rock).
Scientific instrument packages were installed on the lunar surface during all the Apollo landings. Long-lived [instrument stations](/wiki/Apollo_Lunar_Surface_Experiments_Package), including heat flow probes, [seismometers](/wiki/Seismometer), and [magnetometers](/wiki/Magnetometer), were installed at the [Apollo 12](/wiki/Apollo_12), [14](/wiki/Apollo_14), [15](/wiki/Apollo_15), [16](/wiki/Apollo_16), and [17](/wiki/Apollo_17) landing sites. Direct transmission of data to Earth concluded in late 1977 because of budgetary considerations, but as the stations' [lunar laser ranging](/wiki/Lunar_laser_ranging) corner-cube retroreflector arrays are passive instruments, they are still being used.
Apollo 17 in 1972 was the last crewed mission to the Moon's orbit or surface, and the last to fly around the Moon until the [Artemis II](/wiki/Artemis_II) lunar flyby in 2026. [Explorer 49](/wiki/Explorer_49) in 1973 was the last dedicated U.S. lunar probe until 1994.

An example of the robotic and human exploration of the Moon in the first [visit of a separate mission](/wiki/Space_rendezvous) beyond [Low Earth Orbit](/wiki/Low_Earth_Orbit) (1969), [Apollo 12](/wiki/Apollo_12) astronaut [Pete Conrad](/wiki/Pete_Conrad) checking on [Surveyor 3](/wiki/Surveyor_3) at their shared [lunar base](/wiki/Lunar_base), with the Apollo 12 *Intrepid* lander and S-band antenna in the distant background

The Soviet Union continued sending robotic missions to the Moon until 1976, deploying in 1970 with [Luna 17](/wiki/Luna_17) the first remote controlled [rover](/wiki/Rover_(space_exploration)) [Lunokhod 1](/wiki/Lunokhod_1) on an extraterrestrial surface, and collecting and returning 0.3 kg of rock and soil samples with three *Luna* [sample return missions](/wiki/Sample_return_mission) (*[Luna 16](/wiki/Luna_16)* in 1970, *[Luna 20](/wiki/Luna_20)* in 1972, and *[Luna 24](/wiki/Luna_24)* in 1976).

### Moon Treaty and explorational absence (1976–1990)

Main article: [Moon Treaty](/wiki/Moon_Treaty)

Following the [last Soviet mission to the Moon](/wiki/Luna_24) of 1976, there was little further lunar exploration for fourteen years. Astronautics had shifted its focus towards the exploration of the [inner](/wiki/Inner_Solar_System) (e.g. [Venera program](/wiki/Venera_program)) and [outer](/wiki/Outer_Solar_System) (e.g. [Pioneer 10](/wiki/Pioneer_10), 1972) [Solar System](/wiki/Solar_System) [planets](/wiki/Planet), but also towards [Earth orbit](/wiki/Geocentric_orbit), developing and continuously operating, beside [communication satellites](/wiki/Communication_satellite), [Earth observation satellites](/wiki/Earth_observation_satellite) (e.g. [Landsat program](/wiki/Landsat_program), 1972), [space telescopes](/wiki/Space_telescope) and particularly [space stations](/wiki/Space_station) (e.g. [Salyut program](/wiki/Salyut_program), 1971).

Negotiation in 1979 of [Moon treaty](/wiki/Moon_treaty), and its subsequent ratification in 1984 was the only major activity regarding the Moon until 1990.

### Renewed exploration (1990–present)

In 1990 *[Hiten](/wiki/Hiten_(spacecraft))* – *Hagoromo*, the first dedicated lunar mission since 1976, reached the Moon's orbit. Sent by Japan, it became the first lunar mission that was not a Soviet Union or U.S. mission.

In 1994, the U.S. dedicated a mission to fly a spacecraft (*[Clementine](/wiki/Clementine_(spacecraft))*) to the Moon again for the first time since 1973. This mission obtained the first near-global topographic map of the Moon, and the first global [multispectral](/wiki/Multispectral) images of the lunar surface. In 1998, this was followed by the *[Lunar Prospector](/wiki/Lunar_Prospector)* mission, whose instruments indicated the presence of excess hydrogen at the lunar poles, which is likely to have been caused by the presence of water ice in the upper few meters of the regolith within permanently shadowed craters.

The next years saw a row of first missions to the Moon by a new group of states actively exploring the Moon.
Between 2004 and 2006 the first spacecraft by the [European Space Agency](/wiki/European_Space_Agency) (ESA) ([SMART-1](/wiki/SMART-1)) orbited the Moon, recording the first detailed survey of chemical elements on the lunar surface.
The [Chinese Lunar Exploration Program](/wiki/Chinese_Lunar_Exploration_Program) reached the Moon for the first time with the orbiter *[Chang'e 1](/wiki/Chang%27e_1)* (2007–2009), obtaining a full image map of the Moon.
[India](/wiki/Indian_Space_Research_Organisation) reached, orbited, and impacted the Moon in 2008 for the first time with its [Chandrayaan-1](/wiki/Chandrayaan-1) and [Moon Impact Probe](/wiki/Moon_Impact_Probe), becoming the fifth and sixth state to do so, creating a high-resolution chemical, mineralogical and photo-geological map of the lunar surface, and confirming the presence of [water molecules in lunar soil](/wiki/Lunar_water).

The U.S. launched the [Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO)](/wiki/Lunar_Reconnaissance_Orbiter) and the [LCROSS](/wiki/LCROSS) impactor on June 18, 2009. LCROSS completed its mission by making a planned and widely observed impact in the crater [Cabeus](/wiki/Cabeus) on October 9, 2009, whereas *LRO* is currently in operation, obtaining precise lunar [altimetry](/wiki/Altimetry) and high-resolution imagery.

China continued its lunar program in 2010 with [Chang'e 2](/wiki/Chang%27e_2), mapping the surface at a higher resolution over an eight-month period, and in 2013 with [Chang'e 3](/wiki/Chang%27e_3), a lunar [lander](/wiki/Lander_(spacecraft)) along with a [lunar rover](/wiki/Lunar_rover) named *[Yutu](/wiki/Yutu_(rover))* ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 玉兔; lit. 'Jade Rabbit'). This was the first lunar rover mission since *[Lunokhod 2](/wiki/Lunokhod_2)* in 1973 and the first lunar [soft landing](/wiki/Soft_landing) since *[Luna 24](/wiki/Luna_24)* in 1976, making China the third country to achieve this.

In 2014 the first privately funded probe, the [Manfred Memorial Moon Mission](/wiki/Manfred_Memorial_Moon_Mission), flew by the Moon. Another Chinese rover mission, [Chang'e 4](/wiki/Chang%27e_4), achieved the first landing on [the Moon's far side](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon) in early 2019. Also in 2019, India successfully sent its second probe, [Chandrayaan-2](/wiki/Chandrayaan-2), to the Moon. In 2020, China carried out its first robotic [sample return mission](/wiki/Sample_return_mission) ([Chang'e 5](/wiki/Chang%27e_5)), bringing back 1,731 grams of lunar material to Earth.

The U.S. [developed plans](/wiki/Vision_for_Space_Exploration) for returning astronauts to the Moon beginning in 2004, and with the signing of the U.S.-led [Artemis Accords](/wiki/Artemis_Accords) in 2020, the [Artemis program](/wiki/Artemis_program) aims to return humans to the Moon in the 2020s. The Accords have been joined by a growing number of countries. The introduction of the Artemis Accords has fueled a renewed discussion about the international framework and cooperation of lunar activity, building on the [Moon Treaty](/wiki/Moon_Treaty) and the ESA-led [Moon Village](/wiki/Moon_Village) concept.

In 2022, South Korea launched [Danuri](/wiki/Danuri) successfully, its first lunar probe, from the US.
In 2023 and 2024, India and Japan became the fourth and fifth country to [soft land](/wiki/Soft_landing) a spacecraft on the Moon, following the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) and United States in the 1960s, and China in 2013. Notably, Japan's spacecraft, the [Smart Lander for Investigating Moon](/wiki/Smart_Lander_for_Investigating_Moon), survived three lunar nights. In February 2024, the [IM-1](/wiki/IM-1) lander became the first commercially built lander to land on the Moon.

China launched [Chang'e 6](/wiki/Chang%27e_6) in May 2024, which conducted the first sample return from the [far side of the Moon](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon). It also carried a Chinese rover to conduct [infrared spectroscopy](/wiki/Absorption_spectroscopy) of lunar surface. [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan) sent a lunar orbiter called [ICUBE-Q](/wiki/ICUBE-Q) along with Chang'e 6. [Nova-C 2](/wiki/IM-2), [iSpace Lander](/wiki/Ispace_(Japanese_company)#Hakuto-R_program) and [Blue Ghost](/wiki/Blue_Ghost) were all launched to the Moon in 2024.

Photograph of the Moon, along with Earth during *[Earthset](/wiki/Earthset)* and the capsule  *[Integrity](/wiki/Orion_(spacecraft))*, from [cislunar space](/wiki/Cislunar_space) during the crewed [Artemis II](/wiki/Artemis_II) [lunar flyby](/wiki/Lunar_flyby) in 2026, the first crewed circumlunar flight since 1972

NASA's [Artemis II](/wiki/Artemis_II) crewed lunar flyby mission launched in April 2026. It was the first crewed mission around the Moon since the Apollo era. [Christina Koch](/wiki/Christina_Koch) became the first woman, [Victor Glover](/wiki/Victor_Glover) the first person of color, Canadian [Jeremy Hansen](/wiki/Jeremy_Hansen) the first non-American,  and commander [Reid Wiseman](/wiki/Reid_Wiseman) the oldest person, to leave [low Earth orbit](/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit) and to fly around the Moon.

### Future

See also: [List of proposed missions to the Moon](/wiki/List_of_proposed_missions_to_the_Moon)

Both the U.S. and Chinese lunar programs have the goal to establish a [lunar base](/wiki/Lunar_base) in the 2030s with their international partners, being the first crewed lunar landings since [Apollo 17](/wiki/Apollo_17) in 1972.

Following the [Artemis III](/wiki/Artemis_III) test flight in [low Earth orbit](/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit) scheduled for 2027, NASA plans to return humans to the lunar surface in 2028, with two [Artemis IV](/wiki/Artemis_IV) astronauts landing on the lunar south pole. While the Apollo missions were explorational in nature, the Artemis program plans to establish a more permanent presence. To this end, the U.S. is partnering with industry for lunar communications, payload delivery, and [in situ resource utilization](/wiki/In_situ_resource_utilization) through programs such as NASA's [Commercial Lunar Payload Services](/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services), and [DARPA lunar programs](/wiki/DARPA_lunar_programs).

China is continuing its ambitious [Chang'e program](/wiki/Chang%27e_program), having announced joint missions with Russia's struggling [Luna-Glob](/wiki/Luna-Glob) program. Two lunar survey missions are planned, [Chang'e 7](/wiki/Chang%27e_7) in 2026 and [Chang'e 8](/wiki/Chang%27e_8) in 2028, before a crewed landing in 2029 or 2030.

## Human interaction and presence

See also: [Human presence in space](/wiki/Human_presence_in_space)

Map of all the sites of [soft landings](/wiki/Soft_landing) on the Moon (2024)

The first extraterrestrial probes reached the Moon ([Luna program](/wiki/Luna_program)) in 1959, just a year into the [space age](/wiki/Space_age), after the first ever orbital flight. Since then, humans have sent a range of probes and people to the Moon. The first stay of people on the Moon was conducted in 1969, in a series of crewed exploration missions (the [Apollo Program](/wiki/Apollo_Program)), the last having taken place in 1972.

Uninterrupted presence has been the case through the [remains](/wiki/List_of_artificial_objects_on_the_Moon) of impactors, landings and [lunar orbiters](/wiki/List_of_extraterrestrial_orbiters#Moon). Some landings and orbiters have maintained a small lunar infrastructure, providing continuous observation and communication at the Moon. Longterm missions continuing to be active are some orbiters such as the 2009-launched [Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter](/wiki/Lunar_Reconnaissance_Orbiter) surveilling the Moon for future missions, as well as some Landers such as the 2013-launched [Chang'e 3](/wiki/Chang%27e_3) with its Lunar Ultraviolet Telescope still operational.
Five retroreflectors have been installed on the Moon since the 1970s and since used for accurate measurements of the physical [librations](/wiki/Libration) through [laser ranging to the Moon](/wiki/Lunar_Laser_Ranging_experiment).

Increasing human activity in [cislunar space](/wiki/Cislunar_space) as well as on the Moon's surface, particularly missions at the far side of the Moon or the [lunar north](/wiki/Lunar_north_pole) and [south polar](/wiki/Lunar_south_pole) regions, are in need for a lunar infrastructure. For that purpose, orbiters in [orbits around the Moon](/wiki/Lunar_orbit) or [the Earth–Moon Lagrange points](/wiki/List_of_objects_at_Lagrange_points#Earth–Moon_Lagrange_points), have since 2006 been operated. With highly [eccentric orbits](/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity) providing continuous communication, as with the orbit of [Queqiao](/wiki/Queqiao) and [Queqiao-2 relay satellite](/wiki/Queqiao-2_relay_satellite).

There are [several missions by different agencies and companies planned](/wiki/List_of_missions_to_the_Moon#Future_missions) to establish a long-term human presence on the Moon, with the [Artemis program](/wiki/Artemis_program) as the currently most advanced program.

### Human impact

See also: [Space debris](/wiki/Space_debris), [Space sustainability](/wiki/Space_sustainability), [List of artificial objects on the Moon](/wiki/List_of_artificial_objects_on_the_Moon), [Space art § Art in space](/wiki/Space_art#Art_in_space), [Moonbase](/wiki/Moonbase), [Lunar resources § Mining](/wiki/Lunar_resources#Mining), [Tourism on the Moon](/wiki/Tourism_on_the_Moon), and [Space archaeology](/wiki/Space_archaeology)

Artifacts of human activity, [Apollo 17's Lunar Surface Experiments Package](/wiki/Apollo_Lunar_Surface_Experiments_Package)

While the Moon has the lowest [planetary protection target-categorization](/wiki/Planetary_protection#Target_categories), its degradation as a pristine body and scientific place has been discussed. If there is [astronomy performed from the Moon](#Astronomy_from_the_Moon), it will need to be free from any physical and [radio pollution](/wiki/Radio_pollution). While the Moon has no significant atmosphere, traffic and impacts on the Moon causes clouds of dust that can spread far and possibly contaminate the original state of the Moon and its special scientific content. Scholar [Alice Gorman](/wiki/Alice_Gorman) asserts that, although the Moon is inhospitable, describing the Moon as dead negates its dynamics, which requires sustainable human activity to treat the Moon's ecology as a co-participant.

The so-called "[Tardigrade affair](/wiki/Tardigrades_on_the_Moon)" of the 2019 crashed *[Beresheet](/wiki/Beresheet)* lander and its carrying of [tardigrades](/wiki/Tardigrade) has been discussed as an example for lacking measures and lacking international regulation for [planetary protection](/wiki/Planetary_protection).

[Space debris](/wiki/Space_debris) beyond Earth around the Moon has been considered as a future challenge with increasing numbers of missions to the Moon, particularly as a danger for such missions. As such lunar waste management has been raised as an issue which future lunar missions, particularly on the surface, need to tackle.

Human remains have been transported to the Moon, including by private companies such as [Celestis](/wiki/Celestis) and [Elysium Space](/wiki/Elysium_Space). Because the Moon has been sacred or significant to many cultures, the practice of [space burials](/wiki/Space_burial) have attracted criticism from [indigenous peoples](/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) leaders. For example, then–[Navajo Nation](/wiki/Navajo_Nation) president [Albert Hale](/wiki/Albert_Hale) criticized NASA for sending the [cremated](/wiki/Cremated) ashes of scientist [Eugene Shoemaker](/wiki/Eugene_Shoemaker) to the Moon in 1998.

Beside the remains of human activity on the Moon, there have been some intended permanent installations like the *[Moon Museum](/wiki/Moon_Museum)* art piece, [Apollo 11 goodwill messages](/wiki/Apollo_11_goodwill_messages), six [lunar plaques](/wiki/Lunar_plaque), the *[Fallen Astronaut](/wiki/Fallen_Astronaut)* memorial, and other artifacts.

### Astronomy from the Moon

Further information: [Extraterrestrial sky § The Moon](/wiki/Extraterrestrial_sky#The_Moon)

Earth's [exosphere](/wiki/Exosphere) illuminated creating its [geocorona](/wiki/Geocorona), visible in ultraviolet and viewed by the [Far Ultraviolet Camera/Spectrograph](/wiki/Far_Ultraviolet_Camera/Spectrograph) of [Apollo 16](/wiki/Apollo_16) in 1972 from the Moon's surface

The Moon has been used as a site for astronomical and [Earth observations](/wiki/Earth_observation). The Earth appears in the [Moon's sky](/wiki/Extraterrestrial_sky#The_Moon) with an [apparent size](/wiki/Apparent_size) of 1° 48′ to 2°, three to four times the size of the Moon or Sun in Earth's sky, or about the apparent width of two little fingers at an arm's length away. Observations from the Moon started as early as 1966 with the [first images of Earth](/wiki/Timeline_of_first_images_of_Earth_from_space) from the Moon, taken by [Lunar Orbiter 1](/wiki/Lunar_Orbiter_1). Of particular cultural significance is the 1968 photograph called *[Earthrise](/wiki/Earthrise)*, taken by [Bill Anders](/wiki/Bill_Anders) of [Apollo 8](/wiki/Apollo_8) in 1968. In April 1972 the [Apollo 16](/wiki/Apollo_16) mission set up the first dedicated telescope, the [Far Ultraviolet Camera/Spectrograph](/wiki/Far_Ultraviolet_Camera/Spectrograph), recording various astronomical photos and spectra.

The Moon is recognized as an excellent site for telescopes. It is relatively nearby; certain craters near the poles are permanently dark and cold and especially useful for [infrared telescopes](/wiki/Infrared_telescope); and [radio telescopes](/wiki/Radio_telescope) on the far side would be shielded from the radio chatter of Earth. The [lunar soil](/wiki/Lunar_soil), although it poses a problem for any moving parts of [telescopes](/wiki/Telescope), can be mixed with [carbon nanotubes](/wiki/Carbon_nanotube) and [epoxies](/wiki/Epoxy) and employed in the construction of mirrors up to 50 meters in diameter. A lunar [zenith telescope](/wiki/Zenith_telescope) can be made cheaply with an [ionic liquid](/wiki/Ionic_liquid).

With increased human activity on the surface of the Moon the atmospheric circulation of lunar dust will increase, reducing the favourable conditions for astronomy from the surface depending on measures to mitigate the spread of lunar dust.

### Living on the Moon

Main article: [Lunar habitation](/wiki/Lunar_habitation)

Astronaut [Buzz Aldrin](/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin) in [life-supporting suit](/wiki/Apollo/Skylab_spacesuit) looking back at the first [lunar habitat and base](/wiki/Lunar_base), the [Lunar Module *Eagle*](/wiki/Lunar_Module_Eagle) of [Tranquility Base](/wiki/Tranquility_Base), during [Apollo 11](/wiki/Apollo_11) (1969), the first crewed [Moon landing](/wiki/Moon_landing)

Humans have lived on the Moon in groups of two and for up to three days. Altogether twelve people have stayed on the Moon over the course of six visits. They all lived in one type of [surface habitat](/wiki/Space_habitat_(facility)#Extraterrestrial_surface_habitat), the [Apollo Lunar Module](/wiki/Apollo_Lunar_Module). In the course of these stays some people have spent in total up to one day roaming the surface.

Challenges when roaming the surface arise from [lunar dust](/wiki/Lunar_dust) sticking to suits and tools, and has been carried inside the habitats. Astronauts could taste and smell the dust, which smells like gunpowder and was called the "Apollo aroma". This fine lunar dust can [cause health issues](/wiki/Lunar_dust#Harmful_effects_of_lunar_dust).

In 2019, at least one plant seed sprouted in an experiment on the [Chang'e 4 lander](/wiki/Change_4#Lunar_lander). It was carried from Earth along with other small life in its *Lunar Micro Ecosystem*.

### Legal status

See also: [Space law](/wiki/Space_law), [Politics of outer space](/wiki/Politics_of_outer_space), [Space advocacy](/wiki/Space_advocacy), [Colonization of the Moon](/wiki/Colonization_of_the_Moon), [Outer Space Treaty](/wiki/Outer_Space_Treaty), and [Moon Treaty](/wiki/Moon_Treaty)

Although *[Luna](/wiki/Luna_program)* landers scattered pennants of the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) on the Moon, and [U.S. flags](/wiki/Lunar_Flag_Assembly) were symbolically planted at their landing sites by the [Apollo astronauts](/wiki/Apollo_astronauts), no nation claims ownership of any part of the Moon's surface. Likewise no [private ownership of parts of the Moon, or as a whole](/wiki/Extraterrestrial_real_estate), is considered credible.

The 1967 [Outer Space Treaty](/wiki/Outer_Space_Treaty) defines the Moon and all outer space as the "[province of all mankind](/wiki/Common_heritage_of_mankind)". It restricts the use of the Moon to peaceful purposes, explicitly banning military installations and [weapons of mass destruction](/wiki/Weapons_of_mass_destruction). A majority of countries are parties of this treaty.
The 1979 [Moon Agreement](/wiki/Moon_Agreement) was created to elaborate, and restrict the exploitation of the [Moon's resources](/wiki/Lunar_resources) by any single nation, leaving it to a yet unspecified international regulatory regime. As of January 2020, it has been signed and ratified by 18 nations, none of which have [human spaceflight](/wiki/Human_spaceflight) capabilities.

Since 2020, countries have joined the U.S. in their [Artemis Accords](/wiki/Artemis_Accords), which are challenging the treaty. The U.S. has furthermore emphasized in a presidential [executive order](/wiki/Executive_order) ("Encouraging International Support for the Recovery and Use of Space Resources.") that "the United States does not view outer space as a 'global commons'" and calls the Moon Agreement "a failed attempt at constraining free enterprise".

With Australia signing and ratifying both the Moon Treaty in 1986 as well as the Artemis Accords in 2020, there has been a discussion if they can be harmonized. In this light an *Implementation Agreement* for the Moon Treaty has been advocated for, as a way to compensate for the shortcomings of the Moon Treaty and to harmonize it with other laws and agreements such as the Artemis Accords, allowing it to be more widely accepted.

In the face of such increasing commercial and national interest, particularly prospecting territories, U.S. lawmakers have introduced in late 2020 specific regulation for the conservation of historic landing sites and interest groups have argued for making such sites [World Heritage Sites](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site) and zones of scientific value protected zones, all of which add to the legal availability and territorialization of the Moon.

In 2021, the *Declaration of the Rights of the Moon* was created by a group of "lawyers, space archaeologists and concerned citizens", drawing on precedents in the [Rights of Nature](/wiki/Rights_of_Nature) movement and the concept of legal personality for non-human entities in space.

#### Coordination and regulation

Increasing human activity at the Moon has raised the need for coordination to safeguard international and commercial lunar activity. Issues from cooperation to mere coordination, through for example the development of a shared [Lunar time](/wiki/Lunar_time), have been raised.

In particular the establishment of an [international](/wiki/Internationalism_(politics)) or [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) regulatory regime for lunar human activity has been called for by the [Moon Treaty](/wiki/Moon_Treaty) and suggested through an *Implementation Agreement*, but remains contentious. Current lunar programs are [multilateral](/wiki/Multilateralism), with the US-led [Artemis program](/wiki/Artemis_program) and the China-led [International Lunar Research Station](/wiki/International_Lunar_Research_Station). For broader international cooperation and coordination, the [International Lunar Exploration Working Group](/wiki/International_Lunar_Exploration_Working_Group) (ILEWG), the [Moon Village Association](/wiki/Moon_Village) (MVA) and more generally the International Space Exploration Coordination Group (ISECG) has been established.

## In culture and life

### Timekeeping

Further information: [Lunar calendar](/wiki/Lunar_calendar), [Lunisolar calendar](/wiki/Lunisolar_calendar), and [Metonic cycle](/wiki/Metonic_cycle)

The [Venus of Laussel](/wiki/Venus_of_Laussel) (c. 25,000 [BP](/wiki/Before_present)) holding a crescent shaped horn. The 13 notches on the horn may symbolize the average number of days from menstruation to an [ovulation](/wiki/Ovulation), or the approximate number of full [menstrual cycles](/wiki/Menstrual_cycle) and [lunar cycles](/wiki/Lunar_cycle) per year (although these two phenomena are unrelated).

Since pre-historic times people have taken note of [the Moon's phases](/wiki/Lunar_phases) and its [waxing and waning cycle](/wiki/Lunar_cycle) and used it to keep record of time. [Tally sticks](/wiki/Tally_stick), notched bones dating as far back as 20–30,000 years ago, are believed by some to mark the phases of the Moon. The counting of the days between the Moon's phases eventually gave rise to generalized [time periods](/wiki/Unit_of_time) of lunar cycles as [months](/wiki/Month), and possibly of its phases as [weeks](/wiki/Week).

The words for the month in a range of different languages carry this relation between the period of the month and the Moon etymologically. The English *month* as well as *moon*, and its cognates in other Indo-European languages (e.g. the [Latin](/wiki/Latin) *mensis* and [Ancient Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greek) μείς (*meis*) or μήν (mēn), meaning "month") stem from the [Proto-Indo-European](/wiki/Proto-Indo-European) (PIE) root of *moon*, \**méh1nōt*, derived from the PIE verbal root \**meh1*-, "to measure", "indicat[ing] a functional conception of the Moon, i.e. marker of the month" ([cf.](/wiki/Cf.) the English words *measure* and *menstrual*). To give another example from a different [language family](/wiki/Language_family), the [Chinese language](/wiki/Chinese_language) uses the same word ([月](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9C%88)) for *moon* as for *month*, which furthermore can be found in the symbols for the word *week* ([星期](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%98%9F%E6%9C%9F)).

This lunar timekeeping gave rise to the historically dominant, but varied, [lunisolar calendars](/wiki/Lunisolar_calendar). The 7th-century [Islamic calendar](/wiki/Islamic_calendar) is an example of a purely [lunar calendar](/wiki/Lunar_calendar), where months are traditionally determined by the visual sighting of the hilal, or earliest crescent moon, over the horizon.

Of particular significance has been the occasion of [full moon](/wiki/Full_moon), highlighted and celebrated in a range of calendars and cultures, an example being the Buddhist [Vesak](/wiki/Vesak). The full moon around the [southern](/wiki/March_equinox) or [northern autumnal equinox](/wiki/September_equinox) is often called the [harvest moon](/wiki/Harvest_moon) and is celebrated with festivities such as the [Harvest Moon Festival](/wiki/Harvest_Moon_Festival) of the [Chinese lunar calendar](/wiki/Chinese_lunar_calendar), its second most important celebration after [the Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_New_Year) lunisolar [Lunar New Year](/wiki/Lunar_New_Year).

Furthermore, association of time with the Moon can also be found in religion, such as the ancient Egyptian temporal and lunar deity [Khonsu](/wiki/Khonsu).

### Cultural representation

Further information: [Cultural astronomy](/wiki/Cultural_astronomy), [Archaeoastronomy](/wiki/Archaeoastronomy), [Lunar deity](/wiki/Lunar_deity), [Selene](/wiki/Selene), [Luna (goddess)](/wiki/Luna_(goddess)), [Crescent](/wiki/Crescent), and [Man in the Moon](/wiki/Man_in_the_Moon)

See also: [Nocturne (painting)](/wiki/Nocturne_(painting)) and [Moon magic](/wiki/Moon_magic)

Recurring lunar [aspects](/wiki/Aspect_(religion)) of [lunar deities](/wiki/Lunar_deities)

The [crescent](/wiki/Crescent) of [Nanna/Sîn](/wiki/Sin_(mythology)), c. 2100 BC

Crescent headgear, [chariot](/wiki/Luna_(goddess)#Chariot_of_the_Moon) and [velificatio](/wiki/Velificatio) of [Luna](/wiki/Luna_(goddess)), 2nd–5th century

A [Moon rabbit](/wiki/Moon_rabbit) of the [Maya moon goddess](/wiki/Maya_moon_goddess), 6th–9th century

Humans have not only observed the Moon since [prehistoric times](/wiki/Prehistoric_times), but have also developed intricate perceptions of the Moon. Over time the Moon has been characterized and associated in many different ways, from having a [spirit](/wiki/Spirit_(vital_essence)) or being [a deity](/wiki/Lunar_deity), and an [aspect](/wiki/Aspect_(religion)) thereof or an aspect [in astrology](/wiki/Moon_(astrology)), being made an important part of many [cosmologies](/wiki/Cosmology).

This rich history of humans viewing the Moon has been evidenced starting with depictions from 40,000 [BP](/wiki/Before_present) and in written form from the [4th millennium BCE](/wiki/4th_millennium_BCE) in the earliest cases of [writing](/wiki/Writing). The oldest named astronomer and poet [Enheduanna](/wiki/Enheduanna), [Akkadian](/wiki/Akkadian_Empire) high priestess to the lunar deity [Nanna/Sin](/wiki/Sin_(mythology)) and daughter of [Sargon the Great](/wiki/Sargon_the_Great) (c. 2334 – c. 2279 BCE), tracked the Moon and wrote poems about her divine Moon.

#### Crescent

For the representation of the Moon, especially its [lunar phases](/wiki/Lunar_phase), the [crescent](/wiki/Crescent) (🌙) has been a recurring symbol in a range of cultures since at least 3,000 BCE or possibly earlier with bull horns dating to the earliest [cave paintings](/wiki/Cave_painting) at 40,000 [BP](/wiki/Before_present). In [writing systems](/wiki/Writing_system) such as Chinese the crescent has developed into the symbol [月](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9C%88), the word for Moon, and in ancient Egyptian it was the symbol [𓇹](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%93%87%B9), meaning Moon and spelled like the ancient Egyptian lunar deity *[Iah](/wiki/Iah)*, which the other ancient Egyptian lunar deities [Khonsu](/wiki/Khonsu) and [Thoth](/wiki/Thoth) were associated with.

Iconographically the crescent was used in [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia) as the primary symbol of [Nanna/Sîn](/wiki/Sin_(mythology)), the ancient [Sumerian](/wiki/Sumeria) lunar deity, who was the father of [Inanna/Ishtar](/wiki/Inanna), the goddess of the planet [Venus](/wiki/Venus) (symbolized as the [eight pointed](/wiki/Octagram) [Star of Ishtar](/wiki/Star_of_Ishtar)), and [Utu/Shamash](/wiki/Utu), the god of the Sun ([symbolized as a disc, optionally with eight rays](/wiki/Solar_symbol)), all three often depicted next to each other. Nanna/Sîn is, like some other lunar deities, for example Iah and Khonsu of ancient Egypt, [Mene](/wiki/Mene_(goddess))/[Selene](/wiki/Selene) of ancient Greece and [Luna](/wiki/Luna_(mythology)) of ancient Rome, depicted as a [horned deity](/wiki/Horned_deity), featuring crescent shaped headgears or crowns.

The particular arrangement of the crescent with a star known as the [star and crescent](/wiki/Star_and_crescent) (☪️) goes back to the Bronze Age, representing either the Sun and Moon, or the Moon and the planet Venus, in combination. It came to represent the selene goddess [Artemis](/wiki/Artemis), and via the patronage of [Hecate](/wiki/Hecate), which as [triple deity](/wiki/Triple_deity) under the [epithet](/wiki/Epithet) *trimorphos*/*trivia* included aspects of Artemis/[Diana](/wiki/Diana_(mythology)), came to be used as a [symbol of Byzantium](/wiki/Byzantine_flags_and_insignia), with [Virgin Mary](/wiki/Virgin_Mary) ([Queen of Heaven](/wiki/Queen_of_Heaven)) later taking her place, becoming depicted in [Marian veneration](/wiki/Marian_veneration) on a crescent and adorned with stars. Since then the [heraldric](/wiki/Heraldry) use of the star and crescent proliferated, Byzantium's symbolism possibly influencing the development of the [Ottoman flag](/wiki/Ottoman_flag), specifically the combination of the Turkish crescent with a star, and becoming a popular [symbol for Islam](/wiki/Symbols_of_Islam) (as the *[hilal](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/hilal#Noun)* of the [Islamic calendar](/wiki/Islamic_calendar)) and [for a range of nations](/wiki/Star_and_crescent#Contemporary_use).

#### Other associations

The features of the Moon, the contrasting brighter highlands and darker maria, have been seen by different cultures [forming abstract shapes](/wiki/Lunar_pareidolia). Such shapes are among others the [Man in the Moon](/wiki/Man_in_the_Moon) (e.g. [Coyolxāuhqui](/wiki/Coyolx%C4%81uhqui)) or the [Moon Rabbit](/wiki/Moon_Rabbit) (e.g. the Chinese [Tu'er Ye](/wiki/Tu%27er_Ye) or in [Indigenous American mythologies](/wiki/Mythologies_of_the_indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) the aspect of the [Maya moon goddess](/wiki/Maya_moon_goddess), from which possibly [Awilix](/wiki/Awilix) is derived, or of [Metztli](/wiki/Metztli)/[Tēcciztēcatl](/wiki/T%C4%93ccizt%C4%93catl)).

Occasionally some lunar deities have been also depicted [driving a chariot across the sky](/wiki/Luna_(goddess)#Chariot_of_the_Moon), such as the Hindu [Chandra/Soma](/wiki/Chandra), the Greek Artemis, which is associated with Selene, or Luna, Selene's ancient Roman equivalent.

Color and material wise the Moon has been associated in Western [alchemy](/wiki/Alchemy) with [silver](/wiki/Silver), while gold is associated with the Sun.

Through a miracle, the so-called [splitting of the Moon](/wiki/Splitting_of_the_Moon) ([Arabic](/wiki/Arabic_language): انشقاق القمر) in [Islam](/wiki/Islam), association with the Moon applies also to [Muhammad](/wiki/Muhammad).

### Representation in modern culture

See also: [Moon in science fiction](/wiki/Moon_in_science_fiction) and [List of appearances of the Moon in fiction](/wiki/List_of_appearances_of_the_Moon_in_fiction)

The Moon is prominently featured in [Vincent van Gogh](/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh)'s 1889 painting *[The Starry Night](/wiki/The_Starry_Night)*.

An iconic image of the [Man in the Moon](/wiki/Man_in_the_Moon) from the first [science-fiction film](/wiki/Science-fiction_film) set in space, *[A Trip to the Moon](/wiki/A_Trip_to_the_Moon)* (1902, [Georges Méliès](/wiki/Georges_M%C3%A9li%C3%A8s)), inspired by a history of literature about going to the Moon.

The perception of the Moon in the [modern era](/wiki/Modern_era) has been informed by [telescope](/wiki/Telescope)-enabled [modern astronomy](/wiki/Modern_astronomy). These new insights inspired cultural references, connecting romantic reflections about the Moon and speculative fiction such as science-fiction dealing with the Moon.

Later [spaceflight](/wiki/Spaceflight) ushered in the [space age](/wiki/Space_age), which produced especially with the first [Moon landings](/wiki/Moon_landing) historic achievements and references for [global society](/wiki/Global_society), represented through [images of Earth from a distance](/wiki/First_image_of_Earth) and summarized in an awe and awareness called the [overview effect](/wiki/Overview_effect), amounting to [Moon joy](/wiki/Moon_joy) by reaching the Moon.

### Lunar effect

Main article: [Lunar effect](/wiki/Lunar_effect)

The lunar effect is a supposed correlation between specific stages of the roughly 29.5-day lunar cycle, and behavior and physiological changes in humans on Earth. Lunar cycles have significant impacts on human culture, but no solid evidence connects these cycles to human biology.

The Moon has long been associated with insanity and irrationality; the words *lunacy* and *[lunatic](/wiki/Lunatic)* are derived from the Latin name for the Moon, *Luna*. Philosophers [Aristotle](/wiki/Aristotle) and [Pliny the Elder](/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder) argued that the full moon induced insanity in susceptible individuals, believing that the brain, which is mostly water, must be affected by the Moon and its power over the tides, but the Moon's gravity is too slight to affect any single person.
In modern society lunar effects have been claimed to be responsible for supposed increased admissions to psychiatric hospitals, traffic accidents, homicides or suicides during a full moon, but these claims have been invalidated by at least 37 studies.
