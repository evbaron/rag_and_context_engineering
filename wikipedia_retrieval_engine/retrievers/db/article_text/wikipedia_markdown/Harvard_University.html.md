**Harvard University** is a [private](/wiki/Private_university) [Ivy League](/wiki/Ivy_League) [research university](/wiki/Research_university) in [Cambridge, Massachusetts](/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts), United States. Founded in 1636, and named [Harvard College](/wiki/Harvard_College) in 1639 in honor of its first benefactor, [Puritan](/wiki/History_of_the_Puritans_in_North_America) clergyman [John Harvard](/wiki/John_Harvard_(clergyman)), it is the oldest institution of higher learning in the United States. Its influence, wealth, and rankings have made it one of the most prestigious universities in the world.

Harvard was founded and authorized by the [Massachusetts General Court](/wiki/Massachusetts_General_Court#Massachusetts_Bay_Colony), the governing legislature of [colonial](/wiki/Colonial_history_of_the_United_States)-era [Massachusetts Bay Colony](/wiki/Massachusetts_Bay_Colony). While never formally affiliated with any [Protestant denomination](/wiki/Protestant_church), Harvard trained [Congregational](/wiki/Congregationalism_in_the_United_States) clergy until its curriculum and student body were gradually secularized in the 18th century. In 1780, the [Constitution of Massachusetts](/wiki/Constitution_of_Massachusetts) referred to it as a [university](/wiki/University), and a new [medical school](/wiki/Medical_school) established in 1782 was named the [Medical Institution of Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School); but this was a new name, not a new foundation. Harvard College soon became the name of the university's undergraduate division.

By the 19th century, Harvard had emerged as the most prominent academic and cultural institution among the [Boston elite](/wiki/Boston_Brahmin). Following the [American Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War), under [Harvard president](/wiki/President_of_Harvard_University) [Charles William Eliot](/wiki/Charles_William_Eliot)'s long tenure from 1869 to 1909, Harvard developed multiple professional schools, which transformed it into a modern research university. In 1900, Harvard co-founded the [Association of American Universities](/wiki/Association_of_American_Universities). [James B. Conant](/wiki/James_B._Conant) led the university through the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression) and World War II, and liberalized admissions after the war.

The university has ten academic faculties and a faculty attached to [Harvard Radcliffe Institute](/wiki/Harvard_Radcliffe_Institute). The [Faculty of Arts and Sciences](/wiki/Harvard_Faculty_of_Arts_and_Sciences) offers study in a wide range of undergraduate and graduate [academic disciplines](/wiki/Academic_discipline), and other faculties offer graduate degrees, including professional degrees. Harvard has three campuses:
the main campus, a 209-acre (85 ha) in Cambridge centered on [Harvard Yard](/wiki/Harvard_Yard); an adjoining campus immediately across [Charles River](/wiki/Charles_River) in the [Allston](/wiki/Allston) neighborhood of [Boston](/wiki/Boston); and the medical campus in Boston's [Longwood Medical Area](/wiki/Longwood_Medical_and_Academic_Area). [Harvard's endowment](/wiki/Harvard_University_endowment), valued at $55.7 billion, makes it the [wealthiest academic institution](/wiki/List_of_colleges_and_universities_in_the_United_States_by_endowment) in the world. [Harvard Library](/wiki/Harvard_Library), with more than 20 million volumes, is the world's largest [academic library](/wiki/Academic_library).

Harvard alumni, faculty, and researchers include [188 living billionaires](/wiki/List_of_universities_by_number_of_billionaire_alumni), [8 U.S. presidents](/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_United_States_by_education), [24 heads of state and 31 heads of government](/wiki/List_of_Harvard_University_politicians), founders of notable companies, [Nobel laureates](/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates_by_university_affiliation), [Fields Medalists](/wiki/Fields_Medal), [members of Congress](/wiki/United_States_Congress), [MacArthur Fellows](/wiki/MacArthur_Fellows_Program), [Rhodes Scholars](/wiki/Rhodes_Scholarship), [Marshall Scholars](/wiki/Marshall_Scholarship), [Turing Award Recipients](/wiki/Turing_Award), [Pulitzer Prize](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize) recipients, and [Fulbright Scholars](/wiki/Fulbright_Program); by most metrics, Harvard University ranks among the top universities in the world in each of these categories. Harvard students and alumni have also collectively won 10 [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Awards) and [110 Olympic medals](/wiki/List_of_American_universities_with_Olympic_medalist_students_and_alumni), including 46 gold medals.

## History

Main article: [History of Harvard University](/wiki/History_of_Harvard_University)

### 17th century

See also: [John Harvard (clergyman)](/wiki/John_Harvard_(clergyman)), [Nathaniel Eaton](/wiki/Nathaniel_Eaton), and [Increase Mather](/wiki/Increase_Mather)

A 1767 engraving of [Harvard College](/wiki/Harvard_College) by [Paul Revere](/wiki/Paul_Revere)

Harvard was founded in 1636 by a vote of the [Great and General Court](/wiki/Massachusetts_General_Court) of the [Massachusetts Bay Colony](/wiki/Massachusetts_Bay_Colony). Its first headmaster, [Nathaniel Eaton](/wiki/Nathaniel_Eaton), took office the following year. In 1638, the university acquired [English North America](/wiki/British_North_America)'s first known [printing press](/wiki/Printing_press). The same year, on his deathbed, [John Harvard](/wiki/John_Harvard_(clergyman)), a [Puritan](/wiki/Puritans) clergyman who had emigrated to the colony from England, bequeathed the emerging college £780 and his library of some 320 volumes; the following year, it was named [Harvard College](/wiki/Harvard_College).

In 1643, a Harvard publication defined the college's purpose: "[to] advance learning and perpetuate it to posterity, dreading to leave an illiterate ministry to the churches when our present ministers shall lie in the dust."

In its early years, the college trained many Puritan [Congregational](/wiki/Established_Congregational_churches) ministers and offered a [classical curriculum](/wiki/Classical_education) based on the English university model exemplified by the [University of Cambridge](/wiki/University_of_Cambridge), where many colonial Massachusetts leaders had studied prior to emigrating to the colony. Harvard College never formally affiliated with any particular [Protestant](/wiki/Protestantism) denomination, but its curriculum conformed to the tenets of Puritanism. In 1650, the charter for [Harvard Corporation](/wiki/President_and_Fellows_of_Harvard_College), the college's governing body, was granted.

From 1681 to 1701, [Increase Mather](/wiki/Increase_Mather), a Puritan clergyman, served as Harvard's sixth [president](/wiki/President_of_Harvard_University). In 1708, [John Leverett](/wiki/John_Leverett_the_Younger) became Harvard's seventh president and the first president who was not also a clergyman.

### Harvard seal

The earliest known official seal of Harvard College, commonly referred to as the Seal of 1650 or the In Christi Gloriam seal, features a square shield bearing three open books arranged around a central chevron. This design symbolizes the pursuit of learning under divine guidance. The motto IN CHRISTI GLORIAM ("To the glory of Christ") appears prominently on the seal, which is encircled by the Latin inscription SIGILL COL HARVARD CANTAB NOV ANGL 1650, meaning "Seal of Harvard College Cambridge New England 1650." This seal reflects the original religious mission of the institution.

In 1885, the Harvard Corporation adopted a revised design known as the Appleton Seal, based on an earlier version created by President Josiah Quincy in 1843. Designed by William Sumner Appleton (Harvard AB 1860), the seal features a triangular shield bearing three open books with the motto VERITAS ("Truth"). Surrounding the shield is the motto CHRISTO ET ECCLESIÆ ("For Christ and the Church"), and the outer border bears the inscription SIGILLVM ACADEMIÆ HARVARDINÆ IN NOV. ANG. ("Seal of Harvard College in New England"). This version of the seal sought to harmonize the university's intellectual pursuits with its ecclesiastical roots.

### 18th century

Harvard faculty and students largely supported the [Patriot](/wiki/Patriot_(American_Revolution)) cause during the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution).

In the second half of the 18th century, Harvard began granting graduate and doctorate-level degrees. In 1780, the [Constitution of Massachusetts](/wiki/Constitution_of_Massachusetts) recognized Harvard College as a [university](/wiki/University), and when in 1783 a new [medical school](/wiki/Medical_school) was established, it was given the formal name of the [Medical Institution of Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School). This was a re-naming of the college, and not a re-founding.

### 19th century

See also: [Charles William Eliot](/wiki/Charles_William_Eliot) and [Samuel Webber](/wiki/Samuel_Webber)

The [John Harvard statue](/wiki/John_Harvard_statue) in [Harvard Yard](/wiki/Harvard_Yard)

In the 19th century, Harvard was influenced by [Enlightenment Age](/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment) ideas, including reason and free will, which were widespread among [Congregational](/wiki/Congregationalism_in_the_United_States) ministers and which placed these ministers and their congregations at odds with more traditionalist, [Calvinist](/wiki/Reformed_Christianity) pastors and clergies. Following the death of [Hollis Professor of Divinity](/wiki/Hollis_Professor_of_Divinity) [David Tappan](/wiki/David_Tappan) in 1803 and that of [Joseph Willard](/wiki/Joseph_Willard), Harvard's eleventh president, the following year, a struggle broke out over their replacements. In 1805, [Henry Ware](/wiki/Henry_Ware_(Unitarian)) was elected to replace Tappan as Hollis chair. Two years later, in 1807, liberal [Samuel Webber](/wiki/Samuel_Webber) was appointed as Harvard's 13th president, representing a shift from traditional ideas at Harvard to more liberal and [Arminian](/wiki/Arminianism) ideas.

In 1816, Harvard University launched new language programs in the study of [French](/wiki/French_language) and [Spanish](/wiki/Spanish_language), and appointed [George Ticknor](/wiki/George_Ticknor) the university's first professor for these language programs.

From 1869 to 1909, [Charles William Eliot](/wiki/Charles_William_Eliot), Harvard University's 21st president, decreased the historically favored position of [Christianity](/wiki/Christianity) in the curriculum, opening it to student self-direction. Though Eliot was an influential figure in the secularization of U.S. higher education, he was motivated primarily by [Transcendentalist](/wiki/Transcendentalism) and [Unitarian](/wiki/Unitarianism) convictions influenced by [William Ellery Channing](/wiki/William_Ellery_Channing), [Ralph Waldo Emerson](/wiki/Ralph_Waldo_Emerson), and others, rather than secularism. In the late 19th century, Harvard University's graduate schools began admitting women in small numbers.

### 20th century

See also: [A. Lawrence Lowell](/wiki/A._Lawrence_Lowell) and [James B. Conant](/wiki/James_B._Conant)

A 1906 aerial watercolor portrait of Harvard University

In 1900, Harvard became a founding member of the [Association of American Universities](/wiki/Association_of_American_Universities). For the first few decades of the 20th century, the Harvard student body was predominantly "old-stock, high-status [Protestants](/wiki/Protestantism), especially [Episcopalians](/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)), [Congregationalists](/wiki/Congregational_church), and [Presbyterians](/wiki/Presbyterianism)," according to sociologist and author [Jerome Karabel](/wiki/Jerome_Karabel).

Over the 20th century, as its endowment burgeoned and prominent intellectuals and professors affiliated with it, Harvard University's reputation as one of the world's most prestigious universities grew notably. The university's enrollment also underwent substantial growth, a product of both the founding of new graduate academic programs and an expansion of the [undergraduate college](/wiki/Harvard_College). [Radcliffe College](/wiki/Radcliffe_College) emerged as the female counterpart of Harvard College, becoming one of the most prominent schools in the nation for women.

In 1923, a year after the proportion of [Jewish](/wiki/Jews) students at Harvard reached 20%, [A. Lawrence Lowell](/wiki/A._Lawrence_Lowell), the university's 22nd president, unsuccessfully proposed capping the admission of Jewish students to 15% of the undergraduate population. Lowell also refused to mandate forced desegregation in the university's [freshman dormitories](/wiki/List_of_Harvard_College_freshman_dormitories), writing that, "We owe to the colored man the same opportunities for education that we do to the white man, but we do not owe to him to force him and the white into social relations that are not, or may not be, mutually congenial."

Between 1933 and 1953, Harvard University was led by [James B. Conant](/wiki/James_B._Conant), the university's 23rd president, who reinvigorated the university's creative scholarship in an effort to guarantee Harvard's preeminence among the nation and world's emerging research institutions. Conant viewed higher education as a vehicle of opportunity for the talented rather than an entitlement for the wealthy, and devised programs to identify, recruit, and support talented youth. In 1945, under Conant's leadership, an influential 268-page report, *[General Education in a Free Society](/wiki/General_Education_in_a_Free_Society)*, was published by Harvard faculty, which remains one of the most important works in [curriculum studies](/wiki/Curriculum_studies), and women were first admitted to the [medical school](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School).

Between 1945 and 1960, admissions were standardized to open the university to a more diverse group of students. Following the end of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), for example, special exams were developed so veterans could be considered for admission. No longer drawing mostly from prestigious [prep schools](/wiki/College-preparatory_school) in [New England](/wiki/New_England), the undergraduate college became accessible to striving middle class students from public schools; many more Jews and Catholics were admitted, but Blacks, Hispanics, and Asians remained underrepresented. Over the second half of the 20th century, however, the university became incrementally more diverse.

Between 1971 and 1999, Harvard controlled undergraduate admission, instruction, and housing for Radcliffe's women; in 1999, Radcliffe was formally merged into Harvard University.

### 21st century

See also: [Drew Gilpin Faust](/wiki/Drew_Gilpin_Faust), [Lawrence Bacow](/wiki/Lawrence_Bacow), [Claudine Gay](/wiki/Claudine_Gay), and [Alan Garber](/wiki/Alan_Garber)

An aerial view of Harvard University at night in 2017

On July 1, 2007, [Drew Gilpin Faust](/wiki/Drew_Gilpin_Faust), dean of [Harvard Radcliffe Institute](/wiki/Harvard_Radcliffe_Institute), was appointed Harvard's 28th and the university's first female president. On July 1, 2018, Faust retired and joined the board of [Goldman Sachs](/wiki/Goldman_Sachs), and [Lawrence Bacow](/wiki/Lawrence_Bacow) became Harvard's [29th president](/wiki/President_of_Harvard_University).

In February 2023, approximately 6,000 Harvard workers attempted to organize a union.

Bacow retired in June 2023, and on July 1 [Claudine Gay](/wiki/Claudine_Gay), a Harvard professor in the Government and African American Studies departments and dean of the [Faculty of Arts and Sciences](/wiki/Harvard_Faculty_of_Arts_and_Sciences), became Harvard's 30th president. In January 2024, just six months into her presidency, Gay resigned following [allegations of antisemitism](/wiki/Claudine_Gay#Congressional_hearing_on_antisemitism) and [plagiarism](/wiki/Claudine_Gay#Plagiarism_investigations). Gay was succeeded by [Alan Garber](/wiki/Alan_Garber), the university's provost, who was appointed interim president. In August 2024, the university announced that Garber would be appointed Harvard's 31st president through the end of the 2026–27 academic year.

#### Second presidency of Donald Trump

See also: [Education policy of the second Trump administration § Actions against universities](/wiki/Education_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration#Actions_against_universities)

In February 2025, [Leo Terrell](/wiki/Leo_Terrell), the head of the Trump administration's [Task Force to Combat Antisemitism](/wiki/Antisemitism_in_the_United_States#Efforts_to_combat_antisemitism), announced that he would investigate Harvard University as part of the Department of Justice's broader investigation into [antisemitism on college campuses](/wiki/Antisemitism_in_US_higher_education).

In April 2025, the United States federal government under President [Donald Trump](/wiki/Donald_Trump) threatened to withhold nearly $9 billion in government funds from the university unless the university complied with government demands to modify many of its policies. This threat was part of a broader battle over universities' autonomy following contentious [student protests](/wiki/2024_pro-Palestinian_protests_on_university_campuses) against the [Gaza war](/wiki/Gaza_war), and followed [similar demands](/wiki/Education_policy_of_the_second_Donald_Trump_administration#Actions_against_universities) made of [Columbia University](/wiki/Columbia_University). The university's leadership resisted the government's demands, claiming that they were an unlawful overreach of government authority. In response, the [US Department of Education](/wiki/US_Department_of_Education) announced they were freezing $2.3 billion in federal funds to Harvard. The [Department of Homeland Security](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Homeland_Security) subsequently threatened to revoke Harvard's eligibility to host [international students](/wiki/International_student). Harvard responded by filing [a lawsuit](/wiki/President_and_Fellows_of_Harvard_College_v._Department_of_Health_and_Human_Services) against the Trump administration in the [District Court of Massachusetts](/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Massachusetts), arguing that the freezing of funds was unconstitutional.

In May 2025, education secretary [Linda McMahon](/wiki/Linda_McMahon) informed Harvard president Garber that the federal government would no longer provide grant funding until the university complied with the Trump administration's demands. The following week, the Trump administration cut an additional $450 million in grants to the school.

Decertification Letter sent by Kristi Noem on May 22, 2025

Later that same month, Department of Homeland Security secretary [Kristi Noem](/wiki/Kristi_Noem) announced that Harvard's [Student and Exchange Visitor Program](/wiki/Student_and_Exchange_Visitor_Program) certification had been revoked, barring Harvard from hosting international students. The following day, Harvard sued the Trump administration for banning them from enrolling international students and U.S. District Judge [Allison Burroughs](/wiki/Allison_D._Burroughs) granted a temporary restraining order stopping the ban. On June 16, 2025, Burroughs postponed a ruling after hearing arguments from lawyers on both sides, leaving the temporary block in place for another week.

On May 30, 2025, the [State Department](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_State) ordered all US embassies and consulates to conduct "comprehensive and thorough vetting" of the online presence of anyone seeking to visit Harvard from abroad.

On June 4, 2025, Trump issued a proclamation restricting international students from studying at Harvard, and directing the State Department to consider revoking the visas of current international students studying at that university. The following day, Harvard filed a legal challenge, amending their existing federal complaint against the administration.

On June 20, Harvard was granted an injunction allowing it to continue hosting international students as litigation continues. On June 30, a Trump administration investigation found Harvard violated federal civil rights law by failing to protect Jewish students, faculty, and staff.

On September 3, 2025 US District Judge [Allison Burroughs](/wiki/Allison_D._Burroughs) ruled the Trump administration illegally froze more than $2 billion in research funding stating the administration "...violated Harvard's free-speech rights as well as the US Civil Rights Act."

In February 2026, Trump announced that his administration would seek $1 billion in damages from Harvard. In a post on [Truth Social](/wiki/Truth_Social), Trump accused Harvard of supplying misleading information to *The New York Times*. Administration officials claimed that the university failed to adequately address antisemitism during pro-Palestinian protests, a claim Harvard has denied.

On February 6th, Secretary of Defense [Pete Hegseth](/wiki/Pete_Hegseth) announced that the [Pentagon](/wiki/The_Pentagon) would start to sever ties to Harvard with its graduate-level military training courses. Hegseth accused Harvard of becoming a "factory for woke ideology and a breeding ground for anti-American radicals..." as a reason for discontinuing the programs. A week later, Harvard along with other prestigious American Universities was listed as “Moderate to High Risk” in a Department of Defense memo for Assisted Military Tuition, and could be barred for service members for tuition assistance.

## Campuses

### Cambridge

See also: [Harvard Divinity School](/wiki/Harvard_Divinity_School), [Harvard Graduate School of Design](/wiki/Harvard_Graduate_School_of_Design), [Harvard Graduate School of Education](/wiki/Harvard_Graduate_School_of_Education), [Harvard John A. Paulson School of Engineering and Applied Sciences](/wiki/Harvard_John_A._Paulson_School_of_Engineering_and_Applied_Sciences), [Harvard Kennedy School](/wiki/Harvard_Kennedy_School), [Harvard Law School](/wiki/Harvard_Law_School), and [Harvard Radcliffe Institute](/wiki/Harvard_Radcliffe_Institute)

[Memorial Hall](/wiki/Memorial_Hall_(Harvard_University)), built on the main Cambridge campus in 1870

[Harvard Yard](/wiki/Harvard_Yard) at the center of Harvard's main campus in [Cambridge](/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts)

The 209-acre (85 ha) main campus of Harvard University is centered on [Harvard Yard](/wiki/Harvard_Yard), colloquially known as "the Yard", in [Cambridge, Massachusetts](/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts), about three miles (five km) west-northwest of downtown [Boston](/wiki/Boston), and extending to the surrounding [Harvard Square](/wiki/Harvard_Square) neighborhood. The Yard houses several Harvard buildings, including four of the university's libraries, [Houghton](/wiki/Houghton_Library), [Lamont](/wiki/Lamont_Library), [Pusey](/wiki/Pusey_Library), and [Widener](/wiki/Widener_Library). Also on Harvard Yard are [Massachusetts Hall](/wiki/Massachusetts_Hall_(Harvard_University)), built between 1718 and 1720 and the university's oldest still standing building, [Memorial Church](/wiki/Memorial_Church_of_Harvard_University), and [University Hall](/wiki/University_Hall_(Harvard_University)).

Harvard Yard and adjacent areas include the main academic buildings of the [Faculty of Arts and Sciences](/wiki/Harvard_Faculty_of_Arts_and_Sciences), including [Sever Hall](/wiki/Sever_Hall), [Harvard Hall](/wiki/Harvard_Hall), and [freshman dormitories](/wiki/List_of_Harvard_College_freshman_dormitories). Upperclassmen live in the twelve [residential houses](/wiki/Harvard_House_system): nine south of Harvard Yard near the [Charles River](/wiki/Charles_River), and three on the [Radcliffe Quadrangle](/wiki/Radcliffe_Quadrangle_(Harvard)), which formerly housed [Radcliffe College](/wiki/Radcliffe_College) students. Each house is a community of undergraduates, faculty deans, and resident tutors, with its own dining hall, library, and recreational facilities.

Also on the main campus in Cambridge are the [Law](/wiki/Harvard_Law_School), [Divinity](/wiki/Harvard_Divinity_School) (theology), [Engineering and Applied Science](/wiki/Harvard_John_A._Paulson_School_of_Engineering_and_Applied_Sciences), [Design](/wiki/Harvard_Graduate_School_of_Design) (architecture), [Education](/wiki/Harvard_Graduate_School_of_Education), [Kennedy](/wiki/Harvard_Kennedy_School) (public policy), and [Extension](/wiki/Harvard_Extension_School) schools, and [Harvard Radcliffe Institute](/wiki/Harvard_Radcliffe_Institute) in Radcliffe Yard. Harvard also has commercial real estate holdings in Cambridge.

### Allston

Main article: [Harvard University's expansion in Allston, Massachusetts](/wiki/Harvard_University%27s_expansion_in_Allston,_Massachusetts)

[Harvard Business School](/wiki/Harvard_Business_School), [Harvard Innovation Labs](/wiki/Harvard_Innovation_Labs), and many athletics facilities, including [Harvard Stadium](/wiki/Harvard_Stadium), are located on a 358-acre (145 ha) campus in the [Allston](/wiki/Allston) section of [Boston](/wiki/Boston) across the [John W. Weeks Bridge](/wiki/John_W._Weeks_Bridge), which crosses the [Charles River](/wiki/Charles_River) and connects the Allston and Cambridge campuses.

The university is actively expanding into Allston, where it now owns more land than in Cambridge. Plans include new construction and renovation for the Business School, a hotel and conference center, graduate student housing, Harvard Stadium, and other athletics facilities.

In 2021, the [Harvard John A. Paulson School of Engineering and Applied Sciences](/wiki/Harvard_John_A._Paulson_School_of_Engineering_and_Applied_Sciences) expanded into the new Allston-based Science and Engineering Complex (SEC), which is more than 500,000 square feet in size. SEC is adjacent to the Enterprise Research Campus, the Business School, and Harvard Innovation Labs, and designed to encourage technology- and life science-focused startups and collaborations with mature companies.

### Longwood

Main article: [Longwood Medical and Academic Area](/wiki/Longwood_Medical_and_Academic_Area)

[Harvard Medical School](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School) in the [Longwood Medical and Academic Area](/wiki/Longwood_Medical_and_Academic_Area) in [Boston](/wiki/Boston)

The university's schools of [Medicine](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School), [Dental Medicine](/wiki/Harvard_School_of_Dental_Medicine), and [Public Health](/wiki/Harvard_T.H._Chan_School_of_Public_Health) are located on a 21-acre (8.5 ha) campus in the [Longwood Medical and Academic Area](/wiki/Longwood_Medical_and_Academic_Area) in [Boston](/wiki/Boston), about 3.3 miles (5.3 km) south of the Cambridge campus.

Several Harvard-affiliated hospitals and research institutes are also in Longwood, including [Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center](/wiki/Beth_Israel_Deaconess_Medical_Center), [Boston Children's Hospital](/wiki/Boston_Children%27s_Hospital), [Brigham and Women's Hospital](/wiki/Brigham_and_Women%27s_Hospital), [Dana–Farber Cancer Institute](/wiki/Dana%E2%80%93Farber_Cancer_Institute), [Joslin Diabetes Center](/wiki/Joslin_Diabetes_Center), and the [Wyss Institute for Biologically Inspired Engineering](/wiki/Wyss_Institute_for_Biologically_Inspired_Engineering). Additional affiliates, including [Massachusetts General Hospital](/wiki/Massachusetts_General_Hospital), are located throughout [Greater Boston](/wiki/Greater_Boston).

and [Arnold Arboretum](/wiki/Arnold_Arboretum) in the [Jamaica Plain](/wiki/Jamaica_Plain) neighborhood of Boston.

## Organization and administration

### Governance

See also: [Harvard Board of Overseers](/wiki/Harvard_Board_of_Overseers), [President and Fellows of Harvard College](/wiki/President_and_Fellows_of_Harvard_College), and [President of Harvard University](/wiki/President_of_Harvard_University)

Harvard is governed by a combination of its [Board of Overseers](/wiki/Harvard_Board_of_Overseers) and the [President and Fellows of Harvard College](/wiki/President_and_Fellows_of_Harvard_College), which is also known as the Harvard Corporation. These two bodies, in turn, appoint the [president of Harvard University](/wiki/President_of_Harvard_University).

There are 16,000 staff and faculty, including 2,400 professors, lecturers, and instructors in the colleges proper, plus another >10,400 academic appointments in affiliated teaching hospitals)

As of 2025, Harvard differs radically from its peer universities in two important ways. First, Harvard does not make its governing statutes publicly available, meaning that members of the Harvard community interested in reform must first persuade the university to give them a copy of those documents. Second, Harvard does not have an [academic senate](/wiki/Academic_senate) like most of its peers, although it is currently attempting to create one.

### Endowment

Main article: [Harvard University endowment](/wiki/Harvard_University_endowment)

Harvard has the largest [university endowment](/wiki/List_of_colleges_and_universities_in_the_United_States_by_endowment) in the world, valued at about $50.7 billion as of 2023.

During the [recession of 2007–2009](/wiki/Great_Recession), it suffered significant losses that forced large budget cuts, in particular temporarily halting construction on the Allston Science Complex. The endowment has since recovered.

About $2 billion of investment income is annually distributed to fund operations.
Harvard's ability to fund its degree and financial aid programs depends on the performance of its endowment; a poor performance in fiscal year 2016 forced a 4.4% cut in the number of graduate students funded by the [Faculty of Arts and Sciences](/wiki/Harvard_Faculty_of_Arts_and_Sciences).
Endowment income is critical, as only 22% of revenue is from students' tuition, fees, room, and board.

#### Divestment

Since the 1970s, several student-led campaigns have advocated [divesting](/wiki/Divestment) Harvard's endowment from controversial holdings, including investments in [South Africa](/wiki/South_Africa) during [apartheid](/wiki/Apartheid), [Sudan](/wiki/Sudan) during the [Darfur genocide](/wiki/Darfur_genocide_(2003%E2%80%932005)), and [tobacco](/wiki/Tobacco_industry), [fossil fuel](/wiki/Fossil_fuel), and [private prison](/wiki/Private_prison) industries.

In the late 1980s, during the [disinvestment from South Africa](/wiki/Disinvestment_from_South_Africa) movement, student activists erected a symbolic [shanty town](/wiki/Shanty_town) on Harvard Yard and blockaded a speech by South African Vice Consul Duke Kent-Brown.

In response to pressure, the university eventually reduced its South African holdings by $230 million out of a total of $400 million between 1986 and 1987.

### Harvard College

Main article: [Harvard College](/wiki/Harvard_College)

[Harvard College](/wiki/Harvard_College), the four-year, full-time undergraduate program, has a [liberal arts and sciences](/wiki/Liberal_arts_education) focus. To graduate in the usual four years, undergraduates normally take 16 credit hours (usually across four courses) per semester for a total of 128 credit hours.
In most majors, an honors degree requires advanced coursework and a senior thesis.

Though some introductory courses have large enrollments, the median class size is 12 students.

### Harvard Medical School

Main article: [Harvard Medical School](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School)

[Harvard Medical School](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School)

[Harvard Medical School](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School), the third-oldest medical school in the United States, was founded in 1782 as Massachusetts Medical College by [John Warren](/wiki/John_Warren_(surgeon,_born_1753)), [Benjamin Waterhouse](/wiki/Benjamin_Waterhouse), and Aaron Dexter. In 1810, Harvard Medical School relocated from Cambridge to [Boston](/wiki/Boston). The medical school was tied to the rest of the university "only by the tenuous thread of degrees", but its strong faculty gave it a national reputation by the early 19th century.

The medical school moved to its current location on Longwood Avenue in 1906, where the "Great White Quadrangle" or HMS Quad with its five white marble buildings was established.

Harvard Medical School's reputation continued to grow into the 20th century, especially in terms of scientific research and support from regional and national elites. Fifteen scientists won the [Nobel Prize](/wiki/Nobel_Prize) for work done at the Medical School. Its four major flagship teaching hospitals are [Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center](/wiki/Beth_Israel_Deaconess_Medical_Center), [Brigham and Women's Hospital](/wiki/Brigham_and_Women%27s_Hospital), [Boston Children's Hospital](/wiki/Boston_Children%27s_Hospital) and [Massachusetts General Hospital](/wiki/Massachusetts_General_Hospital).

### Harvard Law School

Main article: [Harvard Law School](/wiki/Harvard_Law_School)

[Christopher Columbus Langdell](/wiki/Christopher_Columbus_Langdell), an influential dean of [Harvard Law School](/wiki/Harvard_Law_School) from 1875 to 1890

The establishment of Harvard Law School in 1817 was made possible by a 1779 bequest from [Isaac Royall Jr.](/wiki/Isaac_Royall_Jr.); it is the oldest continuously operating law school in the nation. It was a small operation and grew slowly. By 1827, it was down to one faculty member. [Nathan Dane](/wiki/Nathan_Dane), a prominent alumnus, endowed the Dane Professorship of Law and insisted that it be given to then Supreme Court Justice [Joseph Story](/wiki/Joseph_Story). For a while, the school was called Dane Law School. Story's belief in the need for an elite law school based on merit and dedicated to public service helped build the school's reputation at the time. Enrollment remained low as academic legal education was considered to be of little added benefit to apprenticeships in legal practice.

Radical reform came in the 1870s, under Dean [Christopher Columbus Langdell](/wiki/Christopher_Columbus_Langdell) (1826–1906). Its new curriculum set the national standard and was copied widely in the United States. Langdell developed the [case method](/wiki/Casebook_method) of teaching law, based on his belief that law could be studied as a "science" gave university legal education a reason for being distinct from vocational preparation. The school introduced a first-year curriculum that was widely imitated, based on classes in [contracts](/wiki/Contracts), [property](/wiki/Property), [torts](/wiki/Torts), [criminal law](/wiki/Criminal_law) and [civil procedure](/wiki/Civil_procedure).

Critics bemoaned abandonment of the more traditional lecture method, because of its efficiency and the lower workloads it placed on faculty and students. Advocates of the case method had a sounder theoretical basis in scientific research and the inductive method. Langdell's graduates became leading professors at other law schools where they introduced the case method. From its founding in 1900, the Association of American Law Schools promoted the case method in law schools that sought accreditation.

### Harvard Graduate School of Arts and Science

Main article: [Harvard Graduate School of Arts and Sciences](/wiki/Harvard_Graduate_School_of_Arts_and_Sciences)

As the college modernized in the late 19th century, the faculty was organized into departments and began to add graduate programs, including the PhD. [Charles William Eliot](/wiki/Charles_William_Eliot), president from 1869 to 1909, was a chemist who had spent two years in Germany studying at their universities. Thousands of Americans, mostly Harvard and Yale alumni, had attended German universities, especially Berlin and Göttingen. Eliot used the German model to set up graduate programs at Harvard and he formed a graduate department in 1872, which granted its first Ph.D. degrees in 1873 to [William Byerly](/wiki/William_Elwood_Byerly) in mathematics and Charles Whitney in history. Eliot set up the Graduate School of Arts and Sciences with its own dean and budget in 1890, which dealt with graduate students and funded research programs.

By 2004, there were 3,200 graduate students in 53 separate programs and forty former or current professors had won a Nobel Prize, most of them scientists or economists based in the Graduate School of Arts and Sciences.

### Harvard Business School

Main article: [Harvard Business School](/wiki/Harvard_Business_School)

Aerial view of [Harvard Business School](/wiki/Harvard_Business_School)

From its founding in 1908, [Harvard Business School](/wiki/Harvard_Business_School) has had a close relationship with the corporate world. Within a few years of its founding, many business leaders were its alumni and were hiring other alumni for starting positions in their firms. The school used Rockefeller funding in the 1920s to launch a major research program under [Elton Mayo](/wiki/Elton_Mayo) (1926–1947) for his "Harvard human relations group". Its findings revolutionized human relations in business and raised the reputation of the Business School from its initial "low status as a trainer of money grabbers into a high prestige educator of socially-conscientious administrators". Starting in 1935, the school began weekend and short-term leadership training workshops for executives of major corporations that further expanded its national role.

By 1949, nearly half of all the holders of the [MBA](/wiki/Master_of_Business_Administration) degree in the U.S. were alumni of Harvard Business School, and it was considered "the most influential graduate school of business".

### Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health

Main article: [Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health](/wiki/Harvard_T.H._Chan_School_of_Public_Health)

The Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health is the public health school of Harvard University, located in the [Longwood Medical Area](/wiki/Longwood_Medical_and_Academic_Area) of Boston. The school grew out of the Harvard-MIT School for Health Officers, the nation's first graduate training program in [population health](/wiki/Population_health), which was founded in 1913 and then became the Harvard School of Public Health in 1922.

The school was part of Harvard Medical School until 1946, when it became a fully autonomous institution with its own dedicated public health and medical faculty. It was renamed the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health in 2014 in honor of a $350 million donation from the Morningside Foundation.

### Harvard Kennedy School

Main article: [Harvard Kennedy School](/wiki/Harvard_Kennedy_School)

The Littauer Building at [Harvard Kennedy School](/wiki/Harvard_Kennedy_School)

In 1936, Harvard University founded the Harvard Graduate School of Public Administration, later renamed [Harvard Kennedy School](/wiki/Harvard_Kennedy_School) in honor of former [U.S. President](/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) and 1940 [Harvard College](/wiki/Harvard_College) alumnus [John F. Kennedy](/wiki/John_F._Kennedy).
The Kennedy School has an endowment of $1.7 billion as of 2021 and is routinely ranked at the top of the world's graduate schools in public policy, social policy, international affairs, and government.
Its alumni include 17 [heads of state](/wiki/Head_of_state) or [government](/wiki/Head_of_government).

### Research

Harvard is a founding member of the [Association of American Universities](/wiki/Association_of_American_Universities) and a preeminent research university with "very high" research activity (R1) and comprehensive doctoral programs across the arts, sciences, engineering, and medicine, according to the [Carnegie Classification](/wiki/Carnegie_Classification_of_Institutions_of_Higher_Education).

The [medical school](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School) consistently ranks first among medical schools for research, and biomedical research is an area of particular strength for the university. More than 11,000 faculty and 1,600 graduate students conduct research at the medical school and its 15 affiliated hospitals and research institutes. In 2019, the medical school and its affiliates attracted $1.65 billion in competitive research grants from the [National Institutes of Health](/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health), more than twice that of any other university.

### Libraries

Main article: [Harvard Library](/wiki/Harvard_Library)

[Widener Library](/wiki/Widener_Library), the anchor of [Harvard Library](/wiki/Harvard_Library), the largest [academic library](/wiki/Academic_library) in the world with more than 20 million holdings

[Harvard Library](/wiki/Harvard_Library), the largest [academic library](/wiki/Academic_library) in the world with 20.4 million holdings, is centered in [Widener Library](/wiki/Widener_Library) in [Harvard Yard](/wiki/Harvard_Yard). It includes 25 individual Harvard libraries around the world with a combined staff of more than 800 librarians and personnel.

[Houghton Library](/wiki/Houghton_Library), the Arthur and Elizabeth Schlesinger Library on the History of Women in America, and the Harvard University Archives consist principally of rare and unique materials. The nation's oldest collection of maps, gazetteers, and atlases is stored in [Pusey Library](/wiki/Pusey_Library) on [Harvard Yard](/wiki/Harvard_Yard), which is open to the public. The largest collection of [East-Asian](/wiki/East_Asia) language material outside of East Asia is held in [Harvard-Yenching Library](/wiki/Harvard-Yenching_Library).

Other major libraries in the Harvard Library system include [Baker Library/Bloomberg Center](/wiki/Baker_Library/Bloomberg_Center) at [Harvard Business School](/wiki/Harvard_Business_School), [Cabot Science Library](/wiki/Cabot_Science_Library) at [Harvard Science Center](/wiki/Harvard_Science_Center), [Dumbarton Oaks](/wiki/Dumbarton_Oaks) in [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.), [Gutman Library](/wiki/Monroe_C._Gutman_Library) at the [Harvard Graduate School of Education](/wiki/Harvard_Graduate_School_of_Education), [Harvard Film Archive](/wiki/Harvard_Film_Archive) at the [Carpenter Center for the Visual Arts](/wiki/Carpenter_Center_for_the_Visual_Arts), [Houghton Library](/wiki/Houghton_Library), and [Lamont Library](/wiki/Lamont_Library).

### Ice hockey

#### Men's ice hockey

Main article: [Harvard Crimson men's ice hockey](/wiki/Harvard_Crimson_men%27s_ice_hockey)

See also: [Cornell–Harvard hockey rivalry](/wiki/Cornell%E2%80%93Harvard_hockey_rivalry)

Harvard men's ice hockey team in 2023

The men's ice hockey team is one of the oldest [intercollegiate ice hockey](/wiki/College_ice_hockey) teams in the United States, having played their first game on January 19, 1898, in a 0–6 loss to [Brown](/wiki/Brown_Bears_men%27s_ice_hockey). Former head coach William H. Claflin and former captain George Owen are credited with the first use of [line change](/wiki/Line_(ice_hockey)) in a game against [Yale](/wiki/Yale_Bulldogs_men%27s_ice_hockey) on March 3, 1923, when the Crimson substituted entire forward lines instead of individuals. The men's ice hockey team won the [NCAA Division I Championship](/wiki/NCAA_Men%27s_Ice_Hockey_Championship#Division_I) on April 1, 1989, defeating the [Minnesota Golden Gophers](/wiki/Minnesota_Golden_Gophers_men%27s_ice_hockey) 4–3 in overtime. The [Cleary Cup](/wiki/ECAC_Hockey#Men's_ECAC_championship_games), awarded to the ECAC regular-season champion, is named for former Harvard [All-American](/wiki/All-America) hockey player, coach, and athletic director [Bill Cleary](/wiki/Bill_Cleary_(hockey)), a member of the [U.S. hockey team](/wiki/United_States_men%27s_national_ice_hockey_team) that won the [1960 Winter Olympics](/wiki/Ice_hockey_at_the_1960_Winter_Olympics) [gold medal](/wiki/Gold_medal). The team competes in [ECAC Hockey](/wiki/ECAC_Hockey) along with five other Ivy League schools and is coached by Harvard alumnus, [Olympian](/wiki/Ice_hockey_at_the_1992_Winter_Olympics), and former [NHL](/wiki/NHL) forward, [Ted Donato](/wiki/Ted_Donato). Harvard competes in one of the most heated rivalries of college hockey at least twice each season against Harvard's archrival, the [Cornell Big Red](/wiki/Cornell_Big_Red_men%27s_ice_hockey), in installments of the [Cornell–Harvard hockey rivalry](/wiki/Cornell%E2%80%93Harvard_hockey_rivalry). Cornell and Harvard are the most storied programs currently in the ECAC.

### National championships

In the NCAA Division I era, which began in 1973, Harvard Crimson teams have won five NCAA Division I championships as of 2024: [men's ice hockey](/wiki/Harvard_Crimson_men%27s_ice_hockey) in 1989, women's lacrosse in 1990, women's rowing in 2003, and men's fencing in 2006 and 2024. Including the pre-NCAA era, Harvard has won 159 national championships across all sports. Its [men's squash team](/wiki/Harvard_Crimson_men%27s_squash) holds the record for the most national collegiate championships in the sport. Harvard's first national championship came in 1880, when its track and field team won the national championship.

### Rivalries

Further information: [Cornell–Harvard hockey rivalry](/wiki/Cornell%E2%80%93Harvard_hockey_rivalry) and [Harvard–Yale football rivalry](/wiki/Harvard%E2%80%93Yale_football_rivalry)

Harvard's athletic programs maintain a long-standing rivalry with [Yale](/wiki/Yale_Bulldogs) in all sports, especially in [college football](/wiki/College_football), where [Harvard](/wiki/Harvard_Crimson_football) and [Yale](/wiki/Yale_Bulldogs_football) compete in an [annual football rivalry](/wiki/Harvard%E2%80%93Yale_football_rivalry), which has played 139 times as of 2024, dating back to its first meeting in 1875.

Every two years, Harvard and Yale track and field teams come together to compete against a combined [Oxford](/wiki/University_of_Oxford) and [Cambridge](/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) team in the oldest continuous international amateur competition in the world.

In [men's ice hockey](/wiki/Harvard_Crimson_men%27s_ice_hockey), Harvard maintains a [historic rivalry](/wiki/Cornell%E2%80%93Harvard_hockey_rivalry) with [Cornell](/wiki/Cornell_Big_Red_men%27s_ice_hockey), which dates back to their first meeting in 1910. The two teams play twice annually.

In [men's rugby](/wiki/Rugby_union), Harvard maintains a rivalry with [McGill](/wiki/McGill_University), as demonstrated by the biennial Harvard-McGill rugby games, alternately played in [Montreal](/wiki/Montreal) and Cambridge.

## Notable people

### Alumni

Further information: [List of Harvard University people](/wiki/List_of_Harvard_University_people), [List of Harvard University non-graduate alumni](/wiki/List_of_Harvard_University_non-graduate_alumni), and [List of Nobel laureates by university affiliation](/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates_by_university_affiliation)

Since its founding nearly four centuries ago, Harvard alumni have distinguished themselves in academia, activism, arts, athletics, business, entrepreneurship, government, international affairs, journalism, media, music, non-profit organizations, politics, public policy, science, technology, writing, and other industries and fields. A 2024 study analyzed the educational backgrounds of the most successful and influential Americans—"30 different achievement groups totaling 26,198 people"—and found that Harvard alumni were unusually dominant. A 2025 study of 6,141 of the most influential people in the world discovered that Harvard alumni are massively overrepresented among the global elite, and that this finding remains true when all American elites are removed.

Among the world's universities and colleges, Harvard has the most [U.S. presidents](/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_United_States_by_education) (eight), [living billionaires](/wiki/List_of_universities_by_number_of_billionaire_alumni) (188), [Nobel](/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates_by_university_affiliation) laureates (162), [Pulitzer Prize](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize) winners (48), [Fields Medal](/wiki/Fields_Medal) recipients (seven), [Marshall scholars](/wiki/Marshall_Scholarship) (252), and [Rhodes Scholars](/wiki/Rhodes_Scholarship) (369) among its alumni. Harvard alumni also include nine [Turing Award](/wiki/Turing_Award) laureates, ten [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Awards) winners, and [108 Olympic medalists](/wiki/List_of_American_universities_with_Olympic_medalist_students_and_alumni), including 46 gold medal winners.

## In popular culture

Tower at the [University of Puerto Rico](/wiki/University_of_Puerto_Rico), showing the emblem of Harvard (on right), the oldest in the United States, and that of [National University of San Marcos](/wiki/National_University_of_San_Marcos), Lima (left), the oldest in the [Americas](/wiki/Americas)

Harvard's reputation as a center of elite achievement or elitist privilege has made it a frequent literary and cinematic backdrop. "In the grammar of film, Harvard has come to mean both tradition, and a certain amount of stuffiness," film critic Paul Sherman said in 2010.

### Literature

In contemporary literature, Harvard University features prominently in multiple novels, including:

- *[The Sound and the Fury](/wiki/The_Sound_and_the_Fury)* (1929) and *[Absalom, Absalom!](/wiki/Absalom,_Absalom!)* (1936), two novels by [William Faulkner](/wiki/William_Faulkner), both of which depict Harvard student life.
- *[Of Time and the River](/wiki/Of_Time_and_the_River)* (1935) by [Thomas Wolfe](/wiki/Thomas_Wolfe), a fictionalized autobiography, depicting Wolfe's [alter ego](/wiki/Alter_ego), Eugene Gant, a Harvard student.
- *[The Late George Apley](/wiki/The_Late_George_Apley)* (1937), by 1915 Harvard alumnus [John P. Marquand](/wiki/John_P._Marquand), a novel presenting a satirical view of Harvard men in the early 20th century, which was awarded the [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction).
- *The Second Happiest Day* (1953), by John P. Marquand, portrays Harvard during the [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) generation.

### Films

Harvard University features prominently in the plots of multiple major films, including:

- *[Love Story](/wiki/Love_Story_(1970_film))* (1970), a romance between a wealthy [Harvard ice hockey](/wiki/Harvard_Crimson_men%27s_ice_hockey) player, played by [Ryan O'Neal](/wiki/Ryan_O%27Neal), and a brilliant Radcliffe student of modest means, played by [Ali MacGraw](/wiki/Ali_MacGraw).
- *[The Paper Chase](/wiki/The_Paper_Chase_(film))* (1973), a drama based on the 1971 [novel of the same name](/wiki/The_Paper_Chase_(Osborn_novel)) by Harvard alumnus [John Jay Osborn Jr.](/wiki/John_Jay_Osborn_Jr.), about a first year [Harvard Law School](/wiki/Harvard_Law_School) student facing a demanding [contract law](/wiki/Contract_law) course and professor.
- *[A Small Circle of Friends](/wiki/A_Small_Circle_of_Friends)* (1980), a drama about three Harvard University students in the 1960s
- *[Prozac Nation](/wiki/Prozac_Nation_(film))* (1994), a psychological drama starring [Christina Ricci](/wiki/Christina_Ricci) based on the [novel of the same name](/wiki/Prozac_Nation) by [Elizabeth Wurtzel](/wiki/Elizabeth_Wurtzel), which documents her real life story as a 19-year-old Harvard freshman struggling with [substance abuse](/wiki/Substance_abuse) and [clinical depression](/wiki/Major_depressive_disorder).
- *[Legally Blonde](/wiki/Legally_Blonde)* (2001), a comedy film starring [Reese Witherspoon](/wiki/Reese_Witherspoon) a blonde [sorority](/wiki/Sorority) girl who enrolls in [Harvard Law School](/wiki/Harvard_Law_School) to get her ex-boyfriend back.
- *[Homeless to Harvard: The Liz Murray Story](/wiki/Homeless_to_Harvard:_The_Liz_Murray_Story)* (2003), a [Lifetime](/wiki/Lifetime_(TV_channel)) biographical [television film](/wiki/Television_film), which chronicles the real life story of [Liz Murray](/wiki/Liz_Murray) (played by [Thora Birch](/wiki/Thora_Birch)), who overcomes homelessness and a dysfunctional family to gain entry and a scholarship to Harvard after winning a *[New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*-sponsored essay competition.
- *[The Social Network](/wiki/The_Social_Network)* (2010), a biographical drama film which portrays the founding of social networking website [Facebook](/wiki/Facebook).
