**Bactrian** (Bactrian: Αριαο, romanized: *ariao* [[arjaː]](/wiki/Help:IPA), meaning "Iranian") was an [Eastern Iranian language](/wiki/Eastern_Iranian_language) formerly spoken in the [Central Asian](/wiki/Central_Asia) region of [Bactria](/wiki/Bactria) (some regions of present-day Afghanistan, Uzbekistan and Tajikistan) and used as the official language of the [Kushan](/wiki/Kushan_Empire) and the [Hephthalite](/wiki/Hephthalite_Empire) empires.

## Name

It was long thought that [Avestan](/wiki/Avestan_language) represented "Old Bactrian", but this notion had "rightly fallen into discredit by the end of the 19th century".

Bactrian, which was written predominantly in an alphabet based on the [Greek script](/wiki/Greek_script), was known natively as αριαο [arjaː] ("[Arya](/wiki/Arya)"; an [endonym](/wiki/Endonym) common amongst Indo-Iranian peoples). It has also been known by names such as Greco-Bactrian or Kushan or Kushano-Bactrian.

Under Kushan rule, Bactria became known as *Tukhara* or *Tokhara*, and later as *[Tokharistan](/wiki/Tokharistan)*. When texts in two extinct and [previously unknown Indo-European languages](/wiki/Tocharian_languages) were discovered in the [Tarim Basin](/wiki/Tarim_Basin) of China, during the early 20th century, they were linked circumstantially to Tokharistan, and Bactrian was sometimes referred to as "Eteo-Tocharian" (i.e. "true" or "original" Tocharian). By the 1970s, however, it became clear that there was little evidence for such a connection. For instance, the Tarim "Tocharian" languages were "[centum](/wiki/Centum)" languages within the Indo-European family, whereas Bactrian was an Iranian, thus "[satem](/wiki/Satem)" language.

## Classification

Bactrian is a part of the [Eastern Iranian languages](/wiki/Eastern_Iranian_languages) and shares features with the extinct Middle Iranian languages [Sogdian](/wiki/Sogdian_language) and [Khwarezmian](/wiki/Khwarezmian_language) (Eastern) and [Parthian](/wiki/Parthian_language) ([Western](/wiki/Western_Iranian)), as well as sharing affinity with the modern Eastern Iranian languages such as [Pashto](/wiki/Pashto) and the [Pamir](/wiki/Pamir_languages) subgroup of languages like [Munji](/wiki/Munji_language) and [Yidgha](/wiki/Yidgha_language) which are part of the same branch of the Pamir languages. Its genealogical position is unclear. According to another source, the present-day speakers of Munji, the modern Eastern Iranian language of the Munjan Valley in the [Kuran wa Munjan district](/wiki/Kuran_wa_Munjan_District) of the [Badakhshan province](/wiki/Badakhshan_Province) in northeast [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan), display the closest possible linguistic affinity with the Bactrian language.

## History

The [Rabatak inscription](/wiki/Rabatak_inscription) is an inscription written on a rock in the Bactrian language and the Greek script, which was found in 1993 at the site of [Rabatak](/wiki/Robatak,_Afghanistan), near [Surkh Kotal](/wiki/Surkh_Kotal) in [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan). The inscription relates to the rule of the [Kushan](/wiki/Kushan_Empire) emperor [Kanishka](/wiki/Kanishka), and gives remarkable clues on the genealogy of the Kushan dynasty.

Bactrian became the [lingua franca](/wiki/Lingua_franca) of the Kushan Empire and the region of Bactria, replacing the Greek language. Bactrian was used by successive rulers in Bactria, until the arrival of the [Umayyad Caliphate](/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate).

### Historical development

Following the conquest of Bactria by [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) in 323 BC, for about two centuries [Greek](/wiki/Greek_language) was the administrative language of his [Hellenistic](/wiki/Hellenistic) successors, that is, the [Seleucid](/wiki/Seleucid) and the [Greco-Bactrian kingdoms](/wiki/Greco-Bactrian_kingdom).
Eastern [Scythian](/wiki/Scythians) tribes (the [Saka](/wiki/Saka), or Sacaraucae of Greek sources) invaded the territory around 140 BC, and at some time after 124 BC, Bactria was overrun by a confederation of tribes belonging to the [Great Yuezhi](/wiki/Great_Yuezhi) and [*Tokhari*](/wiki/Bactria#Daxia,_Tukhara_and_Tokharistan). In the 1st century AD, the Kushana, one of the Yuezhi tribes, founded the ruling dynasty of the [Kushan Empire](/wiki/Kushan_Empire).

The Kushan Empire initially retained the [Greek language](/wiki/Greek_language) for administrative purposes but soon began to use Bactrian. The Bactrian [Rabatak inscription](/wiki/Rabatak_inscription) (discovered in 1993 and deciphered in 2000) records that the Kushan king [Kanishka](/wiki/Kanishka) (c. 127 AD) discarded Greek ("Ionian") as the language of administration and adopted Bactrian ("Arya language"). The Greek language accordingly vanished from official use and only Bactrian was later attested. The [Greek script](/wiki/Greek_script), however, remained and was used to write Bactrian.
The territorial expansion of the Kushans helped propagate Bactrian in other parts of [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia) and [South Western Asia](/wiki/Pakistan).

In the 3rd century, the Kushan territories west of the [Indus River](/wiki/Indus_River) fell to the [Sasanians](/wiki/Sassanid_Empire), and Bactrian began to be influenced by [Middle Persian](/wiki/Middle_Persian). The eastern extent of the Kushan Empire in Northwestern India, was conquered by the [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire). Besides the [Pahlavi script](/wiki/Pahlavi_script) and the [Brahmi script](/wiki/Brahmi_script), some coinage of this period is still in the Aryo (Bactrian) script.

From the mid-4th century, Bactria and northwestern India gradually fell under the control the [Hephthalite](/wiki/Hephthalite) and other [Huna tribes](/wiki/Huna_people). The Hephthalite period is marked by linguistic diversity; in addition to Bactrian, Middle Persian, [Indo-Aryan](/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages) and [Latin](/wiki/Latin) vocabulary is also attested. The Hephthalites ruled these regions until the 7th century, when they were overrun by the [Umayyad Caliphate](/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate), after which official use of Bactrian ceased. Although Bactrian briefly survived in other usage, that also eventually ceased, and the latest known examples of the Bactrian script, found in the [Tochi Valley](/wiki/Tochi_Valley) in Pakistan, date to the end of the 9th century.

## Writing system

Bactrian was written using the [Greek script](/wiki/Greek_script), with the addition of the letter [sho](/wiki/Sho_(letter)) (majuscule: Ϸ, minuscule: ϸ) to represent the /ʃ/ sound.

Among Indo-Iranian languages, the use of the Greek script is unique to Bactrian. Although ambiguities remain, some of the disadvantages were overcome by using *[upsilon](/wiki/Upsilon)* (Υ, υ) for /h/ and by introducing an additional 25th letter [sho](/wiki/Sho_(letter)) (Ϸ, ϸ) to represent /ʃ/. *[Xi](/wiki/Xi_(letter))* (Ξ, ξ) and *[psi](/wiki/Psi_(letter))* (Ψ, ψ) were not used for writing Bactrian as the *ks* and *ps* sequences did not occur in Bactrian. They were, however, probably used to represent numbers (just as other Greek letters were). The Greek writing reproduced the Bactrian phonetics only approximately.

### Records

The word αλχοννο (*alkhonno*) in cursive Bactrian script, on a coin of the [Alchon Huns](/wiki/Alchon_Huns) ruler [Khingila](/wiki/Khingila), 5th century AD.

The Bactrian language is known from inscriptions, coins, seals, manuscripts, and other documents.

Sites at which Bactrian language inscriptions have been found are (in north–south order) [Afrasiyab](/wiki/Afrasiyab_(Samarkand)) in [Uzbekistan](/wiki/Uzbekistan); [Kara-Tepe](/wiki/Termez), Airtam, [Delbarjin](/wiki/Delbarjin), [Balkh](/wiki/Balkh), [Kunduz](/wiki/Kunduz), [Baglan](/wiki/Baghlan), [Ratabak/Surkh Kotal](/wiki/Surkh_Kotal), [Oruzgan](/wiki/Oruzgan), [Kabul](/wiki/Kabul), Dasht-e Navur, [Ghazni](/wiki/Ghazni), Jagatu in [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan); and [Islamabad](/wiki/Islamabad), [Shatial Bridge](/wiki/Shatial) and [Tochi Valley](/wiki/Tochi_Valley) in [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan).
 Of eight known manuscript fragments in Greco-Bactrian script, one is from [Lou-lan](/wiki/Loulan) and seven from Toyoq, where they were discovered by the second and third [Turpan](/wiki/Turpan) expeditions under [Albert von Le Coq](/wiki/Albert_von_Le_Coq). One of these may be a [Buddhist](/wiki/Buddhist) text. One other manuscript, in [Manichaean script](/wiki/Manichaean_alphabet), was found at [Qočo](/wiki/Gaochang) by [Mary Boyce](/wiki/Mary_Boyce) in 1958.

Over 150 legal documents, accounts, letters and Buddhist texts have surfaced since the 1990s, the largest collection of which is the [Khalili Collection of Aramaic Documents](/wiki/Khalili_Collection_of_Aramaic_Documents). These have greatly increased the detail in which Bactrian is currently known.

## Phonology

The [Surkh Kotal](/wiki/Surkh_Kotal) inscription (SK4) is the first known substantial document written in Bactrian, an Iranian language. It uses the [Greek script](/wiki/Greek_script). It was written at the time of the [Kushan](/wiki/Kushan_Empire) ruler [Huvishka](/wiki/Huvishka), 2nd century CE. [Kabul Museum](/wiki/Kabul_Museum).

The phonology of Bactrian is not known with certainty, owing to the limitations of the native scripts, and also its status as an extinct language.

A major difficulty in determining Bactrian phonology is that affricates and voiced stops were not consistently distinguished from the corresponding fricatives in the Greek script.

- [Proto-Iranian](/wiki/Proto-Iranian) \*b, \*d, \*g have generally become spirants, as in most other Eastern Iranian languages. A distinctive feature of Bactrian, shared within the Iranian languages with Munji, Yidgha and Pashto, is the development of Proto-Iranian \*d > \*ð further to /l/, which may have been areal in nature. Original \*d remains only in a few consonant clusters, e.g. \*bandaka > βανδαγο 'servant', \*dugdā > λογδο 'daughter'. The clusters /lr/ and /rl/ appear in earlier Bactrian, but revert to /dr/, /rd/ later, e.g. \*drauga > λρωγο (4th to 5th century) > δδρωρο (7th to 8th century) 'lie, falsehood'.
- Proto-Iranian \*p, \*t, \*č, \*k have become voiced between vowels, and after a nasal consonant or \*r.
  - Inside a word, the digraphs ββ, δδ for original voiceless \*p, \*t can be found, which probably represent [b], [d]. The former is attested only in a single word, αββο 'water'. Manichaean Bactrian appears to only have had /v/ in native vocabulary. According to Gholami, instances of single δ may indicate a fricative pronunciation, [ð].
  - γ appears to stand for both the stop [ɡ] and the fricative [ɣ], but it is unclear if a contrast existed, and which instances are which. Evidence from the Manichaean script suggests that γ from \*k may have been /ɡ/ and γ from \*g may have been /ɣ/. According to Greek orthographic practices, γγ represents [ŋɡ].
- σ may continue both Proto-Iranian \*c > \*s and \*č, and the Manichaean script confirms that it represents two phonemes, likely /s/ and /ts/.
- ζ may continue similarly on one hand Proto-Iranian \*dz > \*z, and on the other \*ǰ and \*č, and it represents at least /z/ and /dz/. This distinction is again confirmed by the Manichaean script. Also a third counterpart of ζ is found in Manichaean Bactrian, possibly representing /ʒ/.

The status of θ is unclear; it only appears in the word ιθαο 'thus, also', which may be a loanword from another Iranian language. In most positions Proto-Iranian \*θ becomes /h/ (written υ), or is lost, e.g. \*puθra- > πουρο 'son'. The cluster \*θw, however, appears to become /lf/, e.g. \*wikāθwan > οιγαλφο 'witness'.

ϸ continues, in addition to Proto-Iranian \*š, also Proto-Iranian \*s in the clusters \*sr, \*str, \*rst. In several cases, however, Proto-Iranian \*š becomes /h/ or is lost; the distribution is unclear. E.g. \*snušā > ασνωυο 'daughter-in-law', \*aštā > αταο 'eight', \*xšāθriya > χαρο 'ruler', \*pašman- > παμανο 'wool'.

The Greek script does not consistently represent vowel length. Fewer vowel contrasts yet are found in the Manichaean script, but short /a/ and long /aː/ are distinguished in it, suggesting that Bactrian generally retains the Proto-Iranian vowel length contrast.

It is not clear if ο might represent short [o] in addition to [u], and if any contrast existed. Short [o] may have occurred at least as a reflex of \*a followed by a lost \*u in the next syllable, e.g. \*madu > μολο 'wine', \*pasu > ποσο 'sheep'. Short [e] is also rare. By contrast, long /eː/, /oː/ are well established as reflexes of Proto-Iranian diphthongs and certain vowel-semivowel sequences: η < \*ai, \*aya, \*iya; ω < \*au, \*awa.

An epenthetic vowel [ə] (written α) is inserted before word-initial [consonant clusters](/wiki/Consonant_cluster).

Original word-final vowels and word-initial vowels in open syllables were generally lost. A word-final ο is normally written, but this was probably silent, and it is appended even after retained word-final vowels: e.g. \*aštā > αταο 'eight', likely pronounced /ataː/.

The Proto-Iranian syllabic rhotic \*r̥ is lost in Bactrian, and is reflected as ορ adjacent to labial consonants, ιρ elsewhere; this agrees with the development in the western Iranian languages [Parthian](/wiki/Parthian_language) and [Middle Persian](/wiki/Middle_Persian).
