**Oak Ridge National Laboratory** (**ORNL**) is a [federally funded research and development center](/wiki/Federally_funded_research_and_development_centers) in [Oak Ridge, Tennessee](/wiki/Oak_Ridge,_Tennessee), United States. Founded in 1943, the laboratory is sponsored by the [United States Department of Energy](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy) and administered by [UT–Battelle, LLC](/wiki/UT%E2%80%93Battelle).

Established in 1943, ORNL is the largest science and energy national laboratory in the Department of Energy system by size and third largest by annual budget. It is located in the [Roane County](/wiki/Roane_County,_Tennessee) section of Oak Ridge. Its scientific programs focus on [materials](/wiki/Materials_science), [nuclear science](/wiki/Nuclear_power), [neutron](/wiki/Neutron) science, energy, [high-performance computing](/wiki/High-performance_computing), [environmental science](/wiki/Environmental_science), [systems biology](/wiki/Systems_biology) and [national security](/wiki/National_security), sometimes in partnership with the state of [Tennessee](/wiki/Tennessee), universities and other industries.

ORNL has several of the world's top [supercomputers](/wiki/Supercomputers), including [Frontier](/wiki/Frontier_(supercomputer)), ranked by the [TOP500](/wiki/TOP500) as the world's second most powerful. The lab is a neutron and nuclear power research facility that includes the [Spallation Neutron Source](/wiki/Spallation_Neutron_Source), the [High Flux Isotope Reactor](/wiki/High_Flux_Isotope_Reactor), and the [Center for Nanophase Materials Sciences](/wiki/Center_for_Nanophase_Materials_Sciences).

## Overview

Oak Ridge National Laboratory is managed by [UT–Battelle](/wiki/UT%E2%80%93Battelle), a [limited liability partnership](/wiki/Limited_liability_partnership) between the [University of Tennessee](/wiki/University_of_Tennessee) and the [Battelle Memorial Institute](/wiki/Battelle_Memorial_Institute), formed in 2000 for that purpose. The annual budget is US$2.4 billion. As of 2021 there is a staff of 5,700 working at ORNL, around 2,000 of whom are scientists and engineers, and an additional 3,200 guest researchers annually.

There are five campuses on the Department of Energy's Oak Ridge reservation: the National Laboratory, the [Y-12 National Security Complex](/wiki/Y-12_National_Security_Complex), the East Tennessee Technology Park (formerly the [Oak Ridge Gaseous Diffusion Plant](/wiki/K-25)), the [Oak Ridge Institute for Science and Education](/wiki/Oak_Ridge_Institute_for_Science_and_Education), and the developing Oak Ridge Science and Technology Park, although the four other facilities are unrelated to the National Laboratory. The total area of the reservation is 150 square kilometres (58 sq mi) of which the lab takes up 18 square kilometres (7 sq mi).

## History

Workers in 1943 loading uranium slugs into the [X-10 Graphite Reactor](/wiki/X-10_Graphite_Reactor) (now a National Historic Landmark)

In 1934 the [Freel Farm Mound Site](/wiki/Freel_Farm_Mound_Site), an archaeological site and burial mound of the [Late Woodland period](/wiki/Late_Woodland_period) was excavated. The site is currently inundated by [Melton Hill Lake](/wiki/Melton_Hill_Lake).

The city of [Oak Ridge](/wiki/Oak_Ridge,_Tennessee) was established by the [Army Corps of Engineers](/wiki/United_States_Army_Corps_of_Engineers) as part of the [Clinton Engineer Works](/wiki/Clinton_Engineer_Works) in 1942 on isolated farm land as part of the [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project). During World War II, advanced research for the government was managed at the site by the [University of Chicago](/wiki/University_of_Chicago)'s [Metallurgical Laboratory](/wiki/Metallurgical_Laboratory). In 1943, construction of the Clinton Laboratories, what would later be known as the Oak Ridge National Laboratory, was completed. The site was chosen for the [X-10 Graphite Reactor](/wiki/X-10_Graphite_Reactor), used to produce [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium) from natural [uranium](/wiki/Uranium). [Enrico Fermi](/wiki/Enrico_Fermi) and his colleagues developed the world's second self-sustaining [nuclear reactor](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor) after Fermi's previous experiment, the [Chicago Pile-1](/wiki/Chicago_Pile-1). The X-10 was the first reactor designed for continuous operation.

After the end of World War II, management of the lab was contracted by the US government to [Monsanto](/wiki/Monsanto); however, they withdrew in 1947. The [University of Chicago](/wiki/University_of_Chicago) temporarily re-assumed responsibility, with the site receiving the prestigious "National" laboratory designation, until in December 1947, when [Union Carbide and Carbon Co.](/wiki/Union_Carbide), which already operated two other facilities at Oak Ridge, took control of the laboratory and renamed the site Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL).

Post-war, the demand for [military science](/wiki/Military_science) had fallen dramatically, and the future of the lab was uncertain. The X-10 reactor and the laboratory's 1,000 employees were no longer involved in nuclear weapons. Instead, it was used for scientific research. In 1946 the first [medical isotopes](/wiki/Isotopes_in_medicine) were produced in the X-10 reactor, and by 1950 almost 20,000 samples had been shipped to various hospitals. The quantity and variety of [radionuclides](/wiki/Radionuclide) produced by X-10 for medicine grew steadily in the 1950s. ORNL was the only Western source of [californium-252](/wiki/Californium-252). ORNL scientists also performed the world's first successful [bone marrow transplant](/wiki/Bone_marrow_transplant) in mice by suppressing their [immune systems](/wiki/Immune_system).

The core of the [Molten Salt Reactor Experiment](/wiki/Molten_Salt_Reactor_Experiment)

In 1950 the [Oak Ridge School of Reactor Technology](/wiki/Oak_Ridge_School_of_Reactor_Technology) was established with two courses in reactor operation and safety; almost 1,000 students graduated. Much of the research performed at ORNL in the 1950s was related to nuclear reactors as a form of energy production, both for propulsion and electricity. More reactors were built in the 1950s than in the rest of the ORNL's history combined. One of their most influential projects was the [light-water reactor](/wiki/Light-water_reactor), a precursor to many modern nuclear power stations. The [US Military](/wiki/United_States_Armed_Forces) funded much of its development, for [nuclear-powered submarines and ships](/wiki/Nuclear_marine_propulsion) of the [US Navy](/wiki/US_Navy). The [US Army](/wiki/US_Army) also contracted the design of portable nuclear reactors in 1953 for heat and electricity generation in remote military bases. The reactors were produced by the [American Locomotive Company](/wiki/American_Locomotive_Company) and used in [Greenland](/wiki/Greenland), the [Panama Canal Zone](/wiki/Panama_Canal_Zone), and [Antarctica](/wiki/Antarctica). The [US Air Force](/wiki/United_States_Air_Force) also contributed funding to three reactors, the lab's first computers, and its first particle accelerators. ORNL built its [first molten salt reactor in 1954](/wiki/Aircraft_Reactor_Experiment) as a proof-of-concept for a proposed fleet of [long-range bombers](/wiki/Strategic_bomber), but it was never used.

Cayce Pentecost, [Lyndon B. Johnson](/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson), [Buford Ellington](/wiki/Buford_Ellington) and [Albert Gore Sr.](/wiki/Albert_Gore_Sr.) operating mechanical hands at a hot cell at Oak Ridge, on October 19, 1958

Alvin M. Weinberg was named Director of Research, ORNL, and in 1955 Director of the Laboratory. In the early 1960s there was a large push at ORNL to develop nuclear-powered [desalination](/wiki/Desalination) plants, where deserts met the sea, to provide water. The project, called Water for Peace, was backed by [John F. Kennedy](/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) and [Lyndon B. Johnson](/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) and was presented at a 1964 United Nations conference, but increases in the cost of construction and falling public confidence in nuclear power caused the plan to be shuttered. The Health Physics Research Reactor built in 1962 was used for [radiation exposure](/wiki/Radiation_exposure) experiments leading to more accurate [dosage limits](/wiki/Dosimetry) and [dosimeters](/wiki/Dosimeter), and improved [radiation shielding](/wiki/Radiation_protection).

In 1964 the [Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment](/wiki/Molten-Salt_Reactor_Experiment) began with the construction of the reactor. It operated from 1966 until 1969 (with six months down time to move from [U-235](/wiki/Uranium-235) to [U-233](/wiki/Uranium-233) fuel) and proved the viability of [molten salt reactors](/wiki/Molten_salt_reactor), while also producing fuel for other reactors as a byproduct of its own reaction. The [High Flux Isotope Reactor](/wiki/High_Flux_Isotope_Reactor) built in 1965 had the highest [neutron flux](/wiki/Neutron_flux) of any reactor at the time. It improved upon the work of the X-10 reactor, producing more medical isotopes as well as allowing higher fidelity of materials research. Researchers in the biology division studied the effects of chemicals on mice, including [petrol fumes](/wiki/Air_pollution), [pesticides](/wiki/Health_effects_of_pesticides), and [tobacco](/wiki/Health_effects_of_tobacco).

The inside of ORMAK, an early [tokamak](/wiki/Tokamak), was gold plated for reflectivity

In the late 1960s, cuts in funding led to the cancellation of plans for another particle accelerator, and the [United States Atomic Energy Commission](/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission) cut the breeder reactor program by two-thirds, leading to a downsizing in staff from 5,000 to 3,800. In the 1970s, the prospect of [fusion power](/wiki/Fusion_power) was strongly considered, sparking research at ORNL. A [tokamak](/wiki/Tokamak) called ORMAK, made operational in 1971, was the first tokamak to achieve a plasma temperature of 20 million Kelvin. After the success of the fusion experiments, it was enlarged and renamed ORMAK II in 1973; however, the experiments ultimately failed to lead to fusion power plants.

S.R. Sapirie, Senator [Albert Gore Sr.](/wiki/Albert_Gore_Sr.), Senator [Lyndon Johnson](/wiki/Lyndon_Johnson) and Dr.John Swartout looking at a model of a graphite reactor at [Oak Ridge National Lab](/wiki/Oak_Ridge_National_Lab), on October 19, 1958

The [US Atomic Energy Commission](/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission) (AEC) required improved safety standards in the early 1970s for nuclear reactors, so ORNL staff wrote almost 100 requirements covering many factors including fuel transport and earthquake resistance. In 1972 the AEC held a series of public hearings where emergency cooling requirements were highlighted and the safety requirements became more stringent. Also in 1972, [Peter Mazur](/wiki/Peter_Mazur), a biologist at ORNL, froze with [liquid nitrogen](/wiki/Liquid_nitrogen), thawed and implanted mouse [embryos](/wiki/Embryo) in a [surrogate mother](/wiki/Surrogate_mother). The mouse pups were born healthy. The technique is popular in the livestock industry, as it allows the embryos of valuable cattle to be transported easily and a prize cow can have multiple eggs extracted and thus, through [*in vitro* fertilisation](/wiki/In_vitro_fertilisation), have many more offspring than would naturally be possible.

In 1974 Alvin Weinberg, director of the lab for 19 years, was replaced by [Herman Postma](/wiki/Herman_Postma), a fusion scientist. In 1977 construction began for 6 metre (20 foot) [superconducting](/wiki/Superconductivity) [electromagnets](/wiki/Electromagnet), intended to control [fusion reactions](/wiki/Nuclear_fusion). The project was an international effort: three electromagnets were produced in the US, one in Japan, one in [Switzerland](/wiki/Switzerland) and the final by remaining European states. ORNL was involved in analyzing the damage to the core of the [Three Mile Island Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_Nuclear_Generating_Station) after the [accident in 1979](/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident).

The 1980s brought more changes to ORNL: a focus on efficiency became paramount. An accelerated climate simulation chamber was built that applied varying weather conditions to insulation to test its efficacy and durability faster than real time. Materials research into heat resistant [ceramics](/wiki/Ceramic_engineering) for use in truck and high-tech car engines was performed, building upon the materials research that began in the nuclear reactors of the 1950s. In 1987 the High Temperature Materials Laboratory was established, where ORNL and industry researchers cooperated on ceramic and alloy projects. The materials research budget at ORNL doubled after initial uncertainty regarding [Reagan's economic policy](/wiki/Reaganomics) of less government expenditure. In 1981, the Holifield Heavy Ion Research Facility, a 25 MV [particle accelerator](/wiki/Particle_accelerator), was opened at ORNL. At the time, Holifield had the widest range of ion species and was twice as powerful as other accelerators, attracting hundreds of guest researchers each year.

The Department of Energy was concerned with the pollution surrounding ORNL, and it began clean-up efforts. Burial trenches and leaking pipes had [contaminated the groundwater](/wiki/Groundwater_pollution) beneath the lab, and radiation tanks were sitting idle, full of [waste](/wiki/Radioactive_waste). Estimates of the total cost of clean-up were into the hundreds of millions of US dollars. The five older reactors were subjected to safety reviews in 1987, ordered to be deactivated until the reviews were complete. By 1989 when the High Flux Isotope Reactor was restarted, the US supply of certain medical isotopes was depleted. In 1989 the former executive officer of the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science), [Alvin Trivelpiece](/wiki/Alvin_Trivelpiece), became director of ORNL; he remained in the role until 2000.

In 1992 [whistleblower](/wiki/Whistleblower) Charles Varnadore filed complaints against ORNL, alleging safety violations and retaliation by his superiors. While an administrative law judge ruled in Varnadore's favor, Secretary of Labor [Robert Reich](/wiki/Robert_Reich) overturned that ruling. However, Varnadore's case saw prime contractor [Martin Marietta](/wiki/Martin_Marietta) cited for safety violations and ultimately led to additional whistleblower protection within DOE.

In January 2019 ORNL announced a major breakthrough in its capacity to automate [Pu-238](/wiki/Plutonium-238) production which helped push annual production from 50 grams to 400 grams, moving closer to [NASA](/wiki/NASA)'s goal of 1.5 kilograms per year by 2025 in order to sustain its space exploration programs.

## Areas of research

ORNL conducts [research and development](/wiki/Research_and_development) activities that span a wide range of scientific disciplines. Many research areas have a significant overlap with each other; researchers often work in two or more of the fields listed here. The laboratory's major research areas are described briefly below.

- [Chemical sciences](/wiki/Chemistry): ORNL conducts both fundamental and applied research in numerous areas, including [catalysis](/wiki/Catalysis), [surface science](/wiki/Surface_science) and [interfacial chemistry](/wiki/Surface_chemistry); molecular transformations and fuel chemistry; heavy element chemistry and radioactive materials characterization; aqueous solution chemistry and [geochemistry](/wiki/Geochemistry); [mass spectrometry](/wiki/Mass_spectrometry) and laser spectroscopy; separations chemistry; materials chemistry including synthesis and characterization of [polymers](/wiki/Polymers) and other soft materials; chemical biosciences; and [neutron science](/wiki/Neutron_scattering).
- [Electron microscopy](/wiki/Electron_microscope): the program investigates key issues in [condensed matter](/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics), [materials](/wiki/Materials_science), chemical and [nanosciences](/wiki/Nanotechnology).
- [Nuclear medicine](/wiki/Nuclear_medicine): research is focused on the development of improved reactor production and processing methods to provide medical [radioisotopes](/wiki/Radiopharmacology), the development of new radionuclide generator systems, the design and evaluation of new radiopharmaceuticals for applications in nuclear medicine, and [oncology](/wiki/Oncology).
- [Physics](/wiki/Physics): research is focused primarily on studies of the fundamental properties of [matter](/wiki/Matter) at the [atomic](/wiki/Atom), [nuclear](/wiki/Atomic_nucleus), and [subnuclear](/wiki/Subatomic_particle) levels and the development of experimental devices in support of these studies.
- Population: ORNL provides federal, state and international organizations with a gridded population database, called Landscan, for estimating ambient population. LandScan is a [raster image](/wiki/Raster_graphics), or grid, of population counts, which provides human population estimates every 30 x 30 arc seconds, which translates roughly to population estimates for 1 kilometer square windows or grid cells at the Equator, with cell width decreasing at higher latitudes. Though many population datasets exist, LandScan is the best spatial population dataset, updated annually. Landscan data are accessible through [GIS](/wiki/GIS) applications and a [USAID](/wiki/USAID) public domain application called Population Explorer.

### Energy

The laboratory has a long history of energy research; nuclear reactor experiments have been conducted since the end of World War II in 1945. Because of the availability of reactors and high-performance computing resources, an emphasis on improving the efficiency of nuclear reactors is present. The programs develop more efficient materials, more accurate simulations of aging reactor cores, sensors and controls as well as safety procedures for regulatory authorities.

The Energy Efficiency and Electricity Technologies Program aims to improve [air quality in the US](/wiki/Air_pollution_in_the_United_States) and reduce dependence on foreign oil supplies. There are three key areas of research: electricity, manufacturing and mobility. The electricity division focuses on reducing electricity consumption and finding alternative sources for production. Buildings, which account for 39% of US electricity consumption as of 2012, are a key area of research as the program aims to create affordable, [carbon-neutral](/wiki/Carbon-neutral) homes. Research also takes place into higher efficiency [solar panels](/wiki/Solar_panel), [geothermal electricity](/wiki/Geothermal_power) and [heating](/wiki/Geothermal_heating), lower cost [wind generators](/wiki/Wind_turbine), and the economic and environmental feasibility of potential [hydro power plants](/wiki/Hydroelectricity).

The Fusion Energy Division pursues short-term goals to develop components such as [high-temperature superconductors](/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity), high-speed hydrogen pellet injectors, and suitable materials for future fusion research. Much research into the behaviour and maintenance of plasma takes place at the Fusion Energy Division to further the understanding of [plasma physics](/wiki/Plasma_(physics)), a crucial area for developing a fusion power plant. The US [ITER](/wiki/ITER) office is at ORNL with partners at [Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory](/wiki/Princeton_Plasma_Physics_Laboratory) and [Savannah River National Laboratory](/wiki/Savannah_River_National_Laboratory). The US contribution to the ITER project is 9.1% which is expected to be in excess of US$1.6 billion throughout the contract. ORNL researchers participated in developing of an extensive research plan for the US-ITER collaboration detailed in 2022.

### Biology

Biological research covers [ecology](/wiki/Ecology), [forestry](/wiki/Forestry), [genomics](/wiki/Genomics), [computational biology](/wiki/Computational_biology), [structural biology](/wiki/Structural_biology) and [bioinformatics](/wiki/Bioinformatics). The BioEnergy Program aims to improve the efficiency of all stages of the [biofuel](/wiki/Biofuel) process to improve the [energy security](/wiki/Energy_security) of the United States. The program aims to make genetic improvements to the potential biomass used, formulate methods for refineries that can accept a diverse range of fuels, and to improve the efficiency of energy delivery both to power plants and end users.

The Center for Molecular Biophysics conducts research into the behaviour of biological molecules in various conditions. The center hosts projects that examine [cell walls](/wiki/Cell_walls) for biofuel production, use neutron scattering to analyze [protein folding](/wiki/Protein_folding), and simulate the effect of catalysis on a conventional and [quantum scale](/wiki/Quantum_mechanics). ORNL is home to a field site for the [National Ecological Observatory Network](/wiki/National_Ecological_Observatory_Network) (NEON), which has a field office nearby. The Department of Energy works closely with the [Tennessee Wildlife Resources Agency](/wiki/Tennessee_Wildlife_Resources_Agency) out of ORNL to monitor forest ecology for the surrounding Appalachians & Cumberland Plateau Domain of NEON.

### Neutron science

There are two neutron sources at ORNL; the [High Flux Isotope Reactor](/wiki/High_Flux_Isotope_Reactor) (HFIR) and the [Spallation Neutron Source](/wiki/Spallation_Neutron_Source) (SNS). HFIR provides neutrons in a stable beam resulting from a constant [nuclear reaction](/wiki/Research_reactor) whereas SNS, a particle accelerator, produces pulses of neutrons. HFIR went [critical](/wiki/Critical_mass) in 1965 and has been used for materials research and as a major source of medical radioisotopes since. As of 2013, HFIR provides the world's highest constant [neutron flux](/wiki/Neutron_flux) as a result of various upgrades. [Berkelium](/wiki/Berkelium)-249, used to synthesize [tennessine](/wiki/Tennessine) for the first time, was produced in the HFIR as part of an international effort. HFIR is likely to operate until approximately 2060 before the [reactor pressure vessel](/wiki/Reactor_pressure_vessel) is considered unsafe for continued use.

The SNS has the highest intensity neutron pulses of any human-made neutron source. SNS was made operational in 2006 and has since been upgraded to 1 megawatt with plans to continue up to 3 MW. High-power neutron pulses permit clearer images of the targets, meaning smaller samples can be analyzed and accurate results require fewer pulses.

### Materials

The Advanced Microscopy Laboratory at ORNL

Between 2002 and 2008 ORNL partnered with [Caterpillar Inc.](/wiki/Caterpillar_Inc.) to develop a new steel for their diesel engines that can withstand large temperature fluctuations. The new material, named CF8C Plus, is based on conventional CF8C stainless steel with added [manganese](/wiki/Manganese) and [nitrogen](/wiki/Nitrogen); the result has better high–temperature properties and is easier to cast at a similar cost. In 2003 the partners received an R&D 100 award from *R&D magazine* and in 2009 received an award for "excellence in technology transfer" from the [Federal Laboratory Consortium](/wiki/Federal_Laboratory_Consortium) for the commercialization of the steel.

There is a high-temperature materials lab at ORNL that permits researchers from universities, private companies and other government initiatives to use their facilities. As is the case for all designated user facilities, the resources of the High Temperature Materials Laboratory are available for free if the results are published; private research is permitted but requires payment.

The Center for Nanophase Materials Sciences (CNMS) researches the behaviour and fabrication of [nanomaterials](/wiki/Nanomaterials). The center emphasises discovery of new materials and the understanding of underlying physical and chemical interactions that enable creation of nanomaterials. In 2012, CNMS produced a lithium-sulfide battery with a theoretical energy density three to five times greater than existing [lithium ion batteries](/wiki/Lithium_ion_batteries).

### Security

ORNL provides resources to the [United States Department of Homeland Security](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Homeland_Security) and other defense programs. The Global Security and Nonproliferation (GS&N) program develops and implements policies, both US based and international, to prevent the [proliferation of nuclear material](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation). The program has developed safeguards for nuclear arsenals, guidelines for dismantling arsenals, plans of action should nuclear material fall into unauthorised hands, detection methods for stolen or missing nuclear material, and trade of nuclear material between the US and Russia. The GS&N's work overlaps with that of the Homeland Security Programs Office, providing detection of nuclear material and nonproliferation guidelines. Other areas concerning the Department Homeland Security include nuclear and radiological forensics, [chemical](/wiki/Chemical_weapon) and [biological agent](/wiki/Biological_agent) detection using [mass spectrometry](/wiki/Mass_spectrometry), and simulations of potential national hazards.

### High-performance computing

[Summit](/wiki/Summit_(supercomputer)), developed at ORNL, was the world's fastest [supercomputer](/wiki/Supercomputer) from November 2018 to June 2020.

ORNL has been the site of various [supercomputers](/wiki/Supercomputer), home to the fastest on several occasions. In 1953, ORNL partnered with the [Argonne National Laboratory](/wiki/Argonne_National_Laboratory) to build [ORACLE](/wiki/ORACLE_(computer)) (Oak Ridge Automatic Computer and Logical Engine), a computer to research nuclear physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering. ORACLE had 2048 [words](/wiki/Word_(computer_architecture)) (80 [Kibit](/wiki/Kibibit)) of [memory](/wiki/Random-access_memory) and took approximately 590 microseconds to perform addition or multiplication of integers. In the 1960s ORNL was equipped with an [IBM 360/91](/wiki/IBM_System/360) and an IBM 360/65. In 1995 ORNL bought an [Intel Paragon](/wiki/Intel_Paragon) based computer called the *Intel Paragon XP/S 150* that performed at 154 [gigaFLOPS](/wiki/FLOPS) and ranked third on the [TOP500](/wiki/TOP500) list of supercomputers. In 2005 [Jaguar](/wiki/Jaguar_(supercomputer)) was built, a [Cray XT3](/wiki/Cray_XT3)-based system that performed at 25 teraFLOPS and received incremental upgrades up to the [XT5](/wiki/Cray_XT5) platform that performed at 2.3 petaFLOPS in 2009. It was recognised as the world's fastest from November 2009 until November 2010. Summit was built for Oak Ridge National Laboratory during 2018, which benchmarked at 122.3 petaFLOPS. As of June 2020, Summit was the world's second fastest [clocked] supercomputer with 202,752 CPU cores, 27,648 [Nvidia Tesla GPUs](/wiki/Nvidia_Tesla), and 250 Petabytes of storage, having lost the top position to the Japanese [Fugaku](/wiki/Fugaku_(supercomputer)) supercomputer. In May 2022, the ORNL [Frontier](/wiki/Frontier_(supercomputer)) system broke the exascale barrier, achieving 1.102 exaflop/s using 8,730,112 cores.

Since 1992 the [Center for Computational Sciences](/wiki/National_Center_for_Computational_Sciences) has overseen high performance computing at ORNL. It manages the [Oak Ridge Leadership Computing Facility](/wiki/Oak_Ridge_Leadership_Computing_Facility) that contains the machines. In 2012, Jaguar was upgraded to the [XK7](/wiki/Cray_XK7) platform, a fundamental change as [GPUs](/wiki/GPU) are used for the majority of processing, and renamed [Titan](/wiki/Titan_(supercomputer)). Titan performed at 17.59 petaFLOPS and held the number 1 spot on the TOP500 list for November 2012. Other computers include a 77 node cluster to visualise data that the larger machines output in the *Exploratory Visualization Environment for Research in Science and Technology* (EVEREST), a visualisation room with a 10 by 3 metre (30 by 10 ft) wall that displays 35 megapixel projections. Smoky is an 80 node Linux cluster used for application development. Research projects are refined and tested on Smoky before running on larger machines such as Titan.

In 1989 programmers at the Oak Ridge National Lab wrote the first version of [Parallel Virtual Machine](/wiki/Parallel_Virtual_Machine) (PVM), software that enables [distributed computing](/wiki/Distributed_computing) on machines of differing specifications. PVM is [free software](/wiki/Free_software) and has become the de facto standard for distributed computing. [Jack Dongarra](/wiki/Jack_Dongarra) of ORNL and the [University of Tennessee](/wiki/University_of_Tennessee) wrote the [LINPACK](/wiki/LINPACK) software library and [LINPACK benchmarks](/wiki/LINPACK_benchmarks), used to calculate [linear algebra](/wiki/Linear_algebra) and the standard method of measuring floating point performance of a supercomputer as used by the TOP500 organisation.
