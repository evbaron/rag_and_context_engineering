# Silly Symphony

## Infobox

- **Production company:** Walt Disney Productions
- **Distributed by:** Columbia Pictures (1929–32) United Artists (1932–37) RKO Radio Pictures (1937–39)
- **Country:** United States
- **Language:** English

***Silly Symphony*** (also known as ***Silly Symphonies***) is an American [animated](/wiki/Animation) series of 75 musical [short films](/wiki/Short_film) produced by [Walt Disney Productions](/wiki/Walt_Disney_Animation_Studios) from 1929 to 1939. As the series name implies, the *Silly Symphonies* were originally intended as whimsical accompaniments to pieces of music. As such, the films usually did not feature continuing characters, unlike the *[Mickey Mouse](/wiki/Mickey_Mouse_(film_series))* shorts produced by Disney at the same time (exceptions to this include *[Three Little Pigs](/wiki/Three_Little_Pigs_(film))*, *[The Tortoise and the Hare](/wiki/The_Tortoise_and_the_Hare_(film))*, and *[Three Orphan Kittens](/wiki/Three_Orphan_Kittens)*, which all had sequels). The series is notable for its innovation with [Technicolor](/wiki/Technicolor) and the [multiplane motion picture camera](/wiki/Multiplane_camera), as well as its introduction of the character [Donald Duck](/wiki/Donald_Duck), who made his first appearance in the *Silly Symphony* cartoon *[The Wise Little Hen](/wiki/The_Wise_Little_Hen)* in 1934. Seven shorts won the [Academy Award for Best Animated Short Film](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Animated_Short_Film).

Many of the shorts were adapted into print mediums. Starting in 1932, a [*Silly Symphony* newspaper comic strip](/wiki/Silly_Symphony_(comic_strip)) was distributed by [King Features Syndicate](/wiki/King_Features_Syndicate), as well as a [Dell](/wiki/Dell_Comics) comic book series and numerous children's books.

The *Silly Symphonies* returned to theaters with its re-issues and re-releases, and tied with [Joseph Barbera](/wiki/Joseph_Barbera) and [William Hanna](/wiki/William_Hanna)'s *[Tom and Jerry](/wiki/Tom_and_Jerry)*'s record for most Oscar wins for a cartoon series in the [Academy Award for Best Animated Short Film](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Animated_Short_Film) category.

The first five *Silly Symphony* shorts entered the [public domain on January 1, 2025](/wiki/2025_in_public_domain) with another 10 on [January 1, 2026](/wiki/2026_in_public_domain). The same will happen to subsequent shorts from 1931, 1932, and 1933 in [2027](/wiki/2027_in_public_domain), [2028](/wiki/2028_in_public_domain), and [2029](/wiki/2029_in_public_domain) respectively.

## Production

1935 series poster

While [Walt Disney](/wiki/Walt_Disney) and [Carl Stalling](/wiki/Carl_W._Stalling), a theatre organist from Kansas City, were in New York to add sound to the Mickey Mouse shorts *[The Gallopin' Gaucho](/wiki/The_Gallopin%27_Gaucho)*, *[The Barn Dance](/wiki/The_Barn_Dance)* and *[Plane Crazy](/wiki/Plane_Crazy)*, Stalling suggested the idea of making a series of musical animated shorts that combined the latest sound technology with storytelling. At first Walt did not seem interested, but when they returned to New York in February to record the sound for a fifth Mickey Mouse cartoon, *[The Opry House](/wiki/The_Opry_House)*, they also recorded the soundtrack for *[The Skeleton Dance](/wiki/The_Skeleton_Dance)*, the type of short that Stalling had suggested and the first Silly Symphony cartoon.

Within the animation industry, the series is known for its use by [Walt Disney](/wiki/Walt_Disney) as a platform for experimenting with processes, techniques, characters, and stories in order to further the art of animation. What *The Skeleton Dance* and Silly Symphonies did differently than any other cartoons that had been made previosly in regard of sound, was that the music and sounds were recorded before the actual animated segments, instead of the other way around. The series also provided a venue to try out techniques and technologies, such as Technicolor, [special effects animation](/wiki/Special_effects_animation), and dramatic storytelling in animation, that would be crucial to Disney's plans to eventually begin making feature-length animated films.

Shortly after the switch to United Artists, the series became even more popular. Walt Disney had seen some of Dr. Herbert Kalmus' tests for a new [three-strip](/wiki/Three-strip_Technicolor), full-color [Technicolor](/wiki/Technicolor) process, which would replace the previous [two-tone Technicolor](/wiki/Two-strip_Technicolor) process. Disney signed a contract with Technicolor which gave the Disney studio exclusive rights to the new three-strip process through the end of 1935, and had a 60% complete *Symphony*, *[Flowers and Trees](/wiki/Flowers_and_Trees)*, scrapped and redone in full color. *Flowers and Trees* was the first animated film to use the three-strip Technicolor process, and was a phenomenal success. Within a year, the now-in-Technicolor *Silly Symphonies* series had popularity and success that matched (and later surpassed) that of the *Mickey Mouse* cartoons. The contract Disney had with Technicolor would also later be extended another five years as well.

The success of *Silly Symphonies* would be tremendously boosted after *Three Little Pigs* was released in 1933 and became a box office sensation; the film was featured in movie theaters for several months and also featured the hit song that became the anthem of the Great Depression, "Who's Afraid of the Big Bad Wolf". Several *Silly Symphonies* entries, including *[Three Little Pigs](/wiki/Three_Little_Pigs_(film))* (1933), *[The Grasshopper and the Ants](/wiki/The_Grasshopper_and_the_Ants_(film))* (1934), *[The Tortoise and the Hare](/wiki/The_Tortoise_and_the_Hare_(film))* (1935), *[The Country Cousin](/wiki/The_Country_Cousin)* (1936), *[The Old Mill](/wiki/The_Old_Mill)* (1937), *[Wynken, Blynken, and Nod](/wiki/Wynken,_Blynken_and_Nod_(film))* (1938), and *[The Ugly Duckling](/wiki/The_Ugly_Duckling_(1939_film))* (1939, with an earlier [black-and-white version](/wiki/The_Ugly_Duckling_(1931_film)) from 1931), are among the most notable short films produced by Walt Disney.

Due to problems related to Disney's scheduled productions of cartoons, a deal was made with [Harman and Ising](/wiki/Harman_and_Ising) to produce three Silly Symphonies: *[Merbabies](/wiki/Merbabies)*, *Pipe Dreams*, and *The Little Bantamweight*. Only one of these cartoons, *Merbabies*, ended up being bought by Disney, the remaining two Harman-Ising Silly Symphonies were then sold to MGM who released them as Happy Harmonies cartoons. Disney ceased production of *Silly Symphonies* in 1939.

## Distribution

The series was first distributed by [Pat Powers](/wiki/Pat_Powers_(producer)) from 1929 to 1930 and released by Celebrity Productions (1929–1930) indirectly through [Columbia Pictures](/wiki/Columbia_Pictures). The original basis of the cartoons was musical novelty, and the musical scores of the first cartoons were composed by [Carl Stalling](/wiki/Carl_Stalling).

### Columbia Pictures

After viewing "The Skeleton Dance", the manager at Columbia Pictures quickly became interested in distributing the series, and gained the perfect opportunity to acquire Silly Symphonies after Disney broke with Celebrity Productions head Pat Powers after Powers signed Disney's colleague [Ub Iwerks](/wiki/Ub_Iwerks) to a studio contract. [Columbia Pictures](/wiki/Columbia_Pictures) (1930–1932) agreed to pick up the direct distribution of the [Mickey Mouse](/wiki/Mickey_Mouse) series on the condition that they would have exclusive rights to distribute the *Silly Symphonies* series; at first, *Silly Symphonies* could not even come close to the popularity Mickey Mouse had. The original title cards to the shorts released by Celebrity Productions and Columbia Pictures were all redrawn after Walt Disney stopped distributing his cartoons through them. Meanwhile, more competition spread for Disney after [Max Fleischer](/wiki/Max_Fleischer)'s flapper cartoon character [Betty Boop](/wiki/Betty_Boop) began to gain more and more popularity after starring in the cartoon *[Minnie the Moocher](/wiki/Minnie_the_Moocher_(film))*. By August 1932, Betty Boop became so popular that the [Talkartoon](/wiki/Talkartoon) series was renamed as Betty Boop cartoons.

### United Artists

In 1932, after falling out with Columbia Pictures, Disney began distributing his products through [United Artists](/wiki/United_Artists). UA refused to distribute the *Silly Symphonies* unless Disney associated Mickey Mouse with them somehow, resulting in the "Mickey Mouse presents a *Silly Symphony*" title cards and posters that introduced and promoted the series during its five-year run for UA. United Artists also agreed to double the budget for each cartoon from $7,500 to $15,000. The first short released by United Artists was *[The Bears and Bees](/wiki/The_Bears_and_Bees)*.

### RKO Radio Pictures

In 1937, Disney signed a distribution deal with [RKO Radio Pictures](/wiki/RKO_Pictures) to distribute the Silly Symphony cartoons, along with the Mickey Mouse series. RKO would continue to distribute until the end of the series in 1939.

### Home media

Several *Symphonies* have been released in home media, most of the time as bonus shorts that relate to something within various Disney films. For instance, the original *[Dumbo](/wiki/Dumbo)* VHS included *Father Noah's Ark*, *The Practical Pig* and *Three Orphan Kittens* as bonus shorts to make up for the film's short length. In the UK, several *Silly Symphonies* were released in compilations under Disney Videos' "Storybook Favourites" brand. The three "Storybook Favourites Shorts" volumes released included among others, *The Three Little Pigs*, *The Tortoise and the Hare* and the remake of *The Ugly Duckling*.

On December 4, 2001, Disney released "[Silly Symphonies](/wiki/Walt_Disney_Treasures:_Wave_One#Silly_Symphonies)" as part of its DVD series "[Walt Disney Treasures](/wiki/Walt_Disney_Treasures)". On December 19, 2006, "[More Silly Symphonies](/wiki/Walt_Disney_Treasures:_Wave_Six#More_Silly_Symphonies)" was released, completing the collection and allowing the cartoons to be completely available to the public.

Some Disney [Blu-ray](/wiki/Blu-ray) discs include *Silly Symphonies* as high definition special features. *[Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs](/wiki/Snow_White_and_the_Seven_Dwarfs_(1937_film)#Home_media)* includes six, *[Beauty and the Beast](/wiki/Beauty_and_the_Beast_(1991_film)#Home_media)* and *[Dumbo](/wiki/Dumbo#Home_media)* both contain two and [Pixar](/wiki/Pixar)'s *[A Bug's Life](/wiki/A_Bug%27s_Life#Home_media)* contains one.

The *Silly Symphony* shorts originally aired on [Turner Classic Movies](/wiki/Turner_Classic_Movies)' period program block "Treasures from the Disney Vault".

Some *Silly Symphony* shorts are viewable on [Disney+](/wiki/Disney%2B).

## Reception

Disney's experiments were widely praised within the film industry, and the *Silly Symphonies* won the [Academy Award for Best Animated Short Film](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Animated_Short_Film) seven times, maintaining a six-year-hold on the category after it was first introduced. This record was matched only by [MGM](/wiki/Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer)'s *[Tom and Jerry](/wiki/Tom_and_Jerry)* series during the 1940s and 1950s.

## Legacy

The *Symphonies* changed the course of Disney studio history when Walt's plans to direct his first feature cartoon became problematic after his warm-up to the task *[The Golden Touch](/wiki/The_Golden_Touch_(film))* was widely seen (even by Disney himself) as stiff and slowly paced. This motivated him to embrace his role as being the producer and providing creative oversight (especially of the story) for *[Snow White](/wiki/Snow_White_and_the_Seven_Dwarfs_(1937_film))* while tasking [David Hand](/wiki/David_Hand_(animator)) to handle the actual directing.

*Silly Symphonies* brought along many imitators, including [Warner Bros.](/wiki/Warner_Bros.) cartoon series *[Looney Tunes](/wiki/Looney_Tunes)* and *[Merrie Melodies](/wiki/Merrie_Melodies)*, MGM's *[Happy Harmonies](/wiki/Happy_Harmonies)*, and later, [Universal](/wiki/Universal_Pictures)'s *[Swing Symphony](/wiki/Swing_Symphony)*.

Years later after the *Silly Symphonies* ended, Disney occasionally produced a handful of one-shot cartoons, playing the same style as the *Silly Symphony* series. Unlike the *Silly Symphonies* canon, most of these "Specials" have a narration, usually by Disney legend [Sterling Holloway](/wiki/Sterling_Holloway).

In the 1934 MGM film *[Hollywood Party](/wiki/Hollywood_Party)*, Mickey Mouse appears with [Jimmy Durante](/wiki/Jimmy_Durante), where they introduce *[The Hot Choc-late Soldiers](/wiki/The_Hot_Choc-late_Soldiers)*.

The 1999–2000 television series *[Mickey Mouse Works](/wiki/Mickey_Mouse_Works)* used the *Silly Symphonies* title for some of its new cartoons, but unlike the original cartoons, these did feature continuing characters.

As of 2021, three of the *Silly Symphony* shorts (*[Three Little Pigs](/wiki/Three_Little_Pigs_(film))*, *[The Old Mill](/wiki/The_Old_Mill)*, and *[Flowers and Trees](/wiki/Flowers_and_Trees)*), have been selected for preservation in the United States [National Film Registry](/wiki/National_Film_Registry) by the Library of Congress, for being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".

## Comic adaptations

A Sunday [*Silly Symphony* comic strip](/wiki/Silly_Symphony_(comic_strip)) ran in newspapers from January 10, 1932, to July 12, 1942. The strip featured adaptations of some of the *Silly Symphony* cartoons, including *Birds of a Feather*, *The Robber Kitten*, *Elmer Elephant*, *Farmyard Symphony* and *Little Hiawatha*. This strip began with a two-year sequence about [Bucky Bug](/wiki/Bucky_Bug), a character based on the bugs in *[Bugs in Love](/wiki/Bugs_in_Love)*.

There was also an occasional *Silly Symphonies* comic book, with nine issues published by [Dell Comics](/wiki/Dell_Comics) from September 1952 to February 1959. The first issue of this anthology comic featured adaptations of some *Silly Symphony* cartoons, including *The Grasshopper and the Ants*, *Three Little Pigs*, *The Goddess of Spring* and *Mother Pluto*, but it also included non-*Symphony* cartoons like Mickey Mouse's *[Brave Little Tailor](/wiki/Brave_Little_Tailor)*. By the third issue, there was almost no *Symphony*-related material in the book; the stories and activities were mostly based on other Disney shorts and feature films.
