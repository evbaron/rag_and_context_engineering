The **Atlanta Braves** are an American professional [baseball](/wiki/Baseball) team based in the [Atlanta metropolitan area](/wiki/Atlanta_metropolitan_area). The Braves compete in [Major League Baseball](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball) (MLB) as a member club of the [National League](/wiki/National_League_(baseball)) (NL) [East Division](/wiki/National_League_East). The club was founded in [Boston](/wiki/Boston), Massachusetts, in 1871 as the **Boston Red Stockings**. The Braves are one of two remaining National League charter franchises that debuted in 1876 and are the oldest continuously operating [professional sports franchise in North America](/wiki/Major_professional_sports_teams_in_the_United_States_and_Canada). The franchise was known by various names until it adopted the **Boston Braves** name in 1912.

After 81 seasons and [one World Series title](/wiki/1914_World_Series) in Boston, the club [moved](/wiki/Relocation_of_professional_sports_teams) to [Milwaukee, Wisconsin](/wiki/Milwaukee), in 1953. With a roster of Hall-of-Fame players such as [Hank Aaron](/wiki/Hank_Aaron), [Eddie Mathews](/wiki/Eddie_Mathews), and [Warren Spahn](/wiki/Warren_Spahn), the **Milwaukee Braves** won the [World Series](/wiki/1957_World_Series) in 1957. Despite the team's success, fan attendance declined. The club's owners moved the team to [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta), Georgia, in 1966.

The Braves did not find much success in Atlanta until 1991. From 1991 to 2005, the Braves were one of the most successful teams in baseball, winning an unprecedented 14 consecutive division titles, making an MLB record eight consecutive [National League Championship Series](/wiki/National_League_Championship_Series) appearances, and producing one of the [greatest pitching rotations](/wiki/Big_Three_(Atlanta_Braves)) in the history of baseball including Hall of Famers [Greg Maddux](/wiki/Greg_Maddux), [John Smoltz](/wiki/John_Smoltz), and [Tom Glavine](/wiki/Tom_Glavine).

The club has won an MLB record 23 divisional titles, 18 [National League](/wiki/National_League_Championship_Series) [pennants](/wiki/Pennant_(sports)), and four World Series championships. The Braves are the only Major League Baseball franchise to have won the World Series in three different home cities. At the end of the 2025 season, the Braves' overall win–loss record is 11,190–11,035–154 (.503). Since moving to Atlanta in 1966, the Braves have an overall win–loss record of 4,926–4,547–8 (.520) through the end of 2025.

## History

Main article: [History of the Atlanta Braves](/wiki/History_of_the_Atlanta_Braves)

### Boston (1871–1952)

Main article: [Boston Braves](/wiki/Boston_Braves)

#### 1871–1913

Boston Beaneaters team photo, 1890

The [Cincinnati Red Stockings](/wiki/Cincinnati_Red_Stockings), formed in 1869, were the first openly all-professional baseball team but disbanded after the 1870 season. Manager [Harry Wright](/wiki/Harry_Wright) and players moved to [Boston](/wiki/Boston), forming the *Boston Red Stockings*, a charter team in the [National Association of Professional Base Ball Players](/wiki/National_Association_of_Professional_Base_Ball_Players) (NAPBBP). Led by the Wright brothers, Ross Barnes, and [Al Spalding](/wiki/Albert_Spalding), they dominated the National Association, winning four of five championships. The original Boston Red Stockings team and its successors can lay claim to being the oldest continuously playing franchise in American professional sports.

The club was known as the Boston Red Caps when they played the first [National League](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball#Founding) game in [1876](/wiki/1876_in_baseball), winning against the [Philadelphia Athletics](/wiki/Philadelphia_Athletics_(1860%E2%80%9376)). Despite a weaker roster in the league's first year, they rebounded to secure the 1877 and 1878 pennants. Managed by [Frank Selee](/wiki/Frank_Selee), they were a dominant force in the 19th century, winning eight pennants. By 1898, the team was known as the [Beaneaters](/wiki/1898_Boston_Beaneaters_season) and they won 102 games that season, with stars like [Hugh Duffy](/wiki/Hugh_Duffy), [Tommy McCarthy](/wiki/Tommy_McCarthy_(baseball)), and "Slidin'" [Billy Hamilton](/wiki/Billy_Hamilton_(baseball,_born_1866)).

In 1901, the American League was introduced, causing many Beaneaters players including stars Duffy and [Jimmy Collins](/wiki/Jimmy_Collins) to leave for clubs of the rival league. The team struggled, having only one winning season from 1900 to 1913. In 1907, they temporarily dropped the red color from their stockings due to infection concerns.

The club underwent various nickname changes until becoming the Braves before the [1912 season](/wiki/1912_Boston_Braves_season). The president of the club, John M. Ward, named the club after the owner, [James Gaffney](/wiki/James_E._Gaffney). Gaffney was called one of the "braves" of New York City's political machine, [Tammany Hall](/wiki/Tammany_Hall), which used a Native American chief as their symbol.

#### 1914: Miracle

Main article: [1914 Boston Braves season](/wiki/1914_Boston_Braves_season)

A program from the [1914 World Series](/wiki/1914_World_Series), featuring Braves manager [George Stallings](/wiki/George_Stallings) (left). [Baseball Magazine](/wiki/Baseball_Magazine) cover, 1914 (right).

In 1914, the Boston Braves experienced a remarkable turnaround in what would become one of the most memorable seasons in baseball history. Starting with a dismal 4–18 record, the Braves found themselves in last place, trailing the league-leading [New York Giants](/wiki/1914_New_York_Giants_season) by 15 games after losing a doubleheader to the [Brooklyn Robins](/wiki/1914_Brooklyn_Robins_season) on July 4. However, the team rebounded with an incredible hot streak, going 41–12 from July 6 to September 5. On August 3, [Joseph Lannin](/wiki/Joseph_Lannin) the president of the Red Sox, offered [Fenway Park](/wiki/Fenway_Park) to the Braves free of charge for the remainder of the season since their usual home, the [South End Grounds](/wiki/South_End_Grounds), was too small. On September 7 and 8, they defeated the Giants in two out of three games, propelling them into first place. Despite being in last place as late as July 18, the Braves secured the pennant, becoming the only team under the old eight-team league format to achieve this after being in last place on the Fourth of July. They were in last place as late as July 18, but were close to the pack, moving into fourth on July 21 and second place on August 12.

The Braves entered the [1914 World Series](/wiki/1914_World_Series) led by captain and National League [Most Valuable Player](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball_Most_Valuable_Player), [Johnny Evers](/wiki/Johnny_Evers). The Boston club were slight underdogs against [Connie Mack](/wiki/Connie_Mack)'s [Philadelphia A's](/wiki/1914_Philadelphia_Athletics_season). However, they swept the Athletics and won the world championship. Inspired by their success, owner Gaffney constructed a modern park, [Braves Field](/wiki/Braves_Field), which opened in August 1915 and was the largest park in the majors at the time, boasting 40,000 seats and convenient public transportation access.

#### 1915–1952

The Boston Braves cap logo, 1946 to 1952

From 1917 to 1933, the Boston Braves struggled. After a series of different owners, a syndicate led by [Emil Fuchs](/wiki/Emil_Fuchs_(baseball)) and including pitching great and Fuchs' longtime friend [Christy Mathewson](/wiki/Christy_Mathewson) bought the team in 1923. Mathewson's death in 1925 left Fuchs in control of the team. Despite Fuchs' commitment to success, the team faced challenges overcoming the damage from previous years. It was not until 1933 and 1934, under manager [Bill McKechnie](/wiki/Bill_McKechnie), that the Braves became competitive, but the team's improvement in performance did little to help its finances.

In an effort to boost fan attendance and finances, Fuchs orchestrated a deal with the [New York Yankees](/wiki/New_York_Yankees) to acquire [Babe Ruth](/wiki/Babe_Ruth) in 1935. Ruth was appointed team vice president with promises of profit shares and managerial prospects. Initially, Ruth seemed to provide a spark on opening day, but his declining skills became evident. Ruth's inability to run and poor fielding led to internal strife, and it became clear that his titles were symbolic. Ruth retired on June 1, 1935, shortly after hitting his last three home runs. The Braves finished the season with a dismal 38–115 record, marking the franchise's worst season.

Fuchs lost control of the team in August 1935, leading to a rebranding attempt as the Boston Bees, but it did little to alter the team's fortune. Construction magnate [Lou Perini](/wiki/Lou_Perini) took over, eventually restoring the Braves' name. Despite World War II causing a brief setback, the team, led by pitcher [Warren Spahn](/wiki/Warren_Spahn), enjoyed impressive seasons in 1946 and 1947 under Perini's ownership.

Hall of Fame pitcher [Warren Spahn](/wiki/Warren_Spahn)

In 1948, the [team](/wiki/1948_Boston_Braves_season) won the pennant, behind the pitching of Spahn and [Johnny Sain](/wiki/Johnny_Sain). The remainder of the rotation was so thin that in September, *Boston Post* writer Gerald Hern wrote the poem "[Pray for Rain](/wiki/Pray_for_Rain_(poem))" about the pair:

> First we'll use Spahn
> then we'll use Sain
> Then an off day
> followed by rain
> Back will come Spahn
> followed by Sain
> And followed
> we hope
> by two days of rain.

The poem reached and found favor with an audience so wide that the poem's sentiment, now usually paraphrased as "Spahn and Sain and pray for rain" or "Spahn and Sain, then pray for rain", entered the baseball vocabulary.

The [1948 World Series](/wiki/1948_World_Series), which the Braves lost in six games to the [Indians](/wiki/1948_Cleveland_Indians_season), turned out to be the Braves' last hurrah in Boston. On March 13, 1953, Perini announced he was moving the club to [Milwaukee](/wiki/Milwaukee). Perini cited advent of television and the lack of enthusiasm for the Braves in Boston as the key factors in deciding to move the franchise.

### Milwaukee (1953–1965)

Main article: [Milwaukee Braves](/wiki/Milwaukee_Braves)

The Milwaukee Braves cap logo

The Milwaukee Braves' move to Wisconsin for the 1953 season was an immediate success, as they drew a National League-record 1.8 million fans and finished the season second in the league. Manager [Charlie Grimm](/wiki/Charlie_Grimm) was named [NL Manager of the Year](/wiki/NL_Manager_of_the_Year).

Throughout the 1950s, the Braves were a National League power; driven by sluggers [Eddie Mathews](/wiki/Eddie_Mathews) and [Hank Aaron](/wiki/Hank_Aaron), the team won two pennants and finished second twice between 1956 and 1959. In [1957](/wiki/1957_Milwaukee_Braves_season), Aaron's [MVP season](/wiki/MLB_Most_Valuable_Player_Award) led the Braves to their first pennant in nine years, then a [World Series](/wiki/1957_World_Series) victory against the formidable New York Yankees. Despite a strong start in the World Series [rematch](/wiki/1958_World_Series) the following [season](/wiki/1958_Milwaukee_Braves_season), the Braves ultimately lost the last three games and the World Series. The [1959 season](/wiki/1959_Milwaukee_Braves_season) ended in a tie with the [Los Angeles Dodgers](/wiki/Los_Angeles_Dodgers), who defeated the Braves in a playoff. The ensuing years saw fluctuating success, including the Braves finishing fifth in 1963, their first time in the "second division".

In 1962, team owner Louis Perini sold the Braves to a Chicago-based group led by William Bartholomay. Bartholomay intended to move the team to Atlanta in 1965, but legal hurdles kept them in Milwaukee for an extra season.

### Atlanta (1966–present)

#### 1966–1974

Hall of Fame right fielder and designated hitter [Hank Aaron](/wiki/Hank_Aaron)

After arriving in Atlanta in 1966, the Braves found success in 1969, with the onset of divisional play by winning the first [National League West](/wiki/National_League_West) Division title. In the [National League Championship Series](/wiki/1969_National_League_Championship_Series) the Braves were swept by the "[Miracle Mets](/wiki/1969_New_York_Mets_season)". They would post only two winning seasons between 1970 and 1981. Fans in Atlanta had to be satisfied with the achievements of [Hank Aaron](/wiki/Hank_Aaron), who by the end of the 1973 season, had hit 713 home runs, one short of Ruth's record. On April 4, opening day of the [next season](/wiki/1974_Major_League_Baseball_season), he hit No. 714 in [Cincinnati](/wiki/1974_Cincinnati_Reds_season), and on April 8, in front of his home fans and a national television audience, he finally beat Ruth's mark with a home run to left-center field off left-hander [Al Downing](/wiki/Al_Downing_(baseball)) of the [Los Angeles Dodgers](/wiki/1974_Los_Angeles_Dodgers_season). Aaron spent most of his career as a Milwaukee and Atlanta Brave before being traded to the [Milwaukee Brewers](/wiki/Milwaukee_Brewers) on November 2, 1974.

#### Ted Turner and Time Warner era

##### 1976–1977: Ted Turner buys the team

Media magnate [Ted Turner](/wiki/Ted_Turner) purchased the team in 1976, and played a large role in the team's operation.

In 1976, the team was purchased by media magnate [Ted Turner](/wiki/Ted_Turner), owner of [superstation](/wiki/Superstation) [WTBS](/wiki/TBS_(American_TV_channel)), as a means to keep the team (and one of his main programming staples) in Atlanta. Turner used the Braves as a major programming draw for his fledgling cable network, making the Braves the first franchise to have a nationwide audience and fan base. WTBS marketed the team as "The Atlanta Braves: [America's Team](/wiki/America%27s_Team)", a nickname that still sticks in some areas of the country, especially [the South](/wiki/Southern_United_States). The financially strapped Turner used money already paid to the team for their broadcast rights as a down-payment. Turner quickly gained a reputation as a quirky, hands-on baseball owner. On May 11, 1977, Turner appointed himself [manager](/wiki/Manager_(baseball)), but because MLB passed a rule in the 1950s barring managers from holding a financial stake in their teams, Turner was ordered to relinquish that position after one game (the [Braves](/wiki/1977_Atlanta_Braves_season) lost 2–1 to the [Pittsburgh Pirates](/wiki/1977_Pittsburgh_Pirates_season) to bring their losing streak to 17 games).

##### 1978–1990

[Greg Maddux](/wiki/Greg_Maddux), [Tom Glavine](/wiki/Tom_Glavine), and [John Smoltz](/wiki/John_Smoltz) combined for six [Cy Young Awards](/wiki/Cy_Young_Award) during their time in the Braves pitching rotation

The Braves did not enjoy much success between 1978 and 1990, but in the [1982 season](/wiki/1982_Atlanta_Braves_season), led by manager [Joe Torre](/wiki/Joe_Torre), the Braves secured their first divisional title since 1969. The team was led by standout performances from key players like [Dale Murphy](/wiki/Dale_Murphy), [Bob Horner](/wiki/Bob_Horner), [Chris Chambliss](/wiki/Chris_Chambliss), [Phil Niekro](/wiki/Phil_Niekro), and [Gene Garber](/wiki/Gene_Garber). The Braves were swept in the [NLCS](/wiki/1982_National_League_Championship_Series) in three games by the [Cardinals](/wiki/1982_St._Louis_Cardinals_season). Murphy won the Most Valuable Player award for the National League in 1982 and 1983.

##### 1991–2005: 14 consecutive division titles

From 1991 to 2005, the Atlanta Braves enjoyed a remarkable era of success in baseball, marked by a record-setting 14 consecutive division titles, five National League pennants, and a World Series championship in 1995. Bobby Cox returned as manager in 1990, leading the team's turnaround after finishing the previous season with the worst record in baseball. Notable developments included the drafting of [Chipper Jones](/wiki/Chipper_Jones) in 1990 and the hiring of general manager John Schuerholz from the Kansas City Royals.

The Braves' remarkable journey began in [1991](/wiki/1991_Atlanta_Braves_season), known as the "Worst to First" season. Overcoming a shaky start, the Braves bounced back led by young pitchers [Tom Glavine](/wiki/Tom_Glavine) and [John Smoltz](/wiki/John_Smoltz). The team secured the NL pennant in a memorable playoff race, ultimately losing a closely contested [World Series](/wiki/1991_World_Series) to the Minnesota Twins. The following year, the [Braves](/wiki/1992_Atlanta_Braves_season) won the [NLCS](/wiki/1992_National_League_Championship_Series) in dramatic fashion against the [Pirates](/wiki/1992_Pittsburgh_Pirates_season) but fell short in the [World Series](/wiki/1992_World_Series) against the [Toronto Blue Jays](/wiki/1992_Toronto_Blue_Jays_season).

In 1993, the Braves strengthened their pitching staff with the addition of Cy Young Award winner [Greg Maddux](/wiki/Greg_Maddux) in free agency. Despite posting a franchise-best 104 wins, they lost in the [NLCS](/wiki/1993_National_League_Championship_Series) to the [Philadelphia Phillies](/wiki/1993_Philadelphia_Phillies_season). The team moved to the Eastern Division in 1994, sparking a heated rivalry with the New York Mets.

The [player's strike](/wiki/1994%E2%80%9395_Major_League_Baseball_strike) cut short the 1994 season just before the division championships, but the Braves rebounded in 1995, defeating the [Cleveland Indians](/wiki/1995_Cleveland_Indians_season) to win the [World Series](/wiki/1995_World_Series). With this World Series victory, the Braves became the first team in Major League Baseball to win world championships in three different cities. The Braves reached the World Series in [1996](/wiki/1996_World_Series) and [1999](/wiki/1999_World_Series) but were defeated both times by the [New York Yankees](/wiki/New_York_Yankees).

In 1996, Time Warner acquired Ted Turner's Turner Broadcasting System, including the Braves. Despite their continued success with a ninth consecutive division title in 2000, the Braves faced postseason disappointment with a sweep by the [St. Louis Cardinals](/wiki/2000_St._Louis_Cardinals_season) in the [NLDS](/wiki/2000_National_League_Division_Series). The team won division titles from 2002 to 2004 but experienced early exits in the NLDS each year.

#### Liberty Media era

##### Liberty Media buys the team

Chipper Jones salutes the crowd at [Turner Field](/wiki/Turner_Field) prior to his final regular-season game on September 30, 2012. Jones announced he would retire after 19 seasons with the Braves

In December 2005, Time Warner put the club up for sale, leading to negotiations with Liberty Media. After over a year of talks, a deal was reached in February 2007 for Liberty Media to acquire the Braves for $450 million, a magazine publishing company, and $980 million in cash. The sale, valued at approximately $1.48 billion, was contingent on approval from 75 percent of MLB owners and Commissioner Bud Selig.

##### Bobby Cox and Chipper Jones retire

Bobby Cox's final year as manager in [2010](/wiki/2010_Atlanta_Braves_season) saw the Braves return to the postseason for the first time since 2005. The team secured the NL Wild Card but fell to the San Francisco Giants in the [National League Division Series](/wiki/2010_National_League_Division_Series) in four closely contested games, marking the conclusion of Bobby Cox's managerial career. The [following season](/wiki/2011_Atlanta_Braves_season) the Braves suffered a historic September collapse to miss the postseason. The club bounced back in [2012](/wiki/2012_Atlanta_Braves_season) and returned to the postseason in Chipper Jones' final season. The Braves won 94 games in 2012, but that wasn't enough to win the NL East, so they faced the St. Louis Cardinals in the inaugural [Wild Card Game](/wiki/Wild_Card_Game). Chipper Jones last game was a memorable one: the Braves lost the [one game playoff](/wiki/2012_National_League_Wild_Card_Game) 6–3, but the game would be remembered for a controversial infield fly call that helped end a Braves rally in the 8th inning.

##### Truist Park and return to the World Series

[Truist Park](/wiki/Truist_Park) prior to its first regular-season game

In 2017, the Atlanta Braves began playing at [Truist Park](/wiki/Truist_Park), replacing Turner Field as their home stadium. Following an MLB investigation into international signing rule violations, general manager [John Coppolella](/wiki/John_Coppolella) resigned and faced a baseball ban. [Alex Anthopoulos](/wiki/Alex_Anthopoulos) took over as the new general manager. The team's chairman, [Terry McGuirk](/wiki/Terry_McGuirk), apologized for the scandal and expressed confidence in Anthopoulos' integrity. A new on field mascot named [Blooper](/wiki/Blooper_(mascot)) was introduced at a fan event before the 2017 season. Under Anthopoulos, the Braves made the playoffs in six of his first seven seasons. In [2020](/wiki/2020_Atlanta_Braves_season) the Braves reached the [National League Championship Series](/wiki/2020_National_League_Championship_Series), but ultimately lost to the Dodgers after leading 3–1.

President [Joe Biden](/wiki/Joe_Biden) hosts the 2021 World Series Champion Atlanta Braves on September 26, 2022, in the [East Room](/wiki/East_Room) of the [White House](/wiki/White_House).

In the 2021 season, the Braves won the National League East with an 88–73 record. In the postseason, they quickly defeated the Milwaukee Brewers in the NL Division Series 3–1. The Braves again faced the Dodgers in the [2021 NLCS](/wiki/2021_National_League_Championship_Series), and won in six games to take Atlanta's first National League pennant since [1999](/wiki/1999_National_League_Championship_Series). The Braves advanced to the World Series. They defeated the [Houston Astros](/wiki/Houston_Astros) in six games to win their fourth World Series title.

##### End of postseason run and Brian Snitker era

The Braves failed to qualify for the postseason in 2025, ending a streak of seven consecutive appearances. After 49 years with the organization, including ten seasons as manager, the team announced that Snitker would step down from his managerial role and transition into an advisory position with the club. In November 2025, the Braves announced that longtime bench coach Walt Weiss would be promoted to manager. Weiss, a former All-Star shortstop also spent part of his playing career in Atlanta.

### Logos and uniforms

The Braves logos have evolved over the years, featuring a Native American warrior from 1945 to 1955, followed by a laughing Native American with a mohawk and a feather from 1956 to 1965. The modern logo, introduced in 1987, includes the cursive word "Braves" with a tomahawk below it. Uniforms adopted that year evoked their 1950s classic look. For the 2023 season, the Braves had four uniform combinations, including the classic white home and gray road uniforms, a navy blue road jersey for alternate games, and two alternate uniforms for home games - a Friday night red uniform and a City Connect uniform worn on Saturdays, paying tribute to Hank Aaron. The City Connect uniform features "The A" across the chest, accompanied by a cap with the "A" logo and 1974 uniform colors. The Braves retired the '70s-era City Connects and unveiled a new alternate uniform ahead of the 2026 season. The new jersey is an homage to their cable television-fueled growth in the 1980s; powder-blue pullovers with a script "Atlanta" on the front and a lowercase "A" hat reference their contemporary away uniform, while an "ATL" wordmark on the left sleeve and printing above the laundry tag are designed similar to the classic TBS SuperStation logo.

## Ballparks

### Former parks

The Boston Braves played at the [South End Grounds](/wiki/South_End_Grounds) from their inception. After a fire destroyed the park in 1894, the club temporarily played at the [Congress Street Grounds](/wiki/Congress_Street_Grounds) until the South End Grounds could be rebuilt. During the 1914 season, it became evident that the South End Grounds was too small to accommodate larger crowds, prompting the team to play some games at Fenway Park. To address the need for a larger venue, [Braves Field](/wiki/Braves_Field) was built in 1915.

Braves Field remained the club's home in Boston until the team relocated to Milwaukee. [Milwaukee County Stadium](/wiki/Milwaukee_County_Stadium) was constructed in 1950 to attract a Major League Baseball team and became the Braves' new home in 1953. The team played there until moving to Atlanta in 1966.

The city of Atlanta constructed [Atlanta–Fulton County Stadium](/wiki/Atlanta%E2%80%93Fulton_County_Stadium) in 1965 after reaching an agreement with the Braves to relocate from Milwaukee. The Braves played at Atlanta–Fulton County Stadium until 1997, when they moved to [Turner Field](/wiki/Turner_Field). Originally built as [Centennial Olympic Stadium](/wiki/Centennial_Olympic_Stadium) for the [1996 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1996_Summer_Olympics), the venue was later converted into a ballpark for the Braves. Turner Field served as the Braves' home ballpark through the 2016 season.

### Current parks

#### Truist Park

Main article: [Truist Park](/wiki/Truist_Park)

The Atlanta Braves home ballpark has been Truist Park since 2017. Truist Park is located approximately 10 miles (16 km) northwest of [downtown Atlanta](/wiki/Downtown_Atlanta) in the [unincorporated community](/wiki/Unincorporated_area#United_States) of [Cumberland](/wiki/Cumberland,_Georgia), in [Cobb County](/wiki/Cobb_County), [Georgia](/wiki/Georgia_(U.S._state)). The Braves opened Truist Park on April 14, 2017, with a four-game sweep of the [San Diego Padres](/wiki/San_Diego_Padres). The park received positive reviews. Woody Studenmund of the [Hardball Times](/wiki/Hardball_Times) called the park a "gem" saying that he was impressed with "the compact beauty of the stadium and its exciting approach to combining baseball, business and social activities." J.J. Cooper of [Baseball America](/wiki/Baseball_America) praised the "excellent sight lines for pretty much every seat."

#### CoolToday Park

Main article: [CoolToday Park](/wiki/CoolToday_Park)

Since 2019, the Braves have played [spring training](/wiki/Spring_training) games at CoolToday Park in [North Port, Florida](/wiki/North_Port,_Florida). The ballpark opened on March 24, 2019, with the Braves' 4–2 win over the [Tampa Bay Rays](/wiki/Tampa_Bay_Rays). The Braves left [Champion Stadium](/wiki/Champion_Stadium), their previous Spring Training home, near Orlando, to reduce travel times and to get closer to other teams' facilities. CoolToday Park also serves as the Braves' year-round rehabilitation facility.

## Major rivalry

### New York Mets

Main article: [Braves–Mets rivalry](/wiki/Braves%E2%80%93Mets_rivalry)

Although their first major confrontation occurred when the Mets swept the Braves in the [1969 NLCS](/wiki/1969_National_League_Championship_Series), the rivalry did not become especially heated until the 1994 season when division realignment put both the Mets and the Braves in the [National League East](/wiki/National_League_East) division.

The [Braves](/wiki/1999_Atlanta_Braves_season) faced the [Mets](/wiki/1999_New_York_Mets_season) in the [1999 National League Championship Series](/wiki/1999_National_League_Championship_Series). The Braves initially took a 3–0 series lead, seemingly on the verge of a sweep, but the Mets rallied in Game 4 and Game 5. Despite the Mets' resilience, the Braves eventually won the series in Game 6 with [Andruw Jones](/wiki/Andruw_Jones) securing a dramatic walk-off walk, earning their 5th National League pennant of the decade. In 2022, the [Braves](/wiki/2022_Atlanta_Braves_season) and [Mets](/wiki/2022_New_York_Mets_season) both finished with 101 wins. The National League East title and a first-round bye came down to a crucial three-game series at Truist Park from September 30 to October 2. The Mets entered with a slight lead but faltered as the Braves swept the series. Atlanta claimed the NL East division title and first-round bye, by winning the season series against the Mets.

Since the Mets joined the league, both teams have won two World Series [titles](/wiki/List_of_World_Series_champions). The Braves have captured six [NL pennants](/wiki/List_of_National_League_pennant_winners), while the Mets have won five. The Braves hold the advantage in the all-time head-to-head record between the two teams at 516–425. However, the Mets have the upper hand in playoff matchups with a 5–4 record.

## Nationwide fanbase

Former U.S. President [Jimmy Carter](/wiki/Jimmy_Carter) was a lifelong Braves fan and personal friend of [Hank Aaron](/wiki/Hank_Aaron). Pictured is Aaron meeting President Carter in the [Oval Office](/wiki/Oval_Office) in 1978.

In addition to having strong fan support in the Metro Atlanta area and the state of Georgia, the Braves have been referred to as "[America's Team](/wiki/America%27s_Team#Other_uses)" in reference to the team's games being broadcast nationally on [TBS](/wiki/Braves_TBS_Baseball) from the 1970s until 2007, giving the team a nationwide fan base.

The Braves boast heavy support within the [Southeastern United States](/wiki/Southeastern_United_States) particularly in states such as [Mississippi](/wiki/Mississippi), [Alabama](/wiki/Alabama), [South Carolina](/wiki/South_Carolina), [North Carolina](/wiki/North_Carolina), [Tennessee](/wiki/Tennessee) and [Florida](/wiki/Florida).

## Tomahawk chop

Main article: [Atlanta Braves tomahawk chop and name controversy](/wiki/Atlanta_Braves_tomahawk_chop_and_name_controversy)

The Atlanta Braves encouraged fans to gesture with the "Tomahawk Chop", distributing foam tomahawks at games and other events.

In 1991, fans of the Atlanta Braves popularized the "tomahawk chop" during games. The use of foam tomahawks faced criticism from [Native American](/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) groups, who regarded it as demeaning. Despite protests, the Braves' public relations director defended it as a "proud expression of unification and family." The controversy resurfaced in 2019 when [Cherokee Nation](/wiki/Cherokee_Nation) member and St. Louis Cardinals pitcher [Ryan Helsley](/wiki/Ryan_Helsley) found the chop insulting, prompting the Braves to modify their in-game experience. During the off-season, discussions ensued with Native American representatives, and amid pressure in 2020 to change their name, the Braves announced ongoing talks about the chop but insisted the team name would remain unchanged.

The debate over the tomahawk chop continued into 2021. While some Native American leaders, like [Richard Sneed](/wiki/Richard_Sneed), the Principal Chief of the [Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians](/wiki/Eastern_Band_of_Cherokee_Indians), expressed personal indifference or tolerance, acknowledging it as an acknowledgment of Native American strength, others vehemently opposed it. Sneed emphasized larger issues facing Native American communities and questioned the focus on the chop. The Eastern Cherokee Band of Indians and the Braves initiated efforts to incorporate Cherokee language and culture into the team's activities, stadium, and merchandise, aiming for greater cultural sensitivity despite differing opinions within the Native American community.

### Achievements

#### Awards

Main article: [Atlanta Braves award winners and league leaders](/wiki/Atlanta_Braves_award_winners_and_league_leaders)

Braves players have won seven [Most Valuable Player](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball_Most_Valuable_Player_Award) (MVP) awards, with [Dale Murphy](/wiki/Dale_Murphy) notably earning the honor in back-to-back years, 1982 and 1983. Five Braves pitchers have received the [Cy Young Award](/wiki/Cy_Young_Award), given to the league's best pitcher, including Greg Maddux, who won it three consecutive times in 1993, 1994, and 1995.

Two Braves managers have been named [Manager of the Year](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball_Manager_of_the_Year_Award), with Bobby Cox winning the award three times, in 1991, 2004, and 2005. Additionally, eight Braves players have been honored with the [Rookie of the Year](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball_Rookie_of_the_Year_Award) awards.

### Retired numbers

See also: [List of Major League Baseball retired numbers](/wiki/List_of_Major_League_Baseball_retired_numbers)

The Braves have retired eleven numbers in the history of the franchise. Most recently, [Andruw Jones](/wiki/Andruw_Jones)' number 25 was retired in 2023. Other retired numbers include [Chipper Jones](/wiki/Chipper_Jones)' number 10, [John Smoltz](/wiki/John_Smoltz)'s number 29, [Bobby Cox](/wiki/Bobby_Cox)'s number 6, [Tom Glavine](/wiki/Tom_Glavine)'s number 47, and [Greg Maddux](/wiki/Greg_Maddux)'s number 31. Additionally, the Braves have retired [Hank Aaron](/wiki/Hank_Aaron)'s number 44, [Dale Murphy](/wiki/Dale_Murphy)'s number 3, [Phil Niekro](/wiki/Phil_Niekro)'s number 35, [Eddie Mathews](/wiki/Eddie_Mathews)' number 41, and [Warren Spahn](/wiki/Warren_Spahn)'s number 21. [Jackie Robinson](/wiki/Jackie_Robinson)'s number 42 is also retired across all of Major League Baseball, with the exception of [Jackie Robinson Day](/wiki/Jackie_Robinson_Day).

Six of the eleven numbers (Cox, Chipper Jones, Andruw Jones, Smoltz, Maddux and Glavine) were on the Braves at the same time. Of the eleven Braves whose numbers have been retired, all who are eligible for the [National Baseball Hall of Fame](/wiki/National_Baseball_Hall_of_Fame_and_Museum) have been elected with the exception of Dale Murphy. The color and design of the retired numbers on commemorative markers and other in-stadium signage reflect the primary uniform design at the time the player was on the team.

## Radio and television

Main articles: [BravesVision](/wiki/BravesVision), [List of Atlanta Braves broadcasters](/wiki/List_of_Atlanta_Braves_broadcasters), [Braves TBS Baseball](/wiki/Braves_TBS_Baseball), and [Atlanta Braves Radio Network](/wiki/Atlanta_Braves_Radio_Network)

As of the 2026 season, the Atlanta Braves' regional rights will be held in-house by the team under the banner [BravesVision](/wiki/BravesVision). Games will be distributed via partnerships with television providers in the team's market, as well as via a direct-to-consumer streaming service operated by [MLB Local Media](/wiki/MLB_Local_Media). Selected games are carried by a network of [Gray Media](/wiki/Gray_Media) television stations, led by [WPCH-TV](/wiki/WPCH-TV) (the former WTBS), with Gray producing a package of spring training games, and carrying a package of regular season games in simulcast with BravesVision.

[Brandon Gaudin](/wiki/Brandon_Gaudin) serves as the play-by-play announcer and [C.J. Nitkowski](/wiki/C.J._Nitkowski) as lead analyst. [Jeff Francoeur](/wiki/Jeff_Francoeur) and Tom Glavine will also join the broadcast for a few games during the season. [Peter Moylan](/wiki/Peter_Moylan), [Nick Green](/wiki/Nick_Green_(baseball)), and John Smoltz also appear in the booth for select games as in-game analysts.

The radio broadcast team is led by the tandem of play-by-play announcer Ben Ingram and analyst [Joe Simpson](/wiki/Joe_Simpson_(baseball)). Braves games are broadcast across Georgia and seven other states on at least 172 radio affiliates, including flagship station [680 The Fan](/wiki/WCNN) in Atlanta and stations as far away as Richmond, Virginia; Louisville, Kentucky; and the US Virgin Islands. The games are carried on at least 82 radio stations in Georgia.
