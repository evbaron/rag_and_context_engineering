**Labetalol** is a medication used to treat [high blood pressure](/wiki/Hypertension) and in long term management of [angina](/wiki/Angina). This includes [essential hypertension](/wiki/Essential_hypertension), [hypertensive emergencies](/wiki/Hypertensive_emergencies), and [hypertension of pregnancy](/wiki/Hypertension_of_pregnancy). In essential hypertension it is generally less preferred than a number of other [blood pressure medications](/wiki/Blood_pressure_medications). It can be given [by mouth](/wiki/By_mouth) or by [injection into a vein](/wiki/Intravenous).

Common side effects include [low blood pressure with standing](/wiki/Orthostatic_hypotension), dizziness, feeling tired, and nausea. Serious side effects may include [low blood pressure](/wiki/Low_blood_pressure), [liver problems](/wiki/Liver_problems), [heart failure](/wiki/Heart_failure), and [bronchospasm](/wiki/Bronchospasm). Use appears safe in the latter part of [pregnancy](/wiki/Pregnancy) and it is not expected to cause problems during [breastfeeding](/wiki/Breastfeeding). It works by blocking the activation of [β-](/wiki/Beta-adrenergic_receptor) and [α-adrenergic receptors](/wiki/Alpha-adrenergic_receptor).

Labetalol was patented in 1966 and came into medical use in 1977. It is available as a [generic medication](/wiki/Generic_medication). In 2023, it was the 232nd most commonly prescribed medication in the United States, with more than 1 million prescriptions.

## Medical uses

Labetalol is effective in the management of [hypertensive emergencies](/wiki/Hypertensive_emergency), postoperative hypertension, [pheochromocytoma](/wiki/Pheochromocytoma)-associated hypertension, and [rebound hypertension](/wiki/Rebound_hypertension) from [beta blocker](/wiki/Beta_blocker) withdrawal.

It has a particular indication in the treatment of [pregnancy-induced hypertension](/wiki/Pregnancy-induced_hypertension) which is commonly associated with [pre-eclampsia](/wiki/Pre-eclampsia).

It is also used as an alternative in the treatment of severe hypertension.

Labetalol is useful in the treatment of acute [cardiovascular](/wiki/Cardiovascular) [toxicity](/wiki/Toxicity) (e.g. in [overdose](/wiki/Overdose)) caused by [sympathomimetics](/wiki/Sympathomimetic) like [amphetamine](/wiki/Amphetamine), [methamphetamine](/wiki/Methamphetamine), [cocaine](/wiki/Cocaine), [ephedrine](/wiki/Ephedrine), and [pseudoephedrine](/wiki/Pseudoephedrine). Other beta blockers are also used. However, the controversial yet possible phenomenon of "unopposed α-stimulation" with administration of selective beta blockers to block non-selective sympathomimetics potentially makes dual [alpha-1](/wiki/Alpha-1_blocker) and beta blockers like labetalol and [carvedilol](/wiki/Carvedilol) more favorable for such purposes. The rate of unopposed α-stimulation with selective beta blockers has been reported to be 0.4%, whereas no cases of unopposed α-stimulation have been reported with dual alpha and beta blockers like labetalol.

### Special populations

Pregnancy: studies in lab animals showed no harm to the baby. However, a comparable well-controlled study has not been performed in pregnant women.

Nursing: breast milk has been shown to contain small amounts of labetalol (0.004% original dose). Prescribers should be cautious in the use of labetalol for nursing mothers.

Pediatric: no studies have established safety or usefulness in this population.

Geriatric: the elderly are more likely to experience [dizziness](/wiki/Dizziness) when taking labetalol. Labetalol should be dosed with caution in the elderly and counseled on this side effect.

## Contraindications

Labetalol is [contraindicated](/wiki/Contraindication) in people with overt [cardiac failure](/wiki/Heart_failure), greater-than-first-degree [heart block](/wiki/Heart_block), severe [bradycardia](/wiki/Bradycardia), [cardiogenic shock](/wiki/Cardiogenic_shock), severe [hypotension](/wiki/Hypotension), anyone with a history of [obstructive airway disease](/wiki/Obstructive_lung_disease) including [asthma](/wiki/Asthma), and those with [hypersensitivity](/wiki/Drug_hypersensitivity) to the drug.

## Pharmacology

### Mechanism of action

Labetalol is a [beta blocker](/wiki/Beta_blocker), or an [antagonist](/wiki/Receptor_antagonist) of the [β-adrenergic receptors](/wiki/%CE%92-adrenergic_receptor). It is specifically a [non-selective](/wiki/Binding_selectivity) antagonist of the [β1-](/wiki/Beta-1_adrenergic_receptor) and [β2-adrenergic receptors](/wiki/Beta-2_adrenergic_receptor). Labetalol has [intrinsic sympathomimetic activity](/wiki/Intrinsic_sympathomimetic_activity). It is also an antagonist of the [α1-adrenergic receptor](/wiki/Alpha-1_adrenergic_receptor), and hence is additionally an [alpha blocker](/wiki/Alpha_blocker). The antagonism of the adrenergic receptors by labetalol is [competitive](/wiki/Competitive_antagonist) against other [catecholamines](/wiki/Catecholamine) and its actions on the receptors are [potent](/wiki/Potency_(pharmacology)) and [reversible](/wiki/Reversible_antagonist). Labetalol acts by blocking α- and β-adrenergic receptors, resulting in decreased [peripheral vascular resistance](/wiki/Peripheral_vascular_resistance) without significant alteration of [heart rate](/wiki/Heart_rate) or [cardiac output](/wiki/Cardiac_output).

Labetalol is about [equipotent](/wiki/Equipotent) in blocking β1- and β2-adrenergic receptors. The amount of α to β blockade depends on whether labetalol is administered orally or [intravenously](/wiki/Intravenous_therapy) (IV). Orally, the ratio of α to β blockade is 1:3. [Intravenously](/wiki/Intravenous_therapy), α to β blockade ratio is 1:7. Thus, labetalol can be thought to be a beta blocker with some α-blocking effects. By comparison, labetalol is a weaker β-adrenergic receptor blocker than [propranolol](/wiki/Propranolol), and has a weaker affinity for α-adrenergic receptors compared to [phentolamine](/wiki/Phentolamine).

Labetalol's dual α- and β-adrenergic antagonism has different physiological effects in short- and long-term situations. In short-term, acute situations, labetalol decreases blood pressure by decreasing [systemic vascular resistance](/wiki/Systemic_vascular_resistance) with little effect on [stroke volume](/wiki/Stroke_volume), heart rate and cardiac output. During long-term use, labetalol can reduce heart rate during exercise while maintaining [cardiac output](/wiki/Cardiac_output) by an increase in [stroke volume](/wiki/Stroke_volume).

Labetalol possesses significant intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA). In particular, it is a [partial agonist](/wiki/Partial_agonist) at β2-adrenergic receptors located in the [vascular](/wiki/Blood_vessel) [smooth muscle](/wiki/Smooth_muscle_tissue). Labetalol relaxes vascular smooth muscle by a combination of this partial β2-adrenergic receptor agonism and through α1-adrenergic receptor blockade. Overall, this [vasodilatory](/wiki/Vasodilation) effect can decrease blood pressure. It was originally reported to lack ISA, but a slight degree of activity was subsequently characterized.

Similar to [local anesthetics](/wiki/Local_anesthetic) and [sodium channel blocking](/wiki/Sodium_channel_blocker) [antiarrhythmics](/wiki/Antiarrhythmic_agent), labetalol also has [membrane stabilizing activity](/wiki/Membrane_stabilizing_effect). By decreasing sodium entry, labetalol decreases [action potential](/wiki/Action_potential) firing and thus has [local anesthetic](/wiki/Local_anesthetic) activity.

#### Physiological action

The physiological effects of labetalol when administered acutely (intravenously) are not predictable solely by their receptor blocking effect, i.e. blocking β1-adrenergic receptors should decrease heart rate, but labetalol does not. When labetalol is given in acute situations, it decreases the peripheral vascular resistance and systemic blood pressure while having little effect on the heart rate, cardiac output and stroke volume, despite its α1-, β1- and β2-adrenergic receptor blocking mechanism. These effects are mainly seen when the person is in the upright position.

Long term labetalol use also has different effects from other beta blockers. Other beta blockers, such as [propranolol](/wiki/Propranolol), persistently reduce cardiac output during exercise. The peripheral vascular resistance decreases when labetalol is first administered. Continuous labetalol use further decreases peripheral vascular resistance. However, during exercise, cardiac output remains the same due to a compensatory mechanism that increases [stroke volume](/wiki/Stroke_volume). Thus, labetalol is able to reduce heart rate during exercise while maintaining cardiac output by the increase in stroke volume.

### Pharmacokinetics

#### Distribution

Labetalol is often classified as a beta blocker with low [lipophilicity](/wiki/Lipophilicity) and hence lower potential for crossing the [blood–brain barrier](/wiki/Blood%E2%80%93brain_barrier) and [blood–placenta barrier](/wiki/Placenta). This in turn may result in fewer effects in the [central nervous system](/wiki/Central_nervous_system) as well as a lower risk of [neuropsychiatric](/wiki/Neuropsychiatric) side effects. Paradoxically however, labetalol actually shows high lipophilicity. In any case, labetalol, in [animals](/wiki/Animal_model) including rats, rabbits, and dogs, was found to cross into the brain in negligible amounts, probably for reasons other than low lipophilicity. On the other hand, the drug has been shown to cross the blood–placenta barrier in humans.

## Chemistry

The minimum requirement for [adrenergic agents](/wiki/Adrenergic_agent) is a primary or secondary amine separated from a substituted benzene ring by one or two carbons. This configuration results in strong agonist activity. As the size of the substituent attached to the amine becomes greater, particularly with respect to a t-butyl group, then the molecule typically is found to have [receptor affinity](/wiki/Receptor_affinity) without intrinsic activity, and is, therefore, an antagonist. Labetalol, with its 1-methyl-3-phenylpropyl substituted amine, is greater in size relative to a t-butyl group and therefore acts predominantly as an antagonist. The overall structure of labetalol is very polar. This was created by substituting the isopropyl group in the standard beta blocker structure with an aralkyl group, including a carboxamide group on the meta position, and by adding a hydroxyl group on the para position.

Labetalol has two [chiral](/wiki/Chiral) carbons and consequently exists as four [stereoisomers](/wiki/Stereoisomer). Two of these isomers, the (*S*,*S*)- and (*R*,*S*)- [forms](/wiki/Chirality_(chemistry)#Naming_conventions) are inactive. The third, the (*S*,*R*)-isomer, is a powerful [α1-adrenergic receptor](/wiki/Alpha-1_adrenergic_receptor) blocker. The fourth isomer, the (*R*,*R*)-isomer which is also known as dilevalol, is a mixed non-selective β-adrenergic receptor blocker and selective α1 blocker. Labetalol is typically given as a racemic mixture to achieve both α- and β-adrenergic receptor blocking activity.

The experimental [log P](/wiki/Partition_coefficient) of labetalol is 2.7 to 3.1 and its predicted log P ranges from 1.73 to 3.1. Hence, it has relatively high [lipophilicity](/wiki/Lipophilicity).

## History

Labetalol was the first drug created that combined both α- and β-adrenergic receptor blocking properties. It was created to potentially fix the compensatory reflex issue that occurred when blocking a single receptor subtype, i.e. vasoconstriction after blocking β-adrenergic receptors or tachycardia after blocking α-adrenergic receptors. Because the reflex from blocking the single receptor subtypes acted to prevent the lowering of blood pressure, it was postulated that weak blocking of both α- and β-adrenergic receptors could work together to decrease blood pressure.
