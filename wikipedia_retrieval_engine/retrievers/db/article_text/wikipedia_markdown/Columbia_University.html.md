**Columbia University in the City of New York**, commonly referred to as **Columbia University**, is a [private](/wiki/Private_university) [Ivy League](/wiki/Ivy_League) [research university](/wiki/Research_university) in [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City), United States. Established in 1754 as **King's College** by [royal charter](/wiki/Royal_charter) under [George II of Great Britain](/wiki/George_II_of_Great_Britain) on the grounds of [Trinity Church](/wiki/Trinity_Church_(Manhattan)) in [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan), it is the oldest institution of higher education in [New York](/wiki/New_York_(state)) and the fifth-[oldest in the United States](/wiki/First_university_in_the_United_States).

The university was renamed [Columbia College](/wiki/Columbia_College_(New_York)) in 1784 following the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution), and in 1787 was placed under [a private board of trustees](/wiki/Trustees_of_Columbia_University_in_the_City_of_New_York) headed by former students [Alexander Hamilton](/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton) and [John Jay](/wiki/John_Jay). In 1896, the campus was moved to its current location and renamed Columbia University.

Columbia is organized into 17 schools, two affiliated colleges—[Barnard College](/wiki/Barnard_College) and [Teachers College](/wiki/Teachers_College,_Columbia_University)—and two affiliated seminaries—[Jewish Theological Seminary](/wiki/Jewish_Theological_Seminary_of_America) (JTS) and [Union Theological Seminary](/wiki/Union_Theological_Seminary) (UTS). The university's research efforts include the [Lamont–Doherty Earth Observatory](/wiki/Lamont%E2%80%93Doherty_Earth_Observatory), the [Goddard Institute for Space Studies](/wiki/Goddard_Institute_for_Space_Studies), and accelerator laboratories with [Big Tech](/wiki/Big_Tech) firms such as [Amazon](/wiki/Amazon_(company)) and [IBM](/wiki/IBM). Columbia is a founding member of the [Association of American Universities](/wiki/Association_of_American_Universities) and was the first school in the United States to grant the [MD degree](/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine). The university also supports the administration of the annual [Pulitzer Prize](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize), following the independent Pulitzer Prize Board's decisions.

Columbia scientists and scholars have played a pivotal role in scientific breakthroughs including [brain–computer interface](/wiki/Brain%E2%80%93computer_interface); the [laser](/wiki/Laser) and [maser](/wiki/Maser); [nuclear magnetic resonance](/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance); the first [nuclear pile](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor); the first [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission) reaction in the [Americas](/wiki/Americas); the first evidence for [plate tectonics](/wiki/Plate_tectonics) and [continental drift](/wiki/Continental_drift); and much of the initial research and planning for the [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project) during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II).

As of December 2021, its alumni, faculty, and staff have included 7 of the [Founding Fathers](/wiki/Founding_Fathers_of_the_United_States) of the United States of America; [4 U.S. presidents](/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_United_States_by_education); 34 foreign [heads of state or government](/wiki/Head_of_state); 2 secretaries-general of the United Nations; 10 justices of the [United States Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States); [103 Nobel laureates](/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates_affiliated_with_Columbia_University_as_alumni_or_faculty); 125 [National Academy of Sciences](/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) members; 53 living billionaires; [23 Olympic medalists](/wiki/List_of_American_universities_with_Olympic_medals); 33 [Academy Award](/wiki/Academy_Awards) winners; and 125 Pulitzer Prize recipients.

## History

Main article: [History of Columbia University](/wiki/History_of_Columbia_University)

### 18th century

[Samuel Johnson](/wiki/Samuel_Johnson_(American_educator)), the first [president of Columbia](/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_Columbia_University)

Discussions regarding the founding of a college in the [Province of New York](/wiki/Province_of_New_York) began as early as 1704.

Classes were initially held in July 1754 and were presided over by the college's first president, [Samuel Johnson](/wiki/Samuel_Johnson_(American_educator)). The college was officially founded on October 31, 1754, as King's College by royal charter of [George II](/wiki/George_II_of_Great_Britain), making it the oldest institution of higher learning in the [State of New York](/wiki/State_of_New_York) and the fifth oldest in the United States.

In 1763, Johnson was succeeded in the presidency by [Myles Cooper](/wiki/Myles_Cooper), a graduate of [The Queen's College, Oxford](/wiki/The_Queen%27s_College,_Oxford), and an ardent [Tory](/wiki/Tories_(British_political_party)). In the charged political climate of the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution), his chief opponent in discussions at the college was an undergraduate of the class of 1777, [Alexander Hamilton](/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton). The [Irish](/wiki/Irish_people) anatomist, [Samuel Clossy](/wiki/Samuel_Clossy), was appointed professor of natural philosophy in October 1765 and later the college's first professor of anatomy in 1767.

King's College Hall in 1790

Following the start of the [American Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) and the arrival of [Continental Army](/wiki/Continental_Army) in 1776, the school suspended instruction for eight years. The suspension continued through the military occupation of New York City by British troops until their [departure](/wiki/Evacuation_Day_(New_York)) in 1783. During this time, the college's library was looted and its sole building requisitioned for use as a military hospital first by American and then British forces.

The 1797 [Taylor Map](/wiki/Cartography_of_New_York_City) of [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City), showing "The Colledge [sic]" at its Park Place (then Robinson Street) location and its earlier location, [Trinity Church](/wiki/Trinity_Church_(Manhattan)), on the lower left

The legislature agreed to assist the college. On May 1, 1784, it passed "an Act for granting certain privileges to the College heretofore called King's College". The Act created [a board of regents](/wiki/Board_of_Regents_of_the_University_of_the_State_of_New_York) to oversee the resuscitation of King's College. In an effort to demonstrate its support for the new Republic, the legislature stipulated that "the College within the City of New York heretofore called King's College be forever hereafter called and known by the name of [Columbia College](/wiki/Columbia_College_of_Columbia_University)." In February 1787, the Regents appointed a revision committee, which was headed by [John Jay](/wiki/John_Jay) and Alexander Hamilton. In April of that same year, a new charter was adopted for the college granted the power to [a separate board of 24 trustees](/wiki/Trustees_of_Columbia_University_in_the_City_of_New_York).

For a period in the 1790s, with New York City as the federal and state capital and the country under successive [Federalist](/wiki/Federalist_Party_(United_States)) governments, a revived Columbia operated under Federalists such as Hamilton and Jay. President [George Washington](/wiki/George_Washington) and Vice President [John Adams](/wiki/John_Adams), in addition to both houses of [Congress](/wiki/United_States_Congress) attended [the college's commencement](/wiki/Columbia_University_commencement) on May 6, 1789, as a tribute of honor to the alumni of the school who had been involved in the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution).

The library and law school buildings, both constructed in a Gothic and Revival style, on the [Madison Avenue](/wiki/Madison_Avenue) campus

### 19th century

[Low Memorial Library](/wiki/Low_Memorial_Library), c. 1900

*[Alma Mater](/wiki/Alma_Mater_(New_York_sculpture))*, by [Daniel Chester French](/wiki/Daniel_Chester_French) (1903)

In 1813, the college incorporated its medical school with The College of Physicians and Surgeons, a new school created by the Regents of New York, forming [Columbia University College of Physicians and Surgeons](/wiki/Columbia_University_College_of_Physicians_and_Surgeons). In 1857, the college moved from the King's College campus at Park Place to a primarily [Gothic Revival](/wiki/Gothic_Revival) campus on 49th Street and [Madison Avenue](/wiki/Madison_Avenue), where it remained for the next forty years.

During the last half of the 19th century, under the presidency of [Frederick A. P. Barnard](/wiki/Frederick_A._P._Barnard), for whom [Barnard College](/wiki/Barnard_College) is named, the institution rapidly assumed the shape of a modern university. Barnard College was created in 1889 as a response to the university's refusal to accept women.

In 1896, university president [Seth Low](/wiki/Seth_Low) moved the campus from 49th Street to its present location, a more spacious campus in the developing neighborhood of [Morningside Heights](/wiki/Morningside_Heights).

### 20th century

See also: [Columbia University protests of 1968](/wiki/Columbia_University_protests_of_1968) and [Student activism at Columbia University](/wiki/Student_activism_at_Columbia_University)

In March 1917, as a result of America's entrance into [World War 1](/wiki/World_War_1), Columbia president Nicholas Murray Butler formed the Columbia Reserve Officers Training Corps. All students over the age of 18 who passed a physical exam were entered into this program. Within 4 months, over 1,400 students were enrolled within the program. They were one of 12 universities designated by the War department to create a naval section for the Student Army Training Corps. As part of this new program, the US Army charged Columbia to create a "War Issues" course. In 1919, a similar course was created for Columbia students, a "peace issues" course that evolved into "Introduction to Contemporary Civilization in the West", part of the university's [core curriculum for undergraduate students](/wiki/Core_Curriculum_(Columbia_College)).

In 1919, Columbia established a student application process characterized by *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* as "the first modern college application".

In 1928, [Seth Low Junior College](/wiki/Seth_Low_Junior_College) was established by Columbia University in order to mitigate the number of Jewish applicants to Columbia College. The college was closed in 1936 due to the adverse effects of the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression) and its students were subsequently taught at the Morningside Heights campus, although they did not belong to any college but to the university at large.

In the 1940s, faculty members, including [John R. Dunning](/wiki/John_R._Dunning), [Isidor Isaac Rabi](/wiki/Isidor_Isaac_Rabi), [Enrico Fermi](/wiki/Enrico_Fermi), and [Polykarp Kusch](/wiki/Polykarp_Kusch), began what became the [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project), creating the first nuclear fission reactor in the [Americas](/wiki/Americas) and researching [gaseous diffusion](/wiki/Gaseous_diffusion).

In the aftermath of World War II, the discipline of international relations became a major scholarly focus of the university, and in response, the [School of International and Public Affairs](/wiki/Columbia_University_School_of_International_and_Public_Affairs) was founded in 1946, drawing upon the resources of the faculties of political science, economics, and history.

In 1947, University Extension, an evening school program open to all for a fee, was reorganized as an undergraduate college and designated the [School of General Studies](/wiki/School_of_General_Studies) in response to the return of [GIs](/wiki/GI_(military)) after [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II).

The [Columbia University Bicentennial](/wiki/Columbia_University_Bicentennial) was celebrated in 1954.

During the 1960s, student activism reached a climax with [protests in the spring of 1968](/wiki/Columbia_University_protests_of_1968), when hundreds of students occupied buildings on campus. The incident forced the resignation of Columbia's president, [Grayson Kirk](/wiki/Grayson_Kirk), and the establishment of the University Senate.

Though several schools in the university had admitted women for years, Columbia College first admitted women in the fall of 1983, after a decade of failed negotiations with [Barnard College](/wiki/Barnard_College), the all-female institution affiliated with the university, to merge the two schools. Barnard College still remains affiliated with Columbia, and all Barnard graduates are issued diplomas signed by the [presidents of Columbia University](/wiki/President_of_Columbia_University) and Barnard College.

During the late 20th century, the university underwent significant academic, structural, and administrative changes as it developed into a major research university. For much of the 19th century, the university consisted of decentralized and separate faculties specializing in Political Science, Philosophy, and Pure Science. In 1979, these faculties were merged into the [Graduate School of Arts and Sciences](/wiki/Columbia_Graduate_School_of_Arts_and_Sciences). In 1991, the faculties of Columbia College, the School of General Studies, the Graduate School of Arts and Sciences, the [School of the Arts](/wiki/Columbia_University_School_of_the_Arts), and the [School of Professional Studies](/wiki/Columbia_University_School_of_Professional_Studies) were merged into the Faculty of Arts and Sciences, leading to the academic integration and centralized governance of these schools.

In 1995, the Division of Special Programs, later called the School of Continuing Education and now the [School of Professional Studies](/wiki/Columbia_University_School_of_Professional_Studies), was established to reprise the former role of University Extension.

### 21st century

#### Bollinger presidency (2002–2023)

[Lee C. Bollinger](/wiki/Lee_Bollinger) became Columbia's 19th president in June 2002, succeeding George Rupp. Bollinger was appointed in October 2001 after arriving from the presidency of the University of Michigan. His presidency emphasized campus expansion, defense of affirmative action in college admissions, support for government funding of private sector media, and opposition to disinformation.

Under Bollinger's tenure, the university publicly launched a record $4 billion capital campaign in September 2006. Financial aid was improved, eliminating loans for undergraduates from families earning under $50,000, and additional funds were allocated to support Bollinger's efforts toward the Manhattanville campus expansion, which nearly doubled Columbia's physical footprint. This expansion was supported by a major gift from trustee [Gerry Lenfest](/wiki/Gerry_Lenfest).

Globalization efforts continued with the World Leaders Forum and the creation of the Committee on Global Thought, chaired by [Joseph Stiglitz](/wiki/Joseph_Stiglitz).

The [2008 financial crisis](/wiki/2008_financial_crisis) impacted Columbia's endowment. The endowment recovered, hitting $8.2B in Oct 2013. Despite the downturn, the university pressed on with Manhattanville construction, receiving final state approval in June 2009.

In May 2011, following the repeal of "Don't Ask, Don't Tell," the University Senate voted 51–17 to invite [ROTC](/wiki/Reserve_Officers%27_Training_Corps) back after a 40-year absence, and Bollinger announced an agreement with the Navy. Columbia has maintained its [Global Centers](/wiki/Columbia_Global_Centers) network (Amman, Athens, Beijing, Mumbai, Paris, Nairobi, Istanbul, Santiago, Rio de Janeiro, Tunis, Tel Aviv), aiming to increase global engagement and international student enrollment.

From 2014 to 2021, Columbia University pursued significant physical expansion, notably opening major facilities on the Manhattanville campus (ZMBBI, Lenfest Center, The Forum). Key strategic initiatives launched included the [Knight First Amendment Institute](/wiki/Knight_First_Amendment_Institute_v._Trump), Columbia World Projects, and the new [Columbia Climate School](/wiki/Columbia_Climate_School) (2020). A $5 billion university capital campaign was launched (with a $1.5B A&S target), major gifts like $50M for A&S's Uris Hall renovation were secured, and the endowment grew significantly ($14.35B by mid-2021).

In February 2022, Columbia's mathematics professor [Michael Thaddeus](/wiki/Michael_Thaddeus) published a website and argued that data submitted by Columbia for U.S. News college rankings was "inaccurate, dubious or highly misleading". In July 2022, *[U.S. News & World Report](/wiki/U.S._News_%26_World_Report)* unranked Columbia citing failed verification. In September 2022, Columbia acknowledged the submission of incorrect data to U.S. News college rankings. In June 2023, Columbia announced its non-participation in the rankings. In July 2025, Columbia offered to pay US$9 million to settle a class-action lawsuit by its students in [Manhattan federal court](/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Southern_District_of_New_York) over the alleged submission of false data to boost its U.S. News college rankings.

#### Shafik presidency (2023–2024)

In January 2023, Columbia University announced that Minouche Shafik would be the school's 20th president. Previously, Shafik served as president of the London School of Economics and Political Science. Shafik was inaugurated in October 2023.

A tent in the [Gaza Solidarity Encampment](/wiki/Gaza_Solidarity_Encampment_(Columbia_University)) (April 17-30, 2024), part of a [broader protest movement](/wiki/Columbia_University_pro-Palestinian_campus_protests_during_the_Gaza_war) at the university during the [Gaza war](/wiki/Gaza_war) and [genocide](/wiki/Gaza_genocide), April 17, 2024.

Beginning in fall 2023, [protests at Columbia University](/wiki/Columbia_University_pro-Palestinian_campus_protests_and_occupations_during_the_Gaza_war) against the [Gaza war](/wiki/Gaza_war) and what student protesters described as Columbia's complicity in the [Gaza genocide](/wiki/Gaza_genocide), resulted in police action, student disciplinary measures, and the appointment of [Katrina Armstrong](/wiki/Katrina_Armstrong) as interim president in August 2024 after the resignation of former university president [Minouche Shafik](/wiki/Minouche_Shafik). The federal government also launched an [investigation into antisemitism](/wiki/Antisemitism_at_Columbia_University#During_the_Gaza_war) and then withdrew funding, prompting the university to adopt new policies and adjust disciplinary processes.

Claire Shipman was appointed acting president in March 2025. Columbia also closed its Morningside Heights campus to the general public, allowing access only to holders of Columbia ID cards and approved guests. In July 2025, Columbia finalized a [$220 million settlement](/wiki/Columbia_University%27s_settlement_with_the_Trump_administration) to resume federal funding, agreeing to implement a set of reforms to address anti-semitism. The university also adopted the [IHRA definition](/wiki/IHRA_definition_of_antisemitism) of antisemitism.

In January 2026, the university announced that it had chosen [Jennifer Mnookin](/wiki/Jennifer_Mnookin), the chancellor of the [University of Wisconsin-Madison](/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin-Madison), as the next president of Columbia, effective July 1, 2026.

In March 2026, a $481 million ruling that Columbia University had won against software company Gen Digital for infringing patents related to cybersecurity technology was overturned. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit stated that Columbia's patents may be invalid and sent the case back to Virginia federal court.

## Campus

### Morningside Heights

College Walk

The majority of Columbia's graduate and undergraduate studies are conducted in the [Upper Manhattan](/wiki/Upper_Manhattan) neighborhood of [Morningside Heights](/wiki/Morningside_Heights,_Manhattan) on [Seth Low](/wiki/Seth_Low)'s late-19th century vision of a university campus where all disciplines could be taught at one location. The campus was designed along [Beaux-Arts](/wiki/Beaux-Arts_architecture) planning principles by the architects [McKim, Mead & White](/wiki/McKim,_Mead_%26_White). Columbia's main campus occupies more than six [city blocks](/wiki/City_block), or 32 acres (13 ha), in Morningside Heights, New York City, a neighborhood that contains a number of academic institutions.

The university owns over 7,800 apartments in Morningside Heights, housing faculty, graduate students, and staff. Almost two dozen undergraduate dormitories (purpose-built or converted) are located on campus or in Morningside Heights. Columbia University has [an extensive tunnel system](/wiki/Columbia_University_tunnels), more than a century old, with the oldest portions predating the present campus. Some of these remain accessible to the public, while others have been cordoned off.

[Butler Library](/wiki/Butler_Library)

[Butler Library](/wiki/Butler_Library) is the largest in the [Columbia University Libraries](/wiki/Columbia_University_Libraries) system and one of the largest buildings on the campus. It was completed in 1934 and renamed to Butler Library in 1946. As of 2020, Columbia's library system includes over 15.0 million volumes, making it the eighth largest library system and fifth largest collegiate library system in the United States.

Several buildings on the Morningside Heights campus are listed on the [National Register of Historic Places](/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places). [Low Memorial Library](/wiki/Low_Memorial_Library), a [National Historic Landmark](/wiki/National_Historic_Landmark) and the centerpiece of the campus, is listed for its architectural significance. [Philosophy Hall](/wiki/Philosophy_Hall) is listed as the site of the invention of [FM radio](/wiki/FM_radio). Also listed is [Pupin Hall](/wiki/Pupin_Hall), another [National Historic Landmark](/wiki/National_Historic_Landmark), which houses the physics and astronomy departments. Here the first experiments on the fission of uranium were conducted by [Enrico Fermi](/wiki/Enrico_Fermi). The uranium atom was split there ten days after the world's first atom-splitting in [Copenhagen](/wiki/Copenhagen), Denmark. Other buildings listed include [Casa Italiana](/wiki/Casa_Italiana), the [Delta Psi, Alpha Chapter building](/wiki/Delta_Psi,_Alpha_Chapter_building) of [St. Anthony Hall](/wiki/St._Anthony_Hall), [Earl Hall](/wiki/Earl_Hall), and the buildings of the affiliated [Union Theological Seminary](/wiki/Union_Theological_Seminary_(New_York_City)).

[Union Theological Seminary](/wiki/Union_Theological_Seminary_in_the_City_of_New_York)

A statue by sculptor [Daniel Chester French](/wiki/Daniel_Chester_French) called *[Alma Mater](/wiki/Alma_Mater_(New_York_sculpture))* is centered on the front steps of [Low Memorial Library](/wiki/Low_Memorial_Library). The statue represents a personification of the traditional image of the university as an *[alma mater](/wiki/Alma_mater)*, or "nourishing mother", draped in an academic gown and seated on a throne. She wears a [laurel wreath](/wiki/Laurel_wreath) on her head and holds in her right hand a scepter capped by a King's Crown, a traditional symbol of the university. A book, representing learning, rests on her lap. The arms of her throne end in lamps, representing "Sapientia et Doctrina", or "Wisdom and Learning"; on the back of the throne is embossed an image of [the seal of the university](/wiki/Heraldry_of_Columbia_University).

The small hidden owl on the sculpture is also the subject of many Columbia legends, the main legend being that the first student in the freshmen class to find the hidden owl on the statue will be valedictorian, and that any subsequent Columbia male who finds it will marry a Barnard student, given that Barnard is a [women's college](/wiki/Women%27s_college).

"The Steps", alternatively known as "Low Steps" or the "Urban Beach", are a popular meeting area for Columbia students. The term refers to the long series of granite steps leading from the lower part of campus (South Field) to its upper terrace.

### Other campuses

Lamont Campus entrance in [Palisades](/wiki/Palisades,_New_York), New York

The entrance to the [College of Physicians and Surgeons](/wiki/Columbia_University_Vagelos_College_of_Physicians_and_Surgeons) in [Washington Heights](/wiki/Washington_Heights,_Manhattan)

In April 2007, the university purchased more than two-thirds of a 17 acres (6.9 ha) site for a new campus in [Manhattanville](/wiki/Manhattanville), an industrial neighborhood to the north of the Morningside Heights campus. Stretching from [125th Street](/wiki/125th_Street_(Manhattan)) to [133rd Street](/wiki/133rd_Street_(Manhattan)), Columbia Manhattanville houses buildings for Columbia's Business School, School of International and Public Affairs, Columbia School of the Arts, and the Jerome L. Greene Center for Mind, Brain, and Behavior, where research will occur on neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's and Alzheimer's.

The $7 billion expansion plan included demolishing all buildings, except three that are historically significant (the [Studebaker Building](/wiki/Studebaker_Building_(Columbia_University)), [Prentis Hall](/wiki/Prentis_Hall), and the Nash Building), eliminating the existing light industry and storage warehouses, and relocating tenants in 132 apartments. Replacing these buildings created 6.8 million square feet (630,000 m2) of space for the university. Community activist groups in West Harlem fought the expansion for reasons ranging from property protection and fair exchange for land, to residents' rights.

Subsequent public hearings drew neighborhood opposition. As of December 2008, the State of New York's [Empire State Development Corporation](/wiki/Empire_State_Development_Corporation) approved use of eminent domain, which, through declaration of Manhattanville's "blighted" status, gives governmental bodies the right to appropriate private property for public use. On May 20, 2009, the [New York State Public Authorities Control Board](/wiki/New_York_State_Public_Authorities_Control_Board) approved the Manhanttanville expansion plan.

[NewYork-Presbyterian Hospital](/wiki/NewYork-Presbyterian_Hospital) is affiliated with the medical schools of both Columbia University and [Cornell University](/wiki/Cornell_University). According to *U.S. News & World Report*'s "2020–21 Best Hospitals Honor Roll and Medical Specialties Rankings", it is ranked fourth overall and second among university hospitals. Columbia's [medical school](/wiki/Columbia_University_Vagelos_College_of_Physicians_and_Surgeons) has a strategic partnership with [New York State Psychiatric Institute](/wiki/New_York_State_Psychiatric_Institute), and is affiliated with 19 other hospitals in the U.S. and four hospitals in other countries.

Health-related schools are located at the [Columbia University Medical Center](/wiki/Columbia_University_Medical_Center), a 20-acre (8.1 ha) campus located in the neighborhood of [Washington Heights](/wiki/Washington_Heights,_Manhattan), fifty blocks uptown. Other teaching hospitals affiliated with Columbia through the NewYork-Presbyterian network include the Payne Whitney Clinic in Manhattan, and the Payne Whitney Westchester, a psychiatric institute located in White Plains, New York.

On the northern tip of Manhattan island (in the neighborhood of [Inwood](/wiki/Inwood,_Manhattan)), Columbia owns the 26-acre (11 ha) Baker Field, which includes the [Lawrence A. Wien Stadium](/wiki/Lawrence_A._Wien_Stadium) as well as facilities for field sports, outdoor track, and tennis. There is a third campus on the west bank of the [Hudson River](/wiki/Hudson_River), the 157-acre (64 ha) [Lamont–Doherty Earth Observatory](/wiki/Lamont%E2%80%93Doherty_Earth_Observatory) and Earth Institute in [Palisades](/wiki/Palisades,_New_York), New York. A fourth is the 60-acre (24 ha) [Nevis Laboratories](/wiki/Nevis_Laboratories) in [Irvington, New York](/wiki/Irvington,_New_York), for the study of particle and motion physics. A satellite site in Paris holds classes at [Reid Hall](/wiki/Reid_Hall).

### Sustainability

In 2006, the university established the Office of Environmental Stewardship to initiate, coordinate and implement programs to reduce the university's environmental footprint. The U.S. Green Building Council selected the university's Manhattanville plan for the [Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design](/wiki/Leadership_in_Energy_and_Environmental_Design) (LEED) Neighborhood Design pilot program.

Columbia has been rated "B+" by the 2011 College Sustainability Report Card for its environmental and sustainability initiatives.

Access to Columbia is enhanced by the [116th Street–Columbia University](/wiki/116th_Street%E2%80%93Columbia_University_(IRT_Broadway%E2%80%93Seventh_Avenue_Line)) subway station ([1](/wiki/1_(New_York_City_Subway_service)) train) on the [IRT Broadway–Seventh Avenue Line](/wiki/IRT_Broadway%E2%80%93Seventh_Avenue_Line).

According to the [A. W. Kuchler](/wiki/A._W._Kuchler) U.S. [potential natural vegetation](/wiki/Potential_natural_vegetation) types, Columbia University would have a dominant vegetation type of Appalachian [Oak](/wiki/Oak) (*104*) with a dominant vegetation form of Eastern [Hardwood](/wiki/Hardwood) Forest (*25*).

### Transportation

[Columbia Transportation](/wiki/Columbia_Transportation) is the bus service of the university, operated by [Academy Bus Lines](/wiki/Academy_Bus_Lines). The buses are open to all Columbia faculty, students, Dodge Fitness Center members, and anyone else who holds a Columbia ID card. In addition, all [TSC](/wiki/The_School_at_Columbia) students can ride the buses.

In the [New York City Subway](/wiki/New_York_City_Subway), the  train serves the university at [116th Street-Columbia University](/wiki/116th_Street%E2%80%93Columbia_University_station). The [M4](/wiki/M4_(New_York_City_bus)), [M104](/wiki/M104_(New_York_City_bus)) and [M60](/wiki/M60_(New_York_City_bus)) buses stop on Broadway while the [M11](/wiki/M11_(New_York_City_bus)) stops on Amsterdam Avenue.

The main campus is primarily boxed off by the streets of Amsterdam Avenue, [Broadway](/wiki/Broadway_(Manhattan)), 114th street, and 120th street, with some buildings, including Barnard College, located just outside the area. The nearest major highway is the [Henry Hudson Parkway](/wiki/Henry_Hudson_Parkway) ([NY 9A](/wiki/New_York_State_Route_9A)) to the west of the campus. It is located 3.4 miles (5.5 km) south of the [George Washington Bridge](/wiki/George_Washington_Bridge).

Columbia University is an independent, privately supported, nonsectarian and [not-for-profit](/wiki/Not-for-profit) institution of higher education. Its official corporate name is [Trustees of Columbia University in the City of New York](/wiki/Trustees_of_Columbia_University_in_the_City_of_New_York).

Joint degree programs are available through [Union Theological Seminary](/wiki/Union_Theological_Seminary_in_the_City_of_New_York), the [Jewish Theological Seminary of America](/wiki/Jewish_Theological_Seminary_of_America), and the [Juilliard School](/wiki/Juilliard_School). [Teachers College](/wiki/Teachers_College) and [Barnard College](/wiki/Barnard_College) are official faculties of the university; both colleges' presidents are deans under the university governance structure. The Columbia University Senate includes faculty and student representatives from Teachers College and Barnard College who serve two-year terms; all senators are accorded full voting privileges regarding matters impacting the entire university. Teachers College is an affiliated, financially independent graduate school with their own board of trustees.

Pursuant to an affiliation agreement, Columbia is given the authority to confer "degrees and diplomas" to the graduates of Teachers College. The degrees are signed by presidents of Teachers College and Columbia University in a manner analogous to the university's other graduate schools. Columbia's General Studies school also has joint undergraduate programs available through [University College London](/wiki/University_College_London), [Sciences Po](/wiki/Sciences_Po), [City University of Hong Kong](/wiki/City_University_of_Hong_Kong), [Trinity College Dublin](/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin), and the [Juilliard School](/wiki/Juilliard_School).

The university also has several [Columbia Global Centers](/wiki/Columbia_Global_Centers), in [Amman](/wiki/Amman), [Beijing](/wiki/Beijing), [Istanbul](/wiki/Istanbul), [Mumbai](/wiki/Mumbai), [Nairobi](/wiki/Nairobi), Paris, [Rio de Janeiro](/wiki/Rio_de_Janeiro), [Santiago](/wiki/Santiago), and [Tunis](/wiki/Tunis).

### Research

[Havemeyer Hall](/wiki/Havemeyer_Hall), a [National Historic Chemical Landmark](/wiki/National_Historic_Chemical_Landmarks), where [deuterium](/wiki/Deuterium) was discovered in 1931. Research conducted in Havemeyer has been recognized with seven [Nobel Prizes in Chemistry](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry).

Columbia University has been the site of many scientific milestones, including the first nuclear fission in North America, the invention of FM radio and the laser, and key contributions to neuroscience. Researchers at Columbia have also advanced efforts in technology, such as digital media, cellular imaging, and brain-computer interfaces.

Columbia is [classified](/wiki/Carnegie_Classification_of_Institutions_of_Higher_Education) among "R1: Doctoral Universities – Very high research activity". Columbia scientists have been credited with about 175 new inventions in the health sciences each year. Columbia is affiliated with many research facilities, such as the [Columbia Institute for Tele-Information](/wiki/Columbia_Institute_for_Tele-Information) dedicated to [telecommunications](/wiki/Telecommunications) and the [Goddard Institute for Space Studies](/wiki/Goddard_Institute_for_Space_Studies), which is an [astronomical](/wiki/Astronomy) [observatory](/wiki/Observatory) affiliated with [NASA](/wiki/NASA).

### Military and veteran enrollment

Columbia is a long-standing participant of the [United States Department of Veterans Affairs](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Veterans_Affairs) Yellow Ribbon Program, allowing eligible veterans and their dependents to pursue a Columbia undergraduate degree regardless of socioeconomic status for over 70 years. As a part of the Eisenhower Leader Development Program (ELDP) in partnership with the [United States Military Academy](/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy) at [West Point](/wiki/West_Point,_New_York), Columbia is the only school in the Ivy League to offer a graduate degree program in organizational psychology to aid military officers in tactical decision making and strategic management.

In 2020, Columbia University's student population was 31,455 (8,842 students in undergraduate programs and 22,613 in postgraduate programs), with 45% of the student population identifying themselves as a minority. Twenty-six percent of students at Columbia have family incomes below $60,000. 16% of students at Columbia receive Federal Pell Grants, which mostly go to students whose family incomes are below $40,000. Seventeen percent of students are the first member of their family to attend a four-year college.

Columbia University is home to many [fraternities, sororities](/wiki/Fraternities_and_sororities_in_North_America), and co-educational Greek organizations. Approximately 10–15% of undergraduate students are associated with Greek life. Many Barnard women also join Columbia sororities. There has been a Greek presence on campus since the establishment in 1836 of the Delta chapter of [Alpha Delta Phi](/wiki/Alpha_Delta_Phi).

### Publications

Copies of the *[Columbia Daily Spectator](/wiki/Columbia_Daily_Spectator)* being sold during the [1962–1963 New York City newspaper strike](/wiki/1962%E2%80%931963_New_York_City_newspaper_strike)

The [Art Deco](/wiki/Art_Deco) cover of the November 1931 edition of the [*Jester*](/wiki/Jester_of_Columbia), celebrating the opening of the [George Washington Bridge](/wiki/George_Washington_Bridge)

The *[Columbia Daily Spectator](/wiki/Columbia_Daily_Spectator)* is the nation's second-oldest continuously operating daily student newspaper. *[The Blue and White](/wiki/The_Blue_and_White)* is a monthly literary magazine established in 1890 that discusses campus life and local politics. *[Bwog](/wiki/Bwog)*, originally an offshoot of *The Blue and White* but now fully independent, is an online campus news and entertainment source. *[The Morningside Post](/wiki/The_Morningside_Post)* is a student-run multimedia news publication.

Political publications include *[The Current](/wiki/The_Current_(Columbia_University_journal))*, a journal of politics, culture and Jewish Affairs; the *[Columbia Political Review](/wiki/Columbia_Political_Review)*, the multi-partisan political magazine of the Columbia Political Union; and *AdHoc*, which denotes itself as the "progressive" campus magazine and deals largely with local political issues and arts events.

*Columbia Magazine* is the alumni magazine of Columbia, serving all 340,000+ of the university's alumni. Arts and literary publications include *The Columbia Review*, the nation's oldest college literary magazine; *Surgam*, the literary magazine of [The Philolexian Society](/wiki/The_Philolexian_Society); *Quarto*, Columbia University's official undergraduate literary magazine; *4x4*, a student-run alternative to *Quarto*; *Columbia*, a nationally regarded [literary journal](/wiki/Literary_journal); the *Columbia Journal of Literary Criticism*; and *The Mobius Strip*, an online arts and literary magazine. *[Inside New York](/wiki/Inside_New_York)* is an annual guidebook to New York City, written, edited, and published by Columbia undergraduates. Through a distribution agreement with [Columbia University Press](/wiki/Columbia_University_Press), the book is sold at major retailers and independent bookstores.

Columbia is home to numerous undergraduate academic publications. The *Columbia Undergraduate Science Journal* prints original science research in its two annual publications. The *[Journal of Politics & Society](/wiki/Journal_of_Politics_%26_Society)* is a journal of undergraduate research in the social sciences; *Publius* is an undergraduate journal of politics established in 2008 and published biannually; the *Columbia East Asia Review* allows undergraduates throughout the world to publish original work on China, Japan, Korea, Tibet, and Vietnam and is supported by the [Weatherhead East Asian Institute](/wiki/Weatherhead_East_Asian_Institute); *[The Birch](/wiki/The_Birch)* is an undergraduate journal of Eastern European and Eurasian culture that is the first national student-run journal of its kind; the *Columbia Economics Review* is the undergraduate economic journal on research and policy supported by the Columbia Economics Department; and the *Columbia Science Review* is a science magazine that prints general interest articles and faculty profiles.

Humor publications on Columbia's campus include *[The Fed](/wiki/The_Fed_(Columbia_newspaper))*, a triweekly satire and investigative newspaper, and the *[Jester of Columbia](/wiki/Jester_of_Columbia).* Other publications include *The Columbian*, the undergraduate colleges' annually published yearbook; the *Gadfly*, a biannual journal of popular philosophy produced by undergraduates; and *Rhapsody in Blue*, an undergraduate urban studies magazine. Professional journals published by academic departments at Columbia University include *Current Musicology* and *[The Journal of Philosophy](/wiki/The_Journal_of_Philosophy)*. During the spring semester, graduate students in the Journalism School publish *The Bronx Beat*, a bi-weekly newspaper covering the South Bronx.

Founded in 1961 under the auspices of Columbia University's Graduate School of Journalism, the *[Columbia Journalism Review](/wiki/Columbia_Journalism_Review)* (CJR) examines day-to-day press performance as well as the forces that affect that performance. The magazine is published six times a year.

Former publications include the *Columbia University Forum*, a review of literature and cultural affairs distributed for free to alumni.

### Broadcasting

Columbia is home to two pioneers in undergraduate [campus radio](/wiki/Campus_radio) broadcasting, [WKCR-FM](/wiki/WKCR-FM) and CTV. Many undergraduates are also involved with Barnard's radio station, [WBAR](/wiki/WBAR_(Barnard_College)). WKCR, the student run radio station that broadcasts to the Tri-state area, claims to be the oldest FM radio station in the world, owing to the university's affiliation with [Edwin Howard Armstrong](/wiki/Edwin_Howard_Armstrong). The station has its studios on the second floor of Alfred Lerner Hall on the Morningside campus with its main transmitter tower at [4 Times Square](/wiki/4_Times_Square) in [Midtown Manhattan](/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan). Columbia Television (CTV) is the nation's second oldest [student television station](/wiki/Student_television_station) and the home of CTV News, a weekly live news program produced by undergraduate students.

### Debate and Model UN

The [Philolexian Society](/wiki/Philolexian_Society) is a literary and debating club founded in 1802, making it the oldest student group at Columbia, as well as the third oldest collegiate literary society in the country. The society annually administers the [Joyce Kilmer Memorial Bad Poetry Contest](/wiki/Joyce_Kilmer_Memorial_Bad_Poetry_Contest). The Columbia Parliamentary Debate Team competes in tournaments around the country as part of the [American Parliamentary Debate Association](/wiki/American_Parliamentary_Debate_Association), and hosts both high school and college tournaments on Columbia's campus, as well as public debates on issues affecting the university.

The Columbia International Relations Council and Association (CIRCA), oversees Columbia's [Model United Nations](/wiki/Model_United_Nations) activities. CIRCA hosts college and high school Model UN conferences, hosts speakers influential in international politics to speak on campus, and trains students from underprivileged schools in New York in Model UN.

### Technology and entrepreneurship

[Pupin Hall](/wiki/Pupin_Hall), the physics building, showing the rooftop [Rutherfurd Observatory](/wiki/Rutherfurd_Observatory)

Many engineering entrepreneurs in New York City have attended Columbia. Since 1990, graduates of Columbia have established more than 100 technology companies.

The student-run group Columbia University Organization of Rising Entrepreneurs (CORE) was founded in 1999 with an aim to foster entrepreneurship on campus. Each year CORE hosts events, including #StartupColumbia, a conference and venture competition for $250,000, and Ignite@CU, a weekend for undergrads interested in design, engineering, and entrepreneurship. Notable speakers include [Peter Thiel](/wiki/Peter_Thiel), [Jack Dorsey](/wiki/Jack_Dorsey), [Alexis Ohanian](/wiki/Alexis_Ohanian), [Drew Houston](/wiki/Drew_Houston), and [Mark Cuban](/wiki/Mark_Cuban).

CampusNetwork, an on-campus social networking site called Campus Network that preceded Facebook, was created and popularized by Columbia engineering student Adam Goldberg in 2003. [Mark Zuckerberg](/wiki/Mark_Zuckerberg) later asked Goldberg to join him in [Palo Alto](/wiki/Palo_Alto) to work on Facebook, but Goldberg declined the offer. The [Fu Foundation School of Engineering and Applied Science](/wiki/Fu_Foundation_School_of_Engineering_and_Applied_Science) offers a minor in Technical Entrepreneurship through its Center for Technology, Innovation, and Community Engagement. SEAS' entrepreneurship activities focus on community building initiatives in New York and worldwide, made possible through partners such as [Microsoft Corporation](/wiki/Microsoft_Corporation).

On June 14, 2010, Mayor [Michael R. Bloomberg](/wiki/Michael_R._Bloomberg) launched the NYC Media Lab to promote innovations in New York's media industry. Situated at the [New York University Tandon School of Engineering](/wiki/New_York_University_Tandon_School_of_Engineering), the lab is a consortium of Columbia University, [New York University](/wiki/New_York_University), and [New York City Economic Development Corporation](/wiki/New_York_City_Economic_Development_Corporation) acting to connect companies with universities in new technology research. The Lab is modeled after similar ones at [MIT](/wiki/MIT) and [Stanford](/wiki/Stanford), and was established with a $250,000 grant from the New York City Economic Development Corporation.

### World Leaders Forum

The World Leaders Forum at [Low Memorial Library](/wiki/Low_Memorial_Library)

Established in 2003 by university president [Lee C. Bollinger](/wiki/Lee_C._Bollinger), the World Leaders Forum at Columbia University provides the opportunity for students and faculty to listen to world leaders in government, religion, industry, finance, and academia.

### Other

[Earl Hall](/wiki/Earl_Hall) was listed on the [National Register of Historic Places](/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places) for its role in serving as a venue for meetings and dances of the [Columbia Queer Alliance](/wiki/Columbia_Queer_Alliance).

The Columbia University Orchestra was founded by composer [Edward MacDowell](/wiki/Edward_MacDowell) in 1896, and is the oldest continually operating university orchestra in the United States. Undergraduate student composers at Columbia may choose to become involved with Columbia New Music, which sponsors concerts of music written by undergraduate students from all of Columbia's schools. The Notes and Keys, the oldest [a cappella](/wiki/A_cappella) group at Columbia, was founded in 1909.

There are a number of performing arts groups at Columbia dedicated to producing student theater, including the Columbia Players, King's Crown Shakespeare Troupe (KCST), Columbia Musical Theater Society (CMTS), NOMADS (New and Original Material Authored and Directed by Students), LateNite Theatre, Columbia University Performing Arts League (CUPAL), Black Theatre Ensemble (BTE), sketch comedy group Chowdah, and improvisational troupes Alfred and Fruit Paunch.

The [Columbia Queer Alliance](/wiki/Columbia_Queer_Alliance) is the central Columbia student organization that represents the bisexual, lesbian, gay, transgender, and questioning student population. It is the oldest [gay](/wiki/Gay) student organization in the world, founded as the Student [Homophile](/wiki/Homophile_movement) League in 1967 by students including lifelong activist [Stephen Donaldson](/wiki/Stephen_Donaldson_(activist)).

Columbia University campus military groups include the U.S. Military Veterans of Columbia University and Advocates for Columbia ROTC. In the 2005–06 academic year, the Columbia Military Society, Columbia's student group for ROTC cadets and Marine officer candidates, was renamed the Hamilton Society for "students who aspire to serve their nation through the military in the tradition of [Alexander Hamilton](/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton)".

Columbia has several secret societies, including [St. Anthony Hall](/wiki/St._Anthony_Hall), which was founded at the university in 1847, and two senior societies, the [Nacoms and Sachems](/wiki/Nacoms_and_Sachems).

## Athletics

Main article: [Columbia Lions](/wiki/Columbia_Lions)

[Robert K. Kraft Field at Lawrence A. Wien Stadium](/wiki/Robert_K._Kraft_Field_at_Lawrence_A._Wien_Stadium) in [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan), home field of [Columbia Lions football](/wiki/Columbia_Lions_football)

[Lou Gehrig](/wiki/Lou_Gehrig), who attended Columbia University in 1922 and 1923

A member institution of the [National Collegiate Athletic Association](/wiki/National_Collegiate_Athletic_Association) (NCAA) in [Division I](/wiki/NCAA_Division_I) [FCS](/wiki/Football_Championship_Subdivision), Columbia fields varsity teams in 29 sports and is a member of the [Ivy League](/wiki/Ivy_League). The football Lions play home games at the 17,000-seat [Robert K. Kraft Field at Lawrence A. Wien Stadium](/wiki/Robert_K._Kraft_Field_at_Lawrence_A._Wien_Stadium). The Baker Athletics Complex also includes facilities for baseball, softball, soccer, lacrosse, field hockey, tennis, track, and rowing, as well as the new Campbell Sports Center, which opened in January 2013. The basketball, fencing, swimming & diving, volleyball, and wrestling programs are based at the Dodge Physical Fitness Center on the main campus.

Former students include [Baseball Hall of Famers](/wiki/Baseball_Hall_of_Famers) [Lou Gehrig](/wiki/Lou_Gehrig) and [Eddie Collins](/wiki/Eddie_Collins), [football Hall of Famer](/wiki/Pro_Football_Hall_of_Fame) [Sid Luckman](/wiki/Sid_Luckman), [Marcellus Wiley](/wiki/Marcellus_Wiley), and world champion women's weightlifter [Karyn Marshall](/wiki/Karyn_Marshall). On May 17, 1939, fledgling [NBC](/wiki/NBC) broadcast a doubleheader between the Columbia Lions and the [Princeton Tigers](/wiki/Princeton_Tigers) at Columbia's Baker Field, making it the first televised regular athletic event in history.

Columbia University participated in multiple firsts within collegiate athletics. The football program is best known for its record of futility set during the 1980s: between 1983 and 1988, the team lost 44 games in a row, which is still the record for the NCAA [Football Championship Subdivision](/wiki/Football_Championship_Subdivision). The streak was broken on October 8, 1988, with a 16–13 victory over arch-rival [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University). That was the Lions' first victory at Wien Stadium, which had been opened during the losing streak and was already four years old. A new tradition has developed with the [Liberty Cup](/wiki/Liberty_Cup). The Liberty Cup is awarded annually to the winner of the football game between [Fordham](/wiki/Fordham_University) and Columbia Universities, two of the only three NCAA Division I football teams in New York City.

### The Varsity Show

Main article: [Varsity Show](/wiki/Varsity_Show)

The Varsity Show is one of the oldest traditions at Columbia. Founded in 1893 as a fundraiser for the university's fledgling athletic teams, the Varsity Show now draws together the entire Columbia undergraduate community for a series of performances every April. Dedicated to producing a unique full-length musical that skewers and satirizes many dubious aspects of life at Columbia, the Varsity Show is written and performed exclusively by university undergraduates. Various renowned playwrights, composers, authors, directors, and actors have contributed to the Varsity Show, either as writers or performers, while students at Columbia, including [Richard Rodgers](/wiki/Richard_Rodgers), [Oscar Hammerstein II](/wiki/Oscar_Hammerstein_II), [Lorenz Hart](/wiki/Lorenz_Hart), [Herman J. Mankiewicz](/wiki/Herman_J._Mankiewicz), [I. A. L. Diamond](/wiki/I._A._L._Diamond), [Herman Wouk](/wiki/Herman_Wouk), [Greta Gerwig](/wiki/Greta_Gerwig), and [Kate McKinnon](/wiki/Kate_McKinnon).

Notable past shows include *[Fly With Me](/wiki/Fly_With_Me_(musical))* (1920), [*The* *Streets of New York*](/wiki/The_Streets_of_New_York_(musical)) (1948), *The Sky's the Limit* (1954), and *Angels at Columbia* (1994). In particular, *Streets of New York*, after having been revived three times, opened [off-Broadway](/wiki/Off-Broadway) in 1963 and was awarded a 1964 [Drama Desk Award](/wiki/Drama_Desk_Award). *The Mischief Maker* (1903), written by [Edgar Allan Woolf](/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Woolf) and Cassius Freeborn, premiered at [Madison Square Garden](/wiki/Madison_Square_Garden_(1890)) in 1906 as *[Mam'zelle Champagne](/wiki/Mam%27zelle_Champagne)*.

### Tree Lighting and Yule Log ceremonies

Tree Lighting at College Walk

The first modern Yule Log ceremony in [John Jay Hall](/wiki/John_Jay_Hall), 1910

The campus Tree Lighting ceremony was inaugurated in 1998. It celebrates the illumination of the medium-sized trees lining College Walk in front of Kent Hall and [Hamilton Hall](/wiki/Hamilton_Hall_(Columbia_University)) on the east end and Dodge Hall and Pulitzer Hall on the west, just before finals week in early December. The lights remain on until February 28. Students meet at [the sundial](/wiki/Columbia_University_sundial) for free hot chocolate, performances by *a cappella* groups, and speeches by the university president and a guest.

Immediately following the College Walk festivities is one of Columbia's older holiday traditions, the lighting of the Yule Log. The [Christmas](/wiki/Christmas) ceremony dates to a period prior to the [American Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), but lapsed before being revived by President [Nicholas Murray Butler](/wiki/Nicholas_Murray_Butler) in 1910. A troop of students dressed as [Continental Army](/wiki/Continental_Army) soldiers carry the eponymous log from the sundial to the lounge of [John Jay Hall](/wiki/John_Jay_Hall), where it is lit amid the singing of seasonal carols. The Christmas ceremony is accompanied by a reading of *[A Visit From St. Nicholas](/wiki/A_Visit_From_St._Nicholas)* by [Clement Clarke Moore](/wiki/Clement_Clarke_Moore) and *[Yes, Virginia, There is a Santa Claus](/wiki/Yes,_Virginia,_There_is_a_Santa_Claus)* by [Francis Pharcellus Church](/wiki/Francis_Pharcellus_Church).

As of 2021, Columbia employs 4,381 faculty, including 70 members of the [National Academy of Sciences](/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences), 178 members of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences), and 65 members of the [National Academy of Medicine](/wiki/National_Academy_of_Medicine). In total, the Columbia faculty has included 52 [Nobel laureates](/wiki/Nobel_Prize), 12 [National Medal of Science](/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science) recipients, and 32 [National Academy of Engineering](/wiki/National_Academy_of_Engineering) members.

Columbia University faculty played particularly important roles during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) and the creation of the [New Deal](/wiki/New_Deal) under President [Franklin D. Roosevelt](/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt), who attended Columbia Law School. The three core members of Roosevelt's [Brain Trust](/wiki/Brain_trust#Roosevelt's_"Brain_Trust"): [Adolf A. Berle](/wiki/Adolf_A._Berle), [Raymond Moley](/wiki/Raymond_Moley), and [Rexford Tugwell](/wiki/Rexford_Tugwell), were law professors at Columbia. Former United States Secretary of Labor, Francis Perkins was the principle architect of the New Deal and also earned her Master's from Columbia University. The [Statistical Research Group](/wiki/The_Statistical_Research_Group_of_World_War_II), which used statistics to analyze military problems during World War II, was composed of Columbia researchers and faculty including [George Stigler](/wiki/George_Stigler) and [Milton Friedman](/wiki/Milton_Friedman). Columbia faculty and researchers, including [Enrico Fermi](/wiki/Enrico_Fermi), [Leo Szilard](/wiki/Leo_Szilard), [Eugene T. Booth](/wiki/Eugene_T._Booth), [John R. Dunning](/wiki/John_R._Dunning), [George B. Pegram](/wiki/George_B._Pegram), [Walter Zinn](/wiki/Walter_Zinn), [Chien-Shiung Wu](/wiki/Chien-Shiung_Wu), [Francis G. Slack](/wiki/Francis_G._Slack), [Harold Urey](/wiki/Harold_Urey), [Herbert L. Anderson](/wiki/Herbert_L._Anderson), and [Isidor Isaac Rabi](/wiki/Isidor_Isaac_Rabi), also played a significant role during the early phases of the [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project).

Following the rise of [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany), the exiled [Institute for Social Research](/wiki/University_of_Frankfurt_Institute_for_Social_Research) at [Goethe University Frankfurt](/wiki/Goethe_University_Frankfurt) would affiliate itself with Columbia from 1934 to 1950. It was during this period that thinkers including [Theodor W. Adorno](/wiki/Theodor_W._Adorno), [Max Horkheimer](/wiki/Max_Horkheimer), and [Herbert Marcuse](/wiki/Herbert_Marcuse) wrote and published some of the most seminal works of the [Frankfurt School](/wiki/Frankfurt_School), including *[Reason and Revolution](/wiki/Reason_and_Revolution)*, *[Dialectic of Enlightenment](/wiki/Dialectic_of_Enlightenment)*, and [*Eclipse of Reason*](/wiki/Eclipse_of_Reason_(Horkheimer)). Professors [Edward Said](/wiki/Edward_Said), author of [*Orientalism*](/wiki/Orientalism_(book)), and [Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak](/wiki/Gayatri_Chakravorty_Spivak) are generally considered as founders of the field of [postcolonialism](/wiki/Postcolonialism); other professors that have significantly contributed to the field include [Hamid Dabashi](/wiki/Hamid_Dabashi), [Rashid Khalidi](/wiki/Rashid_Khalidi), and [Joseph Massad](/wiki/Joseph_Massad). The works of professors [Kimberlé Crenshaw](/wiki/Kimberl%C3%A9_Crenshaw), [Patricia J. Williams](/wiki/Patricia_J._Williams), and [Kendall Thomas](/wiki/Kendall_Thomas) were foundational to the field of [critical race theory](/wiki/Critical_race_theory).

Columbia and its affiliated faculty have also made significant contributions to the study of religion. The affiliated Union Theological Seminary is a center of [liberal Christianity](/wiki/Liberal_Christianity) in the United States, having served as the birthplace of [Black theology](/wiki/Black_theology) through the efforts of faculty including [James H. Cone](/wiki/James_H._Cone) and [Cornel West](/wiki/Cornel_West), and [Womanist theology](/wiki/Womanist_theology), through the works of [Katie Cannon](/wiki/Katie_Cannon), [Emilie Townes](/wiki/Emilie_Townes), and [Delores S. Williams](/wiki/Delores_S._Williams). Likewise, the Jewish Theological Seminary of America was the birthplace of [Conservative Judaism](/wiki/Conservative_Judaism) movement in the United States, which was founded and led by faculty members including [Solomon Schechter](/wiki/Solomon_Schechter), [Alexander Kohut](/wiki/Alexander_Kohut), and [Louis Ginzberg](/wiki/Louis_Ginzberg) in the early 20th century, and is a major center for Jewish studies in general.

Other schools of thought in the humanities to which Columbia professors made significant contributions include the [Dunning School](/wiki/Dunning_School), founded by [William Archibald Dunning](/wiki/William_Archibald_Dunning); the anthropological schools of [historical particularism](/wiki/Historical_particularism) and [cultural relativism](/wiki/Cultural_relativism), founded by [Franz Boas](/wiki/Franz_Boas); and [functional psychology](/wiki/Functional_psychology), whose founders and proponents include [John Dewey](/wiki/John_Dewey), [James McKeen Cattell](/wiki/James_McKeen_Cattell), [Edward L. Thorndike](/wiki/Edward_L._Thorndike), and [Robert S. Woodworth](/wiki/Robert_S._Woodworth).
