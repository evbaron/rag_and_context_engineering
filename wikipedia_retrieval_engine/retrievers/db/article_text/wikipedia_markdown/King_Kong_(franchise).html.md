***King Kong*** is an American [monster](/wiki/Monster_movie) [media franchise](/wiki/Media_franchise) that consists of thirteen films, as well as [television](/wiki/Television_show), [novels](/wiki/Novel), [comic books](/wiki/Comic_book), [video games](/wiki/Video_game), attractions, and other [merchandise](/wiki/Product_(business)). The franchise is centered on [King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong), a giant ape living on a primordial island inhabited by prehistoric creatures. The original film *[King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong_(1933_film))* was co-directed by [Merian C. Cooper](/wiki/Merian_C._Cooper) (creator of the character) and [Ernest B. Schoedsack](/wiki/Ernest_B._Schoedsack) and was released on March 2, 1933; it was a box office success, despite opening during the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression). The film's [stop motion](/wiki/Stop_motion) effects by [Willis H. O'Brien](/wiki/Willis_H._O%27Brien) revolutionized special effects, leaving a lasting impact on the film industry worldwide.

Twelve films have followed since the franchise's 1933 debut, with Kong typically serving as a benevolent and, at times, violent protagonist. Ownership over each film varies since Kong was licensed to various studios, but the original 1933 film and its [sequel](/wiki/Son_of_Kong) were produced and owned by [RKO Radio Pictures](/wiki/RKO_Pictures). The films were inherited by [Warner Bros.](/wiki/Warner_Bros.) (via [Turner Entertainment Co.](/wiki/Turner_Entertainment_Co.)) in 1996.

The franchise has had several hiatuses and revivals, and it has inspired other genre films during intervals, such as *[Mighty Joe Young](/wiki/Mighty_Joe_Young_(1949_film))*, *[The Beast from 20,000 Fathoms](/wiki/The_Beast_from_20,000_Fathoms)*, *[Creature from the Black Lagoon](/wiki/Creature_from_the_Black_Lagoon)*, *[Godzilla](/wiki/Godzilla_(1954_film))*, *[Mothra](/wiki/Mothra_(film))* and *[Jurassic Park](/wiki/Jurassic_Park_(film))*.

## History

Live-action and animated variants of King Kong between 1933–2021

### 1930s

Main articles: [King Kong (1933 film)](/wiki/King_Kong_(1933_film)) and [Son of Kong](/wiki/Son_of_Kong)

In 1933, [RKO Pictures](/wiki/RKO_Pictures) produced and released *[King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong_(1933_film))*; it became a box office success despite opening during the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression), earning $90,000 in its first four days. The film's success spawned a sequel nine months later, *[Son of Kong](/wiki/Son_of_Kong)*, as well as what became a multimedia franchise, spanning remakes, reboots, books, videos games, attractions, parodies and references.

The film grossed $10 million at the box office and earned an additional $2.5 million when it was re-released in 1952. The film revolutionized special effects, specifically [stop motion](/wiki/Stop_motion), that left a long lasting impact in the film industry worldwide, with effects animator [Willis H. O'Brien](/wiki/Willis_H._O%27Brien) largely credited for pioneering the medium. *King Kong* (1933) is widely regarded by critics and journalists as a masterpiece and a signature facet of [American cinema](/wiki/Cinema_of_the_United_States), and is cited as one of the greatest monster films ever made. *King Kong* (1933) was selected by the [National Film Registry](/wiki/National_Film_Registry) for preservation.

### 1960s

Main articles: [King Kong vs. Godzilla](/wiki/King_Kong_vs._Godzilla), [The King Kong Show](/wiki/The_King_Kong_Show), and [King Kong Escapes](/wiki/King_Kong_Escapes)

In the early 1960s, O'Brien developed a story outline titled *King Kong vs. Frankenstein*, supplemented with water-color illustrations and sketches, in the hopes of igniting interest for a potential production in color. Impressed with O'Brien's pitch, RKO attorney Daniel O'Shea granted O'Brien permission to use the character and introduced him to independent film producer [John Beck](/wiki/John_Beck_(producer)) to help secure studio funding. Despite hiring [George Worthing Yates](/wiki/George_Worthing_Yates) to adapt O'Brien's outline into a full screenplay (retitled as *King Kong vs. Prometheus*, in reference to the [original Mary Shelley book](/wiki/Frankenstein)) and meeting with several studios and directors, Beck failed to sell the project in Hollywood and reached out to foreign studios.

Beck succeeded in striking a deal with [Toho Co., Ltd.](/wiki/Toho), the producers behind *[Godzilla](/wiki/Godzilla_(1954_film))* (1954). However, Toho was mostly interested in licensing King Kong from RKO and Beck rather than acquiring O'Brien's story. As a result, Frankenstein/Prometheus was replaced with [Godzilla](/wiki/Godzilla) and the project was redeveloped as *[King Kong vs. Godzilla](/wiki/King_Kong_vs._Godzilla)*. Beck's deal with Toho was done without O'Brien's knowledge or consent. His representative prepared a lawsuit (presumably against Beck) for unauthorized use of O'Brien's ideas in the film. However, the lawsuit was dropped due to attorney fees, and O'Brien died on November 8, 1962.

Unlike *King Kong* (1933), the special effects in *Godzilla* were accomplished with [suitmation](/wiki/Suitmation) and miniature sets, also known as *[tokusatsu](/wiki/Tokusatsu)*. King Kong was portrayed by [Shoichi Hirose](/wiki/Shoichi_Hirose) and Godzilla portrayed by [Haruo Nakajima](/wiki/Haruo_Nakajima); stop motion was also used in a few brief scenes.

Toho theatrically released *King Kong vs. Godzilla* on August 11, 1962, in Japan and it was a box office success, having sold 11.2 million tickets during its initial theatrical run. It earned ¥352 million ($972,000) in [distribution rental](/wiki/Theatrical_rental) earnings. The film became the [second-highest-grossing Japanese-produced film in history](/wiki/List_of_highest-grossing_Japanese_films) upon its release and was the fourth-highest-grossing film released in Japan that year, as well as Toho's second-biggest release.

Because Toho was so eager to acquire King Kong, they had agreed to let Beck retain exclusive distribution rights (theatrical and television) for the United States, Canada, Alaska, the United Kingdom, and Israel, while Toho retained exclusive distribution for the Far East. Beck's contract also granted him the right to produce his own version. Beck commissioned a heavily localized version with new footage using American actors and replacing [Akira Ifukube](/wiki/Akira_Ifukube)'s score with stock music from [Universal Pictures](/wiki/Universal_Pictures)' library. Beck sold his localized version to [Universal International](/wiki/Universal_Pictures#Universal-International_and_Decca_Records), who made a deal to retain copyrights to the American version for 40 years.

The American version of *King Kong vs. Godzilla* was released in the United States on June 26, 1963. The Japanese version remained unavailable officially outside of Japan until 2019, when American distributor [The Criterion Collection](/wiki/The_Criterion_Collection) included both Japanese and American versions in a Blu-ray set collecting the [Shōwa era *Godzilla*](/wiki/Godzilla_(franchise)#Shōwa_era_(1954–1975)) films.

In 1966, American studio [Rankin/Bass Animated Entertainment](/wiki/Rankin/Bass_Animated_Entertainment) acquired the rights from RKO to produce an animated series and a new live-action film. A joint venture between Rankin/Bass and Japanese studio [Toei Animation](/wiki/Toei_Animation), *[The King Kong Show](/wiki/The_King_Kong_Show)* premiered on [ABC](/wiki/American_Broadcasting_Company) in September 1966. Around that time, Rankin/Bass made a deal with Toho to co-produced their King Kong film that would have been promotionally tied to *The King Kong Show*. Toho presented a script titled *Operation Robinson Crusoe: King Kong vs. Ebirah*, but Rankin/Bass vetoed the script and Toho proceeded without Rankin/Bass; King Kong was replaced with Godzilla and the film was redeveloped as *[Ebirah, Horror of the Deep](/wiki/Ebirah,_Horror_of_the_Deep)*.

In September 1966, Toho had sent Rankin/Bass a proposed synopsis for a new film titled *[King Kong Escapes](/wiki/King_Kong_Escapes)*, which was loosely based on *The King Kong Show*; though some elements from *Operation Robinson Crusoe: King Kong vs. Ebirah* remained such as over-the-top villains from an unnamed nation, illegal production of fuel for nuclear weapons, and scenes in the South Seas. The proposal was accepted and filming began in spring 1967, with Haruo Nakajima playing King Kong via suitmation. Toho theatrically released *King Kong Escapes* on July 22, 1967, in Japan but failed to match the box office success of *King Kong vs. Godzilla*. Universal Pictures released an English dubbed version in the United States the following year; similar to what they had done with *King Kong vs. Godzilla*, Universal made a deal to retain the copyright of the American version for 40 years.

### 1970s–1980s

Main articles: [King Kong (1976 film)](/wiki/King_Kong_(1976_film)) and [King Kong Lives](/wiki/King_Kong_Lives)

Accounts vary as to the genesis of the [1976 remake](/wiki/King_Kong_(1976_film)). Producer [Dino De Laurentiis](/wiki/Dino_De_Laurentiis) has claimed it was his idea to remake *King Kong* after seeing a poster of the 1933 film in his daughter's room. [Michael Eisner](/wiki/Michael_Eisner), then-vice president of [ABC](/wiki/American_Broadcasting_Company), equally claimed to have conceived the idea of a remake in December 1974 after watching the 1933 film on television, and casually mentioned the idea to [Sidney Sheinberg](/wiki/Sidney_Sheinberg), then-president and CEO of [MCA Inc.](/wiki/MCA_Inc.) and [Universal Pictures](/wiki/Universal_Pictures), and Sheinberg also mentioned the idea to [Barry Diller](/wiki/Barry_Diller), then-president of [Paramount Pictures](/wiki/Paramount_Pictures). Sheinberg and Diller soon began developing their own *King Kong* remakes simultaneously, both unaware of the other's project. Diller hired De Laurentiis to produce on behalf of Paramount, while Sheinberg hired [Hunt Stromberg Jr.](/wiki/Hunt_Stromberg_Jr.) to produce for Universal. On April 15, 1975, Universal's attorney Arnold Shane and De Laurentiis met with RKO's attorney Daniel O'Shea to negotiate in acquiring the rights; Shane and De Laurentiis were unaware of each other's appointment. Despite not signing documents, both parties left believing they had secured the rights for their respected studios. However, Paramount had signed their deal with RKO the following month and soon learned about Universal's own film after they announced [Joseph Sargent](/wiki/Joseph_Sargent) to direct and [Bo Goldman](/wiki/Bo_Goldman) to write the script, titled *The Legend of King Kong*.

Universal and Paramount soon engaged in litigation. Universal insisted that a verbal agreement was made with RKO, demanded $25 million in damages from De Laurentiis and RKO, and asserted that the material was in public domain by then since Universal published a novelization of the *King Kong* story in the 1930s. De Laurentiis countersued for copyright infringement, demanding $90 million in damages and pushed his film into production while also publishing ads that jabbed at Universal (e.g. "There Still is Only One King Kong"). In January 1976, negotiations were made to potentially have both studios co-produce a single film and share profits, but De Laurentiis rejected Universal's proposal of using their script and controlling the merchandise. Litigation ended that same month after De Laurentiis agreed to pay an undisclosed percentage for Universal's cancelled film, with Universal vowing to produce their own *King Kong* film at an undetermined date.

[Carlo Rambaldi](/wiki/Carlo_Rambaldi) lead an effects team that built Kong's giant hand and a 40 foot tall robot of Kong, its construction overseen by [Glen Robinson](/wiki/Glen_Robinson_(visual_effects)). Like the Toho films, an ape-suit was used to bring Kong to life, with special make-up effects artist [Rick Baker](/wiki/Rick_Baker) portraying the character. The 40-foot robot cost roughly $2 million, while the giant hand and suit cost an additional $400,000, and a full-sized static Kong made of styrofoam, used for one scene, cost $300,000. The production was a rushed venture since the studio set the film's release date for December 1976 and De Laurentiis' persistence to beat Universal. As a result, delays occurred due to props and mechanics malfunctioning; two full-scale right hands produced by mistake; director [John Guillermin](/wiki/John_Guillermin)'s tyrant-like behavior on-set; the budget inflating to $24 million and marketing costs of $10 million, figures unheard of at the time. Paramount theatrically released *King Kong* on December 17, 1976, and grossed $90 million. Despite mixed reviews unfavorably comparing the remake to the original, the film won an [Academy Award for Best Visual Effects](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Visual_Effects#1970s), shared with *[Logan's Run](/wiki/Logan%27s_Run_(film))*.

In 1977, De Laurentiis stated that a sequel to the 1976 remake would "definitely" be produced. However, development on a sequel was impaired by the remake's disappointing box office results and legal complications over the ownership of the King Kong character. Writer [Steven Pressfield](/wiki/Steven_Pressfield) stated that various scripts were written that he described as "embarrassing". Despite initially skeptical of a female Kong, De Laurentiis ultimately approved of Pressfield's and [Ronald Shusett](/wiki/Ronald_Shusett) pitch of Kong put in a respirator and restored with an artificial heart. Guillermin returned to direct the sequel after finances collapsed for a film adaptation of *[Tai-Pan](/wiki/Tai-Pan_(novel))* with [Sean Connery](/wiki/Sean_Connery). The film was announced in October 1985 and filming began in April 1986 in Tennessee for a December release later that year. *[King Kong Lives](/wiki/King_Kong_Lives)* was theatrically released on December 19, 1986, by [De Laurentiis Entertainment Group](/wiki/De_Laurentiis_Entertainment_Group) and was met with a poor response. The film flopped at the box office, grossing $4.7 million in the United States and Canada, and was panned by critics.

### 1990s

Main articles: [King Kong (2005 film) § Earlier attempts and 1990s](/wiki/King_Kong_(2005_film)#Earlier_attempts_and_1990s), and [The Mighty Kong](/wiki/The_Mighty_Kong)

In 1993, Toho had considered remaking *King Kong vs. Godzilla*, due to that film being their most successful Godzilla/Kong film to date. However, Toho concluded that re-acquiring the rights to King Kong would prove difficult and instead considered using the cyborg Mechani-Kong, seen in *The King Kong Show* and Toho's *King Kong Escapes*. Effects director [Kōichi Kawakita](/wiki/K%C5%8Dichi_Kawakita) regaled an idea inspired by *[Fantastic Voyage](/wiki/Fantastic_Voyage)* where Mechani-Kong would have injected people inside Godzilla's body to battle the beast internally while Mechani-Kong battled Godzilla externally. Kawakita stated that the concept would have explored different worlds inside Godzilla. However, Toho walked back on using Mechani-Kong since the character bore a likeness to King Kong and thus required obtaining the rights. Mechani-Kong was replaced with [Mechagodzilla](/wiki/Mechagodzilla) and the film was redeveloped as *[Godzilla vs. Mechagodzilla II](/wiki/Godzilla_vs._Mechagodzilla_II)*.

In 1995, Universal Pictures offered filmmaker [Peter Jackson](/wiki/Peter_Jackson) a chance to direct a remake of *King Kong*, after being impressed with Jackson's *[The Frighteners](/wiki/The_Frighteners)*. Licensing the character from RKO and Time Warner was no longer an issue because the character of "Kong" fell into public domain by that point. Jackson initially turned down the offer but reneged on his decision after fearing that the remake would fail under a different director. Around that time, Jackson was attached to direct live-action film adaptations of *[The Hobbit](/wiki/The_Hobbit)* and *[The Lord of the Rings](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings)* but proceeded with *King Kong* due to producer [Harvey Weinstein](/wiki/Harvey_Weinstein) taking too long to acquire the rights. To placate Weinstein's anger, Jackson brokered a co-financing/co-distributing deal with [Miramax](/wiki/Miramax) for *King Kong* that benefited all parties. Pre-production commenced with filmmaker [Robert Zemeckis](/wiki/Robert_Zemeckis) on board as executive producer and Universal approved the script co-written by Jackson and his wife [Fran Walsh](/wiki/Fran_Walsh), with filming planned to commence in 1997 for a potential 1998 release. However, Universal halted progress in January 1997 after growing concern over competition from the then-upcoming releases of [TriStar's *Godzilla*](/wiki/Godzilla_(1998_film)) and [*Mighty Joe Young*](/wiki/Mighty_Joe_Young_(1998_film)), both released in 1998, the latter by Miramax's parent company, Disney. Universal aborted *King Kong* the following month, despite [Wētā FX](/wiki/W%C4%93t%C4%81_FX) and [Wētā Workshop](/wiki/W%C4%93t%C4%81_Workshop) having already designed six months worth of pre-production, and Jackson reverted to helm [*The Lord of the Rings* trilogy](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(film_series)). Years later, Jackson, and his frequent collaborator [Christian Rivers](/wiki/Christian_Rivers), had noted a similar tone between Jackson's unused script and Universal's 1999 remake of *[The Mummy](/wiki/The_Mummy_(1999_film))*.

In 1998, [Warner Bros.](/wiki/Warner_Bros.) released *[The Mighty Kong](/wiki/The_Mighty_Kong)* direct-to-video. An animated musical remake of the 1933 film, it features the voices of [Dudley Moore](/wiki/Dudley_Moore), [Jodi Benson](/wiki/Jodi_Benson), [Bill Sage](/wiki/Bill_Sage), [Jason Gray-Stanford](/wiki/Jason_Gray-Stanford), and [Richard Newman](/wiki/Richard_Newman_(actor)). The film received negative reviews and is placed in the low-tier end of most ranking lists of *King Kong* films.

### 2000s

Main articles: [Kong: The Animated Series](/wiki/Kong:_The_Animated_Series) and [King Kong (2005 film)](/wiki/King_Kong_(2005_film))

In response to the success of [Columbia TriStar Television](/wiki/Columbia_TriStar_Television)'s animated series *[Godzilla: The Series](/wiki/Godzilla:_The_Series)*, [BKN International](/wiki/BKN_International) produced *[Kong: The Animated Series](/wiki/Kong:_The_Animated_Series)* and ran on syndicated television between 2000 and 2001. The series is a direct sequel to the 1933 film and focuses on a new Kong cloned from the DNA of both the 1933 Kong and a young man named Jason and as a result, Jason is able to link with Kong via a special device in moments of crisis. The series generated two direct-to-video animated films, *Kong: King of Atlantis* (2005) and *Kong: Return to the Jungle* (2006).

After the success of *The Lord of the Rings* films, *[The Fellowship of the Ring](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_The_Fellowship_of_the_Ring)* and *[The Two Towers](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_The_Two_Towers)*, Universal approached Jackson in early 2003 during post-production on *[The Return of the King](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_The_Return_of_the_King)* about reviving his *King Kong* remake. In March 2003, Universal scheduled a 2005 release date and [Philippa Boyens](/wiki/Philippa_Boyens) was hired to rewrite Jackson's and Walsh's 1996 script, but Jackson began to work on a new script in October 2003 that was closer to the 1933 film after being dissatisfied with the 1996 script. They included unused elements from [James Ashmore Creelman](/wiki/James_Ashmore_Creelman)'s script of the 1933 film, such as the lost spider-pit scene. [Delos W. Lovelace](/wiki/Delos_W._Lovelace)'s 1932 novelization was also a source of inspiration. Weta Workshop and Weta Digital began to work immediately on *King Kong* after the completion of *The Return of the King* and Jackson had brought back the crew that worked with him on *The Lord of the Rings* trilogy. Filming began in September 2004, with [Andy Serkis](/wiki/Andy_Serkis) portraying Kong via [motion-capture acting](/wiki/Motion-capture_acting). Universal theatrically released *[King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong_(2005_film))* on December 13, 2005, to generally positive reviews, with the film appearing in several top ten lists for 2005. The film received four [Academy Award](/wiki/Academy_Award) nominations, for [Visual Effects](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Visual_Effects), [Sound Mixing](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Sound), [Sound Editing](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Sound_Editing), and [Production Design](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Production_Design), winning all but the last. Jackson had also directed a recreation of the lost spider pit sequence that was featured on the 2005 DVD release of the 1933 film.

### 2010s–present

#### Monsterverse

Main articles: [Monsterverse](/wiki/Monsterverse) and [Kong (Monsterverse)](/wiki/Kong_(Monsterverse))

In 2013, Jackson and producer [Mary Parent](/wiki/Mary_Parent) had intended to produce *Skull Island*, a direct sequel to his 2005 remake and handpicked then up-and-coming director [Adam Wingard](/wiki/Adam_Wingard) to direct it. However, when [Legendary Pictures](/wiki/Legendary_Entertainment) took over the project and redeveloped it as a prequel, Jackson and Wingard dropped out but Parent remained; Parent eventually became Legendary's production chief.

In July 2014, Legendary announced the film at [San Diego Comic-Con](/wiki/San_Diego_Comic-Con)'s Hall H with [Max Borenstein](/wiki/Max_Borenstein) (writer of Legendary's 2014 *[Godzilla](/wiki/Godzilla_(2014_film))* film) to write the script. Legendary initially targeted a November 4, 2016 release and had offered [Joe Cornish](/wiki/Joe_Cornish) to direct it. Around that time, Jackson expressed interest in seeing [Guillermo del Toro](/wiki/Guillermo_del_Toro) direct the film. In September 2014, Legendary announced [Jordan Vogt-Roberts](/wiki/Jordan_Vogt-Roberts) as the director, with [Tom Hiddleston](/wiki/Tom_Hiddleston) leading the film. In September 2015, Legendary moved the film, retitled *[Kong: Skull Island](/wiki/Kong:_Skull_Island)*, from Universal to Warner Bros. to unite the King Kong and Godzilla properties under one studio. In October 2015, Legendary officially announced their plans for a shared cinematic franchise between King Kong and Godzilla that would culminate in a *[Godzilla vs. Kong](/wiki/Godzilla_vs._Kong)* film, at the time slated for a 2020 release. In January 2017, Legendary's Godzilla–Kong series was revealed as the [Monsterverse](/wiki/Monsterverse), after it was announced that Legendary founder [Thomas Tull](/wiki/Thomas_Tull) would resign but remain as producer on the Monsterverse films.

Inspired by *[Heart of Darkness](/wiki/Heart_of_Darkness)* and *[Apocalypse Now](/wiki/Apocalypse_Now)*, Borenstein had originally envisioned a war film that began in the [Vietnam War](/wiki/Vietnam_War) and jumped to the present day; however, Legendary rejected this idea. Borenstein then wrote a draft set in 1917 while retaining the *Apocalypse Now* concept. That version saw Hiddleston playing a character leading a rescue mission into Skull Island to find his brother, who came to the island in search of a cure-all serum. After Vogt-Roberts joined the film, he liked Borenstein's original Vietname-era pitch and convinced Legendary to set the film near the end of the Vietnam war. For Kong's design, Vogt-Roberts took the character back the look of the 1933 iteration, wanting him to look straightforward yet iconic enough that a child could draw him, and wanted Kong to reflect a lonely and morose God. [Terry Notary](/wiki/Terry_Notary) portrayed Kong via performance capture, while [Toby Kebbell](/wiki/Toby_Kebbell) provided additional facial work; Notary sought Serkis' blessing before accepting the role. [Principal photography](/wiki/Principal_photography) began in October 2015, in [Hawaii](/wiki/Hawaii) and [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam) and wrapped in March 2016.

*[Kong: Skull Island](/wiki/Kong:_Skull_Island)* was released on March 10, 2017, to positive reviews, and was a box office success, grossing $566 million worldwide against a budget of $185 million. The film received a nomination for [Best Visual Effects](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Visual_Effects) at the [90th Academy Awards](/wiki/90th_Academy_Awards).

In April 2016, writer and artist Joe DeVito sued Legendary and Warner Bros. for breach of implied contract, alleging that they had stolen ideas and concepts from pitch meetings he had with Legendary in April 2014. He claimed Legendary turned him down yet used his ideas as the basis for their film without credit or compensation and sought $3.5 million in general and punitive damages.

In March 2017, Legendary had assembled a writers room for *Godzilla vs. Kong*, with [Terry Rossio](/wiki/Terry_Rossio) (who had co-written an unproduced script for [TriStar's *Godzilla*](/wiki/Godzilla_(1998_film))) leading a team consisting of Patrick McKay, J. D. Payne, Lindsey Beer, Cat Vasko, [T. S. Nowlin](/wiki/T._S._Nowlin), Jack Paglen, and [J. Michael Straczynski](/wiki/J._Michael_Straczynski). At that time, Wingard had a general meeting with Parent, who recalled that Jackson had approved of Wingard and was announced as the director of *Godzilla vs. Kong* in May 2017.

Filming for *Godzilla vs. Kong* began in November 2018 in Hawaii, Australia, and Hong Kong and wrapped in April 2019. Allan Henry provided the [performance capture](/wiki/Motion-capture_acting) for Kong, while animation supervisor Eric Petey provided Kong's full body performance and [facial capture](/wiki/Facial_motion_capture) for the ocean battle sequence. The film endured several delays, largely due to the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic), but was finally released internationally on March 24, 2021 and on March 31, 2021 in the United States; the latter which saw a concurrent release in theaters and on [HBO Max](/wiki/HBO_Max). The film received generally positive reviews and became a box office and streaming hit during the pandemic; it became the most successful launch item in HBO Max's history until it was overtaken by *[Mortal Kombat](/wiki/Mortal_Kombat_(2021_film))*. The film grossed $470 million worldwide against a break-even point of $330 million, and became the most pirated film of 2021.

In January 2021, Legendary announced an animated series titled *[Skull Island](/wiki/Skull_Island_(TV_series))*. A joint-venture between [Legendary Television](/wiki/Legendary_Entertainment#Television), Tractor Pants Productions, [Powerhouse Animation Studios](/wiki/Powerhouse_Animation_Studios), and [Netflix Animation](/wiki/Netflix_Animation), the series was released on June 22, 2023, on Netflix. In March 2025, Legendary Comics announced a partnership with Titan Comics to expand *Skull Island* into a comic series titled *Return to Skull Island*, slated for June 4, 2025.

Due to the success of *Godzilla vs. Kong*, Legendary announced a sequel in March 2022, scheduled to commence filming later that same year. In May 2022, it was announced that Wingard would return to direct and that [Dan Stevens](/wiki/Dan_Stevens) had been cast in the lead. Wingard and Stevens had previously worked together on *[The Guest](/wiki/The_Guest_(2014_American_film))*. Filming began in July 2022 in [Gold Coast, Queensland](/wiki/Gold_Coast,_Queensland), and finished in November 2022. Allan Henry provided the performance capture for both the Skar King and Kong, reprising his role from *Godzilla vs. Kong*. In April 2023, Legendary released a teaser trailer revealing the film's official title as *[Godzilla x Kong: The New Empire](/wiki/Godzilla_x_Kong:_The_New_Empire)*. The film was theatrically released on March 29, 2024. Due to the film's success, Legendary announced in May 2024 that a follow-up is in development and would be written by [David Callaham](/wiki/David_Callaham).

In January 2024, Kong briefly appeared in the finale of the television series *[Monarch: Legacy of Monsters](/wiki/Monarch:_Legacy_of_Monsters)*. The character's role would further be expanded on in the series' second season.

Due to the success of *Godzilla x Kong: The New Empire*, Legendary announced in May 2024 that a sequel, titled *[Godzilla x Kong: Supernova](/wiki/Godzilla_x_Kong:_Supernova)*, had entered development and would be written by [David Callaham](/wiki/David_Callaham). The following week, Legendary announced that Wingard would not return to direct due to scheduling conflicts, but had expressed interest in having Wingard return. In June 2024, Legendary announced [Grant Sputore](/wiki/Grant_Sputore) (director of *[I Am Mother](/wiki/I_Am_Mother)*) as the new director and that the film would be released on March 26, 2027. Pre-production began in January 2025, with performers being cast such as [Kaitlyn Dever](/wiki/Kaitlyn_Dever), [Jack O'Connell](/wiki/Jack_O%27Connell_(actor)), [Delroy Lindo](/wiki/Delroy_Lindo), Dan Stevens (reprises his role from the previous film), [Matthew Modine](/wiki/Matthew_Modine), [Alycia Debnam-Carey](/wiki/Alycia_Debnam-Carey), and [Sam Neill](/wiki/Sam_Neill).

#### Other projects

Main article: [Kong: King of the Apes](/wiki/Kong:_King_of_the_Apes)

In 2016, [Netflix](/wiki/Netflix) released the animated series *[Kong: King of the Apes](/wiki/Kong:_King_of_the_Apes)*; a joint venture between [41 Entertainment](/wiki/41_Entertainment) and [Avi Arad](/wiki/Avi_Arad), the series ran for two seasons.

In April 2017, [MarVista Entertainment](/wiki/MarVista_Entertainment) and [IM Global](/wiki/IM_Global) announced their own live-action King Kong project for television; written by Jonathan Penner and Stacy Title, the series is to be based on DeVito's materials, approved by the Cooper estate.

In August 2022, [Disney Branded Television](/wiki/Disney_Branded_Television) announced plans for a live-action series of their own for [Disney+](/wiki/Disney%2B). The series is to be co-produced by [James Wan](/wiki/James_Wan)'s Atomic Monster Productions and written by [Stephany Folsom](/wiki/Stephany_Folsom), and would be based on Cooper's original books and DeVito's new novelizations, focusing on a new Kong in modern times with characters exploring the origins and mysteries of his home island.

## Cultural impact

Main article: [King Kong in popular culture](/wiki/King_Kong_in_popular_culture)

King Kong, as well as the series of films featuring him, have been featured many times in popular culture outside of the films themselves, in forms ranging from straight copies to parodies and joke references, and in media from comic books to video games. [The Beatles](/wiki/The_Beatles)' 1968 animated film *[Yellow Submarine](/wiki/Yellow_Submarine_(1968_film))* includes a scene of the characters opening a door to reveal King Kong abducting a woman from her bed. *[The Simpsons](/wiki/The_Simpsons)* episode "[Treehouse of Horror III](/wiki/Treehouse_of_Horror_III)" features a segment called "King Homer" which parodies the plot of the original film, with [Homer](/wiki/Homer_Simpson) as Kong and [Marge](/wiki/Marge_Simpson) in the [Ann Darrow](/wiki/Ann_Darrow) role. It ends with King Homer marrying Marge and eating her father.

The British comedy TV series *[The Goodies](/wiki/The_Goodies_(TV_series))* made an episode called "[Kitten Kong](/wiki/Kitten_Kong)", in which a giant cat called Twinkle roams the streets of [London](/wiki/London), knocking over the [British Telecom Tower](/wiki/British_Telecom_Tower). The controversial [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) [Dutch resistance](/wiki/Dutch_resistance) fighter [Christiaan Lindemans](/wiki/Christiaan_Lindemans) — eventually arrested on suspicion of having betrayed secrets to the Nazis — was nicknamed "King Kong" due to his being exceptionally tall. [Frank Zappa](/wiki/Frank_Zappa) and [The Mothers of Invention](/wiki/The_Mothers_of_Invention) recorded an instrumental about "King Kong" in 1967 and featured it on the album *[Uncle Meat](/wiki/Uncle_Meat)*. Zappa went on to make many other versions of the song on albums such as *[Make a Jazz Noise Here](/wiki/Make_a_Jazz_Noise_Here)*, *[You Can't Do That on Stage Anymore, Vol. 3](/wiki/You_Can%27t_Do_That_on_Stage_Anymore,_Vol._3)*, *[Ahead of Their Time](/wiki/Ahead_of_Their_Time)*, and *[Beat the Boots](/wiki/Beat_the_Boots)*.

[The Kinks](/wiki/The_Kinks) recorded a song called "King Kong" as the B-side to their 1969 "[Plastic Man](/wiki/Plastic_Man_(song))" single. In 1972, a 550 cm (18 ft) fiberglass [statue of King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong_statue) was erected in Birmingham, England. The second track of [The Jimmy Castor Bunch](/wiki/The_Jimmy_Castor_Bunch) album *Supersound* from 1975 is titled "King Kong". [Filk Music](/wiki/Filk_Music) artists [Ookla the Mok](/wiki/Ookla_the_Mok_(band))'s "Song of Kong", which explores the reasons why King Kong and Godzilla should not be roommates, appears on their 2001 album *Smell No Evil*. [Daniel Johnston](/wiki/Daniel_Johnston) wrote and recorded a song called "King Kong" on his fifth self-released [music cassette](/wiki/Music_cassette), *[Yip/Jump Music](/wiki/Yip/Jump_Music)* in 1983, rereleased on [CD](/wiki/CD) and double [LP](/wiki/LP_album) by [Homestead Records](/wiki/Homestead_Records) in 1988. The song is an [a cappella](/wiki/A_cappella) narrative of the original movie's story line. [Tom Waits](/wiki/Tom_Waits) recorded a cover version of the song with various sound effects on the 2004 release, *[The Late Great Daniel Johnston: Discovered Covered](/wiki/The_Late_Great_Daniel_Johnston:_Discovered_Covered)*. [ABBA](/wiki/ABBA) recorded "[King Kong Song](/wiki/King_Kong_Song)" for their 1974 album *[Waterloo](/wiki/Waterloo_(album))* and it was included as a B-side on the singles for "[Honey, Honey](/wiki/Honey,_Honey)" and "[I've Been Waiting for You](/wiki/I%27ve_Been_Waiting_for_You_(ABBA_song))".

### Literature and comics

Main article: [King Kong (comics)](/wiki/King_Kong_(comics))

Cover of the 1932 novelization of *[King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong_(1933_film))* written by [Delos W. Lovelace](/wiki/Delos_W._Lovelace). This novelization was released just over two months before the film premiered in New York City on March 2, 1933.

Over the decades, there have been numerous books, novels, and comic books based on King Kong by various publishers.

In December 1932, as the film *[King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong_(1933_film))* was finishing production, [Merian C. Cooper](/wiki/Merian_C._Cooper) asked his friend [Delos W. Lovelace](/wiki/Delos_W._Lovelace) to adapt the film's screenplay into a novelization. Published by [Grosset & Dunlap](/wiki/Grosset_%26_Dunlap), the book was released later that month on December 27, just over 2 months before the film premiered on March 2, 1933. This was a part of the film's advance marketing campaign. The novelization was credited as being based on the "Screenplay by James A. Creelman and Ruth Rose. Novelized from the Radio Picture". The byline written under the title was "Conceived by Edgar Wallace and Merian C. Cooper". However, despite the credit, Wallace had very little to do with the story or the character. In an interview, author-artist Joe DeVito explains:

> From what I know, Edgar Wallace, a famous writer of the time, died very early in the process. Little if anything of his ever appeared in the final story, but his name was retained for its saleability ... King Kong was Cooper's creation, a [fantasy](/wiki/Fantasy) manifestation of his real life adventures. As many have mentioned before, Cooper was [Carl Denham](/wiki/Carl_Denham). His actual exploits rival anything [Indiana Jones](/wiki/Indiana_Jones_(character)) ever did in the movies.

—

This conclusion about Wallace's contribution was verified in the book *The Making of King Kong* by Orville Goldner and George E. Turner (1975) where Wallace stated in his diary, "Merian Cooper called and we talked over the big animal play we are going to write, or rather I am writing and he is directing", and "An announcement has been made in the local press that I am doing a super horror story with Merian Cooper, but the truth is it is much more his story than mine...I shall get much more credit out of the picture than I deserve if it is a success, but I shall be blamed by the public if it is a failure which seems fair". Wallace died of [pneumonia](/wiki/Pneumonia) complicated by [diabetes](/wiki/Diabetes) on February 10, 1932, and Cooper later had James Creelman and finally Ruth Rose finish the screenplay. Cooper would later state: "Actually, Edgar Wallace didn't write any of *Kong*, not one bloody word...I'd promised him credit and so I gave it to him".

Cooper issued a reprint of the novelization in 1965 that was published by [Bantam Books](/wiki/Bantam_Books). Some time later, the copyright expired and the publishing rights to the book fell into the public domain. Since then a myriad of publishers have reprinted the novelization numerous times. In 1983, Judith Conaway wrote a juvenile adaptation of the novelization called *King Kong (Step Up Adventures)* that featured illustrations by [Mike Berenstain](/wiki/Mike_Berenstain) and was published by Random House books, while [Anthony Browne](/wiki/Anthony_Browne_(author)) wrote and illustrated another juvenile adaptation called *Anthony Browne's King Kong* in 1994. Credited as "From the Story Conceived by Edgar Wallace & Merian C. Cooper", the book was published by the [Turner Publishing Company](/wiki/Turner_Publishing_Company). It was re-released as a paperback in the U.K in 2005 by [Picture Corgi](/wiki/Transworld_Publishers). Blackstone Audio produced an audio recording of the book in 2005 narrated by Stefan Rudnicki, while StarWarp Concepts released an Ebook version complete with 6 new illustrations from pulp-comic artist Paul Tuma in 2017.

Outside of the novelization, the film was serialized in a pulp magazine. In 1933, *Mystery* magazine published a *King Kong* [serial](/wiki/Serial_(literature)) under the byline of Edgar Wallace, and written by Walter F. Ripperger. This serialization was published in two parts in the February and March issues of the magazine.

In the U.K, the film was serialized in two different pulps, both on October 28, 1933: in the juvenile *Boys Magazine* (Vol. 23, No. 608), where the serialization was uncredited, and in that month's issue of *Cinema Weekly* where it was credited to Edgar Wallace and written by Draycott Montagu Dell (1888–1940). This short story adaptation would later appear in the [Peter Haining](/wiki/Peter_Haining_(author)) book called *Movie Monsters* in 1988, published by Severn House in the U.K. The novel was serialized in the [London Daily Herald](/wiki/Daily_Herald_(United_Kingdom)) by H. Kingsley Long as well. The serialization was first published in April 1933 and ran 37 installments.

In 1973, [Philip Jose Farmer](/wiki/Philip_Jose_Farmer) wrote a short story sequel to the Lovelace novelization called *After King Kong Fell* that was published in *OMEGA: a collection of original science fiction stories*.

In 1977, a novelization of the 1976 remake of *King Kong* was published by [Ace Books](/wiki/Ace_Books). This novelization was called *The Dino De Laurentiis Production of King Kong* and was simply the 1976 Lorenzo Semple Jr. script published in book form. The cover was done by [Frank Frazetta](/wiki/Frank_Frazetta).

To coincide with the 2005 remake of *King Kong*, various books were released to tie into the film. A novelization was written by [Christopher Golden](/wiki/Christopher_Golden) based on the screenplay by Fran Walsh, Philippa Boyens, and [Peter Jackson](/wiki/Peter_Jackson). Matt Costello wrote an official prequel to the film called *King Kong: The Island of the Skull*. These books were published by [Pocket Books](/wiki/Pocket_Books). Various illustrated juvenile books were published, as well, by [Harper Books](/wiki/HarperCollins): *Kong's Kingdom* was written by Julia Simon-Kerr; *Meet Kong and Ann* and *Journey to Skull Island* were written by Jennifer Franz; *Escape from Skull Island* and *Kong: The Eighth Wonder of the World—Junior Novel* were written by Laura J. Burns; *The Search for Kong* was written by Catherine Hapka; and finally, a Deluxe Sound Storybook of *Kong: The Eighth Wonder of the World* was written by Don Curry. [Weta Workshop](/wiki/Weta_Workshop) released a collection of concept art from the film entitled *The World of Kong: A Natural History of Skull Island* that was published by Pocket Books. The book was written and designed to resemble and read like an actual nature guide and historical record.

In 2005, Ibooks, Inc., published an unofficial book featuring King Kong called *Kong Reborn*, by [Russell Blackford](/wiki/Russell_Blackford).

Starting in 1996, artist/writer Joe DeVito began working with the Merian C. Cooper estate to write and/or illustrate various books based on the King Kong character. The first of these was an origin story labeled as an authorized sequel/prequel to the 1932 novelization of *King Kong* called *Kong: King of Skull Island*. This illustrated hardcover novel was published in 2004 by [DH Press](/wiki/DH_Press) and featured a story DeVito co-wrote with [Brad Strickland](/wiki/Brad_Strickland) and John Michlig. It also included an introduction by [Ray Harryhausen](/wiki/Ray_Harryhausen). A large paperback edition was then released in 2005, with extra pages at the end of the book. A CD audiobook narrated by Joey D'Auria was released by RadioArchives as well, and an interactive two-part app was released in 2011 and 2013, respectively, by Copyright 1957 LLC. In 2005, DeVito and Strickland co-wrote another book together called *Merian C. Cooper's King Kong* for the Merian C. Cooper estate. This book was published by [St. Martin's Press](/wiki/St._Martin%27s_Press). It was a full rewrite of the original 1932 novelization, which updates the language and paleontology and adds five new chapters. Some additional elements and characters tie into *Kong: King of Skull Island*, enabling the two separate books to form a continuous storyline.
In 2013, the first of two books featuring crossovers with pulp heroes was published. To coincide with the 80th anniversary of both King Kong and [Doc Savage](/wiki/Doc_Savage), [Altus Press](/wiki/Altus_Press) published *Doc Savage: Skull Island* in both softcover and hardcover editions. This officially sanctioned book was written by [Will Murray](/wiki/Will_Murray_(writer)) and based on concepts by DeVito. In 2016, Altus Press published the other crossover book, this time featuring a meeting between King Kong and [Tarzan](/wiki/Tarzan). The novel, called *[King Kong vs. Tarzan](/wiki/King_Kong_vs._Tarzan)*, was once again written by Will Murray and featured artwork by DeVito. In 2017, a new book featuring another origin story, written and illustrated by DeVito, was released, called *King Kong of Skull Island*. Expanded versions of the book titled *King Kong Skull Island: Exodus* and *King Kong Skull Island: The Wall* were released by Markosia in 2020.

In March 2017, to coincide with the release of *[Kong: Skull Island](/wiki/Kong:_Skull_Island)*, [Titan Books](/wiki/Titan_Books) released a novelization of the film written by Tim Lebbon and a hardcover book *The Art and Making of Kong: Skull Island* by Simon Ward.

In 2021, to coincide with the release of *[Godzilla vs. Kong](/wiki/Godzilla_vs._Kong)*, various tie-in books were released. On March 30, [Legendary Comics](/wiki/Legendary_Comics) released a graphic prequel novel called *Kingdom Kong* written by Marie Anello, as well as a children's picture book called *Kong and Me* written by Kiki Thorpe and illustrated by Nidhi Chanani.
On April 6, Titan Books released *Godzilla vs. Kong: The Official Movie Novelization* written by Greg Keyes. Insight Editions released a children's board book called *Godzilla vs. Kong: Sometimes Friends Fight (But They Always Make Up)* written by Carol Herring, and on May 21, also released *Godzilla vs. Kong: One Will Fall, The Art of the Ultimate Battle Royale* written by Daniel Wallace.

In April 2024, to coincide with the release of *[Godzilla x Kong: The New Empire](/wiki/Godzilla_x_Kong:_The_New_Empire)*, [Titan Books](/wiki/Titan_Books) released a novelization of the film written by Greg Keyes.

### Theme park rides

*Skull Island: Reign of Kong* at [Universal's Islands of Adventure](/wiki/Universal%27s_Islands_of_Adventure)

Universal Studios has had popular King Kong attractions at [Universal Studios Hollywood](/wiki/Universal_Studios_Hollywood) in [Universal City, California](/wiki/Universal_City,_California) and [Universal Orlando](/wiki/Universal_Orlando) Resort in [Orlando, Florida](/wiki/Orlando,_Florida).

The first King Kong attraction was called *[King Kong Encounter](/wiki/King_Kong_Encounter)* and was a part of the [Studio Tour](/wiki/Studio_Tour) at Universal Studios Hollywood. Based upon the 1976 film *King Kong*, the tour took the guests in the world of 1976 New York City, where Kong was seen wreaking havoc on the city. It was opened on June 14, 1986, and was destroyed on June 1, 2008, in a major fire. Universal opened a replacement 3D King Kong ride called *[King Kong: 360 3-D](/wiki/King_Kong:_360_3-D)* that opened on July 1, 2010, based upon [Peter Jackson](/wiki/Peter_Jackson)'s 2005 film *King Kong*.

A second more elaborate ride was constructed at [Universal Studios Florida](/wiki/Universal_Studios_Florida) on June 7, 1990, called *[Kongfrontation](/wiki/Kongfrontation)*. The ride featured a stand-alone extended version of *King Kong Encounter* and pinned guests escaping on the [Roosevelt Island Tramway](/wiki/Roosevelt_Island_Tramway) from Kong, who was rampaging across New York City. The ride was closed down on September 8, 2002, and was replaced with *[Revenge of the Mummy](/wiki/Revenge_of_the_Mummy)* on May 21, 2004.

A new King Kong attraction titled *[Skull Island: Reign of Kong](/wiki/Skull_Island:_Reign_of_Kong)* opened at [Islands of Adventure](/wiki/Islands_of_Adventure) on July 13, 2016, making it the first King Kong themed ride in Orlando since Kongfrontation closed down 14 years earlier at Universal Studios Florida.

A ride based on the [Monsterverse](/wiki/Monsterverse) titled *Kong x Godzilla: The Ride* opened at the [Lotte World](/wiki/Lotte_World) Adventure in Seoul, South Korea in December 2025.

### Musical

Further information: [King Kong (2013 musical)](/wiki/King_Kong_(2013_musical))

A musical adaptation of the story (endorsed by Merian C. Cooper's estate) was staged in [Melbourne](/wiki/Melbourne) at the Regent Theatre. The show premiered on June 15, 2013, as *King Kong: The Eighth Wonder of The World*, with music by Marius De Vries. The musical then premiered on Broadway on November 8, 2018, at the [Broadway Theatre](/wiki/Broadway_Theatre_(53rd_Street)) as *King Kong: Alive on Broadway*. The creative team included book writer Jack Thorne, director-choreographer Drew McOnie, and Australian songwriter Eddie Perfect, who replace the former creatives.

The huge King Kong puppet was created by Global Creature Technology. The puppet stands 20 feet tall and weighs 2,400 pounds. It is operated by a large rig with 10 onstage puppeteers, and features an array of microprocessors and tiny motors that power nuanced movements in the facial features. According to Sonny Tilders, who designed the Fiberglas and steel puppet for Global Creatures Company, "It's the most sophisticated marionette puppet ever made". Tilders also stated that Kong is built in layers, and is "quite similar to genuine anatomy". Over the steel skeleton, the body shell is a mixture of hard Fiberglas, enforced inflatables, high-pressure inflatables, and bags full of styrene beans that stretch and contort like muscles. Tilders said that they wanted to create the sense that Kong is a moving sculpture.

### King Kong in the name

A publicity still of Isamu Yamaguchi playing King Kong on stage from *[Wasei Kingu Kongu](/wiki/Wasei_Kingu_Kongu)*

There were other unofficial movies to have borne the "King Kong" name:

- A lost silent Japanese short, *[Japanese King Kong](/wiki/Wasei_Kingu_Kongu)* (和製キングコング, *Wasei Kingu Kongu*), directed by [Torajiro Saito](/wiki/Torajiro_Saito), featuring an all-Japanese cast and produced by the [Shochiku](/wiki/Shochiku) company, was released in 1933. The plot revolves around a down-on-his-luck man who plays the King Kong character in a vaudeville theater to earn money to woo a girl he likes. The film does not actually involve King Kong *per se*.
- *[The King Kong That Appeared in Edo](/wiki/The_King_Kong_That_Appeared_in_Edo)* (江戸に現れたキングコング, *Edo ni Arawareta Kingu Kongu*): a lost two-part silent Japanese period piece that was produced by a company called Zensho Kinema in 1938. The film revolves around kidnapping and revenge amongst the characters. The "King Kong" in this film is a trained ape (that looks more like a Yeti) who is used to kidnap one of the characters. Judging by the plot synopsis presented by periodicals at the time, the "King Kong" is regular-sized and is only depicted as gigantic on the advertisements for promotional purposes.
- The 1959 Hong Kong film *King Kong's Adventures in the Heavenly Palace* (猩猩王大鬧天宮), which features a normal-sized gorilla.
- The Hindi films *King Kong* (1962) and *Tarzan and King Kong* (1965), which feature the professional wrestler [King Kong](/wiki/Emile_Czaja) and have nothing to do with the famous movie monster, although the latter film features a normal-sized gorilla.
- The 1968 Italian film *[Kong Island](/wiki/Kong_Island)* (*Eva, la Venere selvaggia*, lit. 'Eve, the wild Venus'), which was advertised in the U.S. as *[King of Kong Island](/wiki/Kong_Island)*. Despite the American title, the film features normal-sized gorillas and takes place in Africa.
- The 1981 Mexican film *Las Muñecas Del King Kong* (*The Dolls of King Kong*), which features exotic jungle girls. The "King Kong" in the film is simply a giant ape statue on top of a building.

### Related films

- The premise of a giant gorilla brought to the United States for entertainment purposes, and subsequently wreaking havoc, was recycled in *[Mighty Joe Young](/wiki/Mighty_Joe_Young_(1949_film))* (1949), through the same studio and with much of the same principal talent as the 1933 original. It was [remade in 1998](/wiki/Mighty_Joe_Young_(1998_film)).
- *King Kong* bears some similarities with an earlier effort by special effects head [Willis H. O'Brien](/wiki/Willis_H._O%27Brien), *[The Lost World](/wiki/The_Lost_World_(1925_film))* (1925), in which [dinosaurs](/wiki/Dinosaur) are found living on an isolated [plateau](/wiki/Plateau). It was based on Sir [Arthur Conan Doyle](/wiki/Arthur_Conan_Doyle)'s [novel of the same name](/wiki/The_Lost_World_(Doyle_novel)).
- *Banglar King Kong* – an unofficial [Bangladeshi](/wiki/Bangladesh) musical based on the King Kong story and directed by Iftekar Jahan. The film uses large amounts of stock footage from the films *[King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong_(1976_film))* and *[Mighty Joe Young](/wiki/Mighty_Joe_Young_(1998_film))*, as well as [National Geographic](/wiki/National_Geographic_Society) documentaries, and premiered in June 2010 in the Purnima Cinema Hall in [Dhaka](/wiki/Dhaka).
- Other similar giant ape films include:
  - The 1949 American comedy film *[Africa Screams](/wiki/Africa_Screams)*, where a giant ape briefly appears.
  - The 1961 British film *[Konga](/wiki/Konga_(film))*, where a chimpanzee is enlarged after being fed a growth serum by a deranged scientist and attacks [London](/wiki/London).
  - The 1969 American film *[The Mighty Gorga](/wiki/The_Mighty_Gorga)*, which features a circus owner's quest to capture a giant gorilla in an African jungle. Unlike King Kong, Gorga remains in Africa.
  - The 1976 [Korean](/wiki/Korea) [3D](/wiki/3-D_film) film *[Ape](/wiki/Ape_(1976_film))*, where a giant ape runs amok in [Seoul](/wiki/Seoul), [South Korea](/wiki/South_Korea).
  - The 1976 British film *[Queen Kong](/wiki/Queen_Kong)*, a film that parodies *King Kong* with a gender reversal between the giant ape and the object of the ape's affection.
  - The 1977 [Hong Kong](/wiki/Hong_Kong) film *[The Mighty Peking Man](/wiki/The_Mighty_Peking_Man)*, featuring a huge [Bigfoot](/wiki/Bigfoot)/ape-like creature that attacks Hong Kong after it was brought to Hong Kong from its territory somewhere in India near the Himalayas.
  - The 1977 [Italian](/wiki/Italy) film *[Yeti: Giant of the 20th Century](/wiki/Yeti:_Giant_of_the_20th_Century)*, featuring a giant [abominable snowman](/wiki/Yeti) running amok in Ontario after a millionaire industrialist thaws it out of a block of ice.
  - The 2005 [The Asylum](/wiki/The_Asylum) film *[King of the Lost World](/wiki/King_of_the_Lost_World)* is a [mockbuster](/wiki/Mockbuster) of Jackson's *King Kong*, loosely based on the aforementioned *The Lost World*.
  - The 2018 American film *[Rampage](/wiki/Rampage_(2018_film))*, featuring a giant [albino](/wiki/Albinism) [western lowland gorilla](/wiki/Western_lowland_gorilla) named George. The film is based on the 1986 [arcade game](/wiki/Arcade_game) *[Rampage](/wiki/Rampage_(video_game))*, which itself is a video game parody of giant monster films.
