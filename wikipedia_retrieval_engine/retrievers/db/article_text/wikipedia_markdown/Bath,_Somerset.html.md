**Bath** ([RP](/wiki/Received_Pronunciation): [/bɑːθ/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English), locally [[ba(ː)θ]](/wiki/Help:IPA)) is a city in [Somerset](/wiki/Somerset), England, known for and named after its [Roman-built baths](/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)). At the 2021 census, the population was 94,080. Bath is in the valley of the [River Avon](/wiki/River_Avon,_Bristol), 97 miles (156 km) west of London and 11 miles (18 km) southeast of [Bristol](/wiki/Bristol). The city became a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](/wiki/UNESCO_World_Heritage_Site) in 1987, and was later added to the transnational World Heritage Site known as the "[Great Spa Towns of Europe](/wiki/Great_Spa_Towns_of_Europe)" in 2021. Bath is also the largest city and settlement in Somerset.

The city became a [spa](/wiki/Spa) with the [Latin](/wiki/Latin) name **[Aquae Sulis](/wiki/Aquae_Sulis)** ("the waters of [Sulis](/wiki/Sulis)") c. 60 AD when the Romans built [baths](/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)) and a temple in the valley of the River Avon, although [hot springs](/wiki/List_of_geothermal_springs_in_the_United_Kingdom) were known even before then. [Bath Abbey](/wiki/Bath_Abbey) was founded in the 7th century and became a religious centre; the building was rebuilt in the 12th and 16th centuries. In the 17th century, claims were made for the curative properties of water from the springs, and Bath became popular as a [spa town](/wiki/Spa_town) in the [Georgian era](/wiki/Georgian_era). [Georgian architecture](/wiki/Georgian_architecture), crafted from [Bath Stone](/wiki/Bath_Stone), includes the [Royal Crescent](/wiki/Royal_Crescent), [Circus](/wiki/The_Circus,_Bath), [Pump Room](/wiki/Pump_Room), and the [Assembly Rooms](/wiki/Bath_Assembly_Rooms), where [Beau Nash](/wiki/Beau_Nash) presided over the city's social life from 1705 until his death in 1761.

Many of the streets and squares were laid out by [John Wood, the Elder](/wiki/John_Wood,_the_Elder), and in the 18th century the city became fashionable and the population grew. [Jane Austen](/wiki/Jane_Austen) lived in Bath in the early 19th century. Further building was undertaken in the 19th century and following the [Bath Blitz](/wiki/Bath_Blitz) in World War II. Bath became part of the county of [Avon](/wiki/Avon_(county)) in 1974, and, following Avon's abolition in 1996, has been the principal centre of [Bath and North East Somerset](/wiki/Bath_and_North_East_Somerset).

Bath has over 6 million yearly visitors, making it [one of the ten English cities visited most by overseas tourists](/wiki/Tourism_in_England#Cities). Attractions include the spas, canal boat tours, Royal Crescent, [Bath Skyline](/wiki/Bath_Skyline), [Parade Gardens](/wiki/Parade_Gardens), [Sydney Gardens](/wiki/Sydney_Gardens) and [Royal Victoria Park](/wiki/Royal_Victoria_Park,_Bath) which hosts [carnivals](/wiki/Carnival) and seasonal events. Shopping areas include [SouthGate shopping centre](/wiki/SouthGate,_Bath), [the Corridor](/wiki/The_Corridor,_Bath) [arcade](/wiki/Arcade_(architecture)) and artisan shops at [Walcot](/wiki/Walcot,_Bath), [Milsom](/wiki/Milsom_Street,_Bath), [Stall](/wiki/Stall_Street,_Bath) and York Streets. There are theatres, including the [Theatre Royal](/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Bath), as well as several museums including the [Museum of Bath Architecture](/wiki/Museum_of_Bath_Architecture), the [Victoria Art Gallery](/wiki/Victoria_Art_Gallery), the [Museum of East Asian Art](/wiki/Museum_of_East_Asian_Art,_Bath), the [Herschel Museum of Astronomy](/wiki/Herschel_Museum_of_Astronomy), [Fashion Museum](/wiki/Fashion_Museum,_Bath), and the [Holburne Museum](/wiki/Holburne_Museum). The city has two universities – the [University of Bath](/wiki/University_of_Bath) and [Bath Spa University](/wiki/Bath_Spa_University) – with [Bath College](/wiki/Bath_College) providing [further education](/wiki/Further_education). Sporting clubs from the city include [Bath Rugby](/wiki/Bath_Rugby) and [Bath City](/wiki/Bath_City).

## History

See also: [Timeline of Bath, Somerset](/wiki/Timeline_of_Bath,_Somerset)

### Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages

The hills in the locality such as [Bathampton Down](/wiki/Bathampton_Down) saw human activity from the [Mesolithic](/wiki/Mesolithic) period. Several [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age) [round barrows](/wiki/Round_barrow) were opened by [John Skinner](/wiki/John_Skinner_(archaeologist)) in the 18th century. A [long barrow](/wiki/Long_barrow) site believed to be from the [Early Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age_Europe) [Beaker people](/wiki/Beaker_people) was flattened to make way for [RAF Charmy Down](/wiki/RAF_Charmy_Down). [Solsbury Hill](/wiki/Solsbury_Hill) overlooking the current city was an [Iron Age](/wiki/Iron_Age) [hill fort](/wiki/Hill_fort) and the adjacent Bathampton Camp may also have been one.

### Roman baths and town

Main article: [Aquae Sulis](/wiki/Aquae_Sulis)

19th-century [photochrom](/wiki/Photochrom) of the Great Bath at the [Roman Baths](/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)). The entire structure above the level of the pillar bases is a later construction and was not a feature of the building in Roman days.

Archaeological evidence shows that the site of the [Roman baths'](/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)) main spring may have been treated as a shrine by the [Britons](/wiki/British_Iron_Age), and was dedicated to the goddess [Sulis](/wiki/Sulis), whom the [Romans](/wiki/Roman_mythology) identified with [Minerva](/wiki/Minerva); the name Sulis continued to be used after the Roman invasion, appearing in the town's [Roman name](/wiki/List_of_Roman_place_names_in_Britain), **Aquae Sulis** (literally, "the waters of Sulis"). Messages to her scratched onto metal, known as [curse tablets](/wiki/Bath_curse_tablets), have been recovered from the sacred spring by archaeologists. The tablets were written in [Latin](/wiki/Latin), and laid curses on personal enemies. For example, if a citizen had his clothes stolen at the baths, he might write a curse against the suspects on a tablet to be read by the goddess.

A temple was constructed in AD 60–70, and a bathing complex was built up over the next 300 years. Engineers drove oak piles into the mud to provide a stable foundation, and surrounded the spring with an irregular stone chamber lined with lead. In the 2nd century, the spring was enclosed within a wooden [barrel-vaulted](/wiki/Barrel-vaulted) structure that housed the [caldarium](/wiki/Caldarium) (hot bath), [tepidarium](/wiki/Tepidarium) (warm bath), and [frigidarium](/wiki/Frigidarium) (cold bath).

The town was later given [defensive walls](/wiki/Bath_city_walls), probably in the 3rd century. After the failure of Roman authority in the first decade of the 5th century, the baths fell into disrepair and were eventually lost as a result of rising water levels and silting.

In March 2012, a hoard of 30,000 silver Roman coins, one of the largest discovered in Britain, was unearthed in an archaeological dig. The coins, believed to date from the 3rd century, were found about 150 m (490 ft) from the Roman baths.

### Post-Roman and medieval

[Bath Abbey](/wiki/Bath_Abbey)

It was suggested by [Geoffrey of Monmouth](/wiki/Geoffrey_of_Monmouth) that a hillfort on the outskirts of Bath was the site of the 6th century [Battle of Badon](/wiki/Battle_of_Badon), in which [Arthur](/wiki/King_Arthur) defeated the [Anglo-Saxons](/wiki/Anglo-Saxons), though that is not accepted by the majority of modern historians. The town was captured by the [West Saxons](/wiki/History_of_Anglo-Saxon_England) in 577 after the [Battle of Deorham](/wiki/Battle_of_Deorham); the Anglo-Saxon poem *[The Ruin](/wiki/The_Ruin)* may describe the appearance of the Roman site about this time. A monastery was founded at an early date – reputedly by [Saint David](/wiki/Saint_David) although more probably in 675 by [Osric](/wiki/Osric,_king_of_the_Hwicce), King of the [Hwicce](/wiki/Hwicce), perhaps using the [walled area](/wiki/Bath_city_walls) as its precinct. [Nennius](/wiki/Nennius), a 9th-century historian, mentions a "Hot Lake" in the land of the Hwicce along the [River Severn](/wiki/River_Severn), and adds "It is surrounded by a wall, made of brick and stone, and men may go there to bathe at any time, and every man can have the kind of bath he likes. If he wants, it will be a cold bath; and if he wants a hot bath, it will be hot". [Bede](/wiki/Bede) described hot baths in the geographical introduction to the *Ecclesiastical History* in terms very similar to those of Nennius. [King Offa](/wiki/King_Offa) of [Mercia](/wiki/Mercia) gained control of the monastery in 781 and rebuilt the church, which was dedicated to [St. Peter](/wiki/St._Peter).

According to the Victorian churchman [Edward Churton](/wiki/Edward_Churton), during the Anglo-Saxon era Bath was known as *Acemannesceastre* ('Akemanchester'), or 'aching men's city', on account of the reputation these springs had for healing the sick.

Map of Bath by [John Speed](/wiki/John_Speed) published in 1610

By the 9th century, the old Roman street pattern was lost and Bath was a royal possession. [King Alfred](/wiki/King_Alfred) laid out the town afresh, leaving its south-eastern quadrant as the abbey precinct. In the [Burghal Hidage](/wiki/Burghal_Hidage), Bath is recorded as a [burh](/wiki/Burh) (borough) and is described as having walls of 1,375 yards (1,257 m) and was allocated 1000 men for defence. During the reign of [Edward the Elder](/wiki/Edward_the_Elder) coins were [minted](/wiki/Mint_(coin)) in Bath based on a design from the [Winchester](/wiki/Winchester) mint but with 'BAD' on the obverse relating to the Anglo-Saxon name for the town, Baðum, Baðan or Baðon, meaning "at the baths", and this was the source of the present name. [Edgar of England](/wiki/Edgar_of_England) was crowned king of England in [Bath Abbey](/wiki/Bath_Abbey) in 973, in a ceremony that formed the basis of all future [English coronations](/wiki/Coronation_of_the_British_monarch).

[William Rufus](/wiki/William_Rufus) granted the town, abbey and mint to a royal physician, [John of Tours](/wiki/John_of_Tours), who became Bishop of [Wells](/wiki/Wells,_Somerset) and Abbot of Bath, following the sacking of the town during the [Rebellion of 1088](/wiki/Rebellion_of_1088). It was papal policy for bishops to move to more urban seats, and John of Tours [translated](/wiki/Translation_(ecclesiastical)) his own from Wells to Bath. The bishop planned and began a much larger church as his cathedral, to which was attached a priory, with the bishop's palace beside it. New baths were built around the three springs. Later bishops returned the episcopal seat to Wells while retaining the name Bath in the title, [Bishop of Bath and Wells](/wiki/Bishop_of_Bath_and_Wells). [St John's Hospital](/wiki/St_John%27s_Hospital,_Bath) was founded around 1180 by Bishop [Reginald Fitz Jocelin](/wiki/Reginald_Fitz_Jocelin) and is among the oldest [almshouses](/wiki/Almshouse) in England. The 'hospital of the baths' was built beside the hot springs of the [Cross Bath](/wiki/Cross_Bath), for their health-giving properties and to provide shelter for the poor infirm.

Administrative systems fell within the [hundreds](/wiki/Hundred_(county_division)). The [Bath Hundred](/wiki/Bath_Forum) had various names including the Hundred of Le Buri. The Bath Foreign Hundred or Forinsecum covered the area outside the city and was later combined into the Bath Forum Hundred. Wealthy merchants had no status within the hundred courts and formed [guilds](/wiki/English_guilds) to gain influence. They built the first [guildhall](/wiki/Guildhall) probably in the 13th century. Around 1200, the [first mayor](/wiki/List_of_Mayors_of_Bath) was appointed.

### Early modern

The South Prospect of Bath as depicted in Millerd's 1673 map of Bristol

By the 15th century, Bath's abbey church was dilapidated and [Oliver King](/wiki/Oliver_King), Bishop of Bath and Wells, decided to rebuild it on a smaller scale in 1500. The new church was completed just a few years before Bath Priory was [dissolved](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Monasteries) in 1539 by [Henry VIII](/wiki/Henry_VIII). The abbey church became derelict before being restored as the city's [parish church](/wiki/Parish_church) in the [Elizabethan era](/wiki/Elizabethan_era), when the city experienced a revival as a [spa](/wiki/Spa). The baths were improved and the city began to attract the aristocracy. A [royal charter](/wiki/Royal_charter) granted by Queen [Elizabeth I](/wiki/Elizabeth_I) in 1590 confirmed [city status](/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom). James Montagu, Bishop of Bath and Wells from 1608, spent considerable sums in restoring Bath Abbey and actively supported the Baths themselves, aware that the 'towne liveth wholly by them'. In 1613, perhaps at his behest, Queen Anne visited the town to take the waters: the Queen's Bath was named after her. The cue for the visit may have been the completion of the restoration work to Bath Abbey, the last instalment of which had been paid for two years previously. [Anne of Denmark](/wiki/Anne_of_Denmark_and_the_spa_at_Bath) came to Bath in 1613 and 1615. By the beginning of the [English Civil War](/wiki/English_Civil_War), the city was a first-class resort. However, it lost much of this trade in 1642; with the outbreak of war, fiddlers, "ladies who are there", and ale-house guides, lost their customers.

The city was initially garrisoned for [Charles I](/wiki/Charles_I_of_England). Seven thousand pounds was spent on fortifications, but on the appearance of parliamentary forces the gates were thrown open and the city surrendered. It became a significant post for the Western Association army under [William Waller](/wiki/William_Waller). Bath was retaken by the royalists in July 1643 following the [Battle of Lansdowne](/wiki/Battle_of_Lansdowne) and occupied for two years until 1645. The city was spared widespread destruction of property, overcrowding, bubonic plague, or starvation of its inhabitants, etc, unlike nearby Bristol and [Gloucester](/wiki/Gloucester), and it had good water piped in from its surrounding hills. Still, soldiers who were billeted in private houses contributed to disorder and vandalism, though this never caused the general destruction and plundering seen in [Marlborough](/wiki/Marlborough,_Wiltshire) and other towns. Bath remained a health resort, often for wounded soldiers, its markets continued open and well-regulated, and its shopkeepers and craftsmen continued busy. Nevertheless, council spending, rents and grants all decreased and the finances of the Bath City Council were seriously affected.

Normality to the city quickly recovered after the war when the city council achieved a healthy budget surplus. [Thomas Guidott](/wiki/Thomas_Guidott), a student of chemistry and medicine at [Wadham College, Oxford](/wiki/Wadham_College,_Oxford), set up a practice in the city in 1668. He was interested in the curative properties of the waters, and he wrote *A discourse of Bathe, and the hot waters there. Also, Some Enquiries into the Nature of the water* in 1676. It brought the health-giving properties of the hot mineral waters to the attention of the country, and the aristocracy arrived to partake in them.

[Royal Crescent](/wiki/Royal_Crescent) and Circus from the air (connected by link road, thus creating the famous "[question mark](/wiki/Question_mark)" formation). Georgian taste favoured the regularity of Bath's streets and squares and the contrast with adjacent rural nature.

[The Circus](/wiki/The_Circus,_Bath)

Several areas of the city were developed in the [Stuart](/wiki/House_of_Stuart) period, and more building took place during [Georgian](/wiki/Georgian_era) times in response to the increasing number of visitors who required accommodation. Architects [John Wood the Elder](/wiki/John_Wood_the_Elder) and [his son](/wiki/John_Wood,_the_Younger) laid out the new quarters in streets and squares, the identical façades of which gave an impression of palatial scale and classical decorum. Much of the creamy gold [Bath stone](/wiki/Bath_stone), a type of [limestone](/wiki/Limestone) used for construction in the city, was obtained from the [Combe Down and Bathampton Down Mines](/wiki/Combe_Down_and_Bathampton_Down_Mines) owned by [Ralph Allen](/wiki/Ralph_Allen) (1694–1764). Allen, to advertise the quality of his quarried limestone, commissioned the elder John Wood to build a country house on his [Prior Park](/wiki/Prior_Park) estate between the city and the mines. Allen was responsible for improving and expanding the postal service in western England, for which he held the contract for more than forty years. Although not fond of politics, Allen was a civic-minded man and a member of Bath Corporation for many years. He was elected mayor for a single term in 1742.

In the early 18th century, Bath acquired its first purpose-built theatre, the [Old Orchard Street Theatre](/wiki/Old_Orchard_Street_Theatre). It was rebuilt as the [Theatre Royal](/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Bath), along with the [Grand Pump Room](/wiki/Grand_Pump_Room) attached to the Roman Baths and [assembly rooms](/wiki/Bath_Assembly_Rooms). [Master of ceremonies](/wiki/Master_of_ceremonies) [Beau Nash](/wiki/Beau_Nash), who presided over the city's social life from 1704 until his death in 1761, drew up a code of behaviour for public entertainments. Bath had become perhaps the most fashionable of the rapidly developing British spa towns, attracting many notable visitors such as the wealthy London bookseller [Andrew Millar](/wiki/Andrew_Millar_(publisher)) and his wife, who both made long visits. In 1816, it was described as "a seat of amusement and dissipation", where "scenes of extravagance in this receptacle of the wealthy and the idle, the weak and designing" were habitual.

### Late modern

An 1850s photograph of Green Street

Looking north-west from [Bathwick Hill](/wiki/Bathwick_Hill) towards the northern suburbs, showing the variety of housing typical of Bath

The population of the city was 40,020 at the 1801 census, making it one of the largest cities in Britain. [William Thomas Beckford](/wiki/William_Thomas_Beckford) bought a house in [Lansdown Crescent](/wiki/Lansdown_Crescent,_Bath) in 1822, and subsequently two adjacent houses to form his residence. Having acquired all the land between his home and the top of [Lansdown Hill](/wiki/Lansdown,_Bath), he created a garden more than 1⁄2 mile (800 m) in length and built [Beckford's Tower](/wiki/Beckford%27s_Tower) at the top.

Emperor [Haile Selassie](/wiki/Haile_Selassie) of Ethiopia spent four years in exile, from 1936 to 1940, at [Fairfield House](/wiki/Fairfield_House,_Bath) in Bath. During [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), between the evening of 25 April and the early morning of 27 April 1942, Bath suffered three air raids in reprisal for [RAF](/wiki/RAF) raids on the German cities of [Lübeck](/wiki/L%C3%BCbeck) and [Rostock](/wiki/Rostock), part of the [Luftwaffe](/wiki/Luftwaffe) campaign popularly known as the [Baedeker Blitz](/wiki/Baedeker_Blitz). During the [Bath Blitz](/wiki/Bath_Blitz), more than 400 people were killed, and more than 19,000 buildings damaged or destroyed.

Houses in [Royal Crescent](/wiki/Royal_Crescent), [Circus](/wiki/The_Circus,_Bath) and [Paragon](/wiki/The_Paragon,_Bath) were burnt out along with the [Assembly Rooms](/wiki/Bath_Assembly_Rooms). A 500-kilogram (1,100 lb) [high explosive](/wiki/Explosive_material) bomb landed on the east side of [Queen Square](/wiki/Queen_Square_(Bath)), resulting in houses on the south side being damaged and the [Francis Hotel](/wiki/Francis_Hotel,_Bath) losing 24 metres (79 ft) of its frontage. The buildings have all been restored although there are still signs of the bombing.

A postwar review of inadequate housing led to the clearance and redevelopment of areas of the city in a postwar style, often at variance with the local Georgian style. In the 1950s, the nearby villages of [Combe Down](/wiki/Combe_Down), [Twerton](/wiki/Twerton) and [Weston](/wiki/Weston,_Bath) were incorporated into the city to enable the development of housing, much of it [council housing](/wiki/Council_house). In 1965, town planner [Colin Buchanan](/wiki/Colin_Buchanan_(town_planner)) published *Bath: A Planning and Transport Study*, which to a large degree sought to better accommodate the motor car, including the idea of a traffic tunnel underneath the centre of Bath. Though criticised by conservationists, some parts of the plan were implemented.

In the 1970s and 1980s, it was recognised that conservation of historic buildings was inadequate, leading to more care and reuse of buildings and open spaces. In 1987, the city was selected by [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) as a [World Heritage Site](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site), recognising its international cultural significance.

Between 1991 and 2000, Bath was the scene of a series of rapes committed by an unidentified man dubbed the "[Batman rapist](/wiki/Batman_rapist)". The attacker remains at large and is the subject of Britain's longest-running serial rape investigation. He is said to have a [tights](/wiki/Tights) [fetish](/wiki/Sexual_fetish), have a scar below his bottom lip and resides in the Bath area or knows it very well. He has also been linked to the unsolved [murder of Melanie Hall](/wiki/Murder_of_Melanie_Hall), which occurred in the city in 1996. Although the offender's DNA is known and several thousand men in Bath were DNA tested, the attacker continues to evade police.

Since 2000, major developments have included the [Thermae Bath Spa](/wiki/Thermae_Bath_Spa), the [SouthGate](/wiki/SouthGate) shopping centre, the residential Western Riverside project on the [Stothert & Pitt](/wiki/Stothert_%26_Pitt) factory site, and the riverside Bath Quays office and business development. In 2021, Bath become part of a second UNESCO World Heritage Site, a group of spa towns across Europe known as the "[Great Spas of Europe](/wiki/Great_Spas_of_Europe)". This makes it one of the only places to be formally recognised twice as a [World Heritage](/wiki/World_Heritage) site.

## Government

[The Guildhall](/wiki/Guildhall,_Bath)

Since 1996, the city has had a [single tier of local government](/wiki/Unitary_authorities_in_England) — [Bath and North East Somerset Council](/wiki/Bath_and_North_East_Somerset_Council).

### Historical development

Bath had long been an [ancient borough](/wiki/Ancient_borough), having that status since 878 when it became a royal borough ([burh](/wiki/Burghal_Hidage)) of [Alfred the Great](/wiki/Alfred_the_Great), and was reformed into a [municipal borough](/wiki/Municipal_borough) in 1835. It has formed part of the [county](/wiki/Historic_counties_of_England) of Somerset since 878, when ceded to [Wessex](/wiki/Wessex), having previously been in [Mercia](/wiki/Mercia) (the River Avon had acted as the border between the two kingdoms since 628). However, Bath was made a [county borough](/wiki/County_borough) in 1889, independent of the newly created [administrative county](/wiki/Administrative_counties_of_England) and [Somerset County Council](/wiki/Somerset_County_Council). Bath became part of [Avon](/wiki/Avon_(county)) when the [non-metropolitan county](/wiki/Non-metropolitan_county) was created in 1974, resulting in its abolition as a county borough, and instead became a non-metropolitan district with [borough status](/wiki/Borough_status).

With the abolition of Avon in 1996, the non-metropolitan district and borough were abolished too, and Bath has since been part of the [unitary authority](/wiki/Unitary_authority) district of [Bath and North East Somerset](/wiki/Bath_and_North_East_Somerset) (B&NES). The unitary district included also the [Wansdyke](/wiki/Wansdyke_(district)) district and therefore includes a wider area than the city (the 'North East Somerset' element) including [Keynsham](/wiki/Keynsham) which is home to many of the council's offices, though the council meets at the [Guildhall](/wiki/Guildhall,_Bath) in Bath.

Bath was returned to the [ceremonial county](/wiki/Ceremonial_county) of Somerset in 1996, though as B&NES is a unitary authority, it is not part of the area covered by Somerset County Council.

### Charter trustees

Bath City Council was abolished in 1996, along with the [district](/wiki/Districts_of_England) of Bath, and there is no longer a [parish council](/wiki/Parish_councils_in_England) for the city. The City of Bath's ceremonial functions, including its [formal status](/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom) as a city, [its twinning](#Twinning) arrangements, [the mayoralty of Bath](/wiki/List_of_Mayors_of_Bath) – which can be traced back to 1230 – and control of the city's [coat of arms](/wiki/Coat_of_arms), are maintained by the [charter trustees](/wiki/Charter_trustees) of the City of Bath.

The councillors elected by the electoral wards that cover Bath ([see below](#Electoral_wards)) are the trustees, and they elect one of their number as their chair and mayor. The mayor holds office for one municipal year and in modern times the mayor begins their term in office on the first Saturday in June, at a ceremony at Bath Abbey with a civic procession from and to the Guildhall. The 798th mayor, who began his office on 7 June 2025, is Bharat Pankhania. A deputy mayor is also elected.

### Coat of arms

The coat of arms includes a depiction of the [city wall](/wiki/Bath_city_walls), and two silver stripes representing the [River Avon](/wiki/River_Avon,_Bristol) and the hot springs. The sword of [St. Paul](/wiki/St._Paul) is a link to Bath Abbey. The supporters, a lion and a bear, stand on a bed of [acorns](/wiki/Acorn), a link to [Bladud](/wiki/Bladud), the subject of the Legend of Bath. The knight's helmet indicates a municipality and the [crown](/wiki/Crown) is that of King Edgar (referencing his coronation at the Abbey). A [mural crown](/wiki/Mural_crown), indicating a [city](/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom), is alternatively used instead of the helmet and Edgar's crown.

The Arms bear the motto "Aqvae Svlis", the Roman name for Bath in [Latin](/wiki/Latin) script; although not on the Arms, the motto "Floreat Bathon" is sometimes used ("may Bath flourish" in Latin).

### Bath Area Forum

Bath and North East Somerset Council has established the Bath City Forum, comprising B&NES councillors representing wards in Bath and up to 13 co-opted members drawn from the communities of the city. The first meeting of the Forum was held on 13 October 2015, at the Guildhall, where the first chair and vice-chair were elected. In 2021, this was re-launched as the Bath Area Forum.

### Parliamentary elections

See also: [Bath (UK Parliament constituency)](/wiki/Bath_(UK_Parliament_constituency))

Bath is one of the oldest extant [parliamentary constituencies](/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom) in the United Kingdom, being in continuous existence since the [Model Parliament](/wiki/Model_Parliament) of 1295. Before the [Reform Act 1832](/wiki/Reform_Act_1832), Bath elected two members to the [unreformed House of Commons](/wiki/Unreformed_House_of_Commons), as an ancient parliamentary borough. From 1832 until 1918 it elected two MPs and then was reduced to one.

Historically the constituency covered only the city of Bath; however, it was enlarged into some outlying areas between 1997 and 2010. The constituency since 2010 once again covers exactly the city of Bath and is currently represented by [Liberal Democrat](/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_(UK)) [Wera Hobhouse](/wiki/Wera_Hobhouse) who beat [Conservative](/wiki/Conservative_Party_(UK)) [Ben Howlett](/wiki/Ben_Howlett_(politician)) at the [2017 general election](/wiki/2017_United_Kingdom_general_election) and retained her seat at the [2019 general election.](/wiki/2019_United_Kingdom_general_election) Howlett had replaced the retiring [Liberal Democrat](/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_(UK)) [Don Foster](/wiki/Don_Foster,_Baron_Foster_of_Bath) at the [2015 general election](/wiki/2015_United_Kingdom_general_election). Foster's election was a notable result of the [1992 general election](/wiki/1992_United_Kingdom_general_election), as [Chris Patten](/wiki/Chris_Patten), the previous Member (and [Cabinet Minister](/wiki/Cabinet_of_the_United_Kingdom)) played a major part, as [Chairman of the Conservative Party](/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Conservative_Party), in re-electing the government of [John Major](/wiki/John_Major), but failed to defend his marginal seat.

The fifteen [electoral wards](/wiki/Electoral_wards) of Bath are: [Bathwick](/wiki/Bathwick), [Combe Down](/wiki/Combe_Down), [Kingsmead](/wiki/Kingsmead,_Bath), Lambridge, [Lansdown](/wiki/Lansdown,_Bath), Moorlands, [Newbridge](/wiki/Newbridge,_Bath), [Odd Down](/wiki/Odd_Down), Oldfield Park, Southdown, [Twerton](/wiki/Twerton), [Walcot](/wiki/Walcot,_Bath), [Westmoreland](/wiki/Westmoreland,_Bath), [Weston](/wiki/Weston,_Bath) and [Widcombe](/wiki/Widcombe) & [Lyncombe](/wiki/Lyncombe,_Bath). The most recent [elections were held on 4 May 2023](/wiki/2023_Bath_and_North_East_Somerset_Council_election) and all wards returned [Liberal Democrats](/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_(UK)) except for Lambridge and Westmoreland which returned [Green Party](/wiki/Green_Party_of_England_and_Wales) and [independent](/wiki/Independent_politician) councillors respectively.

## Geography and environment

### Physical geography

Bath is in the Avon Valley and is surrounded by limestone hills as it is near the southern edge of the [Cotswolds](/wiki/Cotswolds), a designated [Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty](/wiki/Area_of_Outstanding_Natural_Beauty), and the [Mendip Hills](/wiki/Mendip_Hills) rise around 7 miles (11 km) south of the city. The hills that surround and make up the city have a maximum altitude of 781 feet (238 metres) on the Lansdown plateau. Bath has an area of 11 square miles (28 square kilometres).

Cleveland House and the cast iron bridges of [Sydney Gardens](/wiki/Sydney_Gardens) over the [Kennet and Avon Canal](/wiki/Kennet_and_Avon_Canal)

The [floodplain](/wiki/Floodplain) of the Avon has an altitude of about 59 ft (18 m) above sea level, although the city centre is at an elevation of around 25 metres (82 ft) above sea level. The river, once an unnavigable series of [braided streams](/wiki/Braided_streams) broken up by [swamps](/wiki/Swamp) and ponds, has been controlled by [weirs](/wiki/Weir) into a single channel. Periodic flooding, which shortened the life of many buildings in the lowest part of the city, was normal until major flood control works were completed in the 1970s. Kensington Meadows is an area of mixed woodland and open meadow next to the river which has been designated as a [local nature reserve](/wiki/Local_nature_reserve).

Water bubbling up from the ground as [geothermal springs](/wiki/List_of_geothermal_springs_in_the_United_Kingdom) originates as rain on the [Mendip Hills](/wiki/Mendip_Hills). The rain percolates through limestone aquifers to a depth of between 9,000 to 14,000 ft (2,700 to 4,300 m) where geothermal energy raises the water's temperature to between 64 and 96 °C (approximately 147–205 °F). Under pressure, the heated water rises to the surface along fissures and faults in the limestone. Hot water at a temperature of 46 °C (115 °F) rises here at the rate of 1,170,000 litres (257,364 imp gal) daily, from the Pennyquick [geological fault](/wiki/Geological_fault).

In 1983, a new spa-water bore-hole was sunk, providing a clean and safe supply for drinking in the Pump Room. There is no universal definition to distinguish a [hot spring](/wiki/Hot_spring) from a [geothermal](/wiki/Geothermal_gradient) spring, although, by several [definitions](/wiki/Hot_spring#Definitions), the Bath springs can be considered the only hot springs in the UK. Three of the springs feed the thermal baths.

### Climate

See also: [Climate of south-west England](/wiki/Climate_of_south-west_England)

Along with the rest of [South West England](/wiki/South_West_England), Bath has a [temperate climate](/wiki/Temperate_climate) which is generally wetter and milder than the rest of the country. The annual mean temperature is approximately 11 °C (51.8 °F). Seasonal temperature variation is less extreme than most of the United Kingdom because of the adjacent sea temperatures. The summer months of July and August are the warmest, with mean daily maxima of approximately 22 °C (71.6 °F). In winter, mean minimum temperatures of 2 or 3 °C (35.6 or 37.4 °F) are common. In the summer, the [Azores](/wiki/Azores) high pressure affects the south-west of England bringing fair weather; however, [convective](/wiki/Convective) cloud sometimes forms inland, reducing the number of hours of sunshine. Annual sunshine rates are slightly less than the regional average of 1,600 hours.

Most of the rainfall in the south-west is caused by [Atlantic depressions](/wiki/Low-pressure_area) or by [convection](/wiki/Convection). In summer, a large proportion of the rainfall is caused by sun heating the ground, leading to convection and to showers and thunderstorms. Average rainfall is around 830 mm (33 in). About 8–15 days of snowfall is typical. November to March have the highest mean wind speeds, and June to August have the lightest winds. The predominant wind direction is from the southwest.

### Green belt

Further information: [Avon Green Belt](/wiki/Avon_Green_Belt)

Bath is fully enclosed by [green belt](/wiki/Green_belt_(United_Kingdom)) as a part of a wider environmental and planning policy first designated in the late 1950s, and this extends into much of the surrounding district and beyond, helping to maintain local green space, prevent further [urban sprawl](/wiki/Urban_sprawl) and unplanned expansion towards Bristol and [Bradford-on-Avon](/wiki/Bradford-on-Avon), as well as protecting smaller villages in between. Suburbs of the city bordering the green belt include [Batheaston](/wiki/Batheaston), [Bathford](/wiki/Bathford), [Bathampton](/wiki/Bathampton), the University of Bath campus, Ensleigh, [Twerton](/wiki/Twerton), [Upper Weston](/wiki/Upper_Weston), [Odd Down](/wiki/Odd_Down), and [Combe Down](/wiki/Combe_Down).

Parts of the Cotswolds [AONB](/wiki/AONB) southern extent overlap the green belt north of the city, with other nearby landscape features and facilities within the green belt including the River Avon, Kennet and Avon Canal, [Bath Racecourse](/wiki/Bath_Racecourse), Bath Golf Club, [Bathampton Down](/wiki/Bathampton_Down), Bathampton Meadow Nature Reserve, Bristol and Bath Railway Path, the [Cotswold Way](/wiki/Cotswold_Way), [Limestone Link](/wiki/Limestone_Link) route, Pennyquick Park, [Little Solsbury Hill](/wiki/Little_Solsbury_Hill), and Primrose Hill.

## Economy

### Industry

[Water crane](/wiki/Water_crane). *Stothert & Pitt Engineers*, 1875. [Santiago Railway Museum](/wiki/Santiago_Railway_Museum)

Bath once had an important manufacturing sector, particularly in crane manufacture, furniture manufacture, printing, brass foundries, quarries, dye works and [Plasticine](/wiki/Plasticine) manufacture, as well as many mills. Significant Bath companies included [Stothert & Pitt](/wiki/Stothert_%26_Pitt), [Bath Cabinet Makers](/wiki/Bath_Cabinet_Makers) and [Bath & Portland Stone](/wiki/Portland_stone#History_of_use).

During and after World War II Bath was home to [Ministry of Defence](/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_(United_Kingdom)) offices, with three large sites on the outskirts (Ensleigh, Foxhill and Warminster Road) and a number of smaller central offices, including the [Empire Hotel](/wiki/Empire_Hotel,_Bath). After the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) staff numbers declined, and from 2010 to 2013 about 2,600 remaining staff were moved to [MoD Abbey Wood](/wiki/MoD_Abbey_Wood) in Bristol. In 2013 the three large sites were sold for the development of over 1,000 new houses.

Nowadays, manufacturing is in decline, but the city boasts strong software, publishing and service-oriented industries, and the international manufacturing company [Rotork](/wiki/Rotork) has its headquarters in the city. The city's attraction to tourists has led to a significant number of jobs in tourism-related industries. Important economic sectors in Bath include education and health (30,000 jobs in 2011), retail, tourism and leisure (14,000) and business and professional services (10,000).

Major employers are the [National Health Service](/wiki/National_Health_Service), [Bath Spa University](/wiki/Bath_Spa_University), the [University of Bath](/wiki/University_of_Bath), and Bath and North East Somerset Council. Significant employment sectors include information and communication technologies and creative and cultural industries where Bath is one of the recognised national centres for publishing, with the magazine and digital publisher [Future plc](/wiki/Future_plc) employing around 650. Others include [Buro Happold](/wiki/Buro_Happold) (400) and IPL Information Processing Limited (250). In 2011 the city had over 400 retail shops, half of them run by independent specialist retailers, and around 100 restaurants and cafes primarily supported by tourism.

### Tourism

Bath is popular with tourists all year round. An entertainer is performing in front of [Bath Abbey](/wiki/Bath_Abbey); the [Roman Baths](/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)) are to the right.

One of Bath's principal industries is tourism; in 2011 there were annually more than one million staying visitors and 3.8 million day visitors. The visits mainly fall into the categories of [heritage tourism](/wiki/Heritage_tourism) and [cultural tourism](/wiki/Cultural_tourism), aided by the city's selection in 1987 as a [World Heritage Site](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site) in recognition of its international cultural importance. All significant stages of the [history of England](/wiki/History_of_England) are represented within the city, from the [Roman Baths](/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)) (with a significant [Celtic](/wiki/Celts) presence), to [Bath Abbey](/wiki/Bath_Abbey) and the [Royal Crescent](/wiki/Royal_Crescent), and the more recent [Thermae Bath Spa](/wiki/Thermae_Bath_Spa).

The size of the tourist industry is reflected in the almost 300 places of accommodation as of 2009 – including more than 80 hotels, two of which had 'five-star' ratings, over 180 [bed and breakfasts](/wiki/Bed_and_breakfast) – many of which are in [Georgian buildings](/wiki/Georgian_architecture) – and two campsites on the western edge of the city. The city also has about 100 restaurants and a similar number of [pubs](/wiki/Pub) and bars.

Several companies offer [open top bus](/wiki/Open_top_bus) tours around the city, as well as tours on foot and on the river. Since the opening of Thermae Bath Spa in 2006, the city has attempted to recapture its historical position as the only town or city in the United Kingdom offering visitors the opportunity to bathe in naturally heated spring waters.

In the 2010 [Google Street View](/wiki/Google_Street_View) Best Streets Awards, the Royal Crescent took second place in the "Britain's Most Picturesque Street" award, first place being given to [The Shambles](/wiki/The_Shambles) in [York](/wiki/York). [Milsom Street](/wiki/Milsom_Street) was also awarded "Britain's Best Fashion Street" in the 11,000-strong vote.

## Architecture

Main article: [Buildings and architecture of Bath](/wiki/Buildings_and_architecture_of_Bath)

There are many Roman [archaeological](/wiki/Archaeological) sites throughout the central area of the city. The [baths](/wiki/Roman_Baths_(Bath)) themselves are about 6 metres (20 ft) below the present city street level. Around the hot springs, Roman foundations, pillar bases, and baths can still be seen; however, all the [stonework](/wiki/Stonework) above the level of the baths is from more recent periods.

Bath Abbey was a [Norman](/wiki/Norman_architecture) church built on earlier foundations. The present building dates from the early 16th century and shows a [late Perpendicular](/wiki/Perpendicular_Gothic) style with [flying buttresses](/wiki/Flying_buttress) and [crocketed](/wiki/Crocket) [pinnacles](/wiki/Pinnacle) decorating a [crenellated](/wiki/Crenellated) and pierced [parapet](/wiki/Parapet). The choir and transepts have a [fan vault](/wiki/Fan_vault) by [Robert](/wiki/Robert_Vertue) and [William Vertue](/wiki/William_Vertue). A matching vault was added to the nave in the 19th century. The building is lit by 52 windows.

[Bath Abbey](/wiki/Bath_Abbey) seen from the east

Most buildings in Bath are made from the local, golden-coloured Bath stone, and many date from the 18th and 19th century. The dominant style of architecture in Central Bath is Georgian; this style evolved from the [Palladian](/wiki/Palladian) revival style that became popular in the early 18th century. Many of the prominent architects of the day were employed in the development of the city. The original purpose of much of Bath's architecture is concealed by the honey-coloured classical façades; in an era before the advent of the luxury hotel, these apparently elegant residences were frequently purpose-built lodging houses, where visitors could hire a room, a floor, or (according to their means) an entire house for the duration of their visit, and be waited on by the house's communal [servants](/wiki/Servants). The masons [Reeves of Bath](/wiki/Reeves_of_Bath) were prominent in the city from the 1770s to 1860s.

The Circus consists of three long, curved terraces designed by the elder John Wood to form a circular space or theatre intended for civic functions and games. The games give a clue to the design, the inspiration behind which was the [Colosseum](/wiki/Colosseum) in Rome. Like the Colosseum, the three façades have a different order of architecture on each floor: [Doric](/wiki/Doric_order) on the ground level, then [Ionic](/wiki/Ionic_order) on the [piano nobile](/wiki/Piano_nobile), and finishing with [Corinthian](/wiki/Corinthian_order) on the upper floor, the style of the building thus becoming progressively more ornate as it rises. Wood never lived to see his unique example of town planning completed as he died five days after personally laying the foundation stone on 18 May 1754.

[Fan vaulting](/wiki/Fan_vault) over the [nave](/wiki/Nave) at Bath Abbey

The most spectacular of Bath's terraces is the Royal Crescent, built between 1767 and 1774 and designed by the younger John Wood. Wood designed the great curved façade of what appears to be about 30 houses with Ionic [columns](/wiki/Column) on a rusticated ground floor, but that was the extent of his input: each purchaser bought a certain length of the façade, and then employed their own architect to build a house to their own specifications behind it; hence what appears to be two houses is in some cases just one. This system of town planning is betrayed at the rear of the crescent: while the front is completely uniform and symmetrical, the rear is a mixture of differing roof heights, juxtapositions and fenestration. The "Queen Anne fronts and Mary-Anne backs" architecture occurs repeatedly in Bath and was designed to keep hired women at the back of the house. Other fine terraces elsewhere in the city include Lansdown Crescent and [Somerset Place](/wiki/Somerset_Place,_Bath) on the northern hill.

Around 1770 the [neoclassical](/wiki/Neoclassicism) architect [Robert Adam](/wiki/Robert_Adam) designed [Pulteney Bridge](/wiki/Pulteney_Bridge), using as the prototype for the three-arched bridge spanning the Avon an original, but unused, design by [Andrea Palladio](/wiki/Andrea_Palladio) for the [Rialto Bridge](/wiki/Rialto_Bridge) in Venice. Thus, Pulteney Bridge became not just a means of crossing the river, but also a shopping arcade. Along with the Rialto Bridge and the [Ponte Vecchio](/wiki/Ponte_Vecchio) in [Florence](/wiki/Florence), which it resembles, it is one of the very few surviving bridges in Europe to serve this dual purpose. It has been substantially altered since it was built. The bridge was named after Frances and [William Pulteney](/wiki/Sir_William_Pulteney,_5th_Baronet), the owners of the Bathwick estate for which the bridge provided a link to the rest of Bath.
The Georgian streets in the vicinity of the river tended to be built high above the original ground level to avoid flooding, with the carriageways supported on vaults extending in front of the houses. This can be seen in the multi-storey cellars around Laura Place south of Pulteney Bridge, in the colonnades below Grand Parade, and in the grated coal holes in the pavement of North Parade. In some parts of the city, such as George Street, and London Road near Cleveland Bridge, the developers of the opposite side of the road did not match this pattern, leaving raised pavements with the ends of the vaults exposed to a lower street below.

The heart of the Georgian city was the Pump Room, which, together with its associated Lower Assembly Rooms, was designed by [Thomas Baldwin](/wiki/Thomas_Baldwin_(architect)), a local builder responsible for many other buildings in the city, including the terraces in Argyle Street and the [Guildhall](/wiki/Guildhall,_Bath). Baldwin rose rapidly, becoming a leader in Bath's architectural history.

In 1776, he was made the chief [City Surveyor](/wiki/Bath_City_Surveyor), and [Bath City Architect](/wiki/Bath_City_Architect). [Great Pulteney Street](/wiki/Great_Pulteney_Street), where he eventually lived, is another of his works: this wide [boulevard](/wiki/Boulevard), constructed around 1789 and over 1,000 feet (305 m) long and 100 feet (30 m) wide, is lined on both sides by Georgian terraces.

In the 1960s and early 1970s some parts of Bath were unsympathetically redeveloped, resulting in the loss of some 18th- and 19th-century buildings. This process was largely halted by a popular campaign which drew strength from the publication of Adam Fergusson's *The Sack of Bath*. Controversy has revived periodically, most recently with the demolition of the 1930s Churchill House, a neo-Georgian municipal building originally housing the Electricity Board, to make way for a new [bus station](/wiki/Bath_bus_station). This is part of the Southgate redevelopment in which an ill-favoured 1960s shopping precinct, bus station and multi-storey car park were demolished and replaced by a new area of [neo-Georgian](/wiki/Mock-Georgian) shopping streets.

As a result of this and other changes, notably plans for abandoned industrial land along the Avon, the city's status as a World Heritage Site was reviewed by UNESCO in 2009. The decision was made to let Bath keep its status, but UNESCO asked to be consulted on future phases of the Riverside development, saying that the density and volume of buildings in the second and third phases of the development need to be reconsidered. It also demanded Bath do more to attract world-class architecture in new developments.

In 2021, Bath received its second UNESCO World Heritage inscription, becoming part of a group of 11 spa towns across seven countries that were listed by UNESCO as the "[Great Spas of Europe](/wiki/Great_Spas_of_Europe)".

## Culture

18th-century [Pulteney Bridge](/wiki/Pulteney_Bridge), designed by [Robert Adam](/wiki/Robert_Adam)

Bath became the centre of fashionable life in England during the 18th century when its Old Orchard Street Theatre and [architectural](/wiki/Architectural) developments such as Lansdown Crescent, the Royal Crescent, [The Circus](/wiki/The_Circus_(Bath)), and Pulteney Bridge were built.

Bath's five theatres – [Theatre Royal](/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Bath), [Ustinov Studio](/wiki/Ustinov_Studio), [the Egg](/wiki/The_egg,_Bath), the [Rondo Theatre](/wiki/Rondo_Theatre), and the [Mission Theatre](/wiki/Mission_Theatre) – attract internationally renowned companies and directors and an annual season by [Sir Peter Hall](/wiki/Peter_Hall_(director)). The city has a long-standing musical tradition; Bath Abbey, home to the [Klais Organ](/wiki/Klais_Orgelbau) and the largest concert venue in the city, stages about 20 concerts and 26 organ recitals each year. Another concert venue, the 1,600-seat [art deco](/wiki/Art_deco) [The Forum](/wiki/The_Forum,_Bath), originated as a cinema. The city holds the annual [Bath International Music Festival](/wiki/Bath_International_Music_Festival) and Mozartfest, the annual [Bath Literature Festival](/wiki/Bath_Literature_Festival) (and its [counterpart for children](/wiki/Bath_Festival_of_Children%27s_Literature)), the [Bath Film Festival](/wiki/Bath_Film_Festival), the Bath Digital Festival. the [Bath Fringe Festival](/wiki/Bath_Fringe_Festival), the [Bath Beer Festival](/wiki/Bath_Beer_Festival) and the [Bath Chilli Festival](/wiki/Bath_Chilli_Festival). The Bach Festivals occur at two and a half-year intervals. An annual [Bard of Bath](/wiki/Bard_of_Bath) competition aims to find the best poet, singer or storyteller.

The city is home to the [Victoria Art Gallery](/wiki/Victoria_Art_Gallery), the [Museum of East Asian Art](/wiki/Museum_of_East_Asian_Art,_Bath), and [Holburne Museum](/wiki/Holburne_Museum), numerous commercial art galleries and antique shops, as well as a number of other museums, among them [Bath Postal Museum](/wiki/Bath_Postal_Museum), the [Fashion Museum](/wiki/Fashion_Museum,_Bath), the [Jane Austen Centre](/wiki/Jane_Austen_Centre), the [Herschel Museum of Astronomy](/wiki/Herschel_Museum_of_Astronomy) and the Roman Baths. The [Bath Royal Literary and Scientific Institution](/wiki/Bath_Royal_Literary_and_Scientific_Institution) (BRLSI) in Queen Square was founded in 1824 from the Society for the encouragement of Agriculture, Planting, Manufactures, Commerce and the Fine Arts founded in 1777. In September 1864, BRLSI hosted the 34th annual meeting of the [British Science Association](/wiki/British_Science_Association), which was attended by explorers [David Livingstone](/wiki/David_Livingstone), [Sir Richard Francis Burton](/wiki/Sir_Richard_Francis_Burton), and [John Hanning Speke](/wiki/John_Hanning_Speke). The history of the city is displayed at the [Museum of Bath Architecture](/wiki/Museum_of_Bath_Architecture), which is housed in a building built in 1765 as the Trinity [Presbyterian](/wiki/Presbyterian) Church. It was also known as the [Countess of Huntingdon](/wiki/Selina_Hastings,_Countess_of_Huntingdon)'s Chapel, as she lived in the attached house from 1707 to 1791.

### The arts

[Holburne Museum](/wiki/Holburne_Museum)

During the 18th century [Thomas Gainsborough](/wiki/Thomas_Gainsborough) and [Sir Thomas Lawrence](/wiki/Sir_Thomas_Lawrence) lived and worked in Bath. [John Maggs](/wiki/John_Maggs), a painter best known for coaching scenes, was born and lived in Bath with his artistic family.

[Jane Austen](/wiki/Jane_Austen) lived there from 1801 with her father, mother and sister Cassandra, and the family resided at four different addresses until 1806. Jane Austen never liked the city, and wrote to Cassandra, "It will be two years tomorrow since we left Bath for Clifton, with what happy feelings of escape." Bath has honoured her name with the Jane Austen Centre and a city walk. Austen's *[Northanger Abbey](/wiki/Northanger_Abbey)* and *[Persuasion](/wiki/Persuasion_(novel))* are set in the city and describe taking the waters, social life, and music recitals.

[William Friese-Greene](/wiki/William_Friese-Greene) experimented with celluloid and motion pictures in his studio in the 1870s, developing some of the earliest movie camera technology. He is credited as being one of the inventors of [cinematography](/wiki/Cinematography).

Satirist and political journalist [William Hone](/wiki/William_Hone) was born in Bath in 1780.

Taking the waters is described in [Charles Dickens](/wiki/Charles_Dickens)' novel *[The Pickwick Papers](/wiki/The_Pickwick_Papers)* in which Pickwick's servant, [Sam Weller](/wiki/Sam_Weller_(character)), comments that the water has "a very strong flavour o' warm flat irons". The Royal Crescent is the venue for a chase between two characters, Dowler and Winkle. [Moyra Caldecott](/wiki/Moyra_Caldecott)'s novel *The Waters of Sul* is set in Roman Bath in AD 72, and *The Regency Detective*, by [David Lassman](/wiki/David_Lassman) and [Terence James](/wiki/Terence_James), revolves around the exploits of Jack Swann investigating deaths in the city during the early 19th century. [Richard Brinsley Sheridan](/wiki/Richard_Brinsley_Sheridan)'s play *[The Rivals](/wiki/The_Rivals)* takes place in the city, as does [Roald Dahl](/wiki/Roald_Dahl)'s chilling short story, [*The Landlady*](/wiki/The_Landlady_(short_story)).

Many films and television programmes have been filmed using its architecture as the backdrop, including the 2004 [film](/wiki/Vanity_Fair_(2004_film)) of [Thackeray](/wiki/William_Makepeace_Thackeray)'s *[Vanity Fair](/wiki/Vanity_Fair_(novel))*, *[The Duchess](/wiki/The_Duchess_(film))* (2008), *[The Elusive Pimpernel](/wiki/The_Elusive_Pimpernel_(1950_film))* (1950) and *[The Titfield Thunderbolt](/wiki/The_Titfield_Thunderbolt)* (1953). In 2012, Pulteney Weir was used as a replacement location during post production of the film adaptation of [*Les Misérables*](/wiki/Les_Mis%C3%A9rables_(2012_film)). Stunt shots were filmed in October 2012 after footage acquired during the main filming period was found to have errors. The ITV police drama [McDonald & Dodds](/wiki/McDonald_%26_Dodds) is set and mostly filmed in Bath using many of the city's famous sites.

In August 2003 [The Three Tenors](/wiki/The_Three_Tenors) sang at a concert to mark the opening of the Thermae Bath Spa, a new hot water [spa](/wiki/Thermae) in the city centre, but delays to the project meant the spa actually opened three years later on 7 August 2006. In 2008, 104 decorated pigs were displayed around the city in a public art event called "King Bladud's Pigs in Bath". It celebrated the city, its origins and artists. Decorated pig sculptures were displayed throughout the summer and were auctioned to raise funds for [Two Tunnels Greenway](/wiki/Two_Tunnels_Greenway).

### Parks

Parade Gardens and the [Empire Hotel](/wiki/Empire_Hotel,_Bath)

[Royal Victoria Park](/wiki/Royal_Victoria_Park,_Bath), a short walk from the city centre, was opened in 1830 by the 11-year-old [Princess Victoria](/wiki/Queen_Victoria), and was the first park to carry her name. The [public park](/wiki/Public_park) is overlooked by the Royal Crescent and covers 23 hectares (57 acres). It has a [skatepark](/wiki/Skatepark), tennis courts, a [bowling green](/wiki/Bowling_green), a putting green and a 12- and 18-hole golf course, a pond, open-air concerts, an annual [travelling funfair](/wiki/Travelling_funfair) at Easter, and a children's play area. Much of its area is [lawn](/wiki/Lawn); a notable feature is a [ha-ha](/wiki/Ha-ha) that segregates it from the Royal Crescent while giving the impression from the Crescent of uninterrupted grassland across the park to Royal Avenue. It has a "[Green Flag Award](/wiki/Green_Flag_Award)", the national standard for parks and green spaces in England and Wales, and is registered by [English Heritage](/wiki/English_Heritage) as of [National Historic Importance](/wiki/Register_of_Historic_Parks_and_Gardens_of_special_historic_interest_in_England). The 3.84-hectare (9.5-acre) botanical gardens were formed in 1887 and contain one of the finest collections of plants on limestone in the [West Country](/wiki/West_Country).

A replica Roman Temple was built at the [British Empire Exhibition](/wiki/British_Empire_Exhibition) at [Wembley](/wiki/Wembley) in 1924, and, following the exhibition, was dismantled and rebuilt in Victoria Park in Bath. In 1987, the gardens were extended to include the Great Dell, a disused quarry with a collection of [conifers](/wiki/Conifers).

Other parks include Alexandra Park on a hill overlooking the city; [Parade Gardens](/wiki/Parade_Gardens), along the river near the abbey in the city centre; [Sydney Gardens](/wiki/Sydney_Gardens), an 18th-century pleasure garden; Henrietta Park; Hedgemead Park; and Alice Park. [Jane Austen](/wiki/Jane_Austen) wrote "It would be pleasant to be near the Sydney Gardens. We could go into the Labyrinth every day." Alexandra, Alice and Henrietta parks were built into the growing city among the housing developments. Linear Park is built on the old [Somerset and Dorset Joint Railway](/wiki/Somerset_and_Dorset_Joint_Railway) line, and connects with the [Two Tunnels Greenway](/wiki/Two_Tunnels_Greenway) which contains the longest cycling and walking tunnel in the UK. [Cleveland Pools](/wiki/Cleveland_Pools) were built around 1815 close to the River Avon, now the oldest surviving public outdoor [lido](/wiki/Lido_(swimming_pool)) in England. Restoration was completed in 2023, after a 20-year fund-raising campaign, with the lido opening for the first time in 40 years on 10 September.

### Queen Victoria

Victoria Art Gallery and Royal Victoria Park are named after [Queen Victoria](/wiki/Queen_Victoria), who wrote in her journal in 1837, "The people are really too kind to me." This feeling seemed to have been reciprocated by the people of Bath: "Lord James O'Brien brought a drawing of the intended pillar which the people of Bath are so kind as to erect in commemoration of my 18th birthday."

### Food

Sally Lunn's, home of the [Sally Lunn bun](/wiki/Sally_Lunn_bun)

Several foods have an association with the city. *[Sally Lunn buns](/wiki/Sally_Lunn_bun)* (a type of [teacake](/wiki/Teacake)) have long been baked in Bath. They were first mentioned by name in verses printed in the [Bath Chronicle](/wiki/Bath_Chronicle), in 1772. At that time they were eaten hot at public breakfasts in Spring Gardens. They can be eaten with sweet or savoury toppings and are sometimes confused with *[Bath buns](/wiki/Bath_bun)*, which are smaller, round, very sweet and very rich. They were associated with the city following [The Great Exhibition](/wiki/The_Great_Exhibition). Bath buns were originally topped with crushed [comfits](/wiki/Comfit) created by dipping [caraway](/wiki/Caraway) seeds repeatedly in boiling sugar; but today seeds are added to a 'London Bath Bun' (a reference to the bun's promotion and sale at the Great Exhibition). The seeds may be replaced by crushed sugar granules or 'nibs'.

Bath has lent its name to one other distinctive recipe – *[Bath Olivers](/wiki/Bath_Oliver)* – a dry baked biscuit invented by Dr William Oliver, physician to the [Mineral Water Hospital](/wiki/Mineral_Water_Hospital) in 1740. Oliver was an anti-obesity campaigner and author of a *"Practical Essay on the Use and Abuse of warm Bathing in Gluty Cases"*. In more recent years, Oliver's efforts have been traduced by the introduction of a version of the biscuit with a plain chocolate coating. [Bath chaps](/wiki/Bath_chaps), the salted and smoked cheek and jawbones of the pig, takes its name from the city and is available from a stall in the daily covered market. [Bath Ales](/wiki/Bath_Ales) brewery is located in [Warmley](/wiki/Warmley) and [Abbey Ales](/wiki/Abbey_Ales) are brewed in the city.

### Twinning

[City twinning](/wiki/Sister_city) is the responsibility of the Charter Trustees and each twinning arrangement is managed by a Twinning Association. Bath is twinned with four other cities in Europe:

- [Aix-en-Provence](/wiki/Aix-en-Provence), France
- [Alkmaar](/wiki/Alkmaar), Netherlands
- [Braunschweig](/wiki/Braunschweig), Germany
- [Kaposvár](/wiki/Kaposv%C3%A1r), Hungary

There is also a historic connection with [Manly, New South Wales](/wiki/Manly,_New_South_Wales), Australia, which is referred to as a sister city; a partnership arrangement with [Beppu](/wiki/Beppu), [Ōita Prefecture](/wiki/%C5%8Cita_Prefecture), Japan; and a friendship agreement with [Oleksandriia](/wiki/Oleksandriia), [Kirovohrad Oblast](/wiki/Kirovohrad_Oblast), Ukraine.

## Education

Main article: [Education in Bath, Somerset](/wiki/Education_in_Bath,_Somerset)

[University of Bath](/wiki/University_of_Bath)

Bath has two universities, the [University of Bath](/wiki/University_of_Bath) and [Bath Spa University](/wiki/Bath_Spa_University). Established in 1966, the University of Bath was named University of the Year by *[The Sunday Times](/wiki/The_Sunday_Times)* in 2011. It offers programs in politics, languages, the physical sciences, engineering, mathematics, architecture, management and technology.

Bath Spa University was first granted degree-awarding powers in 1992 as a [university college](/wiki/University_college) before being granted university status in August 2005. It offers courses leading to a [Postgraduate Certificate in Education](/wiki/Postgraduate_Certificate_in_Education). It has schools in the following subject areas: Art and Design, Education, English and Creative Studies, Historical and Cultural Studies, Music and the Performing Arts, Science and the Environment and Social Sciences.

[Bath College](/wiki/Bath_College) offers [further education](/wiki/Further_education), and [Norland College](/wiki/Norland_College) provides education and training in childcare.

## Sport

Main articles: [Bath Rugby](/wiki/Bath_Rugby) and [Bath City F.C.](/wiki/Bath_City_F.C.)

### Rugby

The [Recreation Ground](/wiki/Recreation_Ground_(Bath))

[Bath Rugby](/wiki/Bath_Rugby) is a [rugby union](/wiki/Rugby_union) team who play in the [Premiership](/wiki/English_Premiership_(rugby_union)), England's top division of rugby. It plays in blue, white and black kit at the [Recreation Ground](/wiki/Recreation_Ground_(Bath)) in the city, where it has been since the late 19th century, following its establishment in 1865. Bath Rugby is the joint-most successful club in England, having won 21 major trophies. It was particularly successful between 1984 and 1998, when it won 10 [Domestic Cups](/wiki/Premiership_Rugby_Cup), 6 of its 7 [League titles](/wiki/Premiership_Rugby), and became the first English side to win the [European Cup](/wiki/European_Rugby_Champions_Cup) in [1998](/wiki/1998_Heineken_Cup_final). In [2008](/wiki/2007%E2%80%9308_European_Challenge_Cup) and [2025](/wiki/2024%E2%80%9325_EPCR_Challenge_Cup), Bath also won the [European Challenge Cup](/wiki/EPCR_Challenge_Cup), the continent's second-tier competition.

The team's squad includes several members who also play, or have played in the [English national team](/wiki/England_national_rugby_union_team), including [Tom Dunn](/wiki/Tom_Dunn_(rugby_union)), [Beno Obano](/wiki/Beno_Obano), [Will Stuart](/wiki/Will_Stuart), [Charlie Ewels](/wiki/Charlie_Ewels), [Ted Hill](/wiki/Ted_Hill_(rugby_union)), [Guy Pepper](/wiki/Guy_Pepper), [Sam Underhill](/wiki/Sam_Underhill), [Ben Spencer](/wiki/Ben_Spencer_(rugby_union)), [Ollie Lawrence](/wiki/Ollie_Lawrence), [Max Ojomoh](/wiki/Max_Ojomoh), [Henry Arundell](/wiki/Henry_Arundell), [Joe Cokanasiga](/wiki/Joe_Cokanasiga) and [Will Muir](/wiki/Will_Muir). The former England Rugby Team Manager and former [Scotland national](/wiki/Scotland_national_rugby_union_team) coach [Andy Robinson](/wiki/Andy_Robinson) used to play for Bath Rugby team and was captain and later coach. Both of Robinson's predecessors, [Clive Woodward](/wiki/Clive_Woodward) and [Jack Rowell](/wiki/Jack_Rowell), as well as his successor [Brian Ashton](/wiki/Brian_Ashton_(rugby_union)), were also former Bath coaches and managers.

Bath was described by former head coach Jack Rowell as “a Georgian city, a Roman city, but more so than that, it’s a rugby city”. Bath Rugby routinely sell out matches at the 14,509-capacity Recreation Ground, and in 2024 the club submitted updated plans to redevelop it into a modern, 18,000-capacity stadium. In June 2025, Bath won their first Premiership Rugby title in 29 years, completing the third leg of a historic treble, having already secured the Premiership Rugby Cup and the European Rugby Challenge Cup earlier that season. The following day, thousands of supporters lined the streets for a victory parade. Players travelled on two open-top buses across [Pulteney Bridge](/wiki/Pulteney_Bridge) and up [Milsom Street](/wiki/Milsom_Street), before heading towards [Bath Abbey](/wiki/Bath_Abbey) and ending at The Recreation Ground, where a ticketed party was held.

### Football

[Twerton Park](/wiki/Twerton_Park)

[Bath City F.C.](/wiki/Bath_City_F.C.) is the semi-professional [football](/wiki/Association_football) team. Founded in 1889, the club has played their home matches at [Twerton Park](/wiki/Twerton_Park) since 1932. Bath City's history is entirely in non-league football, predominantly in the 5th tier. Bath narrowly missed out on election to the Football League by a few votes in 1978 and again in 1985. The club have a good history in the FA Cup, reaching the third round six times. The record attendance, 18,020, at the ground was in 1960 against Brighton. The club's colours are black and white and their official nickname is "The Romans", stemming from Bath's Ancient Roman history. The club is sometimes called "The Stripes", referring to their striped kit.

Until 2009 [Team Bath F.C.](/wiki/Team_Bath_F.C.) operated as an affiliate to the University Athletics programme. In 2002, Team Bath became the first university team to enter the [FA Cup](/wiki/FA_Cup) in 120 years, and advanced through four qualifying rounds to the first round proper. The university's team was established in 1999 while the city team has existed since before 1908 (when it entered the [Western League](/wiki/Western_Football_League)). However, in 2009, the [Football Conference](/wiki/Football_Conference) ruled that Team Bath would not be eligible to gain promotion to a National division, nor were they allowed to participate in [Football Association](/wiki/Football_Association) cup competitions. This ruling led to the decision by the club to fold at the end of the 2008–09 Conference South competition. In their final season, Team Bath F.C. finished 11th in the league.

Bath also has [Non-League football](/wiki/Non-League_football) clubs [Odd Down F.C.](/wiki/Odd_Down_F.C.) who play at the Lew Hill Memorial Ground and [Larkhall Athletic F.C.](/wiki/Larkhall_Athletic_F.C.) who play at Plain Ham.

### Other sports

Many [cricket](/wiki/Cricket) clubs are based in the city, including [Bath Cricket Club](/wiki/Bath_Cricket_Club), who are based at the North Parade Ground and play in the [West of England Premier League](/wiki/West_of_England_Premier_League). Cricket is also played on the Recreation Ground, just across from the rugby club. The Recreation Ground is also home to Bath Croquet Club, which was re-formed in 1976 and is affiliated with the South West Federation of [Croquet](/wiki/Croquet) Clubs.

The [Bath Half Marathon](/wiki/Bath_Half_Marathon) is run annually through the city streets, with over 10,000 runners.

[TeamBath](/wiki/TeamBath) is the umbrella name for all of the University of Bath sports teams, including the aforementioned football club. Other sports for which TeamBath is noted are [athletics](/wiki/Track_and_field), badminton, basketball, [bob skeleton](/wiki/Bob_skeleton), [bobsleigh](/wiki/Bobsleigh), [hockey](/wiki/Field_hockey), judo, [modern pentathlon](/wiki/Modern_pentathlon), [netball](/wiki/Netball), rugby union, swimming, tennis, [triathlon](/wiki/Triathlon) and volleyball. The City of Bath Triathlon takes place annually at the university.

[Bath Roller Derby Girls (BRDG)](/wiki/Bath_Roller_Derby_Girls) is a flat track [roller derby](/wiki/Roller_derby) club, founded in 2012, they compete in the British Roller Derby Championships Tier 3. As of 2015, they are full members of the United Kingdom Roller Derby Association (UKRDA.)

Bath is home to a table tennis League, made up of 3 divisions and a number of clubs based in Bath and the surrounding area.

## Transport

### Railways

Bath Spa station

The city is served by [Bath Spa railway station](/wiki/Bath_Spa_railway_station), designed by [Isambard Kingdom Brunel](/wiki/Isambard_Kingdom_Brunel), which is on the [Great Western Main Line](/wiki/Great_Western_Main_Line). Services are provided by [Great Western Railway](/wiki/Great_Western_Railway_(train_operating_company)) on the following routes:

- [London Paddington](/wiki/London_Paddington_railway_station) to [Bristol Temple Meads](/wiki/Bristol_Temple_Meads_railway_station), [Weston-super-Mare](/wiki/Weston-super-Mare_railway_station) and [Taunton](/wiki/Taunton_railway_station)
- Bristol Temple Meads to [Salisbury](/wiki/Salisbury_railway_station)
- Bristol Temple Meads to [Frome](/wiki/Frome_railway_station)
- [Portsmouth Harbour](/wiki/Portsmouth_Harbour_railway_station) to [Cardiff Central](/wiki/Cardiff_Central_railway_station)
- [Gloucester](/wiki/Gloucester_railway_station) to [Westbury](/wiki/Westbury_railway_station).

There is a suburban station on the main line, [Oldfield Park](/wiki/Oldfield_Park_railway_station), which has a limited commuter service to Bristol.

[Bath Green Park station](/wiki/Bath_Green_Park_railway_station) was once the terminus of the [Midland Railway](/wiki/Midland_Railway), and junction for the [Somerset and Dorset Joint Railway](/wiki/Somerset_and_Dorset_Joint_Railway), whose line, always steam hauled, went through the Devonshire tunnel (under the Wellsway, St Luke's Church and the Devonshire Arms), through the [Combe Down Tunnel](/wiki/Combe_Down_Tunnel) and climbed over the [Mendips](/wiki/Mendips) to serve many towns and villages on its 71-mile (114 km) run to [Bournemouth](/wiki/Bournemouth). This example of an English rural line was closed as part of the [Beeching cuts](/wiki/Beeching_cuts) in March 1966. Its Bath station building, now restored, houses shops, small businesses, a Saturday farmers' market and parking for a supermarket, while the route of the Somerset and Dorset through the suburbs to [Midford](/wiki/Midford) has been reused for the [Two Tunnels Greenway](/wiki/Two_Tunnels_Greenway), a shared use path that extends [National Cycle Route 24](/wiki/National_Cycle_Route_24) into the city.

### Buses

A [diesel/electric hybrid bus](/wiki/Hybrid_electric_bus) in SouthGate on a Park and Ride service in 2010

Bath has a network of bus routes, operated by [First West of England](/wiki/First_West_of_England), with services to surrounding towns and cities, such as [Bristol](/wiki/Bristol), [Trowbridge](/wiki/Trowbridge), [Frome](/wiki/Frome) and [Wells](/wiki/Wells,_Somerset).

Faresaver Buses also operate services to surrounding towns. The [Bath Bus Company](/wiki/Bath_Bus_Company) runs open-top double-decker bus tours around the city, as well as frequent services to [Bristol Airport](/wiki/Bristol_Airport). [Stagecoach West](/wiki/Stagecoach_West) also provides services to [Tetbury](/wiki/Tetbury) and the South Cotswolds. The suburbs of Bath are also served by the [WESTlink on demand service](/wiki/WESTlink_(on-demand_bus)), available Monday to Saturday.

[National Express](/wiki/National_Express_Coaches) operates inter-city [coach](/wiki/Coach_(bus)) services from [Bath bus station](/wiki/Bath_bus_station).

### Roads

Bath is approximately 11 miles (18 km) south-east of the larger city and port of Bristol, to which it is linked by the [A4 road](/wiki/A4_road_(England)) and is a similar distance south of the [M4 motorway](/wiki/M4_motorway) at junction 18. The potential new junction 18a linking the M4 with the A4174 [Avon Ring Road](/wiki/Avon_Ring_Road) could provide an additional direct route from Bath to the motorway.

In an attempt to reduce the level of car use, [park and ride](/wiki/Park_and_ride) schemes have been introduced, with sites at Odd Down, Lansdown and Newbridge. A large increase in city centre parking was provided under the 2010 [SouthGate](/wiki/SouthGate) shopping centre development, which introduced more car traffic. A [bus gate](/wiki/Bus_gate) scheme in Northgate aims to reduce private car use in the city centre.

A [transport study](/wiki/Transportation_study) (the [Bristol/Bath to South Coast Study](/wiki/Bristol/Bath_to_South_Coast_Study)) was published in 2004, after being initiated by the [Government Office for the South West](/wiki/Government_Office_for_the_South_West) and [Bath and North East Somerset Council](/wiki/Bath_and_North_East_Somerset_Council) and undertaken by [WSP Global](/wiki/WSP_Global) as a result of the [de-trunking](/wiki/Trunk_road) in 1999 of the A36/A46 trunk road network from Bath to Southampton.

The [Bath Clean Air Zone](/wiki/Bath_Clean_Air_Zone) was introduced for central Bath on 15 March 2021. A Class C zone, it charges the [most polluting commercial vehicles](/wiki/European_emission_standards) £9 per day (and up to £100 per day for coaches and [HGVs](/wiki/Large_goods_vehicle)). It is the first UK road pollution charging zone outside London, and reduced [nitrogen dioxide](/wiki/Nitrogen_dioxide) levels in the city by 26% over the following two years, meeting legal standards.

### Cycling

Bath is on [National Cycle Route 4](/wiki/National_Cycle_Route_4), with one of Britain's first [cycleways](/wiki/Cycling_infrastructure), the [Bristol and Bath Railway Path](/wiki/Bristol_and_Bath_Railway_Path), to the west, and an eastern route toward London on the canal towpath. Bath is about 20 miles (30 km) from [Bristol Airport](/wiki/Bristol_Airport). Bath also benefits from several bridleways and byways.

### Rivers and canals

The city is connected to Bristol and the sea by the River Avon, navigable via [locks](/wiki/Lock_(water_navigation)) by small boats. The river was connected to the [Thames](/wiki/River_Thames) and London by the [Kennet and Avon Canal](/wiki/Kennet_and_Avon_Canal) in 1810 via [Bath Locks](/wiki/Bath_Locks); this waterway – closed for many years but restored in the last years of the 20th century – is now popular with [narrowboat](/wiki/Narrowboat) users.

### Trams

The [Bath Tramways Company](/wiki/Bath_Tramways_Company) began operations on 24 December 1880. The 4 ft (1,219 mm) gauge cars were horse-drawn along a route from London Road to the railway station. The system closed in 1902 and was replaced by electric tramcars on a greatly expanded 4 ft 8+1⁄2 in (1,435 mm) gauge system that opened in 1904. This eventually extended to 18 miles (29 km) with routes to Combe Down, Oldfield Park, Twerton, [Newton St Loe](/wiki/Newton_St_Loe), Weston and [Bathford](/wiki/Bathford). There was a fleet of 40 cars, all but six being double deck. The first line to close was replaced by a bus service in 1938, and the last went on 6 May 1939.

In 2005, a detailed plan was presented to the council to reintroduce trams to Bath, but the plan did not proceed, reportedly due to the focus by the council on the government-supported busway planned to run from the Newbridge park and ride into the city centre. Part of the justification for the plan was pollution from vehicles in the city, which was twice the legal levels, and heavy traffic congestion due to high car usage. In 2015 another group, Bath Trams, building on the earlier tram group proposals, created interest in the idea of reintroducing trams with several public meetings and meetings with the council. In 2017, [Bath and North East Somerset Council](/wiki/Bath_and_North_East_Somerset_Council) announced a feasibility study into implementing a light rail or tram system in the city. In November 2016, the [West of England Local Enterprise Partnership](/wiki/Local_enterprise_partnership) began a consultation process on their Transport Vision Summary Document, outlining potential [light rail](/wiki/Light_rail) or tram routes in the region, one of them a route from [Bristol city centre](/wiki/Bristol_city_centre) along the [A4 road](/wiki/A4_road_(England)) to Bath to relieve pressure on bus and rail services between the two cities.

## Media

Bath's local newspaper is the *Bath Chronicle* owned by [Local World](/wiki/Local_World). Published since 1760, the *Chronicle* was a daily newspaper until mid-September 2007, when it became a weekly. Since 2018 its website has been operated by [Trinity Mirror](/wiki/Trinity_Mirror)'s *[SomersetLive](/wiki/SomersetLive)* platform.

The [BBC Bristol](/wiki/BBC_Bristol) website has featured coverage of news and events within Bath since 2003.

For television, Bath is served by the [BBC West](/wiki/BBC_West) [studios](/wiki/Broadcasting_House,_Bristol) based in Bristol, and by [ITV West Country](/wiki/ITV_West_Country), formerly HTV, also from studios in Bristol.

Radio stations broadcasting to the city include [BBC Radio Bristol](/wiki/BBC_Radio_Bristol) which has a studio in Kingsmead Square in the city centre, [BBC Radio Somerset](/wiki/BBC_Radio_Somerset) in [Taunton](/wiki/Taunton), [Greatest Hits Radio South West](/wiki/Greatest_Hits_Radio_South_West) and [Heart West](/wiki/Heart_West), formerly GWR FM, as well as The University of Bath's [University Radio Bath](/wiki/University_Radio_Bath), a student-focused radio station available on campus and also online. Bath Sound (formerly Bath Hospital Radio and BA1 Radio) is an online community station run by a charity.
