**Sarajevo** is the [capital](/wiki/Capital_city) and [largest city](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) of [Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), with a population of 275,524 in its administrative limits. The [Sarajevo Canton](/wiki/Sarajevo_Canton), containing the city of Sarajevo and nearby municipalities, is home to 413,593 inhabitants. Located within the greater Sarajevo valley of [Bosnia](/wiki/Bosnia_(region)), it is surrounded by the [Dinaric Alps](/wiki/Dinaric_Alps) and situated along the [Miljacka](/wiki/Miljacka) River in the heart of the [Balkans](/wiki/Balkans), a region of [Southeastern Europe](/wiki/Southeastern_Europe).

Sarajevo is the political, financial, social, and cultural centre of Bosnia and Herzegovina and a prominent centre of culture in the Balkans. It exerts region-wide influence in entertainment, media, fashion, and the arts. Due to its long history of religious and cultural diversity, Sarajevo is sometimes called the "[Jerusalem](/wiki/Jerusalem) of Europe" or "Jerusalem of the Balkans". It is one of a few major European cities to have a mosque, Catholic church, Eastern Orthodox church, and synagogue within the same neighborhood. It is also home to the former Yugoslavia's first institution of tertiary education in the form of an Islamic polytechnic, today part of the [University of Sarajevo](/wiki/University_of_Sarajevo).

Although there is evidence of human settlement in the area since prehistoric times, the modern city arose in the 15th century as an [Ottoman](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) stronghold when the Ottoman Empire extended into Europe. Sarajevo has gained international renown several times throughout its history. In 1914, Sarajevo was the site of the [assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand](/wiki/Assassination_of_Archduke_Franz_Ferdinand) by a local [Young Bosnia](/wiki/Young_Bosnia) activist [Gavrilo Princip](/wiki/Gavrilo_Princip), a murder that sparked [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I). This led to the end of [Austro-Hungarian](/wiki/Austria-Hungary) rule in Bosnia and the creation of the multicultural [Kingdom of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia) in the Balkans. Later, after [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), the area was designated the capital of the communist [Socialist Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) within the [Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia), leading to rapid population growth and business expansion, with investment in infrastructure and economic development.

In 1984, Sarajevo hosted the [1984 Winter Olympics](/wiki/1984_Winter_Olympics), which marked a prosperous era for the city. However, after the start of the [Yugoslav Wars](/wiki/Yugoslav_Wars), the city endured the [longest siege of a capital city in modern history](/wiki/Siege_of_Sarajevo), lasting 1,425 days from April 1992 to February 1996, during the [Bosnian War](/wiki/Bosnian_War). With continued [post-war reconstruction](/wiki/Architecture_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina#War-time_destruction_and_post-war_reconstruction) in its aftermath, Sarajevo is the [fastest-growing city](/wiki/Economy_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) in Bosnia and Herzegovina. The travel guide series *[Lonely Planet](/wiki/Lonely_Planet)* ranked Sarajevo as the 43rd best city in the world. In December 2009, it recommended Sarajevo as one of the top ten cities to visit in 2010.

In 2011, Sarajevo was nominated as the 2014 [European Capital of Culture](/wiki/European_Capital_of_Culture). It was also selected with [Istočno Sarajevo](/wiki/Isto%C4%8Dno_Sarajevo) to host the [2019 European Youth Olympic Winter Festival](/wiki/2019_European_Youth_Olympic_Winter_Festival). In addition, in October 2019, Sarajevo was designated as a [UNESCO Creative City](/wiki/Creative_Cities_Network) for having placed culture at the center of its development strategies, and is also ranked as a [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) [City of Film](/wiki/City_of_Film). In October 2024, *[National Geographic](/wiki/National_Geographic)* declared Sarajevo as the best world destination for 2025 according to its readers.

## Etymology

Sarajevo at night

The name *Sarajevo* derives from the Turkish noun *saray*, meaning "palace" or "mansion" (from [Persian](/wiki/Persian_language) *sarāy*, سرای, of the same meaning). Scholars disagree on the origin of the *evo* attached to the end. In Slavic languages, the addition of "-evo" may indicate a possessive noun, thereby making the name of Sarajevo 'city of the palace'.

One theory is that the name may have been derived from the [Ottoman Turkish](/wiki/Ottoman_Turkish) term *saray ovası*, first recorded in 1455, meaning "the plains around the palace" or simply "palace plains".

However, in his *Dictionary of Turkish Loanwords*, Abdulah Škaljić maintains that the *evo* ending is more likely to have come from the widespread [Slavic](/wiki/Slavic_languages) suffix *[evo](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Appendix:Suffixes_-evo,_-ova_and_-ovo)* used to indicate place names, than from the Turkish ending *ova*. The first mention of the name Sarajevo was in a 1507 letter written by [Firuz Bey](/wiki/Firuz_Bey). The official name during the [400 years of Ottoman rule](/wiki/Ottoman_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) was *Saraybosna* ("Palace of Bosnia"), which remains the city's name in Modern Turkish.

Sarajevo has had many nicknames. The earliest is *Šeher*, the term [Isa-Beg Ishaković](/wiki/Isa-Beg_Ishakovi%C4%87) used to describe the town he was going to construct—which is Turkish for "city" (*şehir*), in turn coming from the Persian *shahr* (شهر, meaning "city"). As Sarajevo developed, numerous nicknames came from comparisons to other cities in the Islamic world, i.e. "[Damascus](/wiki/Damascus) of the North" and "European Jerusalem"; the latter being the most popular.

## Environment

### Geography

Sarajevo is near the geometric center of the triangular-shaped [Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and within the historical region of [Bosnia](/wiki/Bosnia_(region)). It is situated 518 m (1,699 ft) [above sea level](/wiki/Height_above_sea_level) and lies in the Sarajevo valley, in the middle of the [Dinaric Alps](/wiki/Dinaric_Alps).

The valley was once an expansive, fertile, and green space, but considerable urban expansion and development took place following World War II. Forested hills and five major mountains surround the city. The highest of the surrounding peaks is [Treskavica](/wiki/Treskavica) at 2,088 m (6,850 ft), followed by [Bjelašnica](/wiki/Bjela%C5%A1nica) mountain at 2,067 m (6,781 ft), [Jahorina](/wiki/Jahorina) at 1,913 m (6,276 ft), [Trebević](/wiki/Trebevi%C4%87) at 1,627 m (5,338 ft), and [Igman](/wiki/Igman) the shortest at 1,502 m (4,928 ft). The last four are also known as the Olympic Mountains of Sarajevo.

When the city hosted the [1984 Winter Olympics](/wiki/1984_Winter_Olympics), venues were constructed at these mountains for many winter sports events. The city is developed within hilly terrain; some steeply inclined streets and residences perch on the hillsides.

The [Miljacka](/wiki/Miljacka) river is one of the city's chief geographic features. It flows through the city from east through the center of Sarajevo to the west part of the city, where it eventually meets up with the [Bosna](/wiki/Bosna_(river)) river. Miljacka River is also known as "The Sarajevo River". Its source (*Vrelo Miljacke*) is 2 km (1.2 mi) south of the town of [Pale](/wiki/Pale,_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) at the foothills of Mount Jahorina, several kilometers to the east of Sarajevo center. The Bosna's source, [Vrelo Bosne](/wiki/Vrelo_Bosne) near [Ilidža](/wiki/Ilid%C5%BEa) (west Sarajevo), is another notable natural landmark and a popular destination for Sarajevans and other tourists. Several smaller rivers and streams, such as [Koševski Potok](/wiki/Ko%C5%A1evski_Potok), also run through the city and its vicinity.

### Cityscape

View of Sarajevo from a popular [Yellow Bastion](/wiki/%C5%BDuta_Tabija) lookout

Sarajevo is close to the center of the triangular shape of Bosnia and Herzegovina in southeastern Europe. The Sarajevo city consists of four municipalities: [Centar](/wiki/Centar,_Sarajevo) (Center), [Novi Grad](/wiki/Novi_Grad,_Sarajevo) (New Town), [Novo Sarajevo](/wiki/Novo_Sarajevo) (New Sarajevo), and [Stari Grad](/wiki/Stari_Grad,_Sarajevo) (Old Town).

The Metropolitan area was reduced in the 1990s after the war and the [Dayton-imposed administrative division](/wiki/Dayton_Agreement) of the country, with several municipalities partitioned along the border of the newly recognized [Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Federation_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) (FBiH) and [Republika Srpska](/wiki/Republika_Srpska) (RS), creating several new municipalities which together form the city of [Istočno Sarajevo](/wiki/Isto%C4%8Dno_Sarajevo) in the Republika Srpska: [Istočna Ilidža](/wiki/Isto%C4%8Dna_Ilid%C5%BEa), [Istočno Novo Sarajevo](/wiki/Isto%C4%8Dno_Novo_Sarajevo), [Istočni Stari Grad](/wiki/Isto%C4%8Dni_Stari_Grad), [Lukavica](/wiki/Lukavica_(Isto%C4%8Dno_Novo_Sarajevo)), [Pale (RS-section)](/wiki/Pale,_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), and [Trnovo (RS-section)](/wiki/Trnovo,_Republika_Srpska), along with the municipality of [Sokolac](/wiki/Sokolac,_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) (which was not traditionally part of the Sarajevo area and was not partitioned).

The city has an urban area of 1,041.5 km2 (402.1 sq mi). Veliki Park (Great Park) is the largest green area in the center of Sarajevo. It is nestled between [Titova](/wiki/Marshal_Tito_street_(Sarajevo)), [Koševo](/wiki/Ko%C5%A1evo), [Džidžikovac](/wiki/D%C5%BEid%C5%BEikovac), Tina Ujevića and Trampina Streets and in the lower part there is a monument dedicated to the Children of Sarajevo.

### Climate

Autumn in Sarajevo – [Veliki Park](/wiki/Veliki_Park_(Sarajevo))

Sarajevo has an [oceanic climate](/wiki/Oceanic_climate) ([Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): **Cfb**) bordering on a [humid continental climate](/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) ([Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): **Dfb**). Sarajevo's climate exhibits four seasons and uniformly spread precipitation. The proximity of the [Adriatic Sea](/wiki/Adriatic_Sea) moderates Sarajevo's climate somewhat, although the mountains to the south of the city greatly reduce this maritime influence. The average yearly temperature is 11.1 °C (52 °F), with January (0.6 °C (33.1 °F) on average) being the coldest month of the year and August (21.3 °C (70.3 °F) on average) the warmest.

The highest recorded temperature was 40.7 °C (105 °F) on 19 August 1946 and on 23 August 2008 (41.0), while the lowest recorded temperature was −26.2 °C (−15.2 °F) on 25 January 1942. On 13 August 2024, Sarajevo recorded a temperature of 39.7 °C (103.5 °F), the highest since 1946. On average, Sarajevo has seven days where the temperature exceeds 32 °C (89.6 °F) and four days where the temperature drops below −15 °C (5 °F) per year. The city typically experiences mildly cloudy skies, with an average yearly [cloud cover](/wiki/Cloud_cover) of 45%.

The cloudiest month is December (75% average cloud cover), while the clearest is August (37%). Moderate precipitation occurs fairly consistently throughout the year, with an average 75 days of rainfall. Suitable climatic conditions have allowed [winter sports](/wiki/Winter_sports) to flourish in the region, as exemplified by the [1984 Winter Olympics](/wiki/1984_Winter_Olympics) that were held in Sarajevo. Average winds are 28–48 km/h (17–30 mph) and the city has 1,769 hours of sunshine.

### Air quality

[Air pollution](/wiki/Air_pollution) is a major issue in Sarajevo. According to the 2016 [World Health Organization](/wiki/World_Health_Organization)'s Ambient Air Pollution Database, the annual average [PM2.5](/wiki/Particulates#Size,_shape_and_solubility_matter) concentration in 2010 was estimated to be 30 μg/m3 based on PM10 measurement, which is 3 times higher than recommended by [WHO Air Quality Guidelines](/wiki/WHO_Air_Quality_Guideline) for the annual average PM2.5. There are no recent direct long-term PM2.5 measurements available in Sarajevo and only estimates can be made from PM10, which is less health-relevant than PM2.5. Real-time air quality data in the form of PM10, ozone, [NO2](/wiki/Nitrogen_dioxide), [CO](/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) and [SO2](/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide) by the [Federal Hydrometeorological Institute](http://www.fhmzbih.gov.ba/latinica/ZRAK/vrijednostiPolutanata.php) [Archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20180913190025/http://www.fhmzbih.gov.ba/latinica/ZRAK/vrijednostiPolutanata.php) 13 September 2018 at the [Wayback Machine](/wiki/Wayback_Machine).

### Ancient times

Main article: [Stari Grad, Sarajevo](/wiki/Stari_Grad,_Sarajevo)

[Neolithic](/wiki/Neolithic) period [Butmir](/wiki/Butmir_culture) vase

One of the earliest findings of settlement in the Sarajevo area is that of the Neolithic [Butmir culture](/wiki/Butmir_culture). The discoveries at [Butmir](/wiki/Butmir) were made on the grounds of the modern-day Sarajevo suburb [Ilidža](/wiki/Ilid%C5%BEa) in 1893 by [Austro-Hungarian](/wiki/Austria-Hungary) authorities during the construction of an agricultural school. The area's richness in [flint](/wiki/Flint) was attractive to Neolithic humans, and the settlement flourished. The settlement developed unique ceramics and pottery designs, which characterize the Butmir people as a unique culture, as described at the International Congress of Archaeologists and [Anthropologists](/wiki/Anthropology) meeting in Sarajevo in 1894.

The next prominent culture in Sarajevo was the [Illyrians](/wiki/Illyrians). The ancient people, who considered most of the [Western Balkans](/wiki/Balkans#Western_Balkans) as their homeland, had several key settlements in the region, mostly around the river [Miljacka](/wiki/Miljacka) and the Sarajevo valley. The Illyrians in the Sarajevo region belonged to the *[Daesitiates](/wiki/Daesitiates)*, the last Illyrian people in Bosnia and Herzegovina to resist [Roman](/wiki/Ancient_Rome) occupation. Their defeat by the [Roman](/wiki/Roman_Empire) emperor [Tiberius](/wiki/Tiberius) in 9 AD marks the start of Roman rule in the region. The Romans never built up the region of modern-day Bosnia, but the [Roman colony](/wiki/Colonies_in_antiquity) of Aquae Sulphurae was near the top of present-day [Ilidža](/wiki/Ilid%C5%BEa), and was the most important settlement of the time. After the Romans, the [Goths](/wiki/Goths) settled the area, followed by the [Slavs](/wiki/Slavs) in the 7th century.

### Middle Ages

See also: [Sarajevo during the Middle Ages](/wiki/Sarajevo_during_the_Middle_Ages)

[Roman bridge](/wiki/Roman_bridge_in_Ilid%C5%BEa), erected 1530 in [Ilidža](/wiki/Ilid%C5%BEa), built of remnants of an old Roman settlement

During the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages), Sarajevo was part of the Bosnian province of [Vrhbosna](/wiki/Vrhbosna) near the traditional center of the [Kingdom of Bosnia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Bosnia). Though a city named *Vrhbosna* existed, the exact settlement in Sarajevo at this time is [debated](/wiki/History_of_Sarajevo#Middle_Ages). Various documents note a place called *Tornik* in the region, most likely in the area of the [Marijin Dvor](/wiki/Marijin_Dvor_(Sarajevo)) neighborhood. By all indications, Tornik was a very small marketplace surrounded by a proportionally small village and was not considered very important by [Ragusan](/wiki/Republic_of_Ragusa) merchants.

Other scholars say that *Vrhbosna* was a major town in the wider area of modern-day Sarajevo. [Papal](/wiki/Holy_See) documents say that in 1238, a cathedral dedicated to [Saint Paul](/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle) was built in the area. Disciples of the notable saints [Cyril](/wiki/Saints_Cyril_and_Methodius) and [Methodius](/wiki/Saints_Cyril_and_Methodius) stopped in the region, founding a church near [Vrelo Bosne](/wiki/Vrelo_Bosne). Whether or not the town was somewhere in the area of modern-day Sarajevo, the documents attest to its and the region's importance. There was also a citadel [Hodidjed](/wiki/Hodidjed) north-east to the [Old City](/wiki/Stari_Grad,_Sarajevo), dating from around 1263 until it was occupied by the [Ottoman Empire](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) in 1429.

### Ottoman era

See also: [Ottoman Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Ottoman_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), [Sacking of Sarajevo](/wiki/Sacking_of_Sarajevo), and [Bosnia and Herzegovina cuisine](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_cuisine)

The [Sebilj](/wiki/Sebilj_in_Sarajevo) is a [pseudo-Ottoman](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) style wooden [fountain](/wiki/Fountain) in the centre of [Baščaršija](/wiki/Ba%C5%A1%C4%8Dar%C5%A1ija) square. The current structure is an 1891 reconstruction of the original, which burnt down in 1852

Sarajevo was founded by the [Ottoman Empire](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) in the 1450s upon its conquest of the region, with 1461 used as the city's founding date. The first Ottoman governor of [Bosnia](/wiki/Bosnia_Eyalet), [Isa-Beg Ishaković](/wiki/Isa-Beg_Ishakovi%C4%87), transformed the cluster of villages into a city and [state capital](/wiki/Capital_city) by building several key structures, including a mosque, a closed marketplace, a [hamam](/wiki/Hamam), a [caravansarai](/wiki/Caravansarai), a bridge, and of course the governor's palace (["Saray"](/wiki/Saray)), which gave the city its present name in conjunction with “evo”. The mosque was named "Careva Džamija" (the [Emperor's Mosque](/wiki/Emperor%27s_Mosque)) in honor of Sultan [Mehmed II](/wiki/Mehmed_the_Conqueror). With the improvements, Sarajevo quickly grew into the largest city in the region. By the [15th century](/wiki/15th_century) the settlement was established as a city, named *Bosna-Saraj*, around the citadel in 1461.

Following the [expulsion of Jews from Spain](/wiki/Expulsion_of_Jews_from_Spain) at the end of the 15th century, and the invitation from the Ottoman Empire to resettle their population, [Sephardic Jews](/wiki/Sephardi_Jews) arrived in Sarajevo, which over time would become a leading center of Sephardic culture and the [Ladino language](/wiki/Judaeo-Spanish). Though relatively small in size, a Jewish quarter would develop over several blocks in [Baščaršija](/wiki/Ba%C5%A1%C4%8Dar%C5%A1ija).

Many local Christians converted to Islam at this time. To accommodate the new pilgrims on the road to [Mecca](/wiki/Mecca), in 1541, [Gazi Husrev-beg](/wiki/Gazi_Husrev-beg)'s quartermaster Vekil-Harrach built a pilgrim's mosque which it is still known to this day as the [Hadžijska Mosque](/wiki/Had%C5%BEijska_Mosque).

Under leaders such as the second governor Gazi Husrev-beg, Sarajevo grew at a rapid rate. Husrev-beg greatly shaped the physical city, as most of what is now the Old Town was built during his reign. Sarajevo became known for its large marketplace and numerous mosques, which by the middle of the 16th century numbered more than 100. At the peak of the empire, Sarajevo was the biggest and most important Ottoman city in the Balkans after [Istanbul](/wiki/Istanbul). By 1660, the population of Sarajevo was estimated to be over 80,000. By contrast, [Belgrade](/wiki/Belgrade) in 1683 had 100,000, and [Zagreb](/wiki/Zagreb) as late as 1851 had 14,000 people. As political conditions changed, Sarajevo became the site of warfare.

[Gazi Husrev-beg Mosque](/wiki/Gazi_Husrev-beg_Mosque) and the [Sarajevo Clock Tower](/wiki/Sarajevo_Clock_Tower)

In 1697, during the [Great Turkish War](/wiki/Great_Turkish_War), a raid was led by [Prince Eugene of Savoy](/wiki/Prince_Eugene_of_Savoy) of the [Habsburg monarchy](/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy) against the Ottoman Empire, [which conquered Sarajevo and left it plague-infected and burned to the ground](/wiki/Sacking_of_Sarajevo). After his men had looted thoroughly, they set the city on fire and destroyed nearly all of it in one day. Only a handful of neighborhoods, some mosques, and an [Orthodox church](/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church) were left standing. Numerous other fires weakened the city, which was later rebuilt but never fully recovered from the destruction. By 1807, it had only some 60,000 residents.

In the 1830s, several battles of the [Bosnian uprising](/wiki/Bosnian_uprising_(1831%E2%80%9332)) had taken place around the city. These had been led by [Husein Gradaščević](/wiki/Husein_Grada%C5%A1%C4%8Devi%C4%87). Today, a major city street is named *Zmaj od Bosne* (Dragon of Bosnia) in his honor. The rebellion failed and for several more decades, the Ottoman state remained in control of Bosnia.

The Ottoman Empire made Sarajevo an important administrative center by 1850. [Baščaršija](/wiki/Ba%C5%A1%C4%8Dar%C5%A1ija) became the central commercial district and cultural center of the city in the 15th century when Isa-Beg Ishaković founded the town. The toponym Baščaršija derives from the [Turkish language](/wiki/Turkish_language).

### Austria-Hungary

See also: [Austro-Hungarian rule in Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_rule_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and [Austro-Hungarian campaign in Bosnia and Herzegovina in 1878](/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_campaign_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_in_1878)

[Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria](/wiki/Archduke_Franz_Ferdinand_of_Austria) arrives at [the city hall](/wiki/National_and_University_Library_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) on the day of his assassination, 28 June 1914

[Austria-Hungary's occupation of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_rule_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) came in 1878 as part of the [Treaty of Berlin](/wiki/Treaty_of_Berlin_(1878)), and [complete annexation](/wiki/Bosnian_Crisis) followed in 1908, angering the [Serbs](/wiki/Serbs_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina). Sarajevo was industrialized by Austria-Hungary, who used the city as a testing area for new inventions such as [tramways](/wiki/Trams_in_Sarajevo), which were established in 1885 before they were later installed in [Vienna](/wiki/Vienna). Architects and engineers wanting to help rebuild Sarajevo as a modern European capital rushed to the city. A fire that burned down a large part of the central city area (*čaršija*) left more room for redevelopment. As a result, the city has a unique blend of the remaining Ottoman city market and contemporary Western architecture. Sarajevo also has some examples of [Secession-](/wiki/Vienna_Secession) and Pseudo-[Moorish](/wiki/Moors) ([Moorish Revival](/wiki/Moorish_Revival_architecture_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina)) styles that date from this period.

The [Latin Bridge](/wiki/Latin_Bridge) was the site of the [assassination of Franz Ferdinand](/wiki/Assassination_of_Archduke_Franz_Ferdinand)

The [Austro-Hungarian](/wiki/Austria-Hungary) period was one of great development for the city, as the Western power brought its new acquisition up to the standards of the [Victorian age](/wiki/Victorian_era). Various factories and other buildings were built at this time, and a large number of institutions were both Westernized and modernized. For the first time in history, Sarajevo's population began writing in [Latin script](/wiki/Latin_alphabet).
For the first time in centuries, the city significantly expanded outside its traditional borders. Much of the city's contemporary central municipality ([Centar](/wiki/Centar,_Sarajevo)) was constructed during this period.

Architecture in Sarajevo quickly developed into a wide range of styles and buildings. The [Sacred Heart Cathedral](/wiki/Sarajevo_cathedral), for example, was constructed using elements of [neo-gothic](/wiki/Gothic_Revival_architecture) and [Romanesque architecture](/wiki/Romanesque_architecture). The [National Museum](/wiki/National_Museum_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), [Sarajevo brewery](/wiki/Sarajevska_pivara), and [City Hall](/wiki/Vije%C4%87nica) were also constructed during this period. Additionally, Austrian officials made Sarajevo the first city in this part of Europe to have a [tramway](/wiki/Tram).

The [Academy of Fine Arts](/wiki/Academy_of_Fine_Arts_Sarajevo) was originally built to serve as an [Evangelical Church](/wiki/Reformed_Church_in_Austria) in 1899

Although the Bosnia Vilayet *[de jure](/wiki/De_jure)* remained part of the Ottoman Empire, it was *[de facto](/wiki/De_facto)* governed as an integral part of Austria-Hungary with the Ottomans having no say in its day-to-day governance. This lasted until 1908 when the territory was formally annexed and turned into a [condominium](/wiki/Condominium_(international_law)), jointly controlled by both Austrian [Cisleithania](/wiki/Cisleithania) and Hungarian [Transleithania](/wiki/Lands_of_the_Crown_of_Saint_Stephen).

The event that triggered [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I) was the [assassination](/wiki/Assassination_of_Archduke_Franz_Ferdinand) of [Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria](/wiki/Archduke_Franz_Ferdinand_of_Austria), along with his wife [Sophie, Duchess of Hohenberg](/wiki/Sophie,_Duchess_of_Hohenberg) in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 by [Gavrilo Princip](/wiki/Gavrilo_Princip), a Bosnian Serb and self-declared Yugoslav, and member of [Young Bosnia](/wiki/Young_Bosnia). This was followed by the [Anti-Serb riots in Sarajevo](/wiki/Anti-Serb_riots_in_Sarajevo), which resulted in two deaths and destruction of property.

In the ensuing war, however, most of the Balkan offensives occurred near Belgrade, and Sarajevo largely escaped damage and destruction. Following the war, Bosnia was annexed into the [Kingdom of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia), and Sarajevo became the capital of the [Drina Province](/wiki/Drina_Banovina).

### Yugoslavia

The [Eternal flame](/wiki/Eternal_flame_(Sarajevo)), a memorial to the military and civilian victims of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) in Sarajevo

After World War I and pressure from the [Royal Serbian Army](/wiki/Royal_Serbian_Army), alongside rebelling [Slavic](/wiki/Slavs) nations in [Austria-Hungary](/wiki/Austria-Hungary), Sarajevo became part of the [Kingdom of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia). Though it held some political significance as the center of first the Bosnian region and then the Drinska Banovina, the city was no longer a national capital and saw a decline in global influence.

During [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), the Kingdom of Yugoslavia's army was overrun by German and Italian forces. Following a German bombing campaign, Sarajevo was captured on 15 April 1941 by the [16th Motorized Infantry Division](/wiki/16th_Infantry_Division_(Wehrmacht)). The [Axis powers](/wiki/Axis_powers) created the [Independent State of Croatia](/wiki/Independent_State_of_Croatia) and included Sarajevo in its territory.

Immediately following the occupation, the main Sephardi Jewish synagogue, [Il Kal Grande](/wiki/Il_Kal_Grande), was looted, burned, and destroyed by the [Nazis](/wiki/Nazism). Within a matter of months, the centuries-old Sephardi and Ashkenazi Jewish communities of Sarajevo, comprising the vast majority of [Bosnian Jewry](/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), would be rounded up in the Old Synagogue (Stari hram) and deported to their deaths in [Croatian concentration camps](/wiki/Concentration_camps_in_the_Independent_State_of_Croatia). Roughly 85% of Bosnia's Jewish population would perish at the hands of the Nazis and the [Ustaše](/wiki/Usta%C5%A1e) during the [Holocaust in the region](/wiki/The_Holocaust_in_the_Independent_State_of_Croatia). The [Sarajevo Haggadah](/wiki/Sarajevo_Haggadah) was the most important artifact which survived this period, smuggled out of Sarajevo and saved from the Nazis and Ustaše by the chief librarian of the National Museum, [Derviš Korkut](/wiki/Dervi%C5%A1_Korkut).

[Vraca Memorial Park](/wiki/Vraca_Memorial_Park) is a park dedicated to World War II victims in the city

On 12 October 1941, a group of 108 notable [Bosniak](/wiki/Bosniaks) citizens of Sarajevo signed the [Resolution of Sarajevo Muslims](/wiki/Resolution_of_Sarajevo_Muslims) by which they condemned the [Genocide of Serbs](/wiki/Genocide_of_Serbs_in_the_Independent_State_of_Croatia) organized by the Ustaše, made a distinction between the Bosniaks who participated in such persecutions and the rest of the Bosniak population, presented information about the persecutions of Bosniaks by the [Ustaše](/wiki/Usta%C5%A1e), and requested security for all citizens of the country, regardless of their identity. During the summer of 1941, Ustaše militia periodically interned and executed groups of [Sarajevo Serbs](/wiki/Serbs_in_Sarajevo). In August 1941, they arrested about one hundred Serbs suspected of ties to the resistance armies, mostly church officials and members of the intelligentsia, and executed them or deported them to concentration camps. By mid-summer 1942, around 20,000 Serbs found refuge in Sarajevo from Ustaše terror.

The city was bombed by the [Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) from 1943 to 1944. The [Yugoslav Partisan](/wiki/Yugoslav_Partisans) movement was represented in the city. In the period February–May 1945, [Maks Luburić](/wiki/Vjekoslav_Luburi%C4%87) set up a Ustaše headquarters in a building known as [Villa Luburić](/wiki/Villa_Luburi%C4%87) and used it as a torture and execution place whose 323 victims were identified after the war. The resistance was led by [Vladimir Perić Valter](/wiki/Vladimir_Peri%C4%87), who died while leading the liberation of the city on 6 April 1945.

View west toward parts of [Novo Sarajevo](/wiki/Novo_Sarajevo)

After the war, Sarajevo was the capital of the [Socialist Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) within the [Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia). The Republic Government invested heavily in Sarajevo, building many new residential blocks in the municipalities of [Novi Grad](/wiki/Novi_Grad,_Sarajevo) and [Novo Sarajevo](/wiki/Novo_Sarajevo), while simultaneously developing the city's industry and transforming Sarajevo into a modern city. Sarajevo grew rapidly as it became an important regional industrial center in Yugoslavia. Between the end of the war and the end of Yugoslavia, the city grew from a population of 115,000 to more than 600,000 people. The [Vraca Memorial Park](/wiki/Vraca_Memorial_Park), a monument for victims of World War II, was dedicated on 25 November, the "[Statehood Day of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Statehood_Day_(Bosnia_and_Herzegovina))" when the [ZAVNOBIH](/wiki/State_Anti-fascist_Council_for_the_National_Liberation_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) held their first meeting in 1943.

A crowning moment of Sarajevo's time in Socialist Yugoslavia was the [1984 Winter Olympics](/wiki/1984_Winter_Olympics). Sarajevo beat out [Sapporo](/wiki/Sapporo), Japan, and [Falun](/wiki/Falun)/[Gothenburg](/wiki/Gothenburg), Sweden, to host the [Olympic Games](/wiki/Olympic_Games). The games were followed by a tourism boom, making the 1980s one of the city's most prosperous decades.

### Bosnian War

Main article: [Siege of Sarajevo](/wiki/Siege_of_Sarajevo)

See also: [Sniper Alley](/wiki/Sniper_Alley)

The [Sarajevo Red Line](/wiki/Sarajevo_Red_Line), a memorial event of the [Siege of Sarajevo](/wiki/Siege_of_Sarajevo)'s 20th anniversary. 11,541 empty chairs symbolized 11,541 victims of the war who were killed during the Siege

The [Bosnian War](/wiki/Bosnian_War) for independence resulted in large-scale destruction and dramatic population shifts during the [Siege of Sarajevo](/wiki/Siege_of_Sarajevo) between 1992 and 1996. Thousands of Sarajevans lost their lives under the constant bombardment and sniper shooting at civilians by the [Serb forces](/wiki/Army_of_Republika_Srpska) during the siege, the longest siege of a capital city in the history of modern warfare. Bosnian Serb forces of the [Republika Srpska](/wiki/Republika_Srpska) and the [Yugoslav People's Army](/wiki/Yugoslav_People%27s_Army) besieged Sarajevo from 5 April 1992 to 29 February 1996.

When [Bosnia and Herzegovina declared independence](/wiki/1992_Bosnian_independence_referendum) from [Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia) and achieved [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) recognition, Serbian leaders declared a new Serbian national state Republika Srpska (RS) which was carved out from the territory of Bosnia and Herzegovina. The Army of Republika Srpska encircled Sarajevo with a siege force of 18,000 stationed in the surrounding hills, from which they assaulted the city with artillery, mortars, tanks, anti-aircraft guns, heavy machine guns, multiple rocket launchers, rocket-launched aircraft bombs, and sniper rifles. From 2 May 1992, the Serbs [blockaded](/wiki/Blockade) the city. The [Bosnian government defense forces](/wiki/Army_of_the_Republic_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) inside the besieged city were poorly equipped and unable to break the siege.

During the siege, 11,541 people were killed, including over 1,500 children. An additional 56,000 people were wounded, including nearly 15,000 children. The [1991 census](/wiki/1991_population_census_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) indicates that before the siege, the city and its surrounding areas had a population of 525,980.

When the siege ended, the concrete scars caused by mortar shell explosions left marks that were filled with red resin. After the red resin was placed, it left floral patterns, which led to them being dubbed [Sarajevo Roses](/wiki/Sarajevo_Rose). Division of the territory according to the [Dayton Agreement](/wiki/Dayton_Agreement) resulted in a [mass exodus](/wiki/Exodus_of_Sarajevo_Serbs) in early 1996 of some 62,000 Sarajevo Serbs from the city and its suburbs, creating today's more monoethnic post-war city.

### Present

Main article: [History of Sarajevo § Modern](/wiki/History_of_Sarajevo#Modern)

[ARIA Centar](/wiki/ARIA_Centar), erected in 2009

Various modern buildings now occupy Sarajevo's skyline, most significantly the [Bosmal City Center](/wiki/Bosmal_City_Center), [ARIA Centar](/wiki/ARIA_Centar), [Sarajevo City Center](/wiki/Sarajevo_City_Center) (all three by architect [Sead Gološ](/wiki/Sead_Golo%C5%A1)) and the [Avaz Twist Tower](/wiki/Avaz_Twist_Tower), which at the time of its building was the tallest skyscraper in former Yugoslavia.

In 2014, the city saw [anti-government protests and riots](/wiki/2014_unrest_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and record rainfall [that caused historic flooding](/wiki/2014_Southeast_Europe_floods). Recent years have seen population growth as well as increases in tourism.

The [Sarajevo cable car](/wiki/Sarajevo_cable_car), also known as the Trebević cable car, Sarajevo's key landmark during the 1984 Winter Olympics, was rebuilt in 2017 and reopened on 6 April 2018. The cable car runs from Sarajevo at Bistrik station to the slopes of [Trebević](/wiki/Trebevi%C4%87) at Vidikovac station.

## Administration

### Largest city of Bosnia and Herzegovina

The [Building of the Presidency of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Building_of_the_Presidency_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) (left) and Bosnia and Herzegovina's [government building](/wiki/Greece%E2%80%93Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_Friendship_Building) (right)

Sarajevo is the capital of the country of [Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and its sub-entity, the [Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Federation_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), as well as of the [Sarajevo Canton](/wiki/Sarajevo_Canton). It is also the *de jure* capital of another entity, [Republika Srpska](/wiki/Republika_Srpska). Each of these levels of government has its parliament or council, as well as judicial courts, in the city. All national institutions and foreign [embassies](/wiki/Diplomatic_mission) are in Sarajevo.

Sarajevo is home to the [Council of Ministers of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), [Parliamentary Assembly of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Parliamentary_Assembly_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), [Presidency of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Presidency_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), the [Constitutional Court of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and the operational command of the [Armed Forces of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina).

Bosnia and Herzegovina's [Parliament office](/wiki/Greece%E2%80%93Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_Friendship_Building) in Sarajevo was damaged heavily in the [Bosnian War](/wiki/Bosnian_War). Due to damage, the staff and documents were moved to a nearby ground-level office to resume work. In late 2006, reconstruction work started on Parliament and was finished in 2007. The cost of reconstruction was 80% funded by the [Greek Government](/wiki/Politics_of_Greece) through the Hellenic Program of Balkans Reconstruction (ESOAV), and 20% by Bosnia and Herzegovina.

### Municipalities and city government

Main articles: [List of mayors of Sarajevo](/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Sarajevo) and [Sarajevo City Council](/wiki/Sarajevo_City_Council)

The four municipalities of the City of Sarajevo ([Novi Grad](/wiki/Novi_Grad,_Sarajevo), [Novo Sarajevo](/wiki/Novo_Sarajevo), [Centar](/wiki/Centar,_Sarajevo) and [Stari Grad](/wiki/Stari_Grad,_Sarajevo)) within the territory of [Sarajevo Canton](/wiki/Sarajevo_Canton)

The City of Sarajevo comprises four municipalities: [Centar](/wiki/Centar,_Sarajevo), [Novi Grad](/wiki/Novi_Grad,_Sarajevo), [Novo Sarajevo](/wiki/Novo_Sarajevo), and [Stari Grad](/wiki/Stari_Grad,_Sarajevo). Each operates their own [municipal government](/wiki/Local_government), while united they form one city government with its constitution. The [executive branch](/wiki/Executive_(government)) ([Bosnian](/wiki/Bosnian_language): *Gradska uprava*) consists of a [mayor](/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Sarajevo), with two deputies and a cabinet.

The [legislative branch](/wiki/Legislature) consists of the [City Council](/wiki/Sarajevo_City_Council), or *Gradsko vijeće*. The council has 28 members, including a council speaker, two deputies, and a secretary. Councilors are elected by the municipality in numbers roughly proportional to their population. The City Statute requires the city council to include at least six councilors from each [constituent people](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and at least two from the ranks of Others.

Sarajevo's Municipalities are further split into "local communities" (Bosnian, *Mjesne zajednice*). Local communities have a small role in city government and are intended as a way for ordinary citizens to get involved in city government. They are based on key neighborhoods in the city.

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Sarajevo](/wiki/Economy_of_Sarajevo)

Central Bank of Bosnia and Herzegovina

Sarajevo's large manufacturing, administrative, and tourism sectors make it the strongest economic region of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Sarajevo Canton generates almost 25% of the country's GDP. After years of war, Sarajevo's economy saw reconstruction and rehabilitation programs. The [Central Bank of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) opened in Sarajevo in 1997 and the [Sarajevo Stock Exchange](/wiki/Sarajevo_Stock_Exchange) began trading in 2002.

While Sarajevo had a large industrial base during its communist period, only a few pre-existing businesses have successfully adapted to the [market economy](/wiki/Market_economy). Sarajevo industries now include tobacco products, furniture, hosiery, automobiles, and communication equipment. Companies based in Sarajevo include [BH Telecom](/wiki/BH_Telecom), [Bosnalijek](/wiki/Bosnalijek), [Energopetrol](/wiki/Energopetrol), [Sarajevo Tobacco Factory](/wiki/Sarajevo_Tobacco_Factory), and [Sarajevska pivara](/wiki/Sarajevska_pivara) (Sarajevo Brewery).

In 2019, the total export for the Sarajevo Canton was worth about 1,427,496,000 [KM](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_convertible_mark). Most of Sarajevo's exports (20.55%) head to [Germany](/wiki/Germany), with [Serbia](/wiki/Serbia) and [Croatia](/wiki/Croatia) following behind at 12% respectively. The largest amount of imported goods comes from Croatia, at 20.95%. With a worth of total import of about 4,872,213,000 [KM](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_convertible_mark), the total import is almost 3.4 times the total export.

In 1981, Sarajevo's GDP per capita was 133% of the Yugoslav average. Gross pay in Sarajevo in March 2023 was [KM](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_convertible_mark) 2,497 or [€](/wiki/Euro)1,269, while net salary was KM 1,585 or €805, indicating stable growth.

## Tourism and recreation

[Sarajevo cable car](/wiki/Sarajevo_cable_car) taking visitors to mount [Trebević](/wiki/Trebevi%C4%87) from the city centre

Sarajevo has a wide tourist industry and a fast-expanding service sector thanks to the strong annual growth in tourist arrivals. Sarajevo also benefits from being both a summer and winter destination with continuity in its tourism throughout the year. The travel guide series, *[Lonely Planet](/wiki/Lonely_Planet)* named Sarajevo as the 43rd best city in the world, and in December 2009, listed Sarajevo as one of the top ten cities to visit in 2010.

*[Vilsonovo Šetalište](/wiki/Vilsonovo_%C5%A0etali%C5%A1te)* (Wilson's Promenade) along the [Miljacka](/wiki/Miljacka)

In 2019, 733,259 tourists visited Sarajevo, giving 1,667,545 overnight stays, which was 20% more than in 2018. Sports-related tourism uses the legacy facilities of the [1984 Winter Olympics](/wiki/1984_Winter_Olympics), especially the skiing facilities on the nearby mountains of [Bjelašnica](/wiki/Bjela%C5%A1nica), [Igman](/wiki/Igman), [Jahorina](/wiki/Jahorina), [Trebević](/wiki/Trebevi%C4%87) and [Treskavica](/wiki/Treskavica).

Sarajevo's 600 years of history, influenced by both Western and Eastern empires, makes it a [tourist attraction](/wiki/Tourist_attraction) with splendid variations. The city has hosted travellers for centuries, because it was an important trading centre during the [Ottoman](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) and [Austro-Hungarian](/wiki/Austria-Hungary) empires and because it was a natural stop for many routes between East and West. Examples of popular destinations in Sarajevo include the [Vrelo Bosne](/wiki/Vrelo_Bosne) park, the [Sarajevo cathedral](/wiki/Sacred_Heart_Cathedral,_Sarajevo), and the [Gazi Husrev-beg Mosque](/wiki/Gazi_Husrev-beg_Mosque). Tourism in Sarajevo is chiefly focused on historical, religious, and cultural sites and winter sports.

The [spring](/wiki/Vrelo_Bosne) of the [Bosna river](/wiki/Bosna_(river)) is located in [Ilidža](/wiki/Ilid%C5%BEa), a chief suburb of Sarajevo

There are many parks throughout the city and on the outskirts. A popular activity among locals is street chess, usually played at [Trg Oslobođenja - Alija Izetbegović](/wiki/Trg_Oslobo%C4%91enja_-_Alija_Izetbegovi%C4%87). Veliki Park is the largest green area in the centre of Sarajevo. It is nestled between [Titova](/wiki/Marshal_Tito_street_(Sarajevo)), [Koševo](/wiki/Ko%C5%A1evo), [Džidžikovac](/wiki/D%C5%BEid%C5%BEikovac), Tina Ujevića and Trampina Streets and in the lower part, there is a monument dedicated to the [Children of Sarajevo](/wiki/Siege_of_Sarajevo#Casualties). [Hastahana](/wiki/Nijaz_Durakovi%C4%87_Park) is a popular place to relax in the Austro-Hungarian neighborhood of [Marijin Dvor](/wiki/Marijin_Dvor_(Sarajevo)). [Goat's Bridge](/wiki/Goat%27s_Bridge), locally known as *Kozija Ćuprija*, in the Miljacka Canyon is also a popular park destination along the [Dariva](/wiki/Goat%27s_Bridge#Dariva) walkway and river Miljacka. On 24 December 2012, a park hosting two brass sculptures resembling two mourning mothers was dedicated as the Friendship Park, commemorating over 45 years of friendship between Sarajevo and [Baku](/wiki/Baku), the capital of Azerbaijan.

Sarajevo is also famous for its city lookouts; including an observation deck on the [Avaz Twist Tower](/wiki/Avaz_Twist_Tower), Park Prinčeva restaurant, Vidikovac lookout (Mt. Trebević), Zmajevac lookout and Yellow/White fortresses lookouts (in [Vratnik](/wiki/Vratnik_(Sarajevo))) as well as numerous other rooftops throughout the city (i.e. Alta Shopping Centre, [ARIA Centar](/wiki/ARIA_Centar), Hotel Hecco Deluxe). A symbol of Sarajevo is the [Trebević cable car](/wiki/Sarajevo_cable_car) which was reconstructed in 2018, also it is one of the most popular tourist attractions in the city taking visitors from the city centre to Mount Trebević.

There is also a [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) tentative monument, the [Old Jewish Cemetery](/wiki/Old_Jewish_Cemetery,_Sarajevo), an almost 500 years old site that is the second-largest Jewish sepulchral complex in Europe, the one in [Prague](/wiki/Prague) being the largest. It is also one of the most significant memorial complexes in the world. It represents the eternal proof of the coexistence of two or more different confessions under different administrations and rules, and the proof of mutual respect and tolerance.

Thanks to steady but constant and stable growth after the war, today's built-up area includes not only previously mentioned urban municipalities but the urban part of [Hadžići](/wiki/Had%C5%BEi%C4%87i) that is uninterruptedly connected to [Ilidža](/wiki/Ilid%C5%BEa), the westernmost part of the Sarajevo urban settlement, is inhabited by more than 419,000 people. It is noticeable that the fastest-growing municipalities are [Novi Grad](/wiki/Novi_Grad,_Sarajevo), one of the main ones and the most inhabited one where the population has increased by almost 4,000 people or 2.95% since the [2013 census](/wiki/2013_population_census_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), and Ilidža that has recorded an increase of almost 7% since 2013.

In June 2016, the final results of the 2013 census were published. According to the census, the population of the [Sarajevo Canton](/wiki/Sarajevo_Canton) was 413,593, with 55,181 residents in [Centar](/wiki/Centar,_Sarajevo), 118,553 in Novi Grad, 64,814 in [Novo Sarajevo](/wiki/Novo_Sarajevo) and 36,976 in [Stari Grad](/wiki/Stari_Grad,_Sarajevo).

The last official Yugoslav census took place in [1991](/wiki/1991_population_census_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and recorded 527,049 people living in the city of Sarajevo (ten [municipalities](/wiki/Municipality)). In the settlement of Sarajevo proper, there were 454,319 inhabitants. The war displaced hundreds of thousands of people, a large majority of whom have not returned.

The war changed the ethnic and religious profile of the city. It had long been a multicultural city, and often went by the nickname of "Europe's Jerusalem". At the time of the 1991 census, 49.2 percent of the city's population of 527,049 were [Bosniaks](/wiki/Bosniaks), 29.8 percent [Serbs](/wiki/Serbs_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), 10.7 percent [Yugoslavs](/wiki/Yugoslavs), 6.6 percent [Croats](/wiki/Croats_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and 3.6 percent other ethnicities (Jews, Romas, etc.).

According to academic Fran Markowitz, there are several "administrative apparatuses and public pressures that push people who might prefer to identify as flexible, multiply constituted hybrids or with one of the now unnamed minority groups into one of the three Bosniac-Croat-Serb constituent nations". These include respondents being encouraged by census interviewers to identify as belonging to one of the three [constituent peoples](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina). Her analysis of marriage registration data shows, for instance, that 67 percent of people marrying in 2003 identified as Bosniak or Muslim, which is significantly lower than the 79.6 percent census figure from 2002 (unlike the census, where people respond to an interviewer, applicants to the marriage registry fill in the form themselves).

## Transportation

### Roads and highways

Sarajevo's location in a valley between mountains makes it a compact city. Narrow city streets and a lack of parking areas restrict automobile traffic but allow better pedestrian and cyclist mobility. The two main roads are [Titova Ulica](/wiki/Marshal_Tito_street_(Sarajevo)) (Street of [Marshal Tito](/wiki/Josip_Broz_Tito)) and the east–west [Zmaj od Bosne](/wiki/Husein_Grada%C5%A1%C4%8Devi%C4%87) (Dragon of Bosnia) highway (E761). Located roughly at the center of the country, Sarajevo is Bosnia's main intersection. The city is connected to all the other major cities by highway or national road like [Zenica](/wiki/Zenica), [Banja Luka](/wiki/Banja_Luka), [Tuzla](/wiki/Tuzla), [Mostar](/wiki/Mostar), [Goražde](/wiki/Gora%C5%BEde) and [Foča](/wiki/Fo%C4%8Da).

Tourists from [Central Europe](/wiki/Central_Europe) and elsewhere visiting [Dalmatia](/wiki/Dalmatia) driving via [Budapest](/wiki/Budapest) through Sarajevo also contribute to the traffic congestion in and around Sarajevo. The trans-European highway, [Corridor Vc](/wiki/European_route_E73), runs through Sarajevo connecting it to Budapest in the north, and [Ploče](/wiki/Plo%C4%8De) at the Adriatic Sea in the south. The highway is being built by the [government](/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and should cost 3.5 billion [Euro](/wiki/Euro). Up until March 2012, the [Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Federation_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) invested around 600 million euros in the A1. In 2014, the sections Sarajevo-Zenica and Sarajevo-[Tarčin](/wiki/Tar%C4%8Din) were completed including the [Sarajevo Beltway](/wiki/Sarajevo_Beltway) ring road.

### Tram, bus and trolleybus

[Sarajevo tram](/wiki/Trams_in_Sarajevo)

[Sarajevo's electric tramways](/wiki/Trams_in_Sarajevo), in operation since 1884 and electrified since 1895, are the oldest form of public transportation in the city. Sarajevo had the first full-time (dawn to dusk) tram line in Europe, and the second in the world. Opened on [New Year's Day](/wiki/New_Year%27s_Day) in 1885, it was the testing line for the tram in [Vienna](/wiki/Vienna) and the [Austro-Hungarian Empire](/wiki/Austria-Hungary), and operated by horses. Originally built to 760 mm (2 ft 5+15⁄16 in) [Bosnian gauge](/wiki/Bosnian_gauge), the present system in 1960 was upgraded to 1,435 mm (4 ft 8+1⁄2 in) [standard gauge](/wiki/Standard_gauge). The trams played a pivotal role in the growth of the city in the 20th century.

[MAN](/wiki/MAN_Truck_%26_Bus) Centrotrans bus

There are seven tramway lines supplemented by five [trolleybus](/wiki/Trolleybus) lines and numerous bus routes. The [main railway station](/wiki/Sarajevo_main_railway_station) in Sarajevo is in the north-central area of the city. From there, the tracks head west before branching off in different directions, including to industrial zones in the city. Sarajevo is undergoing a major infrastructure renewal; many highways and streets are being repaved, the tram system is undergoing modernization, and new bridges and roads are under construction. In January 2021, the city bought 25 new BKM 433 trolleybuses. [Tram track](/wiki/Tramway_track) renovation lasted from August 2021 to September 2023. The city also bought 15 new [Stadler Tango](/wiki/Stadler_Tango) trams in September 2021. The first tram arrived in December 2023, while the rest are expected to arrive by the summer of 2024. An additional 10 new trams were bought, as well as 30 new buses.

### Railway

[Sarajevo main railway station](/wiki/Sarajevo_main_railway_station)

The [Sarajevo main railway station](/wiki/Sarajevo_main_railway_station) was built in 1882 for the [narrow-gauge railway](/wiki/Narrow-gauge_railway). After [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), it was decided to replace the old station by a new [functionalist](/wiki/Functionalism_(architecture)) building. The ceremonial completion of the station building took place in 1949. The station was electrified in 1967, as part of the early electrification program introduced in Bosnia up to 1969.

The [Sarajevo–Ploče railway](/wiki/Sarajevo%E2%80%93Plo%C4%8De_railway) provides a connection to the [Adriatic](/wiki/Adriatic_Sea) coast. It holds the distinction of being the first 25 kV AC-electrified country in the former Yugoslavia, followed by Croatia and Serbia. Once, the [East Bosnian railway](/wiki/Narrow-gauge_railways_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina#East_Bosnian_railway) connected Sarajevo to [Belgrade](/wiki/Belgrade).

#### Metro plans

To solve traffic congestion in the city, Sarajevo-based architect Muzafer Osmanagić proposed a study called "Eco Energy 2010–2015", proposing a subway system underneath the bed of the river [Miljacka](/wiki/Miljacka). The first line of Metro Sarajevo would connect [Baščaršija](/wiki/Ba%C5%A1%C4%8Dar%C5%A1ija) with [Otoka](/wiki/Otoka,_Sarajevo). This line would cost some 150 million [KM](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_convertible_mark) and be financed by the [European Bank for Reconstruction and Development](/wiki/European_Bank_for_Reconstruction_and_Development).

### Airport

[Sarajevo International Airport](/wiki/Sarajevo_International_Airport)

[Sarajevo International Airport](/wiki/Sarajevo_International_Airport) ([IATA](/wiki/IATA_airport_code): **SJJ**) is just a few kilometers southwest of the city and was voted Best European Airport With Under 1,000,000 Passengers at the 15th Annual ACI-Europe in [Munich](/wiki/Munich) in 2005.

The first regular flights to Sarajevo using an airfield in the suburb of [Butmir](/wiki/Butmir) began in 1930 when the domestic airliner [Aeroput](/wiki/Aeroput) opened a regular route linking [Belgrade](/wiki/Belgrade) to [Podgorica](/wiki/Podgorica) through Sarajevo. Later, Aeroput opened a route that linked Sarajevo with [Split](/wiki/Split,_Croatia), [Rijeka](/wiki/Rijeka), and [Dubrovnik](/wiki/Dubrovnik), and in 1938, the first international flights were introduced when Aeroput extended the route Dubrovnik – Sarajevo – Zagreb to [Vienna](/wiki/Vienna), [Brno](/wiki/Brno) and [Prague](/wiki/Prague). The airfield in Butmir remained in use until 1969.

The need for a new airport in Sarajevo, with an asphalt-concrete runway, was acknowledged in the mid-1960s when [JAT](/wiki/Jat_Airways), the Yugoslav national carrier at that time, began acquiring jet planes. The construction of the airport began in 1966 at its present location, not far from the old one.

Sarajevo Airport opened on 2 June 1969 for domestic traffic. In 1970, [Frankfurt](/wiki/Frankfurt) became the first international destination served. Most of the time the airport was a 'feeder' airport where passengers embarked for flights to Zagreb and Belgrade on their way to international destinations. Over time, the traffic volume steadily grew from 70,000 to 600,000 passengers a year. Later, during the [Bosnian War](/wiki/Bosnian_War), the airport was used for [UN](/wiki/United_Nations) flights and humanitarian relief. Since the [Dayton Agreement](/wiki/Dayton_Agreement) in 1995, the airport retook its role as the main air portal to Bosnia and Herzegovina.

In 2017, 957,971 passengers travelled through the airport, which was 61,4% of the total airport traffic in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

Plans for the extension of the passenger terminal, together with upgrading and expanding the taxiway and apron, started in the fall of 2012. The existing terminal was expanded by approximately 7,000 m2 (75,347 sq ft). The upgraded airport was directly linked to the commercial retail center Sarajevo Airport Center, making it easier for tourists and travelers to spend their time before flight boarding shopping and enjoying the many amenities that are offered. Between 2015 and 2018, the airport was upgraded for more than 25 million euros.

## Communications and media

Main article: [Communications and media in Sarajevo](/wiki/Communications_and_media_in_Sarajevo)

The [Avaz Twist Tower](/wiki/Avaz_Twist_Tower), headquarters for the newspaper *[Dnevni avaz](/wiki/Dnevni_avaz)*

As the largest city of Bosnia and Herzegovina, Sarajevo is the main center of the country's media. Most of the communications and media infrastructure was destroyed during the war but reconstruction monitored by the [Office of the High Representative](/wiki/High_Representative_for_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) has helped to modernize the industry as a whole. For example, the Internet was first made available to the city in 1995.

*[Oslobođenje](/wiki/Oslobo%C4%91enje)* (Liberation), founded in 1943, is Sarajevo's longest-running continuously circulating newspaper and the only one to survive the war. However, this long-running and trusted newspaper has fallen behind *[Dnevni avaz](/wiki/Dnevni_avaz)* (Daily Voice), founded in 1995, and *Jutarnje Novine* (Morning News) in circulation in Sarajevo. Other local periodicals include the Croatian newspaper *[Hrvatska riječ](/wiki/Hrvatska_rije%C4%8D)* and the Bosnian magazine *Start*, as well as weekly newspapers *[Slobodna Bosna](/wiki/Slobodna_Bosna)* (*Free Bosnia*) and *BH Dani* (*BH Days*). *[Novi Plamen](/wiki/Novi_Plamen)*, a monthly magazine, is the most left-wing publication.

The [Radio and Television of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Radio_and_Television_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) (BHRT) is Sarajevo's [public television](/wiki/Public_broadcasting) station and was created in 1945 under the umbrella of the [Yugoslav Radio Television](/wiki/Yugoslav_Radio_Television) (JRT). It had its first television program aired in 1961, while continuous programming started in 1969. It is one of three main TV stations in Bosnia and Herzegovina. Other stations based in the city include [Hayat TV](/wiki/Hayat_TV_(Bosnia_and_Herzegovina)), [O Kanal](/wiki/O_Kanal), [OBN](/wiki/OBN_(TV_channel)), [TV Kantona Sarajevo](/wiki/TVSA) and [TV Alfa](/wiki/TV_Alfa).

The headquarters of [Al Jazeera Balkans](/wiki/Al_Jazeera_Balkans) is also in Sarajevo, with a broadcasting studio at the top of the [ARIA Centar](/wiki/ARIA_Centar). The news channel covers [Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), [Serbia](/wiki/Serbia), [Croatia](/wiki/Croatia) and [Montenegro](/wiki/Montenegro) and the surrounding [Balkan](/wiki/Balkans) states.

Many small independent radio stations exist, including established stations such as [Radio M](/wiki/Radio_M), [RSG Radio](/wiki/RSG_Radio) (Radio Old Town), Studentski eFM Radio, Radio 202 and [Radio BIR](/wiki/Radio_BIR). [Radio Free Europe](/wiki/Radio_Free_Europe/Radio_Liberty), as well as several American and Western European stations are available.

## Education

### Higher education

Rectorate and the [Faculty of Law](/wiki/Faculty_of_Law,_University_of_Sarajevo), [University of Sarajevo](/wiki/University_of_Sarajevo)

[National and University Library of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/National_and_University_Library_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina)

Higher education has a long and rich tradition in Sarajevo. The first institution that can be classified as a tertiary educational institution was a school of [Sufi](/wiki/Sufism) philosophy established by [Gazi Husrev-beg](/wiki/Gazi_Husrev-beg) in 1537; numerous other religious schools have been established over time. In 1887, under the Austro-Hungarian Empire, a [Sharia](/wiki/Sharia) Law School began a five-year program. In the 1940s, the [University of Sarajevo](/wiki/University_of_Sarajevo) became the city's first secular higher education institute, effectively building upon the foundations established by the Saraybosna Hanıka in 1537. In the 1950s, post-bachelor graduate degrees became available. Severely damaged during the war, it was recently rebuilt in partnership with more than 40 other universities.

There are also several universities in Sarajevo, including:

- [University of Sarajevo](/wiki/University_of_Sarajevo)
- [Sarajevo School of Science and Technology](/wiki/Sarajevo_School_of_Science_and_Technology)

- [Sarajevo Graduate School of Business](/wiki/Sarajevo_Graduate_School_of_Business)
- [International Burch University](/wiki/International_Burch_University)

### Primary and secondary education

As of 2005, there are 46 elementary schools (Grades 1–9) and 33 high schools (Grades 10–13) in Sarajevo, including three schools for children with special needs.

There are also several [international schools](/wiki/International_school) in Sarajevo, catering to the expatriate community; some of which are [Sarajevo International School](/wiki/Sarajevo_International_School) and the French International School of Sarajevo, established in 1998.

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Sarajevo](/wiki/Culture_of_Sarajevo)

Copies of the [Sarajevo Haggadah](/wiki/Sarajevo_Haggadah)

Sarajevo has been home to many different religions for centuries, giving the city a range of diverse cultures. In the time of Ottoman occupation of Bosnia, [Muslims](/wiki/Islam), [Orthodox Christians](/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church), [Roman Catholics](/wiki/Catholic_Church), and [Sephardi Jews](/wiki/Sephardi_Jews) all shared the city while maintaining distinctive identities. They were joined during the brief occupation by [Austria-Hungary](/wiki/Austria-Hungary) by a smaller number of [Germans](/wiki/Germans), [Hungarians](/wiki/Hungarians), [Slovaks](/wiki/Slovaks), [Czechs](/wiki/Czechs) and [Ashkenazi Jews](/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews). By 1909, about 50% of the city's inhabitants were Muslim, 25% were Catholic, 15% were Orthodox, and 10% were Jewish.

Historically, Sarajevo has been home to several prominent Bosnian poets, scholars, philosophers, and writers. To list only a very few; [Nobel Prize](/wiki/Nobel_Prize)-winner [Vladimir Prelog](/wiki/Vladimir_Prelog) is from the city, as are the writer [Zlatko Topčić](/wiki/Zlatko_Top%C4%8Di%C4%87) and the poet [Abdulah Sidran](/wiki/Abdulah_Sidran). Nobel Prize-winner [Ivo Andrić](/wiki/Ivo_Andri%C4%87) attended high school in Sarajevo for two years. [Academy Award](/wiki/Academy_Awards)-winning director [Danis Tanović](/wiki/Danis_Tanovi%C4%87) lives in the city.

The [Sarajevo National Theatre](/wiki/Sarajevo_National_Theatre) is the oldest professional theater in Bosnia and Herzegovina, having been established in 1921. In 2019, Sarajevo was designated as a [City of Film](/wiki/City_of_Film) by [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO).

A [panoramic](/wiki/Panorama) view of the ruined castle of [Bijela Tabija](/wiki/Bijela_Tabija) *"White Bastion"* in the very east of Sarajevo

### Museums

The [National Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/National_Museum_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) houses many important historical items from the country

Sarajevo is rich in museums, including the [Museum of Sarajevo](/wiki/Museum_of_Sarajevo_1878%E2%80%931918), the [Ars Aevi](/wiki/Ars_Aevi) Museum of Contemporary Art, [Historical Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Historical_Museum_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), The Museum of Literature and Theatre Arts of Bosnia and Herzegovina, and the [National Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/National_Museum_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) (established in 1888) home to the [Sarajevo Haggadah](/wiki/Sarajevo_Haggadah), an [illuminated manuscript](/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript) and the oldest [Sephardic Jewish](/wiki/Sephardi_Jews) document in the world issued in [Barcelona](/wiki/Barcelona) around 1350, containing the traditional Jewish [Haggadah](/wiki/Haggadah), is on permanent display at the museum. It is the only remaining illustrated [Sephardic](/wiki/Sephardi_Jews) [Haggadah](/wiki/Sarajevo_Haggadah) in the world. The National Museum also hosts year-round exhibitions about local, regional and international culture and history, and exhibits over 5,000 artifacts from Bosnia's history.

The [Museum of Sarajevo 1878–1918](/wiki/Museum_of_Sarajevo_1878%E2%80%931918)

The [Alija Izetbegović](/wiki/Alija_Izetbegovi%C4%87) Museum was opened on 19 October 2007 and is in the old town fort, more specifically in the [Vratnik](/wiki/Vratnik_(Sarajevo)) Kapija towers Ploča and Širokac. The museum is a commemoration of the influence and body of work of Alija Izetbegović, the first [president of the Presidency](/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Presidency_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) of the [Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Republic_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina). Sarajevo is also home to the [War Childhood Museum](/wiki/War_Childhood_Museum), an independent not-for-profit museum containing personal belongings from the war and showing stories behind them. In addition, in 2018, the museum won the [Council of Europe Museum Prize award](/wiki/European_Museum_of_the_Year_Award#Council_of_Europe_Museum_Prize_winners_(1977–)) for best museum.

The city also hosts the [Sarajevo National Theatre](/wiki/Sarajevo_National_Theatre), established in 1921, and the [Sarajevo Youth Theatre](/wiki/Sarajevo_Youth_Theatre). Some other [cultural institutions](/wiki/Cultural_institution) include the Center for Sarajevo Culture, [Sarajevo City Library](/wiki/National_and_University_Library_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), [National Gallery of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), and the [Bosniak Institute](/wiki/Bosniak_Institute), a privately owned library and art collection focusing on Bosniak history.

Demolitions associated with the war, as well as reconstruction, destroyed several institutions and cultural or [religious symbols](/wiki/Religious_symbol) including the [Gazi Husrev-beg Library](/wiki/Gazi_Husrev-beg_Library), the national library, the [Sarajevo Oriental Institute](/wiki/Oriental_Institute_in_Sarajevo), and a museum dedicated to the [1984 Winter Olympics](/wiki/1984_Winter_Olympics). Consequently, the different levels of government established strong cultural protection laws and institutions. Bodies charged with cultural preservation in Sarajevo include the Institute for the Protection of the Cultural, Historical and [Natural Heritage](/wiki/Cultural_heritage) of Bosnia and Herzegovina (and their Sarajevo Canton counterpart), and the Bosnia and Herzegovina Commission to Preserve National Monuments.

### Music

See also: [List of Bosnia and Herzegovina patriotic songs](/wiki/List_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_patriotic_songs)

[Bijelo Dugme](/wiki/Bijelo_Dugme), widely considered to have been the most popular band ever to exist in the former [Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia) and one of the most important acts of the [Yugoslav rock scene](/wiki/Popular_music_in_Yugoslavia), originated in Sarajevo. Pictured are [Mladen Vojičić Tifa](/wiki/Mladen_Voji%C4%8Di%C4%87_Tifa) (left) and [Goran Bregović](/wiki/Goran_Bregovi%C4%87)

Sarajevo is and has historically been one of the most important musical enclaves in the region. The Sarajevo school of [pop rock](/wiki/Pop_rock) developed in the city between 1961 and 1991. This type of music began with bands like [Indexi](/wiki/Indexi), [Kodeksi](/wiki/Kodeksi), and singer-songwriter [Kemal Monteno](/wiki/Kemal_Monteno). It continued into the 1980s, with bands such as [Plavi orkestar](/wiki/Plavi_orkestar), [Crvena jabuka](/wiki/Crvena_jabuka), and [Divlje jagode](/wiki/Divlje_jagode), by most accounts, pioneering the [regional](/wiki/Balkans) rock and roll movement. Sarajevo was also the home and birthplace of arguably the most popular and influential Yugoslav [rock band](/wiki/Band_(rock_and_pop)) of all time, [Bijelo Dugme](/wiki/Bijelo_Dugme), somewhat of a Bosnian parallel to the [Rolling Stones](/wiki/The_Rolling_Stones), in both popularity and influence.

Sarajevo was also the home of a very notable [post-punk](/wiki/Post-punk) urban subculture known as the [New Primitives](/wiki/New_Primitivism), which began during the early 1980s with the Baglama Band which was banned shortly after its first LP and was brought into the mainstream through bands such as [Zabranjeno Pušenje](/wiki/Zabranjeno_Pu%C5%A1enje) and [Elvis J. Kurtović & His Meteors](/wiki/Elvis_J._Kurtovi%C4%87_%26_His_Meteors), as well as the *[Top lista nadrealista](/wiki/Top_lista_nadrealista)* radio, and later television show. Other notable bands considered to be part of this subculture are [Bombaj Štampa](/wiki/Bombaj_%C5%A0tampa). Besides and separately from the New Primitives, Sarajevo is the hometown to one of the most significant [ex-Yugoslavian](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia) alternative [industrial](/wiki/Industrial_music)-[noise](/wiki/Noise_music) bands, [SCH](/wiki/SCH_(band)).

[Vedran Smailović](/wiki/Vedran_Smailovi%C4%87) playing the cello on top of the ruins of the [National library](/wiki/National_and_University_Library_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) in 1992

Perhaps more importantly, Sarajevo in the late 19th and throughout the 20th century was home to a burgeoning and large center of [Sevdalinka](/wiki/Sevdalinka) record-making and contributed greatly to bringing this historical genre of music to the mainstream, which had for many centuries been a staple of Bosnian culture. Songwriters and musicians such as [Himzo Polovina](/wiki/Himzo_Polovina), [Safet Isović](/wiki/Safet_Isovi%C4%87), [Zaim Imamović](/wiki/Zaim_Imamovi%C4%87_(musician)), [Zehra Deović](/wiki/Zehra_Deovi%C4%87), [Halid Bešlić](/wiki/Halid_Be%C5%A1li%C4%87), [Hanka Paldum](/wiki/Hanka_Paldum), [Nada Mamula](/wiki/Nada_Mamula), [Meho Puzić](/wiki/Meho_Puzi%C4%87) and many more composed and wrote some of their most important pieces in the city.
Sarajevo also greatly influenced the pop scene of Yugoslavia with musicians like [Zdravko Čolić](/wiki/Zdravko_%C4%8Coli%C4%87), Kemal Monteno, [Dino Merlin](/wiki/Dino_Merlin), [Seid Memić Vajta](/wiki/Seid_Memi%C4%87_Vajta), [Hari Mata Hari](/wiki/Hari_Mata_Hari), [Mladen Vojičić Tifa](/wiki/Mladen_Voji%C4%8Di%C4%87_Tifa), [Željko Bebek](/wiki/%C5%BDeljko_Bebek) and many more.

Many newer Sarajevo-based bands have also found a name and established themselves in Sarajevo, such as [Regina](/wiki/Regina_(Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_band)) who also had two albums out in Yugoslavia, and Letu Štuke, who actually formed their band in Yugoslavia with the famous Bosnian-American writer [Aleksandar Hemon](/wiki/Aleksandar_Hemon) and got their real breakthrough later in the 2000s. Sarajevo is now home to an important and eclectic mix of new bands and independent musicians, which continue to thrive with the ever-increasing number of festivals, creative showcases, and concerts around the country. The city is also home to the region's largest jazz festival, the [Jazz Fest Sarajevo](/wiki/Jazz_Fest_Sarajevo).

American heavy metal band [Savatage](/wiki/Savatage), released a song entitled "[Christmas Eve (Sarajevo 12/24)](/wiki/Christmas_Eve/Sarajevo_12/24)" on their 1995 album *[Dead Winter Dead](/wiki/Dead_Winter_Dead)*, which was about a [cello](/wiki/Cello) player playing a forgotten [Christmas carol](/wiki/Christmas_carol) in war-torn Sarajevo. The song was later re-released by the same band under the name [Trans-Siberian Orchestra](/wiki/Trans-Siberian_Orchestra) on their 1996 debut album *[Christmas Eve and Other Stories](/wiki/Christmas_Eve_and_Other_Stories)*, which the song gave them instant success.

### Festivals

See also: [List of festivals in Sarajevo](/wiki/List_of_festivals_in_Sarajevo)

The [Sarajevo Film Festival](/wiki/Sarajevo_Film_Festival) has been held annually since 1995 at the [National Theatre](/wiki/Sarajevo_National_Theatre)

Sarajevo is internationally renowned for its eclectic and diverse selection of over 50 annual festivals. The [Sarajevo Film Festival](/wiki/Sarajevo_Film_Festival) was established in 1995 during the [Bosnian War](/wiki/Bosnian_War) and has become the premier and largest film festival in [Southeast Europe](/wiki/Southeast_Europe). It has been hosted at the [National Theater](/wiki/Sarajevo_National_Theatre), with screenings at the Open-air theater Metalac and the [Bosnian Cultural Center](/wiki/Bosnian_Cultural_Center), all in downtown Sarajevo. The [MESS International Festival](/wiki/MESS_(festival)) is an experimental theatre festival and the oldest living theatre festival in the Balkans. The annual [Sarajevo Youth Film Festival](/wiki/Sarajevo_Youth_Film_Festival) showcases feature, animated and short films from around the world and is the premier student film festival in the Balkans. The [Sarajevo Winter Festival](/wiki/Sarajevo_Winter_Festival), [Jazz Fest Sarajevo](/wiki/Jazz_Fest_Sarajevo) and Sarajevo International Music Festival are well-known, as is the [Baščaršija Nights](/wiki/Ba%C5%A1%C4%8Dar%C5%A1ija_Nights) festival, a month-long showcase of local culture, music, and dance.

The first incarnation of the Sarajevo Film Festival was hosted in still-warring Sarajevo in 1995, and has now progressed into being the biggest and most significant festival in Southeast Europe. A talent campus is also held during the duration of the festival, with lecturers speaking on behalf of world cinematography and holding workshops for film students from across Southeast Europe.

The [Jazz Fest Sarajevo](/wiki/Jazz_Fest_Sarajevo) is the region's largest and most diverse of its kind. The festival takes place at the [Bosnian Cultural Center](/wiki/Bosnian_Cultural_Center) (aka "Main Stage"), just down the street from the SFF, at the Sarajevo Youth Stage Theater (aka "Strange Fruits Stage"), at the [Dom Vojske Federacije](/wiki/Army_Hall_(Sarajevo)) (aka "Solo Stage"), and at the CDA (aka "Groove Stage").

### Sports

Bosnian [football](/wiki/Association_football) player [Edin Džeko](/wiki/Edin_D%C5%BEeko) was born in Sarajevo. He is the all-time [leading goalscorer](/wiki/List_of_international_goals_scored_by_Edin_D%C5%BEeko) and most capped player of the [Bosnia and Herzegovina national football team](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_national_football_team)

[Koševo City Stadium](/wiki/Ko%C5%A1evo_City_Stadium), home to [FK Sarajevo](/wiki/FK_Sarajevo), is the largest stadium in Bosnia and Herzegovina

Sarajevo hosted the [1984 Winter Olympics](/wiki/1984_Winter_Olympics). Yugoslavia won one medal, a silver in men's giant slalom awarded to [Jure Franko](/wiki/Jure_Franko). Many of the Olympic facilities survived the war or were reconstructed, including the [Zetra Olympic Hall](/wiki/Zetra_Olympic_Hall,_Sarajevo) and [Asim Ferhatović Stadium](/wiki/Ko%C5%A1evo_City_Stadium). In an attempt to bring back some of Sarajevo's Olympic glory, the original Olympic luge and bobsled tracks are being repaired, due to the efforts of both the [Olympic Committee of Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Olympic_Committee_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and local sports enthusiasts.

After co-hosting the Southeast Europe Friendship games, Sarajevo was awarded the 2009 [Special Olympic](/wiki/Special_Olympics) winter games, but canceled these plans. The ice arena for the 1984 Olympics, Zetra Stadium, was used during the war as a temporary hospital and, later, for housing [NATO](/wiki/NATO) troops of the [IFOR](/wiki/Implementation_Force).

In 2011, Sarajevo was the host city of the 51st World Military Skiing Championship with over 350 participants from 23 different nations. This was the first international event of such standing since the 1984 Olympics.
[Football](/wiki/Association_football) is popular in Sarajevo; the city hosts [FK Sarajevo](/wiki/FK_Sarajevo) and [FK Željezničar](/wiki/FK_%C5%BDeljezni%C4%8Dar_Sarajevo), which both compete in European and international cups and tournaments and have a very large trophy cabinet in the former Yugoslavia as well as independent Bosnia and Herzegovina. Other notable football clubs include [Olimpik](/wiki/FK_Olimpik), [SAŠK](/wiki/NK_SA%C5%A0K_Napredak) and [Slavija](/wiki/FK_Slavija_Sarajevo). [Grbavica Stadium](/wiki/Stadion_Grbavica), the home stadium of Željezničar, is one of the most notable sports venues in the country.

[Mirza Delibašić Hall](/wiki/Mirza_Deliba%C5%A1i%C4%87_Hall), home venue of past European champion [KK Bosna](/wiki/KK_Bosna_Royal)

Another popular sport is basketball; the basketball club [KK Bosna](/wiki/KK_Bosna_Royal) won the [European Championship](/wiki/Euroleague_Basketball) in 1979 as well as many Yugoslav and Bosnian national championships, making it one of the greatest basketball clubs in the former Yugoslavia. The chess club, [Bosna Sarajevo](/wiki/%C5%A0K_Bosna), has been a championship team since the 1980s and is the third-ranked chess club in Europe, having won four consecutive European championships in the nineties. Handball club [RK Bosna](/wiki/RK_Bosna_Sarajevo) also competes in the European Champions League and is considered one of the most well-organized handball clubs in Southeast Europe with a very large [fan base](/wiki/Fan_(person)) and excellent national, as well as international results.
Sarajevo often holds international events and competitions in sports such as [tennis](/wiki/Tennis) and [kickboxing](/wiki/Kickboxing).

The popularity of tennis has been picking up in recent years. Since 2003, [BH Telecom Indoors](/wiki/BH_Telecom_Indoors) has been an annual tennis tournament in Sarajevo.

Since 2007, the [Sarajevo Half Marathon](/wiki/Sarajevo_Half_Marathon) has been organized every year in late September. *Giro di Sarajevo* is also a run in the city with over 2,200 cyclists taking part in 2015.

In mountaineering, the local chapter of the [HPS](/wiki/Croatian_Mountaineering_Society) is *HPD "Bjelašnica"*, which had 216 members in 1936 under the Josip Fleger presidency. At the time, it had a ski section. Membership rose to 234 in 1937, and Ante Martinović was elected its president. Membership fell to 230 in 1938, with Josip Fleger again president.

In February 2019, Sarajevo and [East Sarajevo](/wiki/Isto%C4%8Dno_Sarajevo) hosted the [European Youth Olympic Winter Festival](/wiki/2019_European_Youth_Olympic_Winter_Festival) (EYOWF).
