# C'è la luna mezzo mare

## Infobox

- **Language:** Sicilian
- **English title:** Moon amid the sea
- **Released:** 1927 ( 1927 )
- **Recorded:** 1927
- **Genre:** Tarantella

"**Cc'è la luna n menzu ô mari**" ([Sicilian](/wiki/Sicilian_language) for 'There's the moon amid the sea'), mostly known in the English-speaking world as "**C'è la luna mezzo mare**", "**Luna mezz'o mare**" and other similar titles, is a comic [Sicilian song](/wiki/Music_of_Sicily) with worldwide popularity, traditionally styled as a brisk **6**
**8** [tarantella](/wiki/Tarantella). The song portrays a mother-daughter "coming of age" exchange consisting of various comic, and sometimes sexual, innuendos. It is frequently performed at [Italian-American](/wiki/Italian-American) wedding receptions and other festive occasions. Hit versions have included "**Oh! Ma-Ma! (The Butcher Boy)**" by [Rudy Vallée](/wiki/Rudy_Vall%C3%A9e) and "**Lazy Mary (Luna Mezzo Mare)**" by [Lou Monte](/wiki/Lou_Monte).

## Origin

Related music and lyrics appeared as early as 1835, in the art song "[La Danza](/wiki/La_Danza)" ([tarantella napoletana](/wiki/Tarantella_napoletana)) by [Gioachino Rossini](/wiki/Gioachino_Rossini) and [Carlo Pepoli](/wiki/Carlo_Pepoli). By 1871 in [Italy](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy), bawdier versions were circulating. In 1927, [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City)'s Italian Book Company arranged and recorded a version by Sicilian sailor Paolo Citorello (sometimes spelled Citarella), and an American court upheld their copyright in 1928.

## Popularity

Since the first recording in 1927, the song has proliferated with different titles and lyrics in English, Sicilian, and [Neapolitan](/wiki/Neapolitan_Language). Hit recordings in the [United States](/wiki/United_States) have included "Oh! Ma-Ma!" by Rudy Vallée (1938, peaked at No. 8) and "Lazy Mary" by Lou Monte (1958, peaked at No. 12). Monte's version was initially [banned from British broadcasts](/wiki/List_of_songs_banned_by_the_BBC) for undesirable innuendo, but has been played to a family-filled baseball stadium during the [seventh-inning stretch](/wiki/Seventh-inning_stretch) at almost every [New York Mets](/wiki/New_York_Mets) home game since the mid-1990s, as the result of a fan survey. The humorous lyrics center around a young woman wondering about marriage with various tradesmen (butcher, fisherman, fireman, etc.), ensuring the song's sustained popularity at Italian wedding receptions, including the opening scene of *[The Godfather](/wiki/The_Godfather_(film))* (1972). The song was also included in the 2010 videogame *[Mafia II](/wiki/Mafia_II)*, as part of the fictional radio station Empire Classic. In early 2022 the song was used as part of a popular [TikTok](/wiki/TikTok) trend where creators would use the song showing things in their home that "just make sense" while making a stereotypical Italian hand gesture to the beat of the song.

In the [sitcom](/wiki/Sitcom) *[Everybody Loves Raymond](/wiki/Everybody_Loves_Raymond)*, the cast sang the song in the episodes "Mia Famiglia" and "Italy".
