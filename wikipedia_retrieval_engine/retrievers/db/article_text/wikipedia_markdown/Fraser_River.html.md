The **Fraser River** ([/ˈfreɪzər/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English)) is the longest river within [British Columbia](/wiki/British_Columbia), Canada, rising at [Fraser Pass](/wiki/Fraser_Pass) near [Blackrock Mountain](/wiki/Blackrock_Mountain_(Canada)) in the [Rocky Mountains](/wiki/Rocky_Mountains) and flowing for 1,375 kilometres (854 mi), into the [Strait of Georgia](/wiki/Strait_of_Georgia) just south of the [City of Vancouver](/wiki/Vancouver). The river's annual discharge at its mouth is 112 cubic kilometres (27 mi3) or 3,550 cubic metres per second (125,000 cu ft/s), and each year, it discharges about 20 million tons of sediment into the ocean.

## Naming

The river is named after [Simon Fraser](/wiki/Simon_Fraser_(explorer)), who led an expedition in 1808 on behalf of the [North West Company](/wiki/North_West_Company) from the site of present-day [Prince George](/wiki/Prince_George,_British_Columbia) almost to the mouth of the river.
The river's name in the [Halqemeylem](/wiki/Halqemeylem) (Upriver Halkomelem) language is ***Sto:lo***, often seen archaically as **Staulo**, and has been adopted by the Halkomelem-speaking peoples of the Lower Mainland as their collective name, *[Sto:lo](/wiki/Sto:lo)*. The river's name in the [Dakelh language](/wiki/Dakelh_language) is ***Lhtakoh***. The [*Tsilhqot'in*](/wiki/Chilcotin_language) name for the river, not dissimilar to the *Dakelh* name, is ***ʔElhdaqox***, meaning [Sturgeon](/wiki/Sturgeon) *(*ʔElhda-chugh*)* River *(*Yeqox*)*.

## Course

Fraser River drainage basin

The Fraser drains a 220,000-square-kilometre (85,000 mi2) area. Its source is a dripping spring at [Fraser Pass](/wiki/Fraser_Pass) in the [Canadian Rocky Mountains](/wiki/Canadian_Rockies) near the border with [Alberta](/wiki/Alberta). The river then flows north to the [Yellowhead Highway](/wiki/Yellowhead_Highway) and west past [Mount Robson](/wiki/Mount_Robson) to the [Rocky Mountain Trench](/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_Trench) and the [Robson Valley](/wiki/Robson_Valley) near [Valemount](/wiki/Valemount). After running northwest past 54° north, it makes a sharp turn to the south at [Giscome Portage](/wiki/Giscome_Portage), meeting the [Nechako River](/wiki/Nechako_River) at the city of [Prince George](/wiki/Prince_George,_British_Columbia), then continues south, progressively cutting deeper into the [Fraser Plateau](/wiki/Fraser_Plateau) to form the [Fraser Canyon](/wiki/Fraser_Canyon) from roughly the confluence of the [Chilcotin River](/wiki/Chilcotin_River), near the city of [Williams Lake](/wiki/Williams_Lake,_British_Columbia), southwards. It is joined by the [Bridge](/wiki/Bridge_River) and [Seton Rivers](/wiki/Seton_River) at the town of [Lillooet](/wiki/Lillooet), then by the [Thompson River](/wiki/Thompson_River) at [Lytton](/wiki/Lytton,_British_Columbia), where it proceeds south until it is approximately 64 kilometres (40 mi) north of the [49th parallel](/wiki/49th_parallel_north), which is Canada's border with the [United States](/wiki/United_States).

From Lytton southwards it runs through a progressively deeper canyon between the [Lillooet Ranges](/wiki/Lillooet_Ranges) of the [Coast Mountains](/wiki/Coast_Mountains) on its west and the [Cascade Range](/wiki/Cascade_Range) on its east. [Hell's Gate](/wiki/Hell%27s_Gate,_British_Columbia), located immediately downstream of the town of [Boston Bar](/wiki/Boston_Bar,_British_Columbia), is a famous portion of the canyon where the walls narrow dramatically, forcing the entire volume of the river through a gap only 35 metres (115 feet) wide. An [aerial tramway](/wiki/Aerial_tramway) takes visitors out over the river. Hells Gate is visible from [Trans-Canada Highway 1](/wiki/British_Columbia_Highway_1) about 2 kilometres (1.2 mi) south of the tramway. Simon Fraser was forced to [portage](/wiki/Portage) the gorge on his trip through the canyon in June 1808.

At [Yale](/wiki/Yale,_British_Columbia), at the head of navigation on the river, the canyon opens up and the river widens, though without much adjoining lowland until [Hope](/wiki/Hope,_British_Columbia), where the river then turns west and southwest into the [Fraser Valley](/wiki/Fraser_Valley), a lush lowland valley, and runs past [Chilliwack](/wiki/Chilliwack) and the confluence of the [Harrison](/wiki/Harrison_River) and [Sumas Rivers](/wiki/Sumas_River), bending northwest at [Abbotsford](/wiki/Abbotsford,_British_Columbia) and [Mission](/wiki/Mission,_British_Columbia).

The Fraser then flows past [Maple Ridge](/wiki/Maple_Ridge,_British_Columbia), [Pitt Meadows](/wiki/Pitt_Meadows), [Port Coquitlam](/wiki/Port_Coquitlam), and north [Surrey](/wiki/Surrey,_British_Columbia). It turns southwest again just east of [New Westminster](/wiki/New_Westminster), where it splits into the North Arm, which is the southern boundary of the City of [Vancouver](/wiki/Vancouver), and the South Arm, which divides the City of [Richmond](/wiki/Richmond,_British_Columbia) from the [City of Delta](/wiki/Delta,_British_Columbia) to the south. Richmond is on the largest island in the Fraser, [Lulu Island](/wiki/Lulu_Island) and also on [Sea Island](/wiki/Sea_Island_(British_Columbia)), which is the location of [Vancouver International Airport](/wiki/Vancouver_International_Airport), where the Middle Arm branches off to the south from the North Arm. The far eastern end of Lulu Island is named [Queensborough](/wiki/Queensborough,_British_Columbia) and is part of the City of New Westminster. Also in the lowermost Fraser, among other smaller islands, is [Annacis Island](/wiki/Annacis_Island), an important industrial and port area, which lies to the southeast of the eastern end of Lulu Island. Other notable islands in the lower Fraser are [Barnston Island](/wiki/Barnston_Island_(British_Columbia)), [Matsqui Island](/wiki/Matsqui_Island), [Nicomen Island](/wiki/Nicomen_Island) and [Sea Bird Island](/wiki/Sea_Bird_Island_(British_Columbia)). Other islands lie on the outer side of the estuary, most notably [Westham Island](/wiki/Westham_Island), a wildfowl preserve, and [Iona Island](/wiki/Iona_Island_(British_Columbia)), the location of the main sewage plant for the City of Vancouver.

The Fraser's [delta](/wiki/River_delta) empties into the [Strait of Georgia](/wiki/Strait_of_Georgia) between the mainland and [Vancouver Island](/wiki/Vancouver_Island); the lands south of the City of [Vancouver](/wiki/Vancouver), including the cities of [Richmond](/wiki/Richmond,_British_Columbia) and [Delta](/wiki/Delta,_British_Columbia), sit on the flat [flood plain](/wiki/Flood_plain). The islands of the delta include Iona Island, Sea Island, Lulu Island, Annacis Island, and a number of smaller islands. While the vast majority of the river's [drainage basin](/wiki/Drainage_basin) lies within British Columbia, a small portion in the drainage basin lies across the international border in [Washington](/wiki/Washington_(state)) in the United States, namely the upper reaches of the tributary [Chilliwack](/wiki/Vedder_River) and Sumas rivers. Most of lowland [Whatcom County, Washington](/wiki/Whatcom_County,_Washington) is part of the [Fraser Lowland](/wiki/Fraser_Lowland) and was formed also by sediment deposited from the Fraser, though most of the county is not in the Fraser drainage basin.

Like the [Columbia River Gorge](/wiki/Columbia_River_Gorge) east of [Portland, Oregon](/wiki/Portland,_Oregon), the Fraser exploits a topographic cleft between two mountain ranges separating a more continental climate (in this case, that of the [British Columbia Interior](/wiki/British_Columbia_Interior)) from a milder climate near the coast. When an Arctic [high-pressure area](/wiki/High-pressure_area) moves into the [British Columbia](/wiki/British_Columbia) Interior and a relatively [low-pressure area](/wiki/Low-pressure_area) builds over the general [Puget Sound](/wiki/Puget_Sound) and Strait of Georgia region, the cold Arctic air accelerates southwest through the [Fraser Canyon](/wiki/Fraser_Canyon). These outflow winds can gust up to 97 to 129 kilometres per hour (60 to 80 mph) and have at times exceeded 160 kilometres per hour (100 mph). Such winds frequently reach [Bellingham](/wiki/Bellingham,_Washington) and the [San Juan Islands](/wiki/San_Juan_Islands), gaining strength over the open water of the [Strait of Juan de Fuca](/wiki/Strait_of_Juan_de_Fuca).

The estuary at the river's mouth is a site of hemispheric importance in the [Western Hemisphere Shorebird Reserve Network](/wiki/Western_Hemisphere_Shorebird_Reserve_Network).

### Discharge

The [Water Survey of Canada](/wiki/Water_Survey_of_Canada) currently operates 17 [gauge](/wiki/Stream_gauge) stations that measure [discharge](/wiki/Discharge_(hydrology)) and water level along the majority of the [mainstem](/wiki/Mainstem_(hydrology)) from Red Pass just downstream of [Moose Lake](/wiki/Moose_Lake_(British_Columbia)) in the [Mount Robson Provincial Park](/wiki/Mount_Robson_Provincial_Park), to [Steveston](/wiki/Steveston,_British_Columbia) in Vancouver at the river mouth. With an average flow at the mouth of about 3,475 cubic metres per second (122,700 cu ft/s), the Fraser is the largest river by discharge flowing into the Pacific seaboard of Canada and the fifth largest in the country. The average flow is highly seasonal; summer discharge rates can be ten times larger than the flow during the winter.

The Fraser's highest recorded flow, in June 1894, is estimated to have been 17,000 cubic metres per second (600,000 cu ft/s) at [Hope](/wiki/Hope,_British_Columbia). It was calculated using high-water marks near the hydrometric station at Hope and various statistical methods. In 1948 the Fraser River Board adopted the estimate for the 1894 flood. It remains the value specified by regulatory agencies for all flood control work on the river. Further studies and hydraulic models have estimated the maximum discharge of the Fraser River at Hope during the 1894 flood as within a range of about 16,000 to 18,000 cubic metres per second (570,000 to 640,000 cu ft/s).

## History

The [Maris Pacifici](/wiki/Maris_Pacifici) map published in 1589 features on the west coast of North America the first ever representation of two major coastline features: the delta of the Fraser River, labeled "*Baia de las isleas*", and the mouth of the [Columbia River](/wiki/Columbia_River), identified as "*Rio Grande*". These rivers would therefore have been sighted by visitors long before official records would confirm. In spite of limited evidence of the [Francis Drake expedition](/wiki/Francis_Drake%27s_circumnavigation)'s whereabouts in the [Pacific Northwest](/wiki/Pacific_Northwest), BC history commentator [Sam Bawlf](/wiki/Sam_Bawlf) posited that the [Ortelius](/wiki/Abraham_Ortelius) map was a proof that Drake sighted the mouth of the Fraser and came through the [Strait of Juan de Fuca](/wiki/Strait_of_Juan_de_Fuca).

*The Descent of the Fraser River, 1808*, by [C.W. Jefferys](/wiki/C.W._Jefferys)

Source of Fraser River at Fraser Pass

On June 14, 1792, the Spanish explorers [Dionisio Alcalá Galiano](/wiki/Dionisio_Alcal%C3%A1_Galiano) and [Cayetano Valdés](/wiki/Cayetano_Vald%C3%A9s_y_Flores) entered and anchored in the North Arm of the Fraser River, becoming the first Europeans to officially find and enter it. The existence of the river, but not its location, had been deduced during the 1791 voyage of [José María Narváez](/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Mar%C3%ADa_Narv%C3%A1ez), under [Francisco de Eliza](/wiki/Francisco_de_Eliza).

The upper reaches of the Fraser River were first explored by [Sir Alexander Mackenzie](/wiki/Sir_Alexander_Mackenzie) in 1793, and fully traced by [Simon Fraser](/wiki/Simon_Fraser_(explorer)) in 1808, who confirmed that it was not connected with the [Columbia River](/wiki/Columbia_River).

The lower Fraser was revisited in 1824 when the [Hudson's Bay Company](/wiki/Hudson%27s_Bay_Company) sent a crew across Puget Sound from its [Fort George](/wiki/Fort_Astoria) southern post on the [Columbia River](/wiki/Columbia_River). The expedition was led by [James McMillan](/wiki/James_McMillan_(fur_trader)). The Fraser was reached via the [Nicomekl River](/wiki/Nicomekl_River) and the [Salmon River](/wiki/Salmon_River_(Langley)) was reachable after a portage. McMillan's expedition reacquainted themselves with friendly tribes met earlier on by Simon Fraser's crew. A trading post with agricultural potential was to be located.

By 1827, a crew was sent back via the mouth of the Fraser to build and operate the original [Fort Langley](/wiki/Fort_Langley_National_Historic_Site). McMillan also led the undertaking.
The trading post original location would soon become the first ever mixed ancestry and agricultural settlement in southern [British Columbia](/wiki/British_Columbia) on the Fraser (Sto:lo) river.

In 1828, [George Simpson](/wiki/George_Simpson_(administrator)) visited the river, mainly to examine [Fort Langley](/wiki/Fort_Langley_National_Historic_Site) and determine whether it would be suitable as the [Hudson's Bay Company](/wiki/Hudson%27s_Bay_Company)'s main Pacific depot. Simpson had believed the Fraser River might be navigable throughout its length, even though Simon Fraser had described it as non-navigable. Simpson journeyed down the river and through the [Fraser Canyon](/wiki/Fraser_Canyon) and afterwards wrote "I should consider the passage down, to be certain Death, in nine attempts out of Ten. I shall therefore no longer talk about it as a navigable stream". His trip down the river convinced him that Fort Langley could not replace [Fort Vancouver](/wiki/Fort_Vancouver) as the company's main depot on the Pacific coast.

Much of British Columbia's history has been bound to the Fraser, partly because it was the essential route between the Interior and the Lower Coast after the loss of the lands south of the 49th Parallel with the [Oregon Treaty](/wiki/Oregon_Treaty) of 1846. It was the site of its first recorded settlements of Aboriginal people (the [Musqueam](/wiki/Musqueam), *[Sto:lo](/wiki/Sto:lo)*, [St'at'imc](/wiki/St%27at%27imc), [Secwepemc](/wiki/Secwepemc) and Nlaka'pamŭ), the site of the first European-Indigenous mixed ancestry settlement in southern British-Columbia ([Fort Langley](/wiki/Fort_Langley_National_Historic_Site)), the route of multitudes of prospectors during the [Fraser Canyon Gold Rush](/wiki/Fraser_Canyon_Gold_Rush) and the main vehicle of the province's early commerce and industry.

In 1998, the river was designated as a [Canadian Heritage River](/wiki/Canadian_Heritage_Rivers_System) for its natural and human heritage. It remains the longest river with that designation.

## Uses

The Fraser near the community of [Fountain](/wiki/Fountain,_British_Columbia)

The Fraser is heavily exploited by human activities, especially in its lower reaches. Its banks are rich farmland, its water is used by [pulp mills](/wiki/Pulp_mill), and a few dams on some tributaries provide [hydroelectric power](/wiki/Hydroelectric_power). The main flow of the Fraser has never been dammed partly because its high level of sediment flows would result in a short dam lifespan, but mostly because of strong opposition from fisheries and other environmental concerns. In 1858, the Fraser River and surrounding areas were occupied when the gold rush came to the Fraser Canyon and the Fraser River. It is also a popular fishing location for residents of the Lower Mainland.

The delta of the river, especially in the [Boundary Bay](/wiki/Boundary_Bay) area, is an important stopover location for [migrating](/wiki/Bird_migration) [shorebirds](/wiki/Shorebird).

The [Fraser Herald](/wiki/Fraser_Herald), a regional position within the [Canadian Heraldic Authority](/wiki/Canadian_Heraldic_Authority), is named after the river.

## Fishing

The Fraser River is known for the fishing of [white sturgeon](/wiki/White_sturgeon), all five species of Pacific salmon ([chinook](/wiki/Chinook_salmon), [coho](/wiki/Coho), [chum](/wiki/Chum_salmon), [pink](/wiki/Pink_salmon), [sockeye](/wiki/Sockeye_Salmon)), as well as [steelhead trout](/wiki/Steelhead_trout). The Fraser River is also the largest producer of salmon in Canada. A typical white sturgeon catch can average about 500 pounds (230 kg).
A white sturgeon weighing an estimated 500 kilograms (1,100 lb) and measuring 3.76 metres (12 ft 4 in) was caught and released on the Fraser River in July 2012. In 2021, a white sturgeon was caught on the river weighing 890 pounds (400 kg), with a length of 352 cm (11.55 ft). It was estimated to be over 100 years old. The fish was tagged and released.

## Flooding

The most significant Fraser river floods in recorded history occurred in 1894 and 1948.

### 1894 flood

Further information: [1894 Fraser River flood](/wiki/1894_Fraser_River_flood)

The Chilliwack flood of 1894

After European settlement, the first disastrous flood in the [Lower Mainland](/wiki/Lower_Mainland) ([Fraser Valley](/wiki/Fraser_Valley_Regional_District) and [Metro Vancouver](/wiki/Metro_Vancouver_Regional_District)) occurred in 1894. With no protection against the rising waters of the Fraser River, Fraser Valley and Metro Vancouver communities from Chilliwack downstream were inundated with water. In the 1894 floods, the water mark at Mission reached 7.85 metres (25.75 ft).

After the 1894 flood, a [dyking](/wiki/Levee) system was constructed throughout the Fraser Valley. The dyking and drainage projects greatly improved the flood problems, but over time, the dykes were allowed to fall into disrepair and became overgrown with brush and trees. With some dykes constructed of a wooden frame, they gave way in 1948 in several locations, marking the second disastrous flood. Flooding since 1948 has been minor in comparison.

### 1948 flood

Main article: [Fraser River flood of 1948](/wiki/Fraser_River_flood_of_1948)

1948 saw massive flooding in Chilliwack and other areas along the Fraser River. The high-water mark at Mission rose to 7.5 metres (24.7 ft). The peak flow was about 15,600 cubic meters per second.

#### Timeline

- On May 28, 1948, the Semiault Creek Dyke broke.
- On May 29, 1948, dykes near Glendale (now Cottonwood Corners) gave way and in four days, 49 square kilometres (12,000 acres) of fertile ground were under water.
- On June 1, 1948, the Cannor Dyke (east of Vedder Canal near Trans Canada Highway) broke and released tons of Fraser River water onto the Greendale area, destroying homes and fields.
- On June 3, 1948, the steamer *Gladys* supplied flood-stricken Chilliwack with tents and provisions as well as moving people and stock onto high ground.

#### Causes

Cool temperatures in March, April, and early May had delayed the melting of the heavy snowpack that had accumulated over the winter season. Several days of hot weather and warm rains over the holiday weekend in late May hastened the thawing of the snowpack. Rivers and streams quickly swelled with spring runoff, reaching heights surpassed only in 1894. Finally, the poorly maintained dyke systems failed to contain the water.

At the height of the 1948 flood, 200 square kilometres (50,000 acres) stood under water. Dykes broke at Agassiz, Chiliwack, Nicomen Island, Glen Valley and Matsqui. When the flood waters receded a month later, 16,000 people had been evacuated, with damages totaling $20 million, about $225 million in 2020 dollars.

### 1972 flood

Major flooding occurred once again in 1972 due to a significant [spring freshet](/wiki/Freshet), primarily impacting regions around [Prince George](/wiki/Prince_George,_British_Columbia), [Kamloops](/wiki/Kamloops), [Hope](/wiki/Hope,_British_Columbia) and [Surrey](/wiki/Surrey,_British_Columbia).

### 2007 flood

Due to record snowpacks on the mountains in the Fraser River catch basin which began melting, combined with heavy rainfall, water levels on the Fraser River rose in 2007 to a level not reached since 1972. Low-lying land in areas upriver such as [Prince George](/wiki/Prince_George,_British_Columbia) suffered minor flooding. Evacuation alerts were given for the low-lying areas not protected by [dikes](/wiki/Dike_(construction)) in the [Lower Mainland](/wiki/Lower_Mainland). However, the water levels did not breach the dikes, and major flooding was averted.

### 2021 flood

Main article: [November 2021 Pacific Northwest floods](/wiki/November_2021_Pacific_Northwest_floods)

Major flooding occurred in November 2021 as part of the [November 2021 Pacific Northwest floods](/wiki/November_2021_Pacific_Northwest_floods).
