# Thrash metal

## Infobox

- **Stylistic origins:** NWOBHM speed metal hardcore punk
- **Cultural origins:** Early 1980s United States Germany United Kingdom Latin America
- **Derivative forms:** Beatdown hardcore [ 1 ] black metal death metal groove metal tough guy hardcore [ 2 ]

**Thrash metal** (or simply **thrash**) is an [extreme](/wiki/Extreme_metal) subgenre of [heavy metal music](/wiki/Heavy_metal_music) characterized by its overall aggression and fast tempo. The songs usually use fast percussive beats and low-register guitar [riffs](/wiki/Riffs), overlaid with [shredding](/wiki/Shred_guitar)-style lead guitar work.

The genesis of the genre dates back to the 1970s when early heavy metal, [hard rock](/wiki/Hard_rock) and [punk rock](/wiki/Punk_rock) bands released songs that featured traits of what would eventually become thrash metal, [speed metal](/wiki/Speed_metal), or both. The thrash metal genre was established from the early-to-mid-1980s, during which musicians began to fuse the double bass drumming and complex guitar stylings of the [new wave of British heavy metal](/wiki/New_wave_of_British_heavy_metal) (NWOBHM) with the speed and aggression of [skate punk](/wiki/Skate_punk), [hardcore punk](/wiki/Hardcore_punk), and speed metal, and the technicality of [progressive rock](/wiki/Progressive_rock). Philosophically, thrash metal developed as a backlash against both the [conservatism](/wiki/Conservatism) of the [Reagan era](/wiki/Reagan_era) and the much more moderate, [pop](/wiki/Pop_music)-influenced, and widely accessible heavy metal subgenre of [glam metal](/wiki/Glam_metal) which also developed concurrently in the 1980s. Derived genres include [crossover thrash](/wiki/Crossover_thrash), a fusion of thrash metal and hardcore punk.

The early thrash metal movement revolved around independent record labels, including [Megaforce](/wiki/Megaforce_Records), [Metal Blade](/wiki/Metal_Blade_Records), [Combat](/wiki/Combat_Records), [Roadrunner](/wiki/Roadrunner_Records), and [Noise](/wiki/Noise_Records), and the underground [tape trading](/wiki/Tape_trading) industry in both Europe and North America. The genre was commercially successful from approximately 1985 through 1991, bringing prominence to [Metallica](/wiki/Metallica), [Slayer](/wiki/Slayer), [Megadeth](/wiki/Megadeth), and [Anthrax](/wiki/Anthrax_(American_band)), all grouped together as the "Big Four" of U.S. thrash metal. Other bands, such as [Overkill](/wiki/Overkill_(band)), [Metal Church](/wiki/Metal_Church), [Nuclear Assault](/wiki/Nuclear_Assault), [Flotsam and Jetsam](/wiki/Flotsam_and_Jetsam_(band)), and [Bay Area](/wiki/Bay_Area_thrash_metal) acts [Exodus](/wiki/Exodus_(band)), [Testament](/wiki/Testament_(band)) and [Death Angel](/wiki/Death_Angel), never achieved the same level of success as the "Big Four" but had also developed a strong following in the metal community, through [MTV](/wiki/MTV)'s *[Headbangers Ball](/wiki/Headbangers_Ball)* or otherwise. Some of the most popular international thrash metal bands from this era were Brazil's [Sepultura](/wiki/Sepultura), Canada's [Voivod](/wiki/Voivod_(band)) and [Annihilator](/wiki/Annihilator_(band)), Switzerland's [Coroner](/wiki/Coroner_(band)), England's [Onslaught](/wiki/Onslaught_(band)), and the genre's German "[Big Four](/wiki/Teutonic_thrash_metal)": [Kreator](/wiki/Kreator), [Destruction](/wiki/Destruction_(band)), [Sodom](/wiki/Sodom_(band)), and [Tankard](/wiki/Tankard_(band)).

The thrash metal genre had declined in popularity by the mid-1990s, due to the commercial success of numerous genres such as [alternative rock](/wiki/Alternative_rock) and [grunge](/wiki/Grunge). In response, some bands had either disbanded or moved away from their thrash metal roots and more towards influences such as punk (or crossover thrash), [progressive](/wiki/Progressive_metal), [groove metal](/wiki/Groove_metal), or [alternative metal](/wiki/Alternative_metal), and later [industrial](/wiki/Industrial_metal) or [nu metal](/wiki/Nu_metal). The genre has seen a resurgence in popularity since the late 1990s/early 2000s, with the arrival of various bands such as [Bonded by Blood](/wiki/Bonded_by_Blood_(band)), [Evile](/wiki/Evile), [Hatchet](/wiki/Hatchet_(band)), [Havok](/wiki/Havok_(band)), [Lamb of God](/wiki/Lamb_of_God_(band)), [Municipal Waste](/wiki/Municipal_Waste_(band)), and [Warbringer](/wiki/Warbringer), who have all been credited for leading the so-called "thrash metal revival" scene.

## Characteristics

[Kirk Hammett](/wiki/Kirk_Hammett) and [James Hetfield](/wiki/James_Hetfield) of [Metallica](/wiki/Metallica) (pictured in 2008). Metallica's early work is regarded as essential to the development of the thrash metal genre in the 1980s.

Thrash metal generally features fast [tempos](/wiki/Tempos), low-register, complex guitar [riffs](/wiki/Riffs), high-register guitar [solos](/wiki/Guitar_solo), and [double bass drumming](/wiki/Double_bass_drumming). The rhythm guitar parts are played with heavy distortion, [power chords](/wiki/Power_chords) and often [palm muted](/wiki/Palm_mute) to create a tighter and more precise sound. Vocally, thrash metal can employ anything from melodic singing to shouted or screamed vocals. Most guitar solos are played at high speed and technically demanding, as they are usually characterized by [shredding](/wiki/Shred_guitar), and use advanced techniques such as [sweep picking](/wiki/Sweep_picking), [legato phrasing](/wiki/Legato), [alternate picking](/wiki/Alternate_picking), [tremolo picking](/wiki/Tremolo_picking), [string skipping](/wiki/String_skipping), and [two-hand tapping](/wiki/Tapping).

[David Ellefson](/wiki/David_Ellefson), the original bassist of [Megadeth](/wiki/Megadeth), described thrash metal as "a combination of the attitude from punk rock but the riffs and complexities of traditional metal." On the origins of thrash metal, [Dan Lilker](/wiki/Dan_Lilker) (bassist and co-founding member of [Anthrax](/wiki/Anthrax_(American_band)), [S.O.D.](/wiki/Stormtroopers_of_Death) and [Nuclear Assault](/wiki/Nuclear_Assault)) recalls: "Thrash was just what they called 'faster hardcore,' because you literally thrashed around when you were either playing it or reacting to it. And thrash metal was born because it was influenced by thrash hardcore, and they just thought it was more metal, so they said, 'Okay, this is thrash metal.'"

New York band [Anthrax](/wiki/Anthrax_(American_band)) was among the earliest and most successful thrash acts.

The guitar riffs often use [chromatic scales](/wiki/Chromatic_scale) and emphasize the tritone and diminished intervals, instead of using conventional single-scale-based riffing. For example, the intro riff of [Metallica](/wiki/Metallica)'s "[Master of Puppets](/wiki/Master_of_Puppets_(song))" (the title track of the [namesake album](/wiki/Master_of_Puppets)) is a chromatic descent, followed by an ascent based on the [tritone](/wiki/Tritone).

Cymbal stops/[chokes](/wiki/Cymbal_choke) are often used to transition from one riff to another or to precede an acceleration in tempo.

To keep up with the other instruments, many bassists use a [plectrum](/wiki/Plectrum) (pick). However, some prominent thrash metal bassists have [used their fingers](/wiki/Fingerstyle_guitar), most notably [Frank Bello](/wiki/Frank_Bello), [Greg Christian](/wiki/Greg_Christian), [Steve Di Giorgio](/wiki/Steve_Di_Giorgio), [Robert Trujillo](/wiki/Robert_Trujillo), and [Cliff Burton](/wiki/Cliff_Burton). Several bassists use a [distorted bass tone](/wiki/Fuzz_bass), an approach popularized by Burton and [Motörhead](/wiki/Mot%C3%B6rhead)'s [Lemmy](/wiki/Lemmy). Lyrical themes in thrash metal include warfare, corruption, injustice, murder, suicide, isolation, alienation, addiction, and other maladies that afflict the individual and society. In addition, politics, particularly pessimism and dissatisfaction towards politics, are common themes among thrash metal bands. Humor and irony can occasionally be found (Anthrax for example), but they are limited, and are an exception rather than a rule.

## Etymology

The term "thrash" originated as a way of referring to hardcore punk, as shown on the 1982 hardcore compilation album *[New York Thrash](/wiki/New_York_Thrash)*. By 1983, the term "thrash metal" had entered colloquial use as a way to denote the fusion of hardcore and metal. The 1983 debut issue of *[Metal Forces](/wiki/Metal_Forces)* used the term to describe the bands on the first *[Metal Massacre](/wiki/Metal_Massacre)* and [Mike Varney](/wiki/Mike_Varney)'s first three *U.S. Metal* compilation albums. Later the same year, the thirteenth issue of *Metal Mania* used the term in an article, saying it was used synonymously with "heavy thrash" and "punk metal", going on to say acts in the genre included [Tank](/wiki/Tank_(band)), [Metallica](/wiki/Metallica), [MDC](/wiki/MDC_(band)), [GBH](/wiki/GBH_(band)), [Discharge](/wiki/Discharge_(band)) and [Crucifix](/wiki/Crucifix_(band)). The term is sometimes incorrectly credited to a 1984 *[Kerrang!](/wiki/Kerrang!)* magazine article by journalist [Malcolm Dome](/wiki/Malcolm_Dome), who used the term in reference to the song "Metal Thrashing Mad" by [Anthrax](/wiki/Anthrax_(American_band)).

Around 1984, the most dominant name for what is now defined as thrash metal was "power metal", a name which eventually evolved to refer to the separate [power metal](/wiki/Power_metal) genre. Similarly, for much of the 1980s, the names "thrash metal" and "speed metal" were generally used synonymously. The separate [speed metal](/wiki/Speed_metal) genre was defined retrospectively in the 1990s, to refer to 1980s bands who bridged the gap between thrash metal and power metal.

## History

### Roots (1970s–early 1980s)

[Venom](/wiki/Venom_(band))'s early work is considered a major influence on thrash metal.

The term "proto-thrash" has been used to describe bands as having elements of [speed metal](/wiki/Speed_metal) or thrash metal before those genres came to prominence in the early-to-mid-1980s. [Deep Purple](/wiki/Deep_Purple)'s 1970 album *[Deep Purple in Rock](/wiki/Deep_Purple_in_Rock)* is perhaps the earliest proto-thrash/speed metal album, as music journalist [Martin Popoff](/wiki/Martin_Popoff) noted, "It's really about the discipline and classical haughtiness of this record, as well as '[Hard Lovin' Man](/wiki/Hard_Lovin%27_Man)' which is a great contender for first proto-thrash song ever." [Queen](/wiki/Queen_(band))'s 1974 song "[Stone Cold Crazy](/wiki/Stone_Cold_Crazy)" and [Black Sabbath](/wiki/Black_Sabbath)'s "[Symptom of the Universe](/wiki/Symptom_of_the_Universe_(song))", released the following year, have also been cited as examples of proto-thrash/speed metal; the latter of which was a direct inspiration for [Diamond Head](/wiki/Diamond_Head_(English_band))'s pioneering song "[Am I Evil?](/wiki/Am_I_Evil%3F)". The [new wave of British heavy metal](/wiki/New_wave_of_British_heavy_metal) (NWOBHM) bands emerging from Britain in the late 1970s further influenced the development of early thrash. The early work of artists such as Diamond Head, [Iron Maiden](/wiki/Iron_Maiden), [Venom](/wiki/Venom_(band)), [Motörhead](/wiki/Mot%C3%B6rhead), [Tygers of Pan Tang](/wiki/Tygers_of_Pan_Tang), [Raven](/wiki/Raven_(British_band)), [Saxon](/wiki/Saxon_(band)) and [Angel Witch](/wiki/Angel_Witch), among others, introduced the fast-paced and intricate musicianship that became core aspects of thrash. [Phil Taylor](/wiki/Phil_Taylor_(musician))'s double-bass drumming featured in Motörhead's 1979 song "[Overkill](/wiki/Overkill_(Mot%C3%B6rhead_song))" has been acknowledged by many thrash drummers, most notably [Lars Ulrich](/wiki/Lars_Ulrich), as a primary influence on their playing. Thrash metal bands have also taken inspiration from [Judas Priest](/wiki/Judas_Priest), with [Slayer](/wiki/Slayer) guitarist [Kerry King](/wiki/Kerry_King) saying that, "There would be no Slayer without Priest." [Metal Blade Records](/wiki/Metal_Blade_Records) executive [Brian Slagel](/wiki/Brian_Slagel) played a key role in bringing the NWOBHM to a larger audience, as he was responsible for discovering both [Metallica](/wiki/Metallica) and Slayer and producing their earliest studio recordings.

Greg Prato of [Ultimate Guitar](/wiki/Ultimate_Guitar) notes, "Although the thrash movement seemed to have much more in common with punk than prog fashion-wise (leather jackets vs. capes), musically, there were certainly moments when thrash leaned more towards the [prog](/wiki/Progressive_rock) side of things." Canadian progressive rock band [Rush](/wiki/Rush_(band)), for example, has been cited a formative influence on the thrash metal movement. In a 2013 interview with *[Rolling Stone](/wiki/Rolling_Stone)*, Anthrax drummer [Charlie Benante](/wiki/Charlie_Benante) said: "When I was first learning to play drums, I would strap on my headphones, play along with [Rush's live album *[All the World's a Stage](/wiki/All_the_World%27s_a_Stage_(album))*] and be transformed. I remember talking with [Cliff](/wiki/Cliff_Burton) and [Kirk](/wiki/Kirk_Hammett) back when we first met, and we all agreed how much of an influence Rush was on all of us."

The thrash metal genre is also strongly influenced by [punk rock](/wiki/Punk_rock), drawing inspiration from sources ranging from traditional punk bands from the 1970s, including the [New York Dolls](/wiki/New_York_Dolls), the [Ramones](/wiki/Ramones), the [Sex Pistols](/wiki/Sex_Pistols) and the [Dead Boys](/wiki/Dead_Boys), to late 1970s/early 1980s [hardcore punk](/wiki/Hardcore_punk) bands [Discharge](/wiki/Discharge_(band)), [GBH](/wiki/GBH_(band)), [Black Flag](/wiki/Black_Flag_(band)), the [Misfits](/wiki/Misfits_(band)), the [Dead Kennedys](/wiki/Dead_Kennedys), and [Bad Brains](/wiki/Bad_Brains). The Ramones' 1976 [self-titled debut album](/wiki/Ramones_(album)) in particular has been noted as a key influence on the genre, due to its sound, which introduced the three-chord thrash style of guitar. [Void](/wiki/Void_(band)) has been credited as one of the earliest examples of hardcore/heavy metal crossover, whose chaotic musical approach is often cited as particularly influential. Their 1982 [split LP](/wiki/Faith_/_Void_Split) with fellow Washington band [The Faith](/wiki/The_Faith_(American_band)) showed both bands exhibiting quick, fiery, high-speed punk rock. It has been argued that those recordings laid the foundation for early thrash metal, at least in terms of selected tempos, and that thrash is essentially hardcore punk with the technical proficiency missing from that genre. The crossover with hardcore punk has also been cited as important influence on thrash, especially the English hardcore punk band Discharge, whose "influence on heavy metal is incalculable and metal superstars such as Metallica, Anthrax, [Machine Head](/wiki/Machine_Head_(band)), [Sepultura](/wiki/Sepultura), [Soulfly](/wiki/Soulfly), [Prong](/wiki/Prong_(band)) and [Arch Enemy](/wiki/Arch_Enemy_(band)) have covered Discharge's songs in tribute." The eponymous [debut](/wiki/Dirty_Rotten_LP) [albums](/wiki/Suicidal_Tendencies_(album)) by [D.R.I.](/wiki/Dirty_Rotten_Imbeciles) and [Suicidal Tendencies](/wiki/Suicidal_Tendencies), both released in 1983, have been credited for paving the way for [thrashcore](/wiki/Thrashcore).

In Europe, the earliest band of the emerging thrash movement was Venom from [Newcastle upon Tyne](/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne), formed in 1978. Their 1982 album *[Black Metal](/wiki/Black_Metal_(Venom_album))* has been cited as a major influence on many subsequent genres and bands in the extreme metal world, such as [Bathory](/wiki/Bathory_(band)), [Hellhammer](/wiki/Hellhammer), Slayer, and [Mayhem](/wiki/Mayhem_(band)). The European scene was almost exclusively influenced by the most aggressive music Germany and England were producing at the time. British bands such as [Tank](/wiki/Tank_(band)) and [Raven](/wiki/Raven_(British_band)), along with German bands [Accept](/wiki/Accept_(band)) (whose 1982 song "[Fast as a Shark](/wiki/Fast_as_a_Shark)" is often credited as one of the first-ever thrash/speed metal songs) and [Living Death](/wiki/Living_Death_(band)), motivated musicians from central Europe to start bands of their own, eventually producing groups such as [Sodom](/wiki/Sodom_(band)), [Kreator](/wiki/Kreator), and [Destruction](/wiki/Destruction_(band)) from Germany, as well as Switzerland's [Celtic Frost](/wiki/Celtic_Frost) (formed by two-thirds of [Hellhammer](/wiki/Hellhammer)), [Coroner](/wiki/Coroner_(band)) and Carrion (who later became [Poltergeist](/wiki/Poltergeist_(band))) and Denmark's [Artillery](/wiki/Artillery_(band)).

### Thrash metal in the 1980s

#### Birth and underground expansion (1980–1983)

Critics argue over who can be thought of as the first thrash metal band to exist. Most credit either British band [Venom](/wiki/Venom_(band)) or the genre's so-called "Big Four": [Metallica](/wiki/Metallica), [Slayer](/wiki/Slayer), [Anthrax](/wiki/Anthrax_(American_band)), and [Megadeth](/wiki/Megadeth), with commentators crediting Venom as the first thrash metal band and others tending to favor the "Big Four", though many give equal credit to all those five bands. The origins of thrash metal have also been traced to [San Francisco Bay Area](/wiki/San_Francisco_Bay_Area) band [Exodus](/wiki/Exodus_(band)), who formed in 1979 and is the fifth band in what is sometimes considered the "Big Five". The band released its first demo in 1982, simply titled *Demo 1982*, which was widely circulated in the tape trading community and is credited for paving the way for the 1980s [Bay Area thrash metal](/wiki/Bay_Area_thrash_metal) scene. A few commentators argue for other bands, including [Overkill](/wiki/Overkill_(band)) and [Metal Church](/wiki/Metal_Church) (both of whom formed in 1980), as the earliest bands to play thrash metal before releasing albums.

In 1981, [Los Angeles](/wiki/Los_Angeles) band [Leather Charm](/wiki/Leather_Charm) wrote a song entitled "Hit the Lights". Leather Charm soon disbanded and the band's primary songwriter, vocalist/rhythm guitarist [James Hetfield](/wiki/James_Hetfield), met drummer [Lars Ulrich](/wiki/Lars_Ulrich) through a classified advertisement. Together, Hetfield and Ulrich formed Metallica, one of the "Big Four" thrash bands, with lead guitarist [Dave Mustaine](/wiki/Dave_Mustaine), who would later form Megadeth, another of the "Big Four" originators of thrash, and bassist [Ron McGovney](/wiki/Ron_McGovney). McGovney would be replaced by Cliff Burton (formerly of [Trauma](/wiki/Trauma_(American_band))), and Mustaine was later replaced by [Kirk Hammett](/wiki/Kirk_Hammett) of the then-unsigned Exodus, and at Burton's insistence, the band relocated to the San Francisco Bay Area. Before Metallica had even settled on a definitive lineup, Metal Blade Records executive [Brian Slagel](/wiki/Brian_Slagel) asked Hetfield and Ulrich (credited as "Mettallica") to record "Hit the Lights" for the first edition of his *[Metal Massacre](/wiki/Metal_Massacre)* compilation in 1982. A re-recorded version of "Hit the Lights" would later open their first studio album, *[Kill 'Em All](/wiki/Kill_%27Em_All)*, released in July 1983. *Kill 'Em All* is widely regarded as the first thrash metal album, and "[Whiplash](/wiki/Whiplash_(Metallica_song))" from the album has been referred to as one of the first songs of the genre.

Another "Big Four" thrash band formed in Los Angeles in 1981, when guitarists [Jeff Hanneman](/wiki/Jeff_Hanneman) and [Kerry King](/wiki/Kerry_King) met while auditioning for the same band and subsequently decided to form a band of their own. Hanneman and King recruited vocalist/bassist [Tom Araya](/wiki/Tom_Araya) and drummer [Dave Lombardo](/wiki/Dave_Lombardo), and [Slayer](/wiki/Slayer) was formed. Slayer was discovered by Metal Blade Records executive Brian Slagel, and they contributed "Aggressive Perfector" to Slagel's *[Metal Massacre III](/wiki/Metal_Massacre_III)*. In December 1983, five months after the release of Metallica's debut *Kill 'Em All*, Slayer released their debut album, *[Show No Mercy](/wiki/Show_No_Mercy_(Slayer_album))*.

To the north, Canada produced influential thrash and [speed metal](/wiki/Speed_metal) bands such as [Annihilator](/wiki/Annihilator_(band)), [Anvil](/wiki/Anvil_(band)), [Exciter](/wiki/Exciter_(band)), [Razor](/wiki/Razor_(band)), [Sacrifice](/wiki/Sacrifice_(band)), and [Voivod](/wiki/Voivod_(band)).

#### Mainstream popularity (1984–1989)

##### First wave (1984–1986)

The popularity of thrash metal increased in 1984 with the release of Metallica's sophomore record *[Ride the Lightning](/wiki/Ride_the_Lightning)*, as well as Anthrax's debut *[Fistful of Metal](/wiki/Fistful_of_Metal)* and [Metal Church](/wiki/Metal_Church)'s [eponymous debut album](/wiki/Metal_Church_(album)). Slayer and [Overkill](/wiki/Overkill_(band)) released extended plays on independent labels during this era, *[Haunting the Chapel](/wiki/Haunting_the_Chapel)* and *[Overkill](/wiki/Overkill_(EP))* respectively. This led to a heavier-sounding form of thrash, which was reflected in Exodus' debut album *[Bonded by Blood](/wiki/Bonded_by_Blood)*, Slayer's *[Hell Awaits](/wiki/Hell_Awaits)* and Anthrax's *[Spreading the Disease](/wiki/Spreading_the_Disease)*, all three released in 1985. Several other debut albums were released that same year, including Megadeth's *[Killing Is My Business... and Business Is Good!](/wiki/Killing_Is_My_Business..._and_Business_Is_Good!)*, Overkill's *[Feel the Fire](/wiki/Feel_the_Fire_(Overkill_album))*, Kreator's *[Endless Pain](/wiki/Endless_Pain)*, Destruction's *[Infernal Overkill](/wiki/Infernal_Overkill)*, [Possessed](/wiki/Possessed_(band))'s *[Seven Churches](/wiki/Seven_Churches_(album))*, [Celtic Frost](/wiki/Celtic_Frost)'s *[To Mega Therion](/wiki/To_Mega_Therion_(album))*, [Watchtower](/wiki/Watchtower_(band))'s *[Energetic Disassembly](/wiki/Energetic_Disassembly)* and the [Sepultura](/wiki/Sepultura) EP *[Bestial Devastation](/wiki/Bestial_Devastation)*. *Seven Churches* and *To Mega Therion* are often credited for pioneering and popularizing the mid-1980s [extreme metal](/wiki/Extreme_metal) scene (as well as the then-developing genres of [death metal](/wiki/Death_metal) and [black metal](/wiki/Black_metal), respectively), while *Energetic Disassembly* has been cited as the first technical thrash metal album, and as "the recording most responsible for the development of the progressive metal genre".

Slayer released *[Reign in Blood](/wiki/Reign_in_Blood)* in 1986, considered a landmark achievement in the genre's history.

The year 1986 is regarded as the flagship year for thrash metal, as a number of critically acclaimed and genre-defining albums were released.
 Metallica's major label debut *[Master of Puppets](/wiki/Master_of_Puppets)* was released in March, becoming the first thrash album to be certified platinum, being certified 6× platinum by the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA); it would be the band's last album to feature bassist [Cliff Burton](/wiki/Cliff_Burton), who was killed in a bus accident six months after its release. Kreator released *[Pleasure to Kill](/wiki/Pleasure_to_Kill)* in April 1986, which would later be a major influence on the death metal scene. Megadeth released *[Peace Sells... but Who's Buying?](/wiki/Peace_Sells..._but_Who%27s_Buying%3F)* in September, an album which proved to be the band's commercial and critical breakthrough and which [AllMusic](/wiki/AllMusic) later cited as "a classic of early thrash". Slayer, regarded as one of the most sinister thrash metal bands of the early 1980s, released *[Reign in Blood](/wiki/Reign_in_Blood)* in October, an album considered by some to have single-handedly inspired the death metal genre. Also in October, [Nuclear Assault](/wiki/Nuclear_Assault) released their debut album *[Game Over](/wiki/Game_Over_(Nuclear_Assault_album))*, followed a month later by [Dark Angel](/wiki/Dark_Angel_(band))'s *[Darkness Descends](/wiki/Darkness_Descends)*, which marked the debut of drummer [Gene Hoglan](/wiki/Gene_Hoglan). [Flotsam and Jetsam](/wiki/Flotsam_and_Jetsam_(band))'s debut album *[Doomsday for the Deceiver](/wiki/Doomsday_for_the_Deceiver)* (released on the [Fourth of July](/wiki/Independence_Day_(United_States)) in 1986) received some attention as well, due to the album being "the first of only a handful" to ever receive a 6K rating from *[Kerrang!](/wiki/Kerrang!)* magazine, and it is also notable for featuring a then-unknown [Jason Newsted](/wiki/Jason_Newsted), who, not long after the album's release, joined Metallica as Burton's replacement.

Also during the mid-to-late 1980s, bands such as [Suicidal Tendencies](/wiki/Suicidal_Tendencies), [D.R.I.](/wiki/Dirty_Rotten_Imbeciles), [S.O.D.](/wiki/Stormtroopers_of_Death) (who featured three-fifths of Anthrax), and [Corrosion of Conformity](/wiki/Corrosion_of_Conformity) paved the way to what became known as [crossover thrash](/wiki/Crossover_thrash), a fusion genre that lies on a continuum between heavy metal and hardcore punk, and is arguably faster and more aggressive than thrash metal.

##### Second wave (1987–1989)

[Testament](/wiki/Testament_(band)) was one of the most successful [Bay Area thrash metal](/wiki/Bay_Area_thrash_metal) bands of the late 1980s.

By the mid-to-late 1980s, thrash metal began to achieve major mainstream success worldwide, with many bands of the genre receiving heavy rotation on [MTV](/wiki/MTV)'s *[Headbangers Ball](/wiki/Headbangers_Ball)*, and radio stations such as [KNAC](/wiki/KBUE#KNAC) in [Long Beach](/wiki/Long_Beach,_California) and [Z Rock](/wiki/Z_Rock) in [Dallas](/wiki/Dallas), as well as coverage on numerous publications, including *[Kerrang!](/wiki/Kerrang!)* and *[RIP Magazine](/wiki/Larry_Flynt_Publications)*. These outlets not only played a major role in the crossover success of thrash metal during this time, but helped push album sales of the genre's "Big Four" and similar bands or moved them from playing clubs to arenas and stadiums.

Anthrax made its mainstream breakthrough in 1987 with the release of their gold-certified album *[Among the Living](/wiki/Among_the_Living)*, which borrowed elements from their two previous releases, with fast guitar riffs and pounding drums. Shortly after the release of *Among the Living*, three Bay Area bands, [Testament](/wiki/Testament_(band)), [Death Angel](/wiki/Death_Angel) and [Heathen](/wiki/Heathen_(band)), respectively released their debut albums *[The Legacy](/wiki/The_Legacy_(album))*, *[The Ultra-Violence](/wiki/The_Ultra-Violence)* and *[Breaking the Silence](/wiki/Breaking_the_Silence_(album))*. All of the "Big Four" of [Teutonic thrash metal](/wiki/Teutonic_thrash_metal) also released albums in 1987: Kreator's *[Terrible Certainty](/wiki/Terrible_Certainty)*, Destruction's *[Release from Agony](/wiki/Release_from_Agony)*, Sodom's *[Persecution Mania](/wiki/Persecution_Mania)* and Tankard's *[Chemical Invasion](/wiki/Chemical_Invasion)*; those albums cemented their reputations as top-tier German thrash metal bands.

In response to thrash metal's growing popularity during this period, several hardcore punk bands began changing their style to a more heavier direction, including [Venice, Los Angeles](/wiki/Venice,_Los_Angeles) act [Suicidal Tendencies](/wiki/Suicidal_Tendencies), who are often considered to be one of the "fathers of crossover thrash", and became more recognized as a thrash metal band in the late 1980s (thanks in large part to the presence of guitarists [Rocky George](/wiki/Rocky_George) and [Mike Clark](/wiki/Mike_Clark_(guitarist))); the band would reach new heights of success with their first two major-label albums, *[How Will I Laugh Tomorrow When I Can't Even Smile Today](/wiki/How_Will_I_Laugh_Tomorrow_When_I_Can%27t_Even_Smile_Today)* (1988) and *[Controlled by Hatred/Feel Like Shit... Déjà Vu](/wiki/Controlled_by_Hatred/Feel_Like_Shit..._D%C3%A9j%C3%A0_Vu)* (1989). [D.R.I.](/wiki/Dirty_Rotten_Imbeciles)'s music took a similar direction with their last three albums of the 1980s, *[Crossover](/wiki/Crossover_(Dirty_Rotten_Imbeciles_album))* (1987), *[4 of a Kind](/wiki/4_of_a_Kind)* (1988), and *[Thrash Zone](/wiki/Thrash_Zone)* (1989). This trend was expanded by many bands that came from a punk background, including UK acts [Discharge](/wiki/Discharge_(band)) and [The Exploited](/wiki/The_Exploited), Los Angeles bands [Wasted Youth](/wiki/Wasted_Youth_(American_band)), [Cryptic Slaughter](/wiki/Cryptic_Slaughter), [Excel](/wiki/Excel_(band)), [Beowülf](/wiki/Beow%C3%BClf) and [No Mercy](/wiki/No_Mercy_(metal_band)), and [New York hardcore](/wiki/New_York_hardcore) acts [Agnostic Front](/wiki/Agnostic_Front), [Murphy's Law](/wiki/Murphy%27s_Law_(band)), the [Cro-Mags](/wiki/Cro-Mags), [M.O.D.](/wiki/M.O.D.) (fronted by former S.O.D. singer [Billy Milano](/wiki/Billy_Milano)), the [Crumbsuckers](/wiki/Crumbsuckers), [Ludichrist](/wiki/Ludichrist), [Leeway](/wiki/Leeway_(band)), and [Prong](/wiki/Prong_(band)).

From 1987 to 1989, Overkill released three albums often regarded as the peak of the band's classic period: *[Taking Over](/wiki/Taking_Over_(Overkill_album))* refined their early speed-driven aggression, *[Under the Influence](/wiki/Under_the_Influence_(Overkill_album))* presented a more concise and controlled approach, and *[The Years of Decay](/wiki/The_Years_of_Decay)* introduced a darker, more expansive, and more ambitious and progressive sound. Each of the "Big Four" of thrash metal also issued a major release in 1988, though the results pointed in very different directions after the genre-defining breakthroughs of 1986 and 1987. Slayer's *[South of Heaven](/wiki/South_of_Heaven)* was a striking turn away from the unrelenting velocity of *Reign in Blood*, favoring slower, darker, and more melodic songwriting and singing. Megadeth's *[So Far, So Good... So What!](/wiki/So_Far,_So_Good..._So_What!)* was a more uneven record, but it still pushed the band toward a broader and more eclectic style. Anthrax's *[State of Euphoria](/wiki/State_of_Euphoria)* is often seen as one of the weaker entries in their classic period, lacking the consistency of their previous work, though it did produce the popular cover of "Antisocial". Metallica's *[...And Justice for All](/wiki/...And_Justice_for_All_(album))*, meanwhile, intensified the band's increasingly technical and progressive tendencies, expanding song structures and rhythmic complexity in a way they would later scale back, even as the album spawned their first video and Top 40 hit with the [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I)-themed song "[One](/wiki/One_(Metallica_song))". That same year, Metallica joined [Van Halen](/wiki/Van_Halen), [Scorpions](/wiki/Scorpions_(band)), [Dokken](/wiki/Dokken) and [Kingdom Come](/wiki/Kingdom_Come_(German_band)) on the two-month-long arena and stadium tour [Monsters of Rock](/wiki/Monsters_of_Rock) in North America. In the spring of 1989, Anthrax teamed up with Exodus and [Helloween](/wiki/Helloween) on a [US arena tour](/wiki/Headbangers_Ball_Tour) sponsored by *Headbangers Ball*.

Sepultura's third album, *[Beneath the Remains](/wiki/Beneath_the_Remains)* (1989), earned them some mainstream appeal as it was released by [Roadrunner Records](/wiki/Roadrunner_Records). Testament's second and third albums, *[The New Order](/wiki/The_New_Order_(album))* (1988) and *[Practice What You Preach](/wiki/Practice_What_You_Preach)* (1989), nearly gained them the same level of popularity as the "Big Four", while [Exodus](/wiki/Exodus_(band))' third album *[Fabulous Disaster](/wiki/Fabulous_Disaster)* (1989) garnered the band their first music video and one of their most recognized songs, the [mosh-pit](/wiki/Moshing) anthem "[The Toxic Waltz](/wiki/The_Toxic_Waltz_(song))". [Vio-lence](/wiki/Vio-lence), [Forbidden](/wiki/Forbidden_(band)), and [Sadus](/wiki/Sadus), three relative latecomers to the Bay Area thrash metal scene, released their debut albums *[Eternal Nightmare](/wiki/Eternal_Nightmare_(Vio-lence_album))*, *[Forbidden Evil](/wiki/Forbidden_Evil_(album)),* and *[Illusions](/wiki/Illusions_(Sadus_album))*, respectively, in 1988; the latter album demonstrated a sound that was primarily driven by the fretless bass of [Steve Di Giorgio](/wiki/Steve_Di_Giorgio). Also in 1988, [Blind Illusion](/wiki/Blind_Illusion) released its only studio album for more than two decades, *[The Sane Asylum](/wiki/The_Sane_Asylum)*, which received some particular attention as it was produced by [Kirk Hammett](/wiki/Kirk_Hammett), and is also notable for featuring bassist [Les Claypool](/wiki/Les_Claypool) and former Possessed guitarist [Larry LaLonde](/wiki/Larry_LaLonde); after its release, the two would later team up together in Claypool's then-upcoming band [Primus](/wiki/Primus_(band)).

Canadian thrashers [Annihilator](/wiki/Annihilator_(band)) released their highly technical debut *[Alice in Hell](/wiki/Alice_in_Hell)* in 1989, which was praised for its fast riffs and extended guitar solos. The popularity of the German thrash metal scene was expanded that year with albums that included Sodom's *[Agent Orange](/wiki/Agent_Orange_(album))*, Kreator's *[Extreme Aggression](/wiki/Extreme_Aggression)*, [Holy Moses](/wiki/Holy_Moses)' *[The New Machine of Liechtenstein](/wiki/The_New_Machine_of_Liechtenstein)* and [Paradox](/wiki/Paradox_(German_band))'s *[Heresy](/wiki/Heresy_(Paradox_album))*. Several highly acclaimed albums associated with the subgenre of technical thrash metal were also released in 1989, including Coroner's *[No More Color](/wiki/No_More_Color)*, Dark Angel's *[Leave Scars](/wiki/Leave_Scars)*, [Toxik](/wiki/Toxik)'s *[Think This](/wiki/Think_This),* and [Watchtower](/wiki/Watchtower_(band))'s *[Control and Resistance](/wiki/Control_and_Resistance)*, which has been recognized and acknowledged as one of the cornerstones of [jazz-metal fusion](/wiki/Jazz_fusion) and a major influence on the [technical death metal](/wiki/Technical_death_metal) genre, while [Forced Entry](/wiki/Forced_Entry_(band))'s debut album *[Uncertain Future](/wiki/Uncertain_Future)* helped pioneer the late 1980s [Seattle music](/wiki/Music_of_Seattle) scene.

### Thrash metal in the 1990s

#### Continued popularity (1990–1991)

Two of the "Big Four" of thrash metal, [Megadeth](/wiki/Megadeth) and [Slayer](/wiki/Slayer) (both pictured above), released the most successful albums of their careers up to this point (*[Rust in Peace](/wiki/Rust_in_Peace)* and *[Seasons in the Abyss](/wiki/Seasons_in_the_Abyss)*, respectively), while *[Cowboys from Hell](/wiki/Cowboys_from_Hell)* by [Pantera](/wiki/Pantera) (bottom left) and *[Arise](/wiki/Arise_(Sepultura_album))* by [Sepultura](/wiki/Sepultura) (bottom right) are credited for ushering in the styles of [groove metal](/wiki/Groove_metal) and [deathrash](/wiki/Deathrash), respectively.

A number of more typical but technically sophisticated albums were released in 1990, including Megadeth's *[Rust in Peace](/wiki/Rust_in_Peace)*, Anthrax's *[Persistence of Time](/wiki/Persistence_of_Time)*, Slayer's *[Seasons in the Abyss](/wiki/Seasons_in_the_Abyss)*, Suicidal Tendencies' *[Lights...Camera...Revolution!](/wiki/Lights...Camera...Revolution!)*, Testament's *[Souls of Black](/wiki/Souls_of_Black)*, Kreator's *[Coma of Souls](/wiki/Coma_of_Souls)*, Destruction's *[Cracked Brain](/wiki/Cracked_Brain)*, Forbidden's *[Twisted into Form](/wiki/Twisted_into_Form)*, Exodus' *[Impact Is Imminent](/wiki/Impact_Is_Imminent)*, Death Angel's *[Act III](/wiki/Act_III_(Death_Angel_album))*, [Sanctuary](/wiki/Sanctuary_(band))'s *[Into the Mirror Black](/wiki/Into_the_Mirror_Black)*, Annihilator's *[Never, Neverland](/wiki/Never,_Neverland)* and [Sacred Reich](/wiki/Sacred_Reich)'s *[The American Way](/wiki/The_American_Way_(album))*. At the same time, some albums from this wave, though somewhat distinct in style, have also been credited with helping define the then-developing [groove metal](/wiki/Groove_metal) genre, most notably Prong's *[Beg to Differ](/wiki/Beg_to_Differ)*, [Pantera](/wiki/Pantera)'s *[Cowboys from Hell](/wiki/Cowboys_from_Hell)* and [Exhorder](/wiki/Exhorder)'s *[Slaughter in the Vatican](/wiki/Slaughter_in_the_Vatican)*. All of those albums were commercial high points for the aforementioned artists. During this period, Megadeth and Slayer co-headlined one of the most successful tours in thrash metal history called the [Clash of the Titans](/wiki/Clash_of_the_Titans_(tour)); the first leg in Europe included support from Testament and Suicidal Tendencies, while the second leg in North America had Anthrax and then-emerging Seattle band [Alice in Chains](/wiki/Alice_in_Chains), who were the supporting act.

Several albums, some of which had come to be known as technical thrash metal, were released in 1991, including Overkill's *[Horrorscope](/wiki/Horrorscope_(Overkill_album))*, [Heathen](/wiki/Heathen_(band))'s *[Victims of Deception](/wiki/Victims_of_Deception)*, [Dark Angel](/wiki/Dark_Angel_(band))'s *[Time Does Not Heal](/wiki/Time_Does_Not_Heal)*, Sepultura's *[Arise](/wiki/Arise_(Sepultura_album))*, Coroner's *[Mental Vortex](/wiki/Mental_Vortex)*, Prong's *[Prove You Wrong](/wiki/Prove_You_Wrong)*, Forced Entry's *[As Above, So Below](/wiki/As_Above,_So_Below_(Forced_Entry_album))* and [Wrathchild America](/wiki/Wrathchild_America)'s *[3-D](/wiki/3-D_(Wrathchild_America_album))*.

#### Decline (1991–1999)

The era of 1991–1992 marked the beginning of the end of thrash metal's commercial peak, due to the rising popularity of the [alternative metal](/wiki/Alternative_metal) and [grunge](/wiki/Grunge) movements (the latter spearheaded by [Washington](/wiki/Washington_(state))-based bands [Nirvana](/wiki/Nirvana_(band)), [Soundgarden](/wiki/Soundgarden), [Alice in Chains](/wiki/Alice_in_Chains) and [Pearl Jam](/wiki/Pearl_Jam)). In response to this climate change, many thrash metal bands that had emerged from the previous decade had called it quits or went on hiatus during the 1990s, while half of the "Big Four" and other veteran bands began changing to more accessible, radio-friendly styles.

[Metallica](/wiki/Metallica), who achieved its number one debut on the [*Billboard* 200](/wiki/Billboard_200) with their [self-titled *Black Album*](/wiki/Metallica_(album)), was one of the first "Big Four" bands to tone down their music to make it more accessible to a mainstream audience.

Metallica took the first major step in this stylistic shift with the release in 1991 of their new album [eponymous fifth studio album](/wiki/Metallica_(album)), known as *The Black Album*. Produced by [Bob Rock](/wiki/Bob_Rock) (who was then known for working with the likes of [Mötley Crüe](/wiki/M%C3%B6tley_Cr%C3%BCe), [Aerosmith](/wiki/Aerosmith), [Bon Jovi](/wiki/Bon_Jovi) and [The Cult](/wiki/The_Cult)), the album marked a stylistic change in the band, eliminating much of the speed and longer song structures of the band's previous work, and instead focusing on more concise and heavier songs. The album was a change in Metallica's direction from the thrash metal style of the band's previous four studio albums towards a more contemporary heavy metal sound with original [hard rock](/wiki/Hard_rock) elements, but still had remnant characteristics of thrash metal. *Metallica* would go on to become the band's best-selling album and began a wave of thrash metal bands releasing more garage-oriented albums, or else more experimental ones.

Megadeth also moved toward a more accessible heavy metal sound beginning with their 1992 album *[Countdown to Extinction](/wiki/Countdown_to_Extinction)*. Testament, Exodus and Flotsam and Jetsam all took a melodic/progressive approach with the albums *[The Ritual](/wiki/The_Ritual_(Testament_album))*, *[Force of Habit](/wiki/Force_of_Habit)*, and *[Cuatro](/wiki/Cuatro_(Flotsam_and_Jetsam_album))*, respectively. One of the pioneers of crossover thrash, [Corrosion of Conformity](/wiki/Corrosion_of_Conformity), began changing their sound into a slower and [Black Sabbath](/wiki/Black_Sabbath)-influenced heavy metal direction with their post-1980s output, adapting influences and textures of [sludge](/wiki/Sludge_metal), [doom metal](/wiki/Doom_metal), blues, and southern rock on several of their albums, including *[Blind](/wiki/Blind_(Corrosion_of_Conformity_album))* (1991), *[Deliverance](/wiki/Deliverance_(Corrosion_of_Conformity_album))* (1994) and *[Wiseblood](/wiki/Wiseblood_(Corrosion_of_Conformity_album))* (1996).

The remainder of the 1990s saw many thrash metal bands expand their sound by adding elements and influences from the groove metal genre, which was then popularized by Pantera, [White Zombie](/wiki/White_Zombie_(band)) and [Machine Head](/wiki/Machine_Head_(band)). Anthrax, who had recently replaced [Joey Belladonna](/wiki/Joey_Belladonna) with [John Bush](/wiki/John_Bush_(musician)) as their singer, began stepping away from their previously established thrash metal formula to this more accessible alternative/groove metal approach for the remainder of their 1990s output, starting with and including *[Sound of White Noise](/wiki/Sound_of_White_Noise)* (1993). Sacred Reich, Overkill, Coroner, Prong, Testament, and Forbidden followed this trend with their respective albums *[Independent](/wiki/Independent_(Sacred_Reich_album))*, *[I Hear Black](/wiki/I_Hear_Black)*, *[Grin](/wiki/Grin_(Coroner_album))*, *[Cleansing](/wiki/Cleansing_(album))*, *[Low](/wiki/Low_(Testament_album))*, and *[Distortion](/wiki/Distortion_(Forbidden_album))*, in some cases featuring industrial, alternative and even slight death metal elements. Sepultura's 1993 album *[Chaos A.D.](/wiki/Chaos_A.D.)* also marked the beginning of their transition away from death/thrash metal to groove metal which had influenced then-up-and-coming bands like [Korn](/wiki/Korn), who reciprocally became the inspiration behind the [nu metal](/wiki/Nu_metal) style of the band's next album *[Roots](/wiki/Roots_(Sepultura_album))* (1996). *Roots* would influence a generation of bands from [Linkin Park](/wiki/Linkin_Park) to [Slipknot](/wiki/Slipknot_(band)), which during the 1990s meant the replacement of death, thrash, and speed, by nu metal and metalcore as popular epicenters of the hardest metal scene.

Megadeth moved further away from thrash on *[Youthanasia](/wiki/Youthanasia)* in 1994. While its predecessor still retained some noticeable thrash elements, this album placed greater emphasis on mid-tempo heaviness, more concise songwriting, and a more soft and polished melodic approach. Slayer did not radically alter its style on their sixth studio album *[Divine Intervention](/wiki/Divine_Intervention_(Slayer_album))* (1994), but the album marked a rougher and less cohesive phase, combining the band's established speed with a harsher, more grinding sound with groove and hardcore punk elements. On their eighth studio album *[Diabolus in Musica](/wiki/Diabolus_in_Musica)* (1998), Slayer departed more clearly from its earlier formula, emphasizing lower tunings, thicker grooves, and a sound often associated with the late-1990s metal scene.

Metallica then took a second major turn in their stylistic evolution with *[Load](/wiki/Load_(album))* in 1996 and *[ReLoad](/wiki/Reload_(Metallica_album))* in 1997. Rather than returning to thrash, the band moved further into a slower, more grunge-oriented hard rock and alternative sound, drawing on [bluesy](/wiki/Blues_rock) and [southern rock](/wiki/Southern_rock) textures with [country](/wiki/Country_rock) elements, while keeping some of the weight and polish that had made them so commercially powerful. At the same time, many listeners saw them as uneven records, since some of the material lacked the focus, intensity, and distinctive drive of the band’s earlier work, making the shift feel both ambitious and, in part, artistically diluted.

Staying away from this new commercial mainstream of groove metal, alternative metal, metalcore, and especially nu metal, the [second wave of black metal](/wiki/Black_metal#Second_wave) emerged as an opposed underground music scene, initially in [Norway](/wiki/Norway). This crop of new bands differenced themselves from the "first wave" by totally distilling black metal from the combined origins with thrash metal, but they preserved from all these sub-genres the emphasis on atmosphere over rhythm.

As further [extreme metal](/wiki/Extreme_metal) genres came to prominence in the 1990s ([industrial metal](/wiki/Industrial_metal), death metal, and [black metal](/wiki/Black_metal) each finding their own fanbase), the heavy metal "family tree" soon found itself blending aesthetics and styles. For example, bands with all the musical traits of thrash metal began using [death growls](/wiki/Death_growls), a vocal style borrowed from death metal, while black metal bands often utilized the airy feel of [synthesizers](/wiki/Synthesizers), popularized in industrial metal. Today the placing of bands within distinct sub-genres remains a source of contention for heavy metal fans, however, little debate resides over the fact that thrash metal is the sole proprietor of its respective spin-offs.

### Revivals (1999–present)

The resurgence of interest in the thrash metal genre was widely attributed to Testament's 1999 album *[The Gathering](/wiki/The_Gathering_(Testament_album))*. It is significant for not only its lineup (with frontman [Chuck Billy](/wiki/Chuck_Billy) and guitarist [Eric Peterson](/wiki/Eric_Peterson_(musician)) joined by original Slayer drummer [Dave Lombardo](/wiki/Dave_Lombardo), former [Death](/wiki/Death_(metal_band)), [Obituary](/wiki/Obituary_(band)) and [Cancer](/wiki/Cancer_(band)) guitarist [James Murphy](/wiki/James_Murphy_(guitarist)), and [Sadus](/wiki/Sadus) and former Death bassist [Steve Di Giorgio](/wiki/Steve_Di_Giorgio)), but for the return to the band's classic thrash metal sound while the album retained some of the death metal elements of its predecessor *[Demonic](/wiki/Demonic_(Testament_album))*. This resurgence coincided with other veteran bands returning to their classic sound of thrash on their respective albums, including Annihilator's *[Criteria for a Black Widow](/wiki/Criteria_for_a_Black_Widow)* (1999), Artillery's *[B.A.C.K.](/wiki/B.A.C.K.)* (1999), Metal Church's *[Masterpeace](/wiki/Masterpeace)* (1999), Sodom's *[Code Red](/wiki/Code_Red_(Sodom_album))* (1999), and Destruction's *[All Hell Breaks Loose](/wiki/All_Hell_Breaks_Loose_(Destruction_album))* (2000).

The resurgence of thrash metal in the late 1990s and early 2000s was serious enough that it allowed many bands from the genre's heyday an opportunity to reform. The [Thrash of the Titans](/wiki/Thrash_of_the_Titans) festival, which was held in August 2001 as a co-benefit concert for Testament singer Chuck Billy and Death's [Chuck Schuldiner](/wiki/Chuck_Schuldiner) (who were both battling cancer), saw several bands from the 1980s [Bay Area thrash metal](/wiki/Bay_Area_thrash_metal) scene reunite, including Exodus, Death Angel, [Vio-lence](/wiki/Vio-lence), [Forbidden Evil](/wiki/Forbidden_(band)), Sadus and Legacy (a precursor to Testament). Many thrash metal bands outside of the Bay Area would subsequently reunite, including Anthrax (twice with [Joey Belladonna](/wiki/Joey_Belladonna) and briefly with [John Bush](/wiki/John_Bush_(singer))), [Dark Angel](/wiki/Dark_Angel_(band)), [Nuclear Assault](/wiki/Nuclear_Assault), [Hirax](/wiki/Hirax), [Sacred Reich](/wiki/Sacred_Reich), [Whiplash](/wiki/Whiplash_(band)), UK bands [Onslaught](/wiki/Onslaught_(band)), [Sabbat](/wiki/Sabbat_(English_band)), and [Xentrix](/wiki/Xentrix), and Canada's [Sacrifice](/wiki/Sacrifice_(band)), renewing interest in previous decades.

A few thrash metal bands from the 1980s and early 1990s, particularly the genre's U.S. "Big Four", continued recording and touring with success in the 2000s. In 2003, Anthrax released their first studio album in five years *[We've Come for You All](/wiki/We%27ve_Come_for_You_All)*, followed a month later by Metallica's double platinum-certified album *[St. Anger](/wiki/St._Anger)*. After experimenting further with a commercialized sound on their previous few albums, Megadeth returned to its heavier sound with their ninth album *[The World Needs a Hero](/wiki/The_World_Needs_a_Hero)* (2001). It would be the band's final album before disbanding in the following year, due to an arm injury that had left [Dave Mustaine](/wiki/Dave_Mustaine) unable to play guitar; he would eventually reform Megadeth for a handful of albums, including *[The System Has Failed](/wiki/The_System_Has_Failed)* (2004) which was originally going to be released as a solo album by him, before reuniting with co-founding member and bassist [David Ellefson](/wiki/David_Ellefson) in 2010. Slayer released three albums in the 2000s: *[God Hates Us All](/wiki/God_Hates_Us_All)* (2001), which saw a return to their signature thrash metal sound, followed by *[Christ Illusion](/wiki/Christ_Illusion)* (2006) and *[World Painted Blood](/wiki/World_Painted_Blood)* (2009), both of which marked their first studio albums with drummer [Dave Lombardo](/wiki/Dave_Lombardo) in nearly two decades. Although their career had declined from its peak in the 1990s, Overkill was perhaps one of the most-active thrash metal groups outside of the "Big Four", having never disbanded or taken longer breaks in-between records, and by 2000's *[Bloodletting](/wiki/Bloodletting_(Overkill_album))*, they were the first band in the genre to release more than ten studio albums. Overkill's popularity was reignited in the 2010s, with three of their albums, *[The Electric Age](/wiki/The_Electric_Age)* (2012), *[White Devil Armory](/wiki/White_Devil_Armory)* (2014) and *[The Grinding Wheel](/wiki/The_Grinding_Wheel)* (2017), all entering the Top 100 on the *[Billboard](/wiki/Billboard_200)* charts.

The term "thrash-revivalists" has been applied to such bands as [Lamb of God](/wiki/Lamb_of_God_(band)), [Municipal Waste](/wiki/Municipal_Waste_(band)), [Evile](/wiki/Evile), [Havok](/wiki/Havok_(band)), [Warbringer](/wiki/Warbringer), [Vektor](/wiki/Vektor_(band)), [Bonded by Blood](/wiki/Bonded_by_Blood_(band)), [Hatchet](/wiki/Hatchet_(band)), and [Power Trip](/wiki/Power_Trip_(band)). Evile's 2007 debut album *[Enter the Grave](/wiki/Enter_the_Grave)*, produced by former Metallica producer and engineer [Flemming Rasmussen](/wiki/Flemming_Rasmussen), received considerable praise for its sound, which combined elements of the sounds of Slayer and the Bay Area scene (particularly Exodus and Testament). Los Angeles-based bands Warbringer and Bonded by Blood took a similar approach on their respective debut albums, *[War Without End](/wiki/War_Without_End_(album))* and *[Feed the Beast](/wiki/Feed_the_Beast_(Bonded_by_Blood_album))*, both released in 2008. Perhaps the most commercially successful band from the 2000s and 2010s thrash metal revival movement is Lamb of God, who are also considered a key part of the [new wave of American heavy metal](/wiki/New_wave_of_American_heavy_metal) movement, have received two [gold-certified](/wiki/Music_recording_certification) albums in the U.S., and have gone from playing small clubs to arenas and stadiums.

Notable bands returned to their roots with albums such as Kreator's *[Violent Revolution](/wiki/Violent_Revolution)* (2001), Metallica's *[Death Magnetic](/wiki/Death_Magnetic)* (2008), Megadeth's *[Endgame](/wiki/Endgame_(Megadeth_album))* (2009), Slayer's *World Painted Blood* (2009), Exodus' *[Exhibit B: The Human Condition](/wiki/Exhibit_B:_The_Human_Condition)* (2010), Overkill's *[Ironbound](/wiki/Ironbound_(album))* (2010), Anthrax's *[Worship Music](/wiki/Worship_Music_(album))* (2011), Testament's *[Dark Roots of Earth](/wiki/Dark_Roots_of_Earth)* (2012), and Flotsam and Jetsam's *[Ugly Noise](/wiki/Ugly_Noise)* (2012). More recent bands of the genre, such as Havok and [Legion of the Damned](/wiki/Legion_of_the_Damned_(band)) have turned their focus towards a more aggressive rendition of thrash metal, incorporating elements of [melodic death metal](/wiki/Melodic_death_metal).

## Derivative forms

Thrash metal is directly responsible for the development of underground metal genres, such as death metal, [black metal](/wiki/Black_metal), and [groove metal](/wiki/Groove_metal). In addition to this, [metalcore](/wiki/Metalcore), [grindcore](/wiki/Grindcore), and [deathcore](/wiki/Deathcore) employ similar riffs in their composition, the former with more focus on melody rather than chromaticism. The blending of punk ethos and metal's brutal nature led to even more extreme, underground styles after thrash metal began gaining mild commercial success in the late 1980s.

With gorier subject matter, heavier down tuning of guitars, more consistent use of [blast beat drumming](/wiki/Blast_beats), and darker, atonal [death growls](/wiki/Death_growls), death metal was established in the mid-1980s. Black metal, also related to thrash metal, emerged at the same time, with many black metal bands taking influence from thrash metal bands such as [Venom](/wiki/Venom_(band)). Black metal continued deviating from thrash metal, often providing more orchestral overtones, open [tremolo picking](/wiki/Tremolo_picking), [blast beat drumming](/wiki/Blast_beat), [shrieked](/wiki/Screaming_(music)#Black_metal) or raspy vocals and [pagan](/wiki/Paganism) or [occult](/wiki/Occultism)-based aesthetics to distinguish itself from thrash metal. Thrash metal would later combine with its spinoffs, thus giving rise to genres like [blackened thrash metal](/wiki/Blackened_thrash_metal) and [deathrash](/wiki/Deathrash).

### Crossover thrash

Main article: [Crossover thrash](/wiki/Crossover_thrash)

Thrash metal with stronger punk elements is called [crossover thrash](/wiki/Crossover_thrash). Its overall sound is more punk-influenced than traditional thrash metal but has more heavy metal elements than [hardcore punk](/wiki/Hardcore_punk) and [thrashcore](/wiki/Thrashcore).

### Dream thrash

A fusion of thrash metal with shoegaze is called **dream thrash**. According to Emma Cownley of *[Metal Hammer](/wiki/Metal_Hammer)*, "Dream thrash combines the ethereal sounds of shoegaze with the distortion, blast beats and tremolo picking of thrash." One practitioner of this style is [Astronoid](/wiki/Astronoid).

### Groove metal

Main article: [Groove metal](/wiki/Groove_metal)

[Groove metal](/wiki/Groove_metal) takes the intensity and sonic qualities of thrash metal and plays them at mid-[tempo](/wiki/Tempo), with most bands making only occasional forays into fast tempo, but since the early 1990s, it started to favor a more death metal-derived sound.

### Technical thrash metal

**Technical thrash** (also known as **progressive thrash**) is a "far more complex" variant of thrash metal that emphasizes "[progressive](/wiki/Progressive_music)" songwriting. The style is mostly combined with elements and influences from the styles of thrash metal, the [New wave of British heavy metal](/wiki/New_wave_of_British_heavy_metal) (NWOBHM), [progressive rock](/wiki/Progressive_rock), [avant-garde](/wiki/Avant-garde) and [jazz fusion](/wiki/Jazz_fusion). [Watchtower](/wiki/Watchtower_(band)) are said to be pioneers of technical thrash metal, and their debut album *[Energetic Disassembly](/wiki/Energetic_Disassembly)* (1985) is widely regarded as the first album to mix thrash with [progressive metal](/wiki/Progressive_metal). Other practitioners of the style include [Voivod](/wiki/Voivod_(band)), [Coroner](/wiki/Coroner_(band)), [Annihilator](/wiki/Annihilator_(band)), [Deathrow](/wiki/Deathrow_(band)), [Blind Illusion](/wiki/Blind_Illusion), [Toxik](/wiki/Toxik), [Artillery](/wiki/Artillery_(band)), [Mekong Delta](/wiki/Mekong_Delta_(band)), [Sadus](/wiki/Sadus), [Heathen](/wiki/Heathen_(band)), [Forced Entry](/wiki/Forced_Entry_(band)) and [Anacrusis](/wiki/Anacrusis_(band)). During the late 1980s and early 1990s, several veteran thrash metal bands began to expand their sound with a combination of technical, complex and progressive structures; notable examples include Metallica's *[...And Justice for All](/wiki/...And_Justice_for_All_(album))* (1988), Annihilator's *[Alice in Hell](/wiki/Alice_in_Hell)* (1989), Overkill's *[The Years of Decay](/wiki/The_Years_of_Decay)* (1989), Megadeth's *[Rust in Peace](/wiki/Rust_in_Peace)* (1990), Dark Angel's *[Time Does Not Heal](/wiki/Time_Does_Not_Heal)* (1991), Heathen's *[Victims of Deception](/wiki/Victims_of_Deception)* (1991), and much of [Testament](/wiki/Testament_(band))'s late 1980s and 1990s output. Technical thrash metal is considered by some to be a precursor to [technical death metal](/wiki/Technical_death_metal). Coroner guitarist Tommy Vetterli himself took credit for "co-invent[ing] the technical progressive thrash metal movement" in a 2026 interview with *[Metal Hammer](/wiki/Metal_Hammer)*.

## Regional scenes

Thrash metal emerged predominantly from a handful of regional scenes, each of which was generally distinguished by the unique characteristics of its bands.

- **[Bay Area thrash metal](/wiki/Bay_Area_thrash_metal)**, also known as **West Coast thrash metal**: In addition to being the most commercially successful, the West Coast's thrash metal (mostly centered in [California](/wiki/California)) is the most progressive and technical of the major regional thrash scenes, being strongly [NWOBHM](/wiki/New_wave_of_British_heavy_metal)-influenced. [Metallica](/wiki/Metallica) (originally from [Los Angeles](/wiki/Los_Angeles)), [Exodus](/wiki/Exodus_(band)), [Testament](/wiki/Testament_(band)), [Metal Church](/wiki/Metal_Church), [Possessed](/wiki/Possessed_(band)), [Death Angel](/wiki/Death_Angel), [Attitude Adjustment](/wiki/Attitude_Adjustment_(band)), [Lȧȧz Rockit](/wiki/L%C8%A7%C8%A7z_Rockit), [Vio-lence](/wiki/Vio-lence), [Forbidden](/wiki/Forbidden_(band)), [Defiance](/wiki/Defiance_(metal_band)), [Sadus](/wiki/Sadus), [Mordred](/wiki/Mordred_(band)) and [Blind Illusion](/wiki/Blind_Illusion) are prominent examples of bands to emerge from [the Bay Area](/wiki/San_Francisco_Bay_Area). Although [Megadeth](/wiki/Megadeth), [Slayer](/wiki/Slayer), [Dark Angel](/wiki/Dark_Angel_(band)) and [Suicidal Tendencies](/wiki/Suicidal_Tendencies) were all technically from Los Angeles, those bands are often credited for popularizing and contributing to the thrash metal scene in [Northern California](/wiki/Northern_California) during the 1980s by frequently playing shows there, especially early in their careers and/or before they received their first [record deal](/wiki/Recording_contract).
- **East Coast thrash metal**: Centered in [New York](/wiki/New_York_(state)) and [New Jersey](/wiki/New_Jersey), the East Coast's thrash metal tended to display a sound that incorporated a strong [hardcore punk](/wiki/Hardcore_punk) influence. An emphasis was placed on aggression and speed rather than technicality. [Anthrax](/wiki/Anthrax_(American_band)), [Overkill](/wiki/Overkill_(band)), [Carnivore](/wiki/Carnivore_(band)), [Nuclear Assault](/wiki/Nuclear_Assault), [Meanstreak](/wiki/Meanstreak_(band)), [Warzone](/wiki/Warzone_(band)), [Prong](/wiki/Prong_(band)), [Toxik](/wiki/Toxik), [Whiplash](/wiki/Whiplash_(band)), [Wrathchild America](/wiki/Wrathchild_America) and [Demolition Hammer](/wiki/Demolition_Hammer) exemplified the style to emerge from this regional scene.
- **Gulf Coast thrash metal**: While not as popular as the West Coast and East Coast regions, the Gulf Coast spawned at least three thrash metal scenes (including [Texas](/wiki/Texas), [Louisiana](/wiki/Louisiana) and [Florida](/wiki/Florida)) during the late 1980s and early 1990s, which saw a number of bands develop a style that was influenced by punk rock and/or the early-to-mid 1980s heavy metal scene. The most notable bands from the Gulf Coast are [Atheist](/wiki/Atheist_(band)), [Solstice](/wiki/Solstice_(American_band)), [Juggernaut](/wiki/Juggernaut_(band)), [Opprobrium](/wiki/Opprobrium_(band)) (formerly named Incubus), [Watchtower](/wiki/Watchtower_(band)), [Rigor Mortis](/wiki/Rigor_Mortis_(band)), [D.R.I.](/wiki/Dirty_Rotten_Imbeciles), [Absu](/wiki/Absu_(band)), [Nasty Savage](/wiki/Nasty_Savage), [Verbal Abuse](/wiki/Verbal_Abuse_(band)), [Angkor Wat](/wiki/Angkor_Wat_(band)), [Dead Horse](/wiki/Dead_Horse_(band)), [Pantera](/wiki/Pantera) and [Exhorder](/wiki/Exhorder), the latter of the two (along with New York's Prong) are often credited for developing and popularizing the [groove metal](/wiki/Groove_metal) genre in the early 1990s.
- **British thrash metal**: The British thrash scene originated from NWOBHM bands like [Raven](/wiki/Raven_(British_band)), [Jaguar](/wiki/Jaguar_(band)), Warfare and [Dragonslayer](/wiki/Dragonslayer_(band)) (originally called Slayer) beginning to play accelerated [heavy metal](/wiki/Heavy_metal_music). This led to British thrash metal bands leaning towards a more traditional heavy metal approach, often heavier though less aggressive than their American counterparts. Additionally, [crust punk](/wiki/Crust_punk) is an integral part of the British thrash metal scene. The most notable bands from this scene are [Onslaught](/wiki/Onslaught_(band)), [Cancer](/wiki/Cancer_(band)), [Amebix](/wiki/Amebix), [Sabbat](/wiki/Sabbat_(English_band)), [Detritus](/wiki/Detritus_(band)), [Xentrix](/wiki/Xentrix), [Sacrilege](/wiki/Sacrilege_(band)), [Nightlord](/wiki/Nightlord), [Atomkraft](/wiki/Atomkraft), [Hellbastard](/wiki/Hellbastard), [Cerebral Fix](/wiki/Cerebral_Fix), [Seventh Angel](/wiki/Seventh_Angel), [Acid Reign](/wiki/Acid_Reign) and [Lawnmower Deth](/wiki/Lawnmower_Deth).
- **[Brazilian thrash metal](/wiki/Brazilian_thrash_metal)**: The Brazilian thrash scene is notable for producing a handful of bands that would become principal parts of thrash metal's prevalence in the early 1990s. There were three scenes where Brazilian thrash metal originated: Belo Horizonte (the most prominent), São Paulo, and Rio de Janeiro. The most notable bands from this scene are [Sepultura](/wiki/Sepultura), [Dorsal Atlântica](/wiki/Dorsal_Atl%C3%A2ntica), [Executer](/wiki/Executer), [Mutilator](/wiki/Mutilator), [Chakal](/wiki/Chakal), [Korzus](/wiki/Korzus), [Holocausto](/wiki/Holocausto_(band)), [Claustrofobia](/wiki/Claustrofobia_(band)), [Torture Squad](/wiki/Torture_Squad), [Ratos de Porão](/wiki/Ratos_de_Por%C3%A3o), [Sagrado Inferno](/wiki/Sagrado_Inferno), [Vulcano](/wiki/Vulcano_(band)) and [Sarcófago](/wiki/Sarc%C3%B3fago).
- **[Teutonic thrash metal](/wiki/Teutonic_thrash_metal)**: The German and Swiss regions have spawned dozens of bands since the mid-1980s that developed their own style. Their style was more aggressive than American and Canadian thrash. The most prominent bands from this scene are [Kreator](/wiki/Kreator), [Destruction](/wiki/Destruction_(band)), [Sodom](/wiki/Sodom_(band)), [Tankard](/wiki/Tankard_(band)), [Celtic Frost](/wiki/Celtic_Frost), [Holy Moses](/wiki/Holy_Moses), [Desaster](/wiki/Desaster), [Coroner](/wiki/Coroner_(band)), [Exumer](/wiki/Exumer), [Despair](/wiki/Despair_(band)), [Paradox](/wiki/Paradox_(German_band)), [Messiah](/wiki/Messiah_(Swiss_band)), [Assassin](/wiki/Assassin_(German_band)), [Poltergeist](/wiki/Poltergeist_(band)), [Living Death](/wiki/Living_Death_(band)) and [Sieges Even](/wiki/Sieges_Even) (who were originally called Sodom).
- **Canadian thrash metal**: The Canadian region has seen numerous thrash metal bands create a unique blend of speed metal, progressive and hardcore punk into their music, influenced by a variety of acts such as [Rush](/wiki/Rush_(band)), [Iron Maiden](/wiki/Iron_Maiden), [Judas Priest](/wiki/Judas_Priest), [Motörhead](/wiki/Mot%C3%B6rhead) and D.R.I., as well as fellow U.S. thrash metal bands like Metallica and Slayer. [Anvil](/wiki/Anvil_(band)) and [Exciter](/wiki/Exciter_(band)) are considered to be the pioneers of this scene, while [Voivod](/wiki/Voivod_(band)), [Sacrifice](/wiki/Sacrifice_(band)), [Razor](/wiki/Razor_(band)), and [Annihilator](/wiki/Annihilator_(band)) are often referred to as the country's "Big Four". Notable crossover bands from the hardcore punk scene include West Coast bands Death Sentence and Beyond Possession. Other notable Canadian thrash metal bands include [Infernäl Mäjesty](/wiki/Infern%C3%A4l_M%C3%A4jesty), [Anonymus](/wiki/Anonymus_(band)), [Eudoxis](/wiki/Eudoxis), [Piledriver](/wiki/Piledriver_(band)), [Slaughter](/wiki/Slaughter_(Canadian_band)), [DBC](/wiki/Dead_Brain_Cells) and [Obliveon](/wiki/Obliveon).
- **[Australian thrash metal](/wiki/Australian_thrash_metal)**: While not considered a "major" part of the worldwide thrash metal scene due to distance from the major Bay Area and Teutonic scenes, Australian thrash metal has had a fairly substantial following in overseas markets, while local audiences have always been difficult to gather. The most notable Australian thrash metal bands from the 1980s are [Mortal Sin](/wiki/Mortal_Sin_(band)), [Nothing Sacred](/wiki/Nothing_Sacred_(band)) and [Hobbs' Angel of Death](/wiki/Hobbs%27_Angel_of_Death).
