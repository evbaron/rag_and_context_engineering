**Poland**, officially the **Republic of Poland**, is a country in [Central Europe](/wiki/Central_Europe). It extends from the [Baltic Sea](/wiki/Baltic_Sea) in the north to the [Sudetes](/wiki/Sudetes) and [Carpathian Mountains](/wiki/Carpathian_Mountains) in the south, and borders [Lithuania](/wiki/Lithuania) and [Russia](/wiki/Russia) to the northeast; [Belarus](/wiki/Belarus) and [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine) to the east; [Slovakia](/wiki/Slovakia) and the [Czech Republic](/wiki/Czech_Republic) to the south; and [Germany](/wiki/Germany) to the west. The territory has a varied landscape, diverse ecosystems, and a [temperate climate](/wiki/Temperate_climate). Poland is composed of [sixteen voivodeships](/wiki/Voivodeships_of_Poland) and is the fifth most populous [member state of the European Union](/wiki/Member_state_of_the_European_Union) (EU), with over 38 million people, and the [fifth largest EU country](/wiki/List_of_European_countries_by_area) by land area, covering 312,696 km2 (120,733 sq mi). The capital and [largest city](/wiki/List_of_cities_and_towns_in_Poland) is [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw); other major cities include [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w), [Wrocław](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw), [Łódź](/wiki/%C5%81%C3%B3d%C5%BA), [Poznań](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84), and [Gdańsk](/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk).

[Prehistoric human activity on Polish soil](/wiki/Prehistory_and_protohistory_of_Poland) dates to the [Lower Paleolithic](/wiki/Lower_Paleolithic), with continuous settlement since the end of the [Last Glacial Period](/wiki/Last_Glacial_Period). Culturally diverse throughout [late antiquity](/wiki/Late_antiquity), in the [early medieval period](/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages) the region became inhabited by the [West Slavic](/wiki/West_Slavs) tribal [Polans](/wiki/Polans_(western)), who gave [Poland its name](/wiki/Names_of_Poland). The process of establishing [statehood](/wiki/Statehood) coincided with the conversion of a [pagan ruler of the Polans](/wiki/Mieszko_I) to Christianity in 966 under the auspices of the [Roman Catholic Church](/wiki/Catholic_Church). The [Kingdom of Poland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland) emerged in 1025, and in 1569 cemented its long-standing [association with Lithuania](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_union), thus forming the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth). At the time, the Commonwealth was one of the [great powers](/wiki/List_of_modern_great_powers) of Europe, with an [elective monarchy](/wiki/Elective_monarchy_in_Poland) and a [uniquely liberal](/wiki/Golden_Liberty) political system, which adopted [Europe's first modern constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_3_May_1791) in 1791.

With the passing of the prosperous [Polish Golden Age](/wiki/Polish_Golden_Age), the country was [partitioned by neighbouring states](/wiki/Partitions_of_Poland) at the end of the 18th century. At the end of [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I) in 1918, Poland regained [its independence](/wiki/National_Independence_Day_(Poland)) with the [founding](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1795-1918)#Recovery_of_statehood) of the [Second Polish Republic](/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic), which emerged [victorious](/wiki/Battle_of_Warsaw_(1920)) in [various conflicts](/wiki/List_of_wars_involving_Poland#Second_Polish_Republic_(1918–1939)) of the [interbellum](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1918%E2%80%931939)) period. In September 1939, the [invasion of Poland](/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland) by [Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany) [and the](/wiki/Soviet_invasion_of_Poland) [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) marked the beginning of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), which resulted in [the Holocaust](/wiki/The_Holocaust_in_Poland) and millions of [Polish casualties](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1939%E2%80%931945)). Forced into the [Eastern Bloc](/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) in the global [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War), the [Polish People's Republic](/wiki/Polish_People%27s_Republic) was a signatory of the [Warsaw Pact](/wiki/Warsaw_Pact). Through the 1980 [emergence](/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk_Agreement) and contributions of the [Solidarity movement](/wiki/Solidarity_(Polish_trade_union)), which initiated the [fall of the Iron Curtain](/wiki/Fall_of_the_Iron_Curtain), the [communist government](/wiki/Polish_United_Workers%27_Party) was [dissolved](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1945%E2%80%931989)) and Poland as the first of the [communist](/wiki/Communism) countries re-established itself as a [liberal democracy](/wiki/Liberal_democracy) in 1989, and [held free elections](/wiki/1989_Polish_parliamentary_election).

Poland is a [parliamentary republic](/wiki/Parliamentary_republic) with its [bicameral legislature](/wiki/Bicameralism) comprising the [Sejm](/wiki/Sejm) and the [Senate](/wiki/Senate_of_Poland). Considered a [middle power](/wiki/Middle_power) and a [regional power](/wiki/Regional_power), it is a [developed market](/wiki/Developed_market) and [high-income economy](/wiki/World_Bank_high-income_economy). In terms of nominal GDP, it is the [20th largest economy](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)) in the world and the sixth in the European Union, and [fifth largest by PPP-adjusted GDP](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(PPP)) in the EU. Poland has a high standard of living, safety, and [economic freedom](/wiki/Economic_freedom), as well as free [university education](/wiki/Education_in_Poland) and [universal health care](/wiki/Health_care_in_Poland). It has [17](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Poland) UNESCO [World Heritage Sites](/wiki/World_Heritage_Sites), over 100 [Historic Monuments](/wiki/List_of_Historic_Monuments_(Poland)) and 23 [national parks](/wiki/National_Parks_of_Poland). Poland is a founding member of the [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations), and a member of the [Council of Europe](/wiki/Council_of_Europe), [World Trade Organisation](/wiki/World_Trade_Organization), [OECD](/wiki/OECD), [NATO](/wiki/NATO), and EU (including the [Schengen Area](/wiki/Schengen_Area)).

## Etymology

Main article: [Names of Poland](/wiki/Names_of_Poland)

The native [Polish](/wiki/Polish_language) name for Poland is *Polska*. It is believed that the name derives from the [Polans](/wiki/Polans_(western)), a [West Slavic](/wiki/West_Slavs) tribe who inhabited the [Warta River](/wiki/Warta_River) basin of present-day [Greater Poland](/wiki/Greater_Poland) region (6th–8th century CE). The tribe's name stems from the [Proto-Slavic](/wiki/Proto-Slavic) noun *pole*, meaning field, which itself originates from the [Proto-Indo-European](/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) word *\*pleh₂-* indicating flatland. The etymology alludes to the [topography](/wiki/Topography) of the region and the flat landscape of Greater Poland. During the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages), the [Latin](/wiki/Latin) form *Polonia* was widely used throughout Europe.

The country's alternative archaic name is *[Lechia](/wiki/Lechia)* and its root syllable remains in official use in several languages, notably [Hungarian](/wiki/Hungarian_language), [Lithuanian](/wiki/Lithuanian_language), and [Persian](/wiki/Persian_language). The [exonym](/wiki/Exonym) possibly derives from either [Lech](/wiki/Lech_and_Czech), a legendary ruler of the [Lechites](/wiki/Lechites), or from the [Lendians](/wiki/Lendians), a West Slavic tribe that dwelt on the south-easternmost edge of [Lesser Poland](/wiki/Lesser_Poland). The origin of the tribe's name lies in the [Old Polish](/wiki/Old_Polish_language) word *lęda* (plain). Initially, both names *Lechia* and *Polonia* were used interchangeably when referring to Poland by chroniclers during the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages).

## History

Main article: [History of Poland](/wiki/History_of_Poland)

### Prehistory and protohistory

Main articles: [Stone Age Poland](/wiki/Stone_Age_Poland), [Bronze and Iron Age Poland](/wiki/Bronze_and_Iron_Age_Poland), [Poland in antiquity](/wiki/Poland_in_antiquity), [Early Slavs](/wiki/Early_Slavs), [West Slavs](/wiki/West_Slavs), [Lechites](/wiki/Lechites), and [Poland in the Early Middle Ages](/wiki/Poland_in_the_Early_Middle_Ages)

A reconstruction of a [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age) [Lusatian culture](/wiki/Lusatian_culture) settlement in [Biskupin](/wiki/Biskupin), 8th century BC

The first [Stone Age](/wiki/Stone_Age) archaic humans and *[Homo erectus](/wiki/Homo_erectus)* species settled what was to become Poland approximately 500,000 years ago, though the ensuing hostile climate prevented early humans from founding more permanent encampments. The arrival of *[Homo sapiens](/wiki/Homo_sapiens)* and [anatomically modern humans](/wiki/Early_European_modern_humans) coincided with the climatic discontinuity at the end of the [Last Glacial Period](/wiki/Last_Glacial_Period) ([Northern Polish glaciation](/wiki/Weichselian_glaciation) 10,000 BC), when Poland became habitable. [Neolithic](/wiki/Neolithic) excavations indicated broad-ranging development in that era; the earliest evidence of European cheesemaking (5500 BC) was discovered in Polish [Kuyavia](/wiki/Kuyavia), and the [Bronocice pot](/wiki/Bronocice_pot) is incised with the earliest known depiction of what may be a wheeled vehicle (3400 BC).

The period spanning the [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age) and the [Early Iron Age](/wiki/Early_Iron_Age) (1300 BC–500 BC) was marked by an increase in population density, establishment of [palisaded](/wiki/Palisade) settlements ([gords](/wiki/Gord_(archaeology))) and the expansion of [Lusatian culture](/wiki/Lusatian_culture). A significant archaeological find from [the protohistory of Poland](/wiki/Prehistory_and_protohistory_of_Poland) is a fortified settlement at [Biskupin](/wiki/Biskupin), attributed to the Lusatian culture of the [Late Bronze Age](/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age) (mid-8th century BC).

Throughout [antiquity](/wiki/Classical_antiquity) (400 BC–500 AD), many distinct ancient populations inhabited the territory of present-day Poland, notably [Celtic](/wiki/Celts), [Scythian](/wiki/Scythia), [Germanic](/wiki/Germanic_peoples), [Sarmatian](/wiki/Sarmatians), [Baltic](/wiki/Balts) and [Slavic](/wiki/Early_Slavs) tribes. Furthermore, archaeological findings confirmed the presence of [Roman Legions](/wiki/Roman_Legions) sent to protect the [amber trade](/wiki/Amber_Road). The [Polish tribes](/wiki/Polish_tribes) emerged following the [second wave of the Migration Period](/wiki/Migration_Period#Second_wave) around the 6th century AD; they were [Slavic](/wiki/Slavs) and may have included assimilated remnants of peoples that earlier dwelled in the area. Beginning in the early 10th century, the [Polans](/wiki/Polans_(western)) would come to dominate other [Lechitic](/wiki/Lechites) tribes in the region, initially forming a tribal federation and later a centralised monarchical state.

### Kingdom of Poland

Main articles: [History of Poland during the Piast dynasty](/wiki/History_of_Poland_during_the_Piast_dynasty), [Baptism of Poland](/wiki/Baptism_of_Poland), and [Kingdom of Poland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland)

Poland under the rule of Duke [Mieszko I](/wiki/Mieszko_I), whose acceptance of Christianity under the auspices of the [Roman Church](/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church) and the [Baptism of Poland](/wiki/Baptism_of_Poland) marked the beginning of statehood in 966. His son, [Bolesław I the Brave](/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_I_the_Brave), was crowned [king](/wiki/King_of_Poland) in 1025

Poland began to form into a recognisable unitary and territorial entity around the middle of the 10th century under the [Piast dynasty](/wiki/Piast_dynasty). In 966, the ruler of the Polans, [Mieszko I](/wiki/Mieszko_I_of_Poland), accepted [Western Christianity](/wiki/Western_Christianity) under the auspices of the [Roman Catholic Church](/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church) which was tantamount to the adoption of [Christianity by Poland](/wiki/Christianization_of_Poland). In 968, a missionary [bishopric](/wiki/Bishopric) was established in [Poznań](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84). An [incipit](/wiki/Incipit) titled [Dagome iudex](/wiki/Dagome_iudex) first defined Poland's geographical boundaries with its capital in [Gniezno](/wiki/Gniezno) and affirmed that its monarchy was under the protection of the [Apostolic See](/wiki/Apostolic_See). The country's early origins were described by [Gallus Anonymus](/wiki/Gallus_Anonymus) in *[Gesta principum Polonorum](/wiki/Gesta_principum_Polonorum)*, the oldest Polish chronicle. An important national event of the period was the [martyrdom](/wiki/Martyrdom) of [Saint Adalbert](/wiki/Adalbert_of_Prague), who was killed by [Prussian](/wiki/Old_Prussians) pagans in 997 and whose remains were reputedly bought back for their weight in gold by Mieszko's successor, [Bolesław I the Brave](/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_I_the_Brave).

In 1000, at the [Congress of Gniezno](/wiki/Congress_of_Gniezno), Bolesław I the Brave obtained the right of [investiture](/wiki/Investiture) from [Otto III, Holy Roman Emperor](/wiki/Otto_III,_Holy_Roman_Emperor), who assented to the creation archdioceses in Gniezno and three new dioceses on Polish lands in [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w), [Kołobrzeg](/wiki/Ko%C5%82obrzeg), and [Wrocław](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw). Also, Otto bestowed upon Bolesław royal [regalia](/wiki/Regalia) and a replica of the [Holy Lance](/wiki/Holy_Lance), which were later used at his coronation as the first [King of Poland](/wiki/List_of_Polish_monarchs) c. 1025, when Bolesław received permission for his coronation from [Pope John XIX](/wiki/Pope_John_XIX). Bolesław also expanded the realm considerably by seizing parts of German [Lusatia](/wiki/Lusatia), Czech [Moravia](/wiki/Moravia), [Upper Hungary](/wiki/Upper_Hungary), and southwestern regions of the [Kievan Rus'](/wiki/Kievan_Rus%27).

[Casimir III the Great](/wiki/Casimir_III_the_Great) is the only Polish king to receive the title of *Great*. He built extensively during his reign, and reformed the Polish army along with the country's legal code, 1333–1370.

The transition from [paganism](/wiki/Slavic_paganism) in Poland was not instantaneous and resulted in the [pagan reaction of the 1030s](/wiki/Pagan_reaction_in_Poland). In 1031, [Mieszko II Lambert](/wiki/Mieszko_II_Lambert) lost the title of king and fled amidst the violence. The unrest led to the transfer of the capital to Kraków in 1038 by [Casimir I the Restorer](/wiki/Casimir_I_the_Restorer). In 1076, [Bolesław II](/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_II_the_Generous) re-instituted the office of king, but was banished in 1079 for murdering his opponent, [Bishop Stanislaus](/wiki/Stanislaus_of_Szczepan%C3%B3w). In 1138, the country [fragmented](/wiki/Testament_of_Boles%C5%82aw_III_Wrymouth) into five principalities when [Bolesław III Wrymouth](/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_III_Wrymouth) divided his lands among his sons. These were [Lesser Poland](/wiki/Lesser_Poland), Greater Poland, [Silesia](/wiki/Silesia), [Masovia](/wiki/Masovia), and [Sandomierz](/wiki/Sandomierz), with intermittent hold over [Pomerania](/wiki/Pomerania). In 1226, [Konrad I of Masovia](/wiki/Konrad_I_of_Masovia) invited the [Teutonic Knights](/wiki/Teutonic_Knights) to aid in combating the [Baltic](/wiki/Balts) Prussians; a decision that later led to centuries of warfare with the Knights.

In the first half of the 13th century, [Henry I the Bearded](/wiki/Henry_I_the_Bearded) and [Henry II the Pious](/wiki/Henry_II_the_Pious) aimed to unite the fragmented dukedoms, but the [Mongol invasion](/wiki/First_Mongol_invasion_of_Poland) and the death of Henry II in [battle](/wiki/Battle_of_Legnica) hindered the unification. As a result of the devastation which followed, depopulation and the demand for craft labour spurred a migration of [German and Flemish settlers](/wiki/History_of_Germans_in_Poland) into Poland, which was encouraged by the Polish dukes. In 1264, the [Statute of Kalisz](/wiki/Statute_of_Kalisz) introduced unprecedented autonomy for the [Polish Jews](/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Poland), who came to Poland fleeing persecution elsewhere in Europe.

In 1320, [Władysław I the Short](/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_I_the_Elbow-high) became the first king of [a reunified Poland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland_(1025%E2%80%931385)) since [Przemysł II](/wiki/Przemys%C5%82_II) in 1296, and the first to be crowned at [Wawel Cathedral](/wiki/Wawel_Cathedral) in Kraków. Beginning in 1333, the reign of [Casimir III the Great](/wiki/Casimir_III_the_Great) was marked by developments in [castle infrastructure](/wiki/Trail_of_the_Eagle%27s_Nests), army, judiciary and [diplomacy](/wiki/Congress_of_Krak%C3%B3w). Under his authority, Poland transformed into a major European power; he instituted Polish rule over [Ruthenia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ruthenia) in 1340 and imposed quarantine that prevented the spread of [Black Death](/wiki/Black_Death). In 1364, Casimir inaugurated the [University of Kraków](/wiki/Jagiellonian_University), one of the oldest institutions of higher learning in Europe. Upon his death in 1370, the Piast dynasty came to an end. He was succeeded by his closest male relative, [Louis of Anjou](/wiki/Louis_I_of_Hungary), who ruled Poland, [Hungary](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary), and [Croatia](/wiki/Croatia_in_personal_union_with_Hungary) in a [personal union](/wiki/Personal_union). Louis' younger daughter [Jadwiga](/wiki/Jadwiga_of_Poland) became Poland's first female monarch in [1384](/wiki/1384).

### The Golden Age

Main articles: [History of Poland during the Jagiellonian dynasty](/wiki/History_of_Poland_during_the_Jagiellonian_dynasty) and [Polish Golden Age](/wiki/Polish_Golden_Age)

The [Battle of Grunwald](/wiki/Battle_of_Grunwald) was fought against the [German Order of Teutonic Knights](/wiki/Teutonic_Knights), and resulted in a decisive victory for the [Kingdom of Poland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland_(1385%E2%80%931569)), 15 July 1410.

In 1386, Jadwiga of Poland entered a marriage of convenience with [Władysław II Jagiełło](/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_II_Jagie%C5%82%C5%82o), the [Grand Duke of Lithuania](/wiki/Grand_Duke_of_Lithuania), thus forming the [Jagiellonian dynasty](/wiki/Jagiellonian_dynasty) and the [Polish–Lithuanian union](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_union) which spanned the late [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages) and early [Modern Era](/wiki/Modern_history). The partnership between Poles and Lithuanians brought the vast multi-ethnic [Lithuanian](/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Lithuania) territories into Poland's sphere of influence and proved beneficial for its inhabitants, who coexisted in one of the largest European [political entities](/wiki/Personal_union) of the time.

In the Baltic Sea region, the struggle of Poland and Lithuania with the [Teutonic Knights](/wiki/Teutonic_Knights) continued and culminated at the [Battle of Grunwald](/wiki/Battle_of_Grunwald) in [1410](/wiki/1410), where a combined Polish–Lithuanian army inflicted a decisive victory against them. In 1466, after the [Thirteen Years' War](/wiki/Thirteen_Years%27_War_(1454%E2%80%9366)), king [Casimir IV Jagiellon](/wiki/Casimir_IV_Jagiellon) gave royal consent to the [Peace of Thorn](/wiki/Second_Peace_of_Thorn_(1466)), which created the future [Duchy of Prussia](/wiki/Duchy_of_Prussia) under Polish suzerainty and forced the Prussian rulers to pay [tributes](/wiki/Homage_(feudal)). The Jagiellonian dynasty also established dynastic control over the kingdoms of [Bohemia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Bohemia) ([1471](/wiki/1471) onwards) and Hungary. In the south, Poland confronted the [Ottoman Empire](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) (at the [Varna Crusade](/wiki/Battle_of_Varna)) and the [Crimean Tatars](/wiki/Crimean_Khanate), and in the east helped Lithuania to combat [Russia](/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Moscow).

Poland was developing as a [feudal](/wiki/Feudalism) state, with a predominantly agricultural economy and an increasingly powerful [landed nobility](/wiki/Landed_nobility) that confined the population to private manorial farmstead known as [*folwarks*](/wiki/Folwark). In 1493, [John I Albert](/wiki/John_I_Albert) sanctioned the creation of a [bicameral parliament](/wiki/Bicameral_parliament) (the Sejm) composed of a lower house, the chamber of deputies, and an upper house, the chamber of senators. The *[Nihil novi](/wiki/Nihil_novi)* act adopted by the Polish [General Sejm](/wiki/General_Sejm) in 1505, transferred most of the [legislative power](/wiki/Legislature) from the monarch to the parliament, an event which marked the beginning of the period known as [Golden Liberty](/wiki/Golden_Liberty), when the state was ruled by the seemingly free and equal [Polish nobles](/wiki/Szlachta).

[Wawel Castle](/wiki/Wawel_Castle) in [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w), seat of [Polish kings](/wiki/List_of_Polish_monarchs) from 1038 until the capital was moved to [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw) in 1596

The 16th century saw [Protestant Reformation](/wiki/Protestant_Reformation) movements making deep inroads into Polish Christianity, which resulted in the establishment of policies promoting religious tolerance, unique in Europe at that time. This tolerance allowed the country to avoid the religious turmoil and [wars of religion](/wiki/European_wars_of_religion) that beset Europe. In Poland, [Nontrinitarian Christianity](/wiki/Nontrinitarianism) became the doctrine of the so-called [Polish Brethren](/wiki/Polish_Brethren), who separated from their [Calvinist](/wiki/Calvinism) denomination and became the co-founders of global [Unitarianism](/wiki/Unitarianism).

The European [Renaissance](/wiki/Renaissance) evoked under [Sigismund I the Old](/wiki/Sigismund_I_the_Old) and [Sigismund II Augustus](/wiki/Sigismund_II_Augustus) a sense of urgency in the need to promote a [cultural awakening](/wiki/Renaissance_in_Poland). During the [Polish Golden Age](/wiki/Polish_Golden_Age), the nation's economy and culture flourished. The Italian-born [Bona Sforza](/wiki/Bona_Sforza), daughter of the [Duke of Milan](/wiki/Gian_Galeazzo_Sforza) and queen consort to Sigismund I, made considerable contributions to [architecture](/wiki/Architecture_of_Poland), [cuisine](/wiki/Polish_cuisine), language and court customs at [Wawel Castle](/wiki/Wawel_Castle).

### Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

Main articles: [History of Poland in the Early Modern era (1569–1795)](/wiki/History_of_Poland_in_the_Early_Modern_era_(1569%E2%80%931795)), [Crown of the Kingdom of Poland](/wiki/Crown_of_the_Kingdom_of_Poland), and [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth)

The [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth) at its greatest extent in 1619. At that time it was the largest country in Europe

The [Union of Lublin](/wiki/Union_of_Lublin) of 1569 established the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth), a unified federal state with an [elective monarchy](/wiki/Elective_monarchy) that was largely governed by the nobility. The latter coincided with a period of prosperity. The Polish-dominated union thereafter became a leading power and a major cultural entity, exercising political control over parts of Central, [Eastern](/wiki/Eastern_Europe), [Southeastern](/wiki/Southeastern_Europe) and Northern Europe. The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth occupied approximately 1 million km2 (390,000 sq mi) [at its peak](/wiki/Truce_of_Deulino) and was the largest state in Europe. Simultaneously, Poland imposed [Polonisation](/wiki/Polonisation) policies in newly acquired territories which were met with resistance from ethnic and religious minorities.

In [1573](/wiki/1573), [Henry de Valois of France](/wiki/Henry_III_of_France), the first elected king, approbated the [Henrician Articles](/wiki/Henrician_Articles) which obliged future monarchs to respect the rights of nobles. When he left Poland to become [King of France](/wiki/List_of_French_monarchs), his successor, [Stephen Báthory](/wiki/Stephen_B%C3%A1thory), led a successful [campaign](/wiki/Livonian_campaign_of_Stephen_B%C3%A1thory) in the [Livonian War](/wiki/Livonian_War), granting Poland more [lands across the eastern shores](/wiki/Livonia) of the Baltic Sea. State affairs were then headed by [Jan Zamoyski](/wiki/Jan_Zamoyski), the [Crown Chancellor](/wiki/Chancellor_(Poland)). Stephen's successor, [Sigismund III](/wiki/Sigismund_III_Vasa), defeated a rival [Habsburg](/wiki/House_of_Habsburg) electoral candidate, [Archduke Maximilian III](/wiki/Maximilian_III,_Archduke_of_Austria), in the [War of the Polish Succession (1587–1588)](/wiki/War_of_the_Polish_Succession_(1587%E2%80%931588)). In [1592](/wiki/1592), Sigismund succeeded his father [John Vasa](/wiki/John_III_of_Sweden), in [Sweden](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Sweden). The [Polish-Swedish union](/wiki/Polish-Swedish_union) endured until 1599, when he was [deposed](/wiki/War_against_Sigismund) by the Swedes.

King [John III Sobieski](/wiki/John_III_Sobieski) defeated the [Ottoman Turks](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) at the [Battle of Vienna](/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna) on 12 September 1683.

In 1609, Sigismund [invaded](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Muscovite_War_(1605%E2%80%931618)) [Russia](/wiki/Tsardom_of_Russia) which was engulfed in a [civil war](/wiki/Time_of_Troubles), and a year later the Polish [winged hussar](/wiki/Polish_hussars) units under [Stanisław Żółkiewski](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_%C5%BB%C3%B3%C5%82kiewski) [occupied](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_occupation_of_Moscow) Moscow for two years after defeating the Russians at [Klushino](/wiki/Battle_of_Klushino). Sigismund also countered the [Ottoman Empire](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) in the southeast; at [Khotyn](/wiki/Battle_of_Khotyn_(1621)) in [1621](/wiki/1621) [Jan Karol Chodkiewicz](/wiki/Jan_Karol_Chodkiewicz) achieved a decisive victory against the Turks, which ushered the downfall of Sultan [Osman II](/wiki/Osman_II). Sigismund's long reign in Poland coincided with the [Silver Age](/wiki/Sigismund_III_Vasa#Legacy). The liberal [Władysław IV](/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_IV_Vasa) effectively defended Poland's territorial possessions but after his death the vast Commonwealth began declining from internal disorder and constant warfare.

In [1648](/wiki/1648), the Polish hegemony over Ukraine sparked the [Khmelnytsky Uprising](/wiki/Khmelnytsky_Uprising), followed by the decimating [Swedish Deluge](/wiki/Deluge_(history)) during the [Second Northern War](/wiki/Second_Northern_War), and Prussia's [independence](/wiki/Treaty_of_Bromberg) in 1657. In 1683, [John III Sobieski](/wiki/John_III_Sobieski) re-established military prowess when he halted the advance of an [Ottoman Army](/wiki/Ottoman_Army_(15th-19th_centuries)) into Europe at the [Battle of Vienna](/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna). The [Saxon](/wiki/House_of_Wettin) era, under [Augustus II](/wiki/Augustus_II_the_Strong) and [Augustus III](/wiki/Augustus_III_of_Poland), saw neighbouring powers grow in strength at the expense of Poland. Both Saxon kings faced opposition from [Stanisław Leszczyński](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Leszczy%C5%84ski) during the [Great Northern War](/wiki/Great_Northern_War) (1700) and the [War of the Polish Succession](/wiki/War_of_the_Polish_Succession) (1733).

### Partitions

Main articles: [History of Poland (1795–1918)](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1795%E2%80%931918)) and [Partitions of Poland](/wiki/Partitions_of_Poland)

[Stanisław II Augustus](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_II_Augustus), the last [King of Poland](/wiki/King_of_Poland), reigned from 1764 until his abdication on 25 November 1795.

The [royal election](/wiki/Royal_elections_in_Poland) of 1764 resulted in the elevation of [Stanisław II Augustus Poniatowski](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_August_Poniatowski) to the monarchy. His candidacy was extensively funded by his sponsor and former lover, Empress [Catherine II of Russia](/wiki/Catherine_II_of_Russia). The new king maneuvered between his desire to implement necessary modernising reforms, and the necessity to remain at peace with surrounding states. His ideals led to the formation of the 1768 [Bar Confederation](/wiki/Bar_Confederation), a rebellion directed against the Poniatowski and all external influence, which ineptly aimed to preserve Poland's sovereignty and privileges held by the nobility. The failed attempts at government restructuring as well as the domestic turmoil provoked its neighbours to invade.

In 1772, the [First Partition of the Commonwealth](/wiki/First_Partition_of_Poland) by Prussia, Russia, and Austria took place, an act which the [Partition Sejm](/wiki/Partition_Sejm), under considerable duress, eventually ratified as a [fait accompli](/wiki/List_of_French_words_and_phrases_used_by_English_speakers#F). Disregarding the territorial losses, in 1773 a plan of critical reforms was established, in which the [Commission of National Education](/wiki/Commission_of_National_Education), the first government education authority in Europe, was inaugurated. Corporal punishment of schoolchildren was officially prohibited in 1783. Poniatowski was the head figure of the [Enlightenment](/wiki/Polish_Enlightenment), encouraged the development of industries, and embraced republican [neoclassicism](/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture). For his contributions to the arts and sciences, he was awarded a [Fellowship of the Royal Society](/wiki/Fellowship_of_the_Royal_Society).

In 1791, the [Great Sejm parliament](/wiki/Great_Sejm) adopted the [3 May Constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_May_3,_1791), the first set of supreme national laws, and introduced a [constitutional monarchy](/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy). The [Targowica Confederation](/wiki/Targowica_Confederation), an organisation of nobles and deputies opposing the act, appealed to Catherine and caused the [1792 Polish–Russian War](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Russian_War_of_1792). Fearing the reemergence of Polish hegemony, Russia and Prussia arranged and, in 1793, executed the [Second Partition](/wiki/Second_Partition_of_Poland), which left the country deprived of territory and incapable of independent existence. On 24 October 1795, the Commonwealth was [partitioned for the third time](/wiki/Third_Partition_of_Poland) and ceased to exist as a territorial entity. Stanisław Augustus, the last King of Poland, abdicated the throne on 25 November 1795.

### Era of insurrections

Main articles: [Austrian Partition](/wiki/Austrian_Partition), [Prussian Partition](/wiki/Prussian_Partition), and [Russian Partition](/wiki/Russian_Partition)

The [partitions of Poland](/wiki/Partitions_of_Poland), carried out by the [Kingdom of Prussia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) (*blue*), the [Russian Empire](/wiki/Russian_Empire) (*brown*), and the [Austrian Habsburg Monarchy](/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy) (*green*) in [1772](/wiki/First_Partition_of_Poland), [1793](/wiki/Second_Partition_of_Poland) and [1795](/wiki/Third_Partition_of_Poland)

The Polish people [rose several times against the partitioners](/wiki/List_of_wars_involving_Poland) and occupying armies. An unsuccessful attempt at defending Poland's sovereignty took place in the 1794 [Kościuszko Uprising](/wiki/Ko%C5%9Bciuszko_Uprising), where a popular and distinguished general [Tadeusz Kościuszko](/wiki/Tadeusz_Ko%C5%9Bciuszko), who had several years earlier served under [George Washington](/wiki/George_Washington) in the [American Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), led Polish insurgents. Despite the victory at the [Battle of Racławice](/wiki/Battle_of_Rac%C5%82awice), his ultimate defeat ended Poland's independent existence [for 123 years](/wiki/Third_Partition_of_Poland).

In 1806, an [insurrection](/wiki/Greater_Poland_uprising_(1806)) organised by [Jan Henryk Dąbrowski](/wiki/Jan_Henryk_D%C4%85browski) liberated western Poland ahead of [Napoleon](/wiki/Napoleon_I_of_France)'s advance into Prussia during the [War of the Fourth Coalition](/wiki/War_of_the_Fourth_Coalition). In accordance with the 1807 [Treaty of Tilsit](/wiki/Treaty_of_Tilsit), Napoleon proclaimed the [Duchy of Warsaw](/wiki/Duchy_of_Warsaw), a [client state](/wiki/Client_state) ruled by his ally [Frederick Augustus I of Saxony](/wiki/Frederick_Augustus_I_of_Saxony). The Poles actively aided French troops in the [Napoleonic Wars](/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars), particularly those under [Józef Poniatowski](/wiki/J%C3%B3zef_Poniatowski) who became [Marshal of France](/wiki/Marshal_of_the_Empire) shortly before his death at [Leipzig](/wiki/Battle_of_Leipzig) in 1813. In the aftermath of Napoleon's exile, the Duchy of Warsaw was abolished at the [Congress of Vienna](/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna) in 1815 and its territory was divided into Russian [Congress Kingdom of Poland](/wiki/Congress_Kingdom_of_Poland), the Prussian [Grand Duchy of Posen](/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Posen), and [Austrian Galicia](/wiki/Austrian_Poland) with the [Free City of Kraków](/wiki/Free_City_of_Krak%C3%B3w).

[Tadeusz Kościuszko](/wiki/Tadeusz_Ko%C5%9Bciuszko) was a veteran and hero of both the [Polish](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Russian_War_of_1792) and [American](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) wars of independence.

In 1830, [non-commissioned officers](/wiki/Non-commissioned_officer) at Warsaw's [Officer Cadet School](/wiki/Corps_of_Cadets_(Warsaw)) rebelled in what was the [November Uprising](/wiki/November_Uprising). After its collapse, Congress Poland lost its [constitutional autonomy](/wiki/Constitution_of_Congress_Poland), [army](/wiki/Army_of_Congress_Poland) and legislative assembly. During the [European Spring of Nations](/wiki/Spring_of_Nations), Poles took up arms in the [Greater Poland Uprising of 1848](/wiki/Greater_Poland_Uprising_(1848)) to resist [Germanisation](/wiki/Germanisation), but its failure saw duchy's status reduced to a mere [province](/wiki/Province_of_Posen); and subsequent integration into the [German Empire](/wiki/German_Empire) in 1871. In Russia, the fall of the [January Uprising](/wiki/January_Uprising) (1863–1864) prompted severe [political, social and cultural reprisals](/wiki/January_Uprising#The_decades_of_reprisals), followed by deportations and [pogroms](/wiki/Pogroms) of the Polish-Jewish population. Towards the end of the 19th century, Congress Poland became heavily industrialised; its primary exports being coal, [zinc](/wiki/Zinc), iron and textiles.

### Second Polish Republic

Main articles: [History of Poland (1918–1939)](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1918%E2%80%931939)), [Battle of Warsaw (1920)](/wiki/Battle_of_Warsaw_(1920)), and [Second Polish Republic](/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic)

[Chief of State](/wiki/Naczelnik_pa%C5%84stwa) Marshal [Józef Piłsudski](/wiki/J%C3%B3zef_Pi%C5%82sudski) was a hero of the Polish independence campaign and the nation's premiere statesman from 1918 until his death on 12 May 1935.

In the aftermath of [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I), the [Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I) agreed on the reconstitution of Poland, confirmed through the [Treaty of Versailles](/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles) of June 1919. A total of 2 million Polish troops fought with the armies of the three occupying powers, and over 450,000 died. Following the [armistice with Germany](/wiki/Armistice_with_Germany_(Compi%C3%A8gne)) in November 1918, Poland regained its independence as the [Second Polish Republic](/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic).

The Second Polish Republic reaffirmed its sovereignty after [a series of military conflicts](/wiki/List_of_wars_involving_Poland), most notably the [Polish–Soviet War](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Soviet_War), when Poland inflicted a crushing defeat on the [Red Army](/wiki/Red_Army) at the [Battle of Warsaw](/wiki/Battle_of_Warsaw_(1920)).

The inter-war period heralded a new era of Polish politics. Whilst Polish political activists had faced heavy censorship in the decades up until [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I), a new political tradition was established in the country. Many exiled Polish activists, such as [Ignacy Jan Paderewski](/wiki/Ignacy_Jan_Paderewski), who would later become prime minister, returned home. A significant number of them then went on to take key positions in the newly formed political and governmental structures. Tragedy struck in 1922 when [Gabriel Narutowicz](/wiki/Gabriel_Narutowicz), inaugural holder of the presidency, was assassinated at the [Zachęta](/wiki/Zach%C4%99ta) Gallery in Warsaw by a painter and right-wing nationalist [Eligiusz Niewiadomski](/wiki/Eligiusz_Niewiadomski).

In 1926, the [May Coup](/wiki/May_Coup_(Poland)), led by the hero of the Polish independence campaign Marshal [Józef Piłsudski](/wiki/J%C3%B3zef_Pi%C5%82sudski), turned rule of the Second Polish Republic over to the nonpartisan [Sanacja](/wiki/Sanacja) (*Healing*) movement to prevent radical political organisations on both the left and the right from destabilising the country. By the late 1930s, due to increased threats posed by political extremism inside the country, the Polish government became increasingly heavy-handed, banning a number of radical organisations, including communist and ultra-nationalist political parties, which threatened the stability of the country.

### World War II

Main articles: [History of Poland (1939–1945)](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1939%E2%80%931945)), [Invasion of Poland](/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland), [Military history of Poland during World War II](/wiki/Military_history_of_Poland_during_World_War_II), and [War crimes in occupied Poland during World War II](/wiki/War_crimes_in_occupied_Poland_during_World_War_II)

[Polish Army](/wiki/Polish_Armed_Forces_(Second_Polish_Republic)) [7TP](/wiki/7TP) tanks on military manoeuvres shortly before the [invasion of Poland](/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland) in 1939

World War II began with the [Nazi German](/wiki/Nazi_German) [invasion of Poland](/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland) on 1 September 1939, followed by the [Soviet invasion of Poland](/wiki/Soviet_invasion_of_Poland) on 17 September. As agreed in the [Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact](/wiki/Molotov%E2%80%93Ribbentrop_Pact), Poland was split into two zones, [one occupied by Nazi Germany](/wiki/Polish_areas_annexed_by_Nazi_Germany), the other by [the Soviet Union](/wiki/Territories_of_Poland_annexed_by_the_Soviet_Union). The [wartime resistance movement](/wiki/Polish_resistance_movement_in_World_War_II), and the [Armia Krajowa](/wiki/Armia_Krajowa) (*Home Army*), fought against the occupation. It was one of the three largest resistance movements of the entire war, and encompassed a range of clandestine activities, which functioned as an [underground state](/wiki/Polish_Underground_State) complete with [degree-awarding universities](/wiki/Education_in_Poland_during_World_War_II) and [a court system](/wiki/Underground_court). The resistance was loyal to the [Polish Government in Exile](/wiki/Polish_Government_in_Exile) and generally resented the idea of a post-war communist Poland; for this reason, in the summer of 1944 it initiated [Operation Tempest](/wiki/Operation_Tempest), of which the [Warsaw Uprising](/wiki/Warsaw_Uprising) that began on 1 August 1944 is the best-known operation.

Poland made the fourth-largest troop contribution in Europe, and its troops served both the exiled government in the [west](/wiki/Polish_Armed_Forces_in_the_West) and Soviet leadership in the [east](/wiki/Polish_Armed_Forces_in_the_East). Polish troops played an important role in the [Normandy](/wiki/Operation_Overlord), [Italian](/wiki/Italian_Campaign_(World_War_II)), [North African Campaigns](/wiki/North_African_Campaign) and [Netherlands](/wiki/Operation_Pheasant) and are particularly remembered for the [Battle of Britain](/wiki/Battle_of_Britain) and [Battle of Monte Cassino](/wiki/Battle_of_Monte_Cassino). Polish intelligence operatives proved extremely valuable to the Allies, providing much of the intelligence from Europe and beyond, [Polish code breakers](/wiki/Polish_Cipher_Bureau) were responsible for [cracking the Enigma cipher](/wiki/Cryptanalysis_of_the_Enigma) and Polish scientists participating in the [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project) were co-creators of the American [atomic bomb](/wiki/Atomic_bomb). In the east, the Soviet-backed [Polish 1st Army](/wiki/First_Polish_Army_(1944%E2%80%931945)) distinguished itself in the battles for [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw_Uprising) and [Berlin](/wiki/Battle_of_Berlin).

Map of [the Holocaust in German-occupied Poland](/wiki/The_Holocaust_in_occupied_Poland) with marked deportation routes, major [ghettos](/wiki/Jewish_ghettos_in_German-occupied_Poland) and Nazi [extermination camps](/wiki/Extermination_camps). The border in 1941 between [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany) and the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) is marked in red.

The actions undertaken by both occupiers within Polish territory were [genocidal](/wiki/Genocide). In 1939–1941, the Soviets deported hundreds of thousands of Poles and the Soviet [NKVD](/wiki/NKVD) executed thousands of Polish [prisoners of war](/wiki/Prisoners_of_war) ahead of [Operation Barbarossa](/wiki/Operation_Barbarossa), notably in the [Katyn massacre](/wiki/Katyn_massacre). Around 150,000 Polish civilians were killed by Soviets during the Soviet Union's occupation of eastern Poland ([Kresy](/wiki/Kresy)). Similarly, German planners had in November 1939 called for "the complete destruction of all Poles" and their fate as outlined in the *[Generalplan Ost](/wiki/Generalplan_Ost)*; six German [extermination camps](/wiki/Extermination_camp) were established in occupied Poland, including [Treblinka](/wiki/Treblinka_extermination_camp), [Majdanek](/wiki/Majdanek_concentration_camp) and [Auschwitz](/wiki/Auschwitz_concentration_camp). The Germans [transported millions of Jews](/wiki/Holocaust_train) from across occupied Europe to be murdered in those camps. Altogether, 3 million Polish Jews – approximately 90% of Poland's pre-war Jewry – and between 1.8 and 2.8 million ethnic Poles were killed, including up to 100,000 members of the Polish [intelligentsia](/wiki/Intelligentsia). The [Romani people](/wiki/Romani_people) were also exterminated. During the [Warsaw Uprising](/wiki/Warsaw_Uprising) alone, over 150,000 Polish civilians were killed, most were murdered by the Germans during the [Wola](/wiki/Wola_massacre) and [Ochota](/wiki/Ochota_massacre) massacres. At least 100,000 Poles were also murdered by the [Ukrainian Insurgent Army](/wiki/Ukrainian_Insurgent_Army) (UPA) between 1943 and 1944 in what became known as the [Wołyń Massacres](/wiki/Massacres_of_Poles_in_Volhynia_and_Eastern_Galicia).

[Of all the countries](/wiki/World_War_II_casualties) in the war, Poland lost the highest percentage of its citizens: around 6 million perished – more than one-sixth of Poland's pre-war population – [half of them](/wiki/Holocaust_in_Poland) Polish Jews. About 90% of deaths were non-military in nature. In 1945, Poland's borders [were shifted westwards](/wiki/Territorial_changes_of_Poland_immediately_after_World_War_II). Over two million Polish inhabitants of [Kresy](/wiki/Kresy) [were expelled](/wiki/Polish_population_transfers_(1944%E2%80%931946)) along the [Curzon Line](/wiki/Curzon_Line) by [Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin). The western border became the [Oder-Neisse line](/wiki/Oder-Neisse_line). As a result, Poland's territory was reduced by 20%, or 77,500 square kilometres (29,900 sq mi). The shift forced the migration of [millions of other people](/wiki/World_War_II_evacuation_and_expulsion), most of whom were Poles, Germans, Ukrainians, and Jews.

### Post-war communism

Main articles: [History of Poland (1945–1989)](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1945%E2%80%931989)), [Polish People's Republic](/wiki/Polish_People%27s_Republic), [History of Solidarity](/wiki/History_of_Solidarity), and [Polish Round Table Agreement](/wiki/Polish_Round_Table_Agreement)

*At [High Noon](/wiki/High_Noon), 4 June 1989*—political poster featuring [Gary Cooper](/wiki/Gary_Cooper) to encourage votes for the [Solidarity](/wiki/Solidarity_(Polish_trade_union)) party in the [1989 elections](/wiki/Contract_Sejm)

At the insistence of [Joseph Stalin](/wiki/Joseph_Stalin), the [Yalta Conference](/wiki/Yalta_Conference) sanctioned the formation of a new provisional pro-Communist coalition government in Moscow, which ignored the [Polish government-in-exile](/wiki/Polish_government-in-exile) based in London. This action angered many Poles who considered it a [betrayal](/wiki/Yalta_betrayal) by the Allies. In 1944, Stalin had made guarantees to [Churchill](/wiki/Winston_Churchill) and [Roosevelt](/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) that he would maintain Poland's sovereignty and allow democratic elections to take place. However, upon achieving victory in 1945, the elections organised by the occupying Soviet authorities were falsified and were used to provide a veneer of legitimacy for Soviet hegemony over Polish affairs. The Soviet Union instituted a new [communist](/wiki/Communist_state) government in Poland, analogous to much of the rest of the [Eastern Bloc](/wiki/Eastern_Bloc). [As elsewhere in Communist Europe](/wiki/Anti-Communism_in_the_Communist_Bloc), the Soviet influence over Poland was met with [armed resistance](/wiki/Cursed_soldiers) from the outset which continued into the 1950s.

Despite widespread objections, the new Polish government accepted the Soviet annexation of the pre-war eastern regions of Poland (in particular the cities of [Wilno](/wiki/Wilno) and [Lwów](/wiki/Lw%C3%B3w)) and agreed to the permanent garrisoning of [Red Army](/wiki/Red_Army) units on Poland's territory. Military alignment within the [Warsaw Pact](/wiki/Warsaw_Pact) throughout the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) came about as a direct result of this change in Poland's political culture. In the European scene, it came to characterise the full-fledged integration of Poland into the brotherhood of communist nations.

The new communist government took control with the adoption of the [Small Constitution](/wiki/Small_Constitution_of_1947) on 19 February 1947. The [Polish People's Republic](/wiki/Polish_People%27s_Republic) (*Polska Rzeczpospolita Ludowa*) [was officially proclaimed in 1952](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_Poland). In 1956, after the death of [Bolesław Bierut](/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_Bierut), the régime of [Władysław Gomułka](/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_Gomu%C5%82ka) became temporarily more liberal, freeing many people from prison and expanding some personal freedoms. [Collectivisation in the Polish People's Republic](/wiki/Collectivization_in_the_Polish_People%27s_Republic) failed. A similar situation repeated itself in the 1970s under [Edward Gierek](/wiki/Edward_Gierek), but most of the time persecution of [anti-communist opposition](/wiki/Anti-communist_resistance_in_Poland_(1944%E2%80%931989)) groups persisted. Despite this, Poland was at the time considered to be one of the least oppressive states of the Eastern Bloc.

Labour turmoil in 1980 led to the formation of the independent trade union "[Solidarity](/wiki/Solidarity_(Polish_trade_union))" ("*Solidarność*"), which over time became a political force. Despite persecution and imposition of [martial law in 1981](/wiki/Martial_law_in_Poland) by General [Wojciech Jaruzelski](/wiki/Wojciech_Jaruzelski), it eroded the dominance of the [Polish United Workers' Party](/wiki/Polish_United_Workers%27_Party) and by 1989 had triumphed in Poland's first [partially free and democratic parliamentary elections](/wiki/Contract_Sejm) since the end of the Second World War. [Lech Wałęsa](/wiki/Lech_Wa%C5%82%C4%99sa), a Solidarity candidate, eventually [won the presidency in 1990](/wiki/1990_Polish_presidential_election). The Solidarity movement heralded the [collapse of communist regimes and parties across Europe](/wiki/Revolutions_of_1989).

### Third Polish Republic

Main article: [History of Poland (1989–present)](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1989%E2%80%93present))

Flowers in front of the [Presidential Palace](/wiki/Presidential_Palace,_Warsaw) following the [death of Poland's top government officials](/wiki/2010_Polish_Air_Force_Tu-154_crash) in a plane crash on 10 April 2010

A [shock therapy](/wiki/Shock_therapy_(economics)) programme, initiated by [Leszek Balcerowicz](/wiki/Leszek_Balcerowicz) in the early 1990s, enabled the country to transform its [Soviet-style](/wiki/Soviet-style_socialism) [planned economy](/wiki/Planned_economy) into a [market economy](/wiki/Market_economy). As with other [post-communist countries](/wiki/Post-communism), Poland suffered temporary declines in social, economic, and living standards, but it became the first post-communist country to reach its pre-1989 [GDP levels](/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) as early as 1995, although the unemployment rate increased. Poland became a member of the [Visegrád Group](/wiki/Visegr%C3%A1d_Group) in 1991, and joined [NATO](/wiki/NATO) in 1999. Poles then voted to join the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union) in [a referendum](/wiki/2003_Polish_European_Union_membership_referendum) in June 2003, with [Poland becoming a full member](/wiki/Poland_in_the_European_Union) on 1 May 2004, following the [consequent enlargement of the union](/wiki/2004_enlargement_of_the_European_Union).

Poland joined the [Schengen Area](/wiki/Schengen_Area) in 2007, as a result of which, [the country's borders](/wiki/Borders_of_Poland) with other member states of the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union) were dismantled, allowing for [full freedom of movement](/wiki/Freedom_of_movement#European_Union) within most of the European Union. On 10 April 2010, the [President of Poland](/wiki/President_of_Poland) [Lech Kaczyński](/wiki/Lech_Kaczy%C5%84ski), along with 89 other high-ranking Polish officials [died in a plane crash](/wiki/2010_Polish_Air_Force_Tu-154_crash) near [Smolensk](/wiki/Smolensk), Russia.

In 2011, the ruling [Civic Platform](/wiki/Civic_Platform) won [parliamentary elections](/wiki/2011_Polish_parliamentary_election). In 2014, the [Prime Minister of Poland](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Poland), [Donald Tusk](/wiki/Donald_Tusk), was chosen to be [President of the European Council](/wiki/President_of_the_European_Council), and resigned as prime minister. The [2015](/wiki/2015_Polish_parliamentary_election) and [2019 elections](/wiki/2019_Polish_parliamentary_election) were won by the national-conservative [Law and Justice](/wiki/Law_and_Justice) Party (PiS) led by [Jarosław Kaczyński](/wiki/Jaros%C5%82aw_Kaczy%C5%84ski), resulting in increased [Euroscepticism](/wiki/Euroscepticism) and [increased friction](/wiki/Polish_constitutional_crisis) with the European Union. In December 2017, [Mateusz Morawiecki](/wiki/Mateusz_Morawiecki) was sworn in as the Prime Minister, succeeding [Beata Szydlo](/wiki/Beata_Szyd%C5%82o), in office since 2015. President [Andrzej Duda](/wiki/Andrzej_Duda), supported by Law and Justice party, was re-elected in the 2020 presidential [election](/wiki/2020_Polish_presidential_election).

As of November 2023, the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) had led to 17 million [Ukrainian refugees](/wiki/Ukrainian_refugee_crisis) crossing the border to Poland. As of November 2023, 0.9 million of those had stayed in Poland. In October 2023, the ruling Law and Justice (PiS) party won the largest share of the vote in the [election](/wiki/2023_Polish_parliamentary_election), but lost its majority in parliament. In December 2023, Donald Tusk became the new Prime Minister leading a coalition made up of [Civic Coalition](/wiki/Civic_Coalition_(Poland)), [Third Way](/wiki/Third_Way_(Poland)), and [The Left](/wiki/The_Left_(Poland)). Law and Justice became the leading opposition party.

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Poland](/wiki/Geography_of_Poland)

[Topographic](/wiki/Topography) map of Poland

Poland covers an administrative area of 312,722 km2 (120,743 sq mi), and is the [ninth-largest country in Europe](/wiki/List_of_European_countries_by_area). Approximately 311,895 km2 (120,423 sq mi) of the country's territory consists of land, 2,041 km2 (788 sq mi) is internal waters and 8,783 km2 (3,391 sq mi) is territorial sea. Topographically, the landscape of Poland is characterised by diverse [landforms](/wiki/Landform), [water bodies](/wiki/Hydrology) and [ecosystems](/wiki/Ecosystem). The central and northern region bordering the [Baltic Sea](/wiki/Baltic_Sea) lie within the flat [Central European Plain](/wiki/Northern_European_Plain), but its south is hilly and mountainous. The average [elevation above the sea level](/wiki/Height_above_sea_level) is estimated at 173 metres.

The country has a coastline spanning 770 km (480 mi); extending from the shores of the Baltic Sea, along the [Bay of Pomerania](/wiki/Bay_of_Pomerania) in the west to the [Gulf of Gdańsk](/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk_Bay) in the east. The beach coastline is abundant in [sand dune fields](/wiki/Sand_dunes) or [coastal ridges](/wiki/Beach_ridge) and is indented by [spits](/wiki/Spit_(landform)) and lagoons, notably the [Hel Peninsula](/wiki/Hel_Peninsula) and the [Vistula Lagoon](/wiki/Vistula_Lagoon), which is shared with Russia. The largest Polish island on the Baltic Sea is [Wolin](/wiki/Wolin), located within [Wolin National Park](/wiki/Wolin_National_Park). Poland also shares the [Szczecin Lagoon](/wiki/Szczecin_Lagoon) and the [Usedom](/wiki/Usedom) island with Germany.

[Morskie Oko](/wiki/Morskie_Oko) alpine lake in the [Tatra Mountains](/wiki/Tatra_Mountains), part of the [Carpathian massif](/wiki/Carpathian_Mountains)

The mountainous belt in the extreme south of Poland is divided into two major [mountain ranges](/wiki/Mountain_ranges); the [Sudetes](/wiki/Sudetes) in the west and the [Carpathians](/wiki/Carpathian_Mountains) in the east. The highest part of the Carpathian massif are the [Tatra Mountains](/wiki/Tatra_Mountains), extending along Poland's southern border. Poland's highest point is the northwestern summit of [Rysy](/wiki/Rysy) at 2,500 metres (8,202 ft) in elevation, located in the Tatras. The highest summit of the Sudetes massif is [Mount Śnieżka](/wiki/%C5%9Anie%C5%BCka) at 1,603.3 metres (5,260 ft), shared with the Czech Republic. The lowest point in Poland situated at [Marzęcino](/wiki/Marz%C4%99cino) on the [Vistula Fens](/wiki/Vistula_Fens), which is 2.2 metres (7.2 ft) below sea level.

In Poland, [Trójmorski Wierch](/wiki/Tr%C3%B3jmorski_Wierch) is one of the six tripoints of the [European watershed](/wiki/European_watershed).

Poland's [longest rivers](/wiki/Rivers_of_Poland) are the [Vistula](/wiki/Vistula_River), the [Oder](/wiki/Oder_River), the [Warta](/wiki/Warta), and the [Bug](/wiki/Bug_River). The country also possesses one of the highest densities of lakes in the world, numbering around ten thousand and mostly concentrated in the north-eastern region of [Masuria](/wiki/Masuria), within the [Masurian Lake District](/wiki/Masurian_Lake_District). The largest lakes, covering more than 100 square kilometres (39 sq mi), are [Śniardwy](/wiki/%C5%9Aniardwy) and [Mamry](/wiki/Lake_Mamry), and the deepest is [Lake Hańcza](/wiki/Ha%C5%84cza) at 108.5 metres (356 ft) in depth.

### Climate

Main article: [Geography of Poland § Climate](/wiki/Geography_of_Poland#Climate)

[Köppen-Geiger climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) map of Poland

The climate of Poland is [temperate transitional](/wiki/Temperateness), and varies from [oceanic](/wiki/Oceanic_climate) in the north-west to [continental](/wiki/Continental_climate) in the south-east. The mountainous southern fringes are situated within an [alpine climate](/wiki/Alpine_climate). Poland is characterised by warm summers, with a mean temperature of around 20 °C (68.0 °F) in July, and moderately cold winters averaging −1.2 °C (29.8 °F) in January. In summer, maximum temperatures can reach 40 °C (104.0 °F), while in winter in the Tatra Mountains and Bieszczady Mountains they can even drop below −30 °C (−22.0 °F).

The warmest and sunniest part of Poland is [Lower Silesia](/wiki/Lower_Silesia) in the southwest and the coldest region is the northeast corner, around [Suwałki](/wiki/Suwa%C5%82ki) and [Olecko](/wiki/Olecko) where the climate is affected by [cold fronts](/wiki/Cold_front) from [Scandinavia](/wiki/Scandinavia) and [Siberia](/wiki/Siberia). [Precipitation](/wiki/Precipitation_(meteorology)) is more frequent during the summer months, with highest rainfall recorded from June to September.

There is a considerable fluctuation in day-to-day weather and the arrival of a particular season can differ each year. [Climate change](/wiki/Climate_change) and other factors have further contributed to interannual [thermal anomalies](/wiki/Temperature_anomaly) and increased temperatures; the average annual air temperature between 2011 and 2020 was 9.33 °C (48.8 °F), around 1.11 °C higher than in the 2001–2010 period. Winters are also becoming increasingly drier, with less [snowfall](/wiki/Snowfall).

### Biodiversity

Main article: [Geography of Poland § Biodiversity](/wiki/Geography_of_Poland#Biodiversity)

The [European bison](/wiki/European_bison) (*żubr*), one of [Poland's national animals](/wiki/National_symbols_of_Poland), is commonly found at the ancient and [UNESCO](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Poland)-protected [Białowieża Forest](/wiki/Bia%C5%82owie%C5%BCa_Forest).

[Phytogeographically](/wiki/Phytogeography), Poland belongs to the Central European province of the [Circumboreal Region](/wiki/Circumboreal_Region) within the [Boreal Kingdom](/wiki/Boreal_Kingdom). The country has four [Palearctic ecoregions](/wiki/Palearctic_realm) – Central, Northern, Western European [temperate broadleaf and mixed forest](/wiki/Temperate_broadleaf_and_mixed_forest), and the [Carpathian montane conifer](/wiki/Carpathian_montane_conifer_forests). Forests occupy 31% of Poland's land area, the largest of which is the [Lower Silesian Wilderness](/wiki/Lower_Silesian_Wilderness). The most common [deciduous trees](/wiki/Deciduous_trees) found across the country are [oak](/wiki/Oak), [maple](/wiki/Maple), and [beech](/wiki/Beech); the most common conifers are [pine](/wiki/Pine), [spruce](/wiki/Spruce), and [fir](/wiki/Fir). An estimated 69% of all forests are [coniferous](/wiki/Coniferous).

The [flora](/wiki/Flora) and [fauna](/wiki/Fauna_of_Poland) in Poland is that of [Continental Europe](/wiki/Continental_Europe), with the [wisent](/wiki/European_bison), [white stork](/wiki/White_stork) and [white-tailed eagle](/wiki/White-tailed_eagle) designated as national animals, and the [red common poppy](/wiki/Papaver_rhoeas) being the unofficial floral emblem. Among the most protected species is the [European bison](/wiki/European_bison), Europe's heaviest land animal, as well as the [Eurasian beaver](/wiki/Eurasian_beaver), the [lynx](/wiki/Eurasian_lynx), the [gray wolf](/wiki/Gray_wolf) and the [Tatra chamois](/wiki/Tatra_chamois). The region was also home to the extinct [aurochs](/wiki/Aurochs), the last individual dying in Poland in 1627. Game animals such as [red deer](/wiki/Red_deer), [roe deer](/wiki/Roe_deer), and [wild boar](/wiki/Wild_boar) are found in most woodlands. Poland is also a significant breeding ground for [migratory birds](/wiki/Migratory_birds) and hosts around one quarter of the global population of white storks.

Around 315,100 hectares (1,217 sq mi), equivalent to 1% of Poland's territory, is protected within 23 [Polish national parks](/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_Poland), two of which – [Białowieża](/wiki/Bia%C5%82owie%C5%BCa_Forest) and [Bieszczady](/wiki/Bieszczady_National_Park) – are [UNESCO World Heritage Sites](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Poland). There are 123 areas designated as [landscape parks](/wiki/Landscape_Park_(Poland)), along with numerous [nature reserves](/wiki/Nature_reserve) and other [protected areas](/wiki/Protected_areas_of_Poland) under the [Natura 2000](/wiki/Natura_2000) network.

## Government and politics

Main article: [Politics of Poland](/wiki/Politics_of_Poland)

[Karol Nawrocki](/wiki/Karol_Nawrocki)
[President](/wiki/President_of_Poland)

[Donald Tusk](/wiki/Donald_Tusk)
[Prime Minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Poland)

Poland is a [unitary](/wiki/Unitary_state) [parliamentary republic](/wiki/Parliamentary_republic) and a [representative democracy](/wiki/Representative_democracy), with [a president](/wiki/President_of_the_Republic_of_Poland) as the [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state). The executive power is exercised further by the [Council of Ministers](/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_of_the_Republic_of_Poland) and the [prime minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_Republic_of_Poland) who acts as the [head of government](/wiki/Head_of_government). The council's individual members are selected by the prime minister, approved by parliament and sworn in by the president. The head of state is elected by [popular vote](/wiki/Direct_election) for a five-year term. The current president is [Karol Nawrocki](/wiki/Karol_Nawrocki) and the prime minister is [Donald Tusk](/wiki/Donald_Tusk).

Poland's [legislative](/wiki/Legislature) assembly is a [bicameral](/wiki/Bicameralism) parliament consisting of a 460-member lower house ([Sejm](/wiki/Sejm)) and a 100-member upper house ([Senate](/wiki/Senate_of_the_Republic_of_Poland)). The Sejm is elected under [proportional representation](/wiki/Proportional_representation) according to the [d'Hondt method](/wiki/D%27Hondt_method) for vote-seat conversion. The Senate is elected under the [first-past-the-post](/wiki/First-past-the-post) electoral system, with one senator being returned from each of the one hundred constituencies. The Senate has the right to amend or reject a statute passed by the Sejm, but the Sejm may override the Senate's decision with a majority vote.

The [Sejm](/wiki/Sejm) is the [lower house](/wiki/Lower_house) of the [parliament of Poland](/wiki/Parliament_of_Poland).

With the exception of ethnic minority parties, only candidates of [political parties](/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Poland) receiving at least 5% of the total national vote can enter the Sejm. Both the lower and upper houses of parliament in Poland are elected for a four-year term and each member of the Polish parliament is guaranteed [parliamentary immunity](/wiki/Parliamentary_immunity). Under current legislation, a person must be 21 years of age or over to assume the position of deputy, 30 or over to become senator and 35 to run in a presidential election.

Members of the Sejm and Senate jointly form the [National Assembly of the Republic of Poland](/wiki/National_Assembly_of_the_Republic_of_Poland). The National Assembly, headed by the [marshal of the Sejm](/wiki/Marshal_of_the_Sejm), or [marshal of the Senate](/wiki/Marshal_of_the_Senate) in their absence, is formed on three occasions – when a new president takes the [oath of office](/wiki/Oath_of_office); when an indictment against the president is brought to the [State Tribunal](/wiki/State_Tribunal_(Poland)); and in case a president's permanent incapacity to exercise his duties due to the state of his health is declared.

According to [International IDEA's](/wiki/International_Institute_for_Democracy_and_Electoral_Assistance) Global State of Democracy (GSoD) Indices and Democracy Tracker, Poland performs in the mid-range on overall democratic measures, with particular weaknesses in civic engagement and judicial independence.

### Administrative divisions

Main article: [Administrative divisions of Poland](/wiki/Administrative_divisions_of_Poland)

Poland is divided into 16 provinces or states known as [voivodeships](/wiki/Voivodeships_of_Poland). As of 2022, the voivodeships are subdivided into 380 counties (*[powiats](/wiki/Powiat)*), which are further fragmented into 2,477 municipalities (*[gminas](/wiki/Gmina)*). Major cities normally have the status of both *gmina* and *powiat*. The provinces are largely founded on the borders of [historic regions](/wiki/Polish_historical_regions), or named for individual cities. Administrative authority at the voivodeship level is shared between a government-appointed governor ([voivode](/wiki/Voivodes_of_Poland_(since_1999))), an elected regional assembly ([sejmik](/wiki/Voivodeship_sejmik)) and a [voivodeship marshal](/wiki/Voivodeship_marshal), an executive elected by the assembly.

### Law

Main article: [Law of Poland](/wiki/Law_of_Poland)

The [Constitution of 3 May](/wiki/Constitution_of_May_3,_1791) adopted in 1791 was the first modern constitution in Europe.

The [Constitution of Poland](/wiki/Constitution_of_Poland) is the enacted supreme law, and Polish judicature is based on the principle of civil rights, governed by the code of [civil law](/wiki/Civil_law_(legal_system)). The current democratic constitution was adopted by the [National Assembly of Poland](/wiki/National_Assembly_of_Poland) on 2 April 1997; it guarantees a [multi-party state](/wiki/Multi-party_state) with freedoms of religion, speech and gatherings, prohibits the practices of forced [medical experimentation](/wiki/Medical_experimentation), torture or [corporal punishment](/wiki/Corporal_punishment), and acknowledges the inviolability of the home, the right to form trade unions, and the right to [strike](/wiki/Strike_action).

The [judiciary](/wiki/Judiciary_of_Poland) in Poland is composed of the [Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Poland) as the country's highest judicial organ, the [Supreme Administrative Court](/wiki/Supreme_Administrative_Court_of_Poland) for the judicial control of public administration, Common Courts ([District](/wiki/District_Court), [Regional](/wiki/Regional_Court_(Poland)), [Appellate](/wiki/Appellate_court)) and the [Military Court](/wiki/Court-martial). The [Constitutional](/wiki/Constitutional_Tribunal_(Poland)) and State Tribunals are separate judicial bodies, which rule the constitutional liability of people holding the highest offices of state and supervise the compliance of [statutory law](/wiki/Statutory_law), thus protecting the Constitution. Judges are nominated by the [National Council of the Judiciary](/wiki/National_Council_of_the_Judiciary) and are appointed for life by the [president](/wiki/President_of_Poland). With the approval of the Senate, the Sejm appoints an [ombudsman](/wiki/Ombudsman) for a five-year term to guard the observance of social justice.

Poland has a low [homicide](/wiki/Homicide) rate at 0.7 murders per 100,000 people, as of 2018. Rape, assault and violent crime remain at a very low level. The country has imposed strict regulations on [abortion](/wiki/Abortion_in_Poland), which is permitted only in cases of [rape](/wiki/Rape), [incest](/wiki/Incest) or when the woman's life is in danger; [congenital disorder](/wiki/Congenital_disorder) is not covered by the law, prompting some women to seek abortion abroad.

Historically, the most significant Polish legal act is the [Constitution of 3 May 1791](/wiki/Constitution_of_3_May_1791). Instituted to redress long-standing political defects of the [federative](/wiki/Federation) [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth) and its [Golden Liberty](/wiki/Golden_Liberty), it was the first modern constitution in Europe and influenced many later [democratic movements](/wiki/Democratic_movement) across the globe. In 1918, the [Second Polish Republic](/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic) became one of the first countries to introduce universal [women's suffrage](/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage).

### Foreign relations

Main articles: [Foreign relations of Poland](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Poland) and [List of diplomatic missions of Poland](/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_of_Poland)

The [Ministry of Foreign Affairs](/wiki/Ministry_of_Foreign_Affairs_(Poland)), located in [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw)

Poland is a [middle power](/wiki/Middle_power) and a [regional power](/wiki/Regional_power) in Europe. It has a total of 53 representatives in the [European Parliament](/wiki/European_Parliament) as of 2024. [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw) serves as the headquarters for [Frontex](/wiki/Frontex), the European Union's agency for external border security as well as [ODIHR](/wiki/ODIHR), one of the principal institutions of the [OSCE](/wiki/Organization_for_Security_and_Co-operation_in_Europe). Apart from the European Union, Poland has been a member of [NATO](/wiki/NATO), the United Nations, the [World Trade Organization](/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) and regional alliances such as the [Visegrád Group](/wiki/Visegr%C3%A1d_Group), the [Weimar Triangle](/wiki/Weimar_Triangle) and the Polish-led [Three Seas Initiative](/wiki/Three_Seas_Initiative).

In recent years, Poland significantly strengthened its [relations](/wiki/Poland%E2%80%93United_States_relations) with the United States, becoming one of its closest [allies](/wiki/Alliance) and strategic partners in Europe. Historically, Poland maintained strong cultural and political ties with [Hungary](/wiki/Hungary%E2%80%93Poland_relations) and [France](/wiki/France%E2%80%93Poland_relations).

### Military

Main article: [Polish Armed Forces](/wiki/Polish_Armed_Forces)

[Polish Air Force](/wiki/Polish_Air_Force) [F-16s](/wiki/F-16_Fighting_Falcon), a single-engine [multirole](/wiki/Multirole_combat_aircraft) [fighter aircraft](/wiki/Fighter_aircraft)

The [amphibious](/wiki/Amphibious_vehicle) [infantry fighting vehicle](/wiki/Infantry_fighting_vehicle) [Borsuk](/wiki/Borsuk_(infantry_fighting_vehicle))

The Polish Armed Forces are composed of five branches – the [Land Forces](/wiki/Polish_Land_Forces), the [Navy](/wiki/Polish_Navy), the [Air Force](/wiki/Polish_Air_Force), the [Special Forces](/wiki/Special_forces_of_Poland) and the [Territorial Defence Force](/wiki/Territorial_Defence_Force_(Poland)). The military is subordinate to the [Ministry of National Defence of the Republic of Poland](/wiki/Ministry_of_National_Defence_of_the_Republic_of_Poland). However, its commander-in-chief in peacetime is the president, who nominates officers, the Minister for National Defence and the chief of staff. Polish military tradition is generally commemorated by the [Armed Forces Day](/wiki/Armed_Forces_Day_(Poland)), celebrated annually on 15 August. In July 2024, the Polish Armed Forces had a combined strength of 216,100 active soldiers, making it the largest standing army in the European Union and the third largest in [NATO](/wiki/NATO).

Poland ranks [14th in the world](/wiki/List_of_countries_with_highest_military_expenditures) in terms of military expenditures; the country allocated 4.12% of its total GDP on military spending, equivalent to approximately US$35 billion in 2024. From 2022, Poland initiated a programme of mass modernisation of its armed forces, in close cooperation with American, South Korean and local Polish [defence manufacturers](/wiki/Arms_industry) concentrated in the [Polish Armaments Group](/wiki/Polish_Armaments_Group). Also, the Polish military is set to increase its size to 250,000 enlisted and officers, and 50,000 Territorial Defence Force. According to [SIPRI](/wiki/SIPRI), the country exported €487 million worth of arms and armaments to foreign countries in 2020.

Compulsory [military service](/wiki/Conscription) for men, was discontinued in 2008. Polish military doctrine reflects the same defensive nature as that of its NATO partners and the country actively hosts NATO's [military exercises](/wiki/Military_exercises). The Polish Special Forces, consisting of six units ([Grom](/wiki/GROM_Military_Unit), [Formoza](/wiki/Formoza_Military_Unit), [Agat](/wiki/Agat_Military_Unit), [Nil](/wiki/Nil_Military_Unit), [Commandos Military Unit](/wiki/Commandos_Military_Unit), [Airborne Special Operations Unit](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powietrzna_Jednostka_Operacji_Specjalnych)), have the authority to command full-scale as well as joint NATO operations, with special forces from all NATO member states. Since 1953, the country has been a large contributor to various United Nations peacekeeping missions, and currently maintains military presence in the Middle East, Africa, the [Baltic states](/wiki/Baltic_states) and southeastern Europe. Moreover, there are approximately 10,000 troops from the [United States Armed Forces](/wiki/United_States_Armed_Forces) stationed across Poland. From 2024, Poland also provides mandatory [firearms training](/wiki/Gun_safety) for pupils in [primary](/wiki/Primary_school) and [secondary schools](/wiki/Secondary_school).

### Security, law enforcement and emergency services

A [Toyota Auris](/wiki/Toyota_Auris) patrol car belonging to the Polish [State Police Service](/wiki/Policja) (*Policja*)

Main articles: [Law enforcement in Poland](/wiki/Law_enforcement_in_Poland), [Emergency medical services in Poland](/wiki/Emergency_medical_services_in_Poland), and [State Fire Service](/wiki/State_Fire_Service)

Law enforcement in Poland is performed by several agencies which are subordinate to the [Ministry of Interior and Administration](/wiki/Ministry_of_Interior_and_Administration_(Poland)) – the [State Police](/wiki/Policja) (*Policja*), assigned to investigate crimes or transgression; the [Municipal City Guard](/wiki/City_Guard_(Poland)), which maintains [public order](/wiki/Public-order_crime); and several specialised agencies, such as the [Polish Border Guard](/wiki/Polish_Border_Guard). Municipal guards are primarily headed by provincial, regional or city councils; individual guards are not permitted to carry [firearms](/wiki/Firearms) unless instructed by the superior commanding officer. Security service personnel conduct regular patrols in both large urban areas or smaller suburban localities.

Emergency services in Poland consist of the [emergency medical services](/wiki/Emergency_medical_services_in_Poland), [search and rescue](/wiki/Search_and_rescue#Poland) units of the [Polish Armed Forces](/wiki/Polish_Armed_Forces) and [State Fire Service](/wiki/State_Fire_Service). Emergency medical services in Poland are operated by local and regional governments, but are a part of the centralised national agency – the [National Medical Emergency Service](/wiki/Emergency_medical_services_in_Poland) (*Państwowe Ratownictwo Medyczne*). Thanks to its location, Poland is a country essentially free from the threat of natural disasters such as [earthquakes](/wiki/Earthquakes), [volcanic eruptions](/wiki/Volcanic_eruptions), [tornadoes](/wiki/Tornadoes) and [tropical cyclones](/wiki/Tropical_cyclones). However, [floods](/wiki/Flood) have occurred in low-lying areas from time to time during periods of extreme rainfall, for example during the [2010 Central European floods](/wiki/2010_Central_European_floods).

The [Internal Security Agency](/wiki/Internal_Security_Agency) (ABW, or ISA in English) is the chief [counterintelligence instrument](/wiki/Intelligence_agency) safeguarding Poland's internal security, along with [Agencja Wywiadu](/wiki/Agencja_Wywiadu) (AW) which identifies threats and collects secret information abroad. The [Central Investigation Bureau of Police](/wiki/Centralne_Biuro_%C5%9Aledcze_Policji) (CBŚP) and the [Central Anticorruption Bureau](/wiki/Central_Anticorruption_Bureau) (CBA) are responsible for countering organised crime and corruption in state and private institutions.

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Poland](/wiki/Economy_of_Poland)

Poland has a [social market economy](/wiki/Social_market_economy) and is a regional [economic power](/wiki/Economic_power) in [East-Central Europe](/wiki/East-Central_Europe). As of 2025, the country's gross domestic product (GDP) is the sixth largest in the European Union by [nominal standards](/wiki/Economy_of_the_European_Union), the fifth largest by [purchasing power parity](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_in_Europe_by_GDP_(PPP)), and ranks [20th globally](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)). It is one of the fastest growing within the Union and reached a [developed market](/wiki/Developed_market) status in 2018. The unemployment rate published by [Eurostat](/wiki/Eurostat) in 2023 amounted to 2.8%, which was the second-lowest in the EU. As of 2023, around 62% of the employed population works in the [service sector](/wiki/Tertiary_sector_of_the_economy), 29% in manufacturing, and 8% in the agricultural sector, thus manifesting a highly [diversified economy](/wiki/Economic_diversity). Although Poland is a member of the [European single market](/wiki/European_single_market), the country has not adopted the [Euro](/wiki/Euro) as legal tender and maintains its own currency – the [Polish złoty](/wiki/Polish_z%C5%82oty) (zł, PLN).

[Gdańsk](/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk), located on the [Baltic](/wiki/Baltic_Sea) in the north of Poland, is a major [seaport](/wiki/Seaport) as well as an economic and tourist zone.

Poland is a regional European leader in terms of [foreign direct investment](/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment) and possesses around 40 percent of the 500 biggest companies in the region by revenues whilst maintaining a [high globalisation rate](/wiki/Globalisation_index) and relatively high economic competitiveness. The country's largest firms compose the [WIG20](/wiki/WIG20) and [WIG30](/wiki/WIG30) [stock market indexes](/wiki/Stock_market_index), which are traded on the [Warsaw Stock Exchange](/wiki/Warsaw_Stock_Exchange). The [Central Statistical Office](/wiki/Central_Statistical_Office_(Poland)) estimated that in 2014 there were 1,437 Polish corporations with interests in 3,194 foreign entities. Poland also has the largest banking sector in [East-Central Europe](/wiki/East-Central_Europe), with 32.3 branches per 100,000 adults. The monetary policy is determined by the [National Bank of Poland](/wiki/National_Bank_of_Poland) (NBP), which controls the issuing of the national currency. It was the only European economy to have avoided the [recession of 2008](/wiki/Great_Recession). Since 2019, workers under the age of 26 are exempt from paying the [income tax](/wiki/Income_tax).

The country is the [19th largest exporter](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_exports) of goods and [services](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_service_exports) in the world. Exports of goods and services are valued at approximately 58% of GDP, as of 2023. Poland's largest trade partners are Germany, the United Kingdom, the [Czech Republic](/wiki/Czech_Republic), France, Italy, the [Netherlands](/wiki/Netherlands), and the [United States](/wiki/United_States). Among its lead exports are motor cars, buses, and vehicle-related accessories, machinery, [electronics](/wiki/Electronics), [electric batteries](/wiki/Electric_batteries), [home appliances](/wiki/Home_appliance), furniture, cosmetics, military equipment, and [tobacco](/wiki/Tobacco) as well as materials such as silver, [copper](/wiki/Copper), [steel](/wiki/Steel), [coal](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_coal_production), [zinc](/wiki/Zinc), [tar](/wiki/Tar), and [coke](/wiki/Coke_(fuel)). In 2023, the country produced 1300 tonnes of silver and was the 5th largest silver producer globally. As of 2025, Poland holds the world's 12th largest [gold reserve](/wiki/Gold_reserve), estimated at 509 tonnes.

### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in Poland](/wiki/Tourism_in_Poland)

[Malbork Castle](/wiki/Malbork_Castle) is the largest castle in the world measured by land area and a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Poland).

In 2020, the total value of the [tourism industry](/wiki/Tourism) in Poland was 104.3 billion [PLN](/wiki/Polish_z%C5%82oty), then equivalent to 4.5% of the Polish GDP. Tourism contributes considerably to the overall economy and makes up a relatively large proportion of the country's service market. Nearly 200,000 people were employed in the [accommodation and catering](/wiki/Hospitality_industry) (hospitality) sector in 2020. In 2021, Poland ranked [12th most visited country](/wiki/World_Tourism_rankings) in the world by international arrivals.

Tourist attractions in Poland range from the mountains in the south to the beaches on the [Baltic Sea](/wiki/Baltic_Sea) in the north. Many trail of rich architectural and cultural heritage. Among the most recognisable landmarks are Old Towns in [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w_Old_Town), [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw_Old_Town), [Wrocław](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw) ([dwarf statues](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw%27s_dwarfs)), [Gdańsk](/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk), [Poznań](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84), [Lublin](/wiki/Lublin), [Toruń](/wiki/Toru%C5%84) and [Zamość](/wiki/Zamo%C5%9B%C4%87) as well as museums, zoological gardens, theme parks and the [Wieliczka Salt Mine](/wiki/Wieliczka_Salt_Mine), with its labyrinthine tunnels, [underground lake](/wiki/Underground_lake) and chapels carved by miners out of [rock salt](/wiki/Rock_salt) beneath the ground. There are [over 100 castles](/wiki/List_of_castles_in_Poland) in the country, largely within the [Lower Silesian Voivodeship](/wiki/Lower_Silesian_Voivodeship) (including the Piast Castles Trail), and also on the [Trail of the Eagles' Nests](/wiki/Trail_of_the_Eagles%27_Nests); the largest castle in the world by land area is situated in [Malbork](/wiki/Malbork_Castle). The German [Auschwitz concentration camp](/wiki/Auschwitz_concentration_camp) in [Oświęcim](/wiki/O%C5%9Bwi%C4%99cim), and the [Skull Chapel](/wiki/Skull_Chapel) in [Kudowa-Zdrój](/wiki/Kudowa-Zdr%C3%B3j) constitute [dark tourism](/wiki/Dark_tourism). Regarding nature based travel, notable sites include the [Masurian Lake District](/wiki/Masurian_Lake_District) and [Białowieża Forest](/wiki/Bia%C5%82owie%C5%BCa_Forest) in the east; on the south [Karkonosze](/wiki/Karkonosze), the [Table Mountains](/wiki/Table_Mountains), the [Pieniny](/wiki/Pieniny) and the [Tatra Mountains](/wiki/Tatra_Mountains), where [Rysy](/wiki/Rysy) and the [Eagle's Path](/wiki/Orla_Per%C4%87) trail are located. The [Bieszczady Mountains](/wiki/Bieszczady_Mountains) lie in the extreme south-east.

### Transport

Main article: [Transport in Poland](/wiki/Transport_in_Poland)

[PKP Intercity](/wiki/Polish_State_Railways) [Pendolino](/wiki/New_Pendolino) at the [Wrocław railway station](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_G%C5%82%C3%B3wny_railway_station)

Transport in Poland is provided by means of [rail](/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Poland), [road](/wiki/Highways_in_Poland), [marine shipping](/wiki/Polish_Merchant_Navy) and [air travel](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_Poland). The country is part of EU's [Schengen Area](/wiki/Schengen_Area) and is an important transport hub due to its strategic geographical position in Central Europe. Some of the longest European routes, including the [E30](/wiki/European_route_E30) and [E40](/wiki/European_route_E40), run through Poland. The country has a good network of [highways](/wiki/Highways_in_Poland) consisting of [express roads](/wiki/Limited-access_road) and [motorways](/wiki/Controlled-access_highway). As of August 2023, Poland has the world's [21st-largest road network](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_road_network_size), maintaining over 5,000 km (3,100 mi) of highways in use. In larger cities, [public transport](/wiki/Public_transport) is heavily utilised; Poland is one of Europe's largest bus and tram manufacturers, and hosts one of the biggest [tram and light rail transit systems](/wiki/List_of_largest_tram_and_light_rail_transit_systems_ever).

In 2022, the nation had 19,393 kilometres (12,050 mi) of railway track, the third longest in the European Union after Germany and France. The [Polish State Railways](/wiki/Polish_State_Railways) (PKP) is the dominant railway operator, with certain major voivodeships or urban areas possessing their own [commuter](/wiki/Commuter_rail) and [regional rail](/wiki/Regional_rail). Poland has a number of international airports, the largest of which is [Warsaw Chopin Airport](/wiki/Warsaw_Chopin_Airport). It is the primary global hub for [LOT Polish Airlines](/wiki/LOT_Polish_Airlines), the country's [flag carrier](/wiki/Flag_carrier).

Seaports exist all along Poland's Baltic coast, with most freight operations using [Świnoujście](/wiki/Port_of_%C5%9Awinouj%C5%9Bcie), [Police](/wiki/Port_of_Police), [Szczecin](/wiki/Port_of_Szczecin), [Kołobrzeg](/wiki/Port_of_Ko%C5%82obrzeg), [Gdynia](/wiki/Port_of_Gdynia), [Gdańsk](/wiki/Port_of_Gda%C5%84sk) and [Elbląg](/wiki/Elbl%C4%85g) as their base. The [Port of Gdańsk](/wiki/Port_of_Gda%C5%84sk) is the only port in the [Baltic Sea](/wiki/Baltic_Sea) adapted to receive oceanic vessels. [Polferries](/wiki/Polferries) and [Unity Line](/wiki/Unity_Line) are the largest Polish ferry operators, with the latter providing [roll-on/roll-off](/wiki/Roll-on/roll-off) and [train ferry](/wiki/Train_ferry) services to [Scandinavia](/wiki/Scandinavia).

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in Poland](/wiki/Energy_in_Poland)

The electricity generation sector in Poland is largely [fossil-fuel](/wiki/Fossil_fuel)–based. Coal production in Poland is a major source of employment and the largest source of the nation's [greenhouse gas emissions](/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions). Many power plants nationwide use Poland's position as a major European exporter of coal to their advantage by continuing to use coal as the primary raw material in the production of their energy. The three largest Polish coal mining firms ([Węglokoks](/wiki/W%C4%99glokoks), [Kompania Węglowa](/wiki/Kompania_W%C4%99glowa) and [JSW](/wiki/Jastrz%C4%99bska_Sp%C3%B3%C5%82ka_W%C4%99glowa)) extract around 100 million tonnes of coal annually. After coal, Polish energy supply relies significantly on [natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas) and oil.

The new [Energy Policy of Poland until 2040](/wiki/Energy_in_Poland) (EPP2040) would reduce the share of coal and [lignite](/wiki/Lignite) in electricity generation by 25% from 2017 to 2030. The plan involves deploying new nuclear plants, increasing energy efficiency, and decarbonising the Polish transport system in order to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and prioritise long-term energy security. In March 2026, [PEJ](/wiki/Polskie_Elektrownie_J%C4%85drowe) submitted an application for a permit to build a nuclear power plant.

### Science and technology

Main articles: [Timeline of Polish science and technology](/wiki/Timeline_of_Polish_science_and_technology) and [List of Polish Nobel laureates](/wiki/List_of_Polish_Nobel_laureates)

Physicist and chemist [Maria Skłodowska-Curie](/wiki/Maria_Sk%C5%82odowska-Curie) was the first person to win two [Nobel Prizes](/wiki/Nobel_Prizes).

Astronomer [Nicolaus Copernicus](/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus) formulated the [heliocentric](/wiki/Heliocentrism) model of the [Solar System](/wiki/Solar_System).

Over the course of history, the Polish people have made considerable contributions in the fields of science, technology and mathematics. Perhaps the most renowned Pole to support this theory was [Nicolaus Copernicus](/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus), who triggered the [Copernican Revolution](/wiki/Copernican_Revolution) by placing the [Sun rather than the Earth at the centre of the universe](/wiki/Heliocentrism). He also derived a [quantity theory of money](/wiki/Quantity_theory_of_money), which made him a pioneer of economics. Copernicus' achievements and discoveries are considered the basis of Polish culture and cultural identity. Poland was ranked 39th in the [Global Innovation Index](/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2025.

Poland's tertiary education institutions; traditional [universities](/wiki/List_of_universities_in_Poland), as well as technical, medical, and economic institutions, employ around tens of thousands of researchers and staff members. There are hundreds of research and development institutes. However, in the 19th and 20th centuries many Polish scientists worked abroad; one of the most important of these exiles was [Maria Skłodowska-Curie](/wiki/Maria_Sk%C5%82odowska-Curie), a physicist and chemist who lived much of her life in France. In 1925, she established Poland's [Radium Institute](/wiki/Curie_Institute_(Warsaw)).

In the first half of the 20th century, Poland was a flourishing centre of mathematics. Outstanding Polish mathematicians formed the [Lwów School of Mathematics](/wiki/Lw%C3%B3w_School_of_Mathematics) (with [Stefan Banach](/wiki/Stefan_Banach), [Stanisław Mazur](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Mazur), [Hugo Steinhaus](/wiki/Hugo_Steinhaus), [Stanisław Ulam](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Ulam)) and [Warsaw School of Mathematics](/wiki/Warsaw_School_of_Mathematics) (with [Alfred Tarski](/wiki/Alfred_Tarski), [Kazimierz Kuratowski](/wiki/Kazimierz_Kuratowski), [Wacław Sierpiński](/wiki/Wac%C5%82aw_Sierpi%C5%84ski) and [Antoni Zygmund](/wiki/Antoni_Zygmund)). Numerous mathematicians, scientists, chemists or economists emigrated due to historic vicissitudes, among them [Benoit Mandelbrot](/wiki/Benoit_Mandelbrot), [Leonid Hurwicz](/wiki/Leonid_Hurwicz), [Alfred Tarski](/wiki/Alfred_Tarski), [Joseph Rotblat](/wiki/Joseph_Rotblat) and Nobel Prize laureates [Roald Hoffmann](/wiki/Roald_Hoffmann), [Georges Charpak](/wiki/Georges_Charpak) and [Tadeusz Reichstein](/wiki/Tadeusz_Reichstein).

## Demographics

Main articles: [Demographics of Poland](/wiki/Demographics_of_Poland), [List of cities and towns in Poland](/wiki/List_of_cities_and_towns_in_Poland), [Metropolitan areas in Poland](/wiki/Metropolitan_areas_in_Poland), [Polish people](/wiki/Polish_people), and [Polish diaspora](/wiki/Polish_diaspora)

[Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw) is the largest [city in Poland](/wiki/List_of_cities_and_towns_in_Poland), and an [economic hub of the country](/wiki/Economy_of_Poland).

Poland has a population of approximately 38.2 million as of 2021, and is the [ninth-most populous country](/wiki/List_of_European_countries_by_population) in Europe, as well as the fifth-most populous member state of the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union). It has a population density of 122 inhabitants per square kilometre (320 inhabitants/mi2). The [total fertility rate](/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) was estimated at 1.2 children born to a woman in 2023, which is [among the world's lowest](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_and_dependencies_by_total_fertility_rate). Furthermore, Poland's population is [aging significantly](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_median_age), and the country has a [median age](/wiki/Median_age) of 42.2.

Around 60% of the country's population lives in urban areas or major cities and 40% in rural zones. In 2020, 50.2% of Poles resided in [detached dwellings](/wiki/Detached_dwelling) and 44.3% in apartments. The most populous administrative province or state is the [Masovian Voivodeship](/wiki/Masovian_Voivodeship) and the most populous city is the capital, [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw), at 1.8 million inhabitants with a further 2–3 million people living in its [metropolitan area](/wiki/Warsaw_metropolitan_area). The [metropolitan area](/wiki/Katowice_urban_area) of [Katowice](/wiki/Katowice) is the largest urban [conurbation](/wiki/Conurbation) with a population between 2.7 million and 5.3 million residents. Population density is higher in the south of Poland and mostly concentrated in [Upper Silesia](/wiki/Upper_Silesia), between the cities of [Wrocław](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw) and [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w).

The [metropolitan area](/wiki/Katowice_urban_area) of [Katowice](/wiki/Katowice) is the largest urban [conurbation](/wiki/Conurbation), with [Upper Silesia](/wiki/Upper_Silesia) being the most densely populated region in Poland.

In the [2011 Polish census](/wiki/Polish_census_of_2011), 37,310,341 people reported [Polish](/wiki/Polish_people) identity, 846,719 [Silesian](/wiki/Silesians), 232,547 [Kashubian](/wiki/Kashubians) and 147,814 [German](/wiki/German_minority_in_Poland). Other [identities](/wiki/Polish_people#National_minorities) were reported by 163,363 people (0.41%) and 521,470 people (1.35%) did not specify any nationality. Official population statistics do not include migrant workers who do not possess a permanent residency permit or [Karta Polaka](/wiki/Karta_Polaka). More than 1.7 million [Ukrainian](/wiki/Ukrainians) citizens worked legally in Poland in 2017. The number of migrants is rising steadily; the country approved 504,172 work permits for foreigners in 2021 alone.

According to the 2021 census, ethnic Poles comprise 98.84% of the population, including people who declared Polish heritage alone (96.28%) or both Polish and another ethnicity (2.56%) as responders were allowed to select up to two ethnicities. People who declared only non-Polish ethnicities made up 1.13% of the population and people who did not report their ethnicity numbered 0.03%. The province with the highest percentage of ethnic Poles was the [Holy Cross Voivodeship](/wiki/Holy_Cross_Voivodeship) (99.70%), and the region with the lowest share of ethnic Poles was the [Silesian Voivodeship](/wiki/Silesian_Voivodeship) (95.49%).

### Languages

Main articles: [Polish language](/wiki/Polish_language), [Languages of Poland](/wiki/Languages_of_Poland), and [Bilingual communes in Poland](/wiki/Bilingual_communes_in_Poland)

*[Dolina Jadwigi](/wiki/Dolina_Jadwigi)*—a [bilingual](/wiki/Bilingual) Polish-[Kashubian](/wiki/Kashubian_language) road sign with the village name

[Polish](/wiki/Polish_language) is the [official](/wiki/Official_language) and predominant spoken language in Poland, and is one of the official [languages of the European Union](/wiki/Languages_of_the_European_Union). It is also a [second language](/wiki/Second_language) in parts of neighbouring [Lithuania](/wiki/Lithuania), where it is taught in Polish-minority schools. Contemporary Poland is a linguistically [homogeneous](/wiki/Homogeneous) nation, with 97% of respondents declaring Polish as their mother tongue. There are currently 15 minority languages in Poland, including one recognised regional language, [Kashubian](/wiki/Kashubian_language), which is spoken by approximately 100,000 people on a daily basis in the northern regions of [Kashubia](/wiki/Kashubia) and [Pomerania](/wiki/Pomerania). Poland also recognises [secondary administrative languages or auxiliary languages in bilingual municipalities](/wiki/Bilingual_communes_in_Poland), where bilingual signs and placenames are commonplace. According to the [Centre for Public Opinion Research](/wiki/Centre_for_Public_Opinion_Research), around 32% of Polish citizens declared knowledge of the English language in 2015.

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in Poland](/wiki/Religion_in_Poland)

[John Paul II](/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II), born Karol Wojtyła, held the papacy between 1978 and 2005 and was the first [Pole](/wiki/Polish_people) to become [Pope](/wiki/Pope).

According to the 2021 census, 71.3% of all Polish citizens adhere to the [Catholic Church](/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Poland), with 6.9% identifying as having no religion and 20.6% refusing to answer.

Poland is one of the [most religious countries in Europe](/wiki/Religion_in_Europe), where Catholicism remains a part of national identity and Polish-born [Pope John Paul II](/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II) is widely revered. In 2015, 61.6% of respondents outlined that religion is of high or very high importance. However, church attendance has greatly decreased in recent years; only 28% of Catholics attended [mass](/wiki/Mass_(liturgy)) weekly in 2021, down from around half in 2000. According to *[The Wall Street Journal](/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal)*, "Of [the] more than 100 countries studied by the *[Pew Research Center](/wiki/Pew_Research_Center)* in 2018, Poland was [secularising](/wiki/Secularization) the fastest, as measured by the disparity between the religiosity of young people and their elders."

Freedom of religion in Poland is guaranteed by the Constitution, and Poland's [concordat](/wiki/Concordat) with the [Holy See](/wiki/Holy_See) enables the teaching of religion in public schools. Historically, the Polish state maintained a high degree of [religious tolerance](/wiki/Warsaw_Confederation) and provided asylum for refugees fleeing religious persecution in other parts of Europe. Poland hosted Europe's largest [Jewish diaspora](/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Poland), and the country was a centre of [Ashkenazi Jewish](/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jewish) culture and traditional learning until the [Holocaust](/wiki/The_Holocaust).

Contemporary religious minorities include [Eastern Orthodox Church](/wiki/Polish_Orthodox_Church), [Protestants](/wiki/Protestantism), including [Lutherans](/wiki/Lutheranism) of the [Evangelical-Augsburg Church](/wiki/Evangelical-Augsburg_Church_in_Poland), [Pentecostals](/wiki/Pentecostalism) in the [Pentecostal Church in Poland](/wiki/Pentecostal_Church_in_Poland), [Adventists](/wiki/Adventists) in the [Seventh-day Adventist Church](/wiki/Seventh-day_Adventist_Church). Other smaller Christian denominations include [Eastern Catholics](/wiki/Eastern_Catholic_Churches), [Mariavites](/wiki/Mariavite_Church), [Evangelicalism](/wiki/Evangelicalism) denominations. Other religions include [Jehovah's Witnesses](/wiki/Jehovah%27s_Witnesses), [Jews](/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Poland), [Muslims](/wiki/Islam_in_Poland) ([Tatars](/wiki/Tatars)), and [neopagans](/wiki/Modern_Paganism), some of whom are members of the [Native Polish Church](/wiki/Native_Polish_Church).

### Health

Main article: [Health in Poland](/wiki/Health_in_Poland)

Medical service providers and [hospitals](/wiki/Hospitals) in Poland are subordinate to the [Ministry of Health](/wiki/Ministry_of_Health_of_the_Republic_of_Poland); it provides administrative oversight and scrutiny of general medical practice and is obliged to maintain a high standard of [hygiene](/wiki/Hygiene) and patient care. Poland has a [universal healthcare system](/wiki/Universal_health_care) based on an all-inclusive [insurance system](/wiki/Health_insurance); state subsidised healthcare is available to all citizens covered by the general health insurance programme of the [National Health Fund](/wiki/National_Health_Fund) (NFZ). Private medical complexes exist nationwide; over 50% of the population uses both public and private sectors.

According to the [Human Development Report](/wiki/HDI) from 2020, the average life expectancy at birth is 79 years (around 75 years for an infant male and 83 years for an infant female); the country has a low [infant mortality rate](/wiki/Infant_mortality_rate) (4 per 1,000 births). In 2019, the principal cause of death was [ischemic heart disease](/wiki/Ischemic_heart_disease); diseases of the [circulatory system](/wiki/Circulatory_system) accounted for 45% of all deaths. In the same year, Poland was also the 15th-largest importer of [medications](/wiki/Medications) and pharmaceutical products.

### Education

Main articles: [Education in Poland](/wiki/Education_in_Poland) and [Universities in Poland](/wiki/Universities_in_Poland)

[Jagiellonian University](/wiki/Jagiellonian_University) in [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w), one of the world's oldest institutions of higher learning

The [Jagiellonian University](/wiki/Jagiellonian_University) founded in 1364 by [Casimir III](/wiki/Casimir_III_the_Great) in [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w) was the first institution of higher learning established in Poland, and is one of the [oldest universities](/wiki/List_of_oldest_universities_in_continuous_operation) still in continuous operation. Poland's [Commission of National Education](/wiki/Commission_of_National_Education) (*Komisja Edukacji Narodowej*), established in 1773, was the world's first state ministry of education. In 2018, the [Programme for International Student Assessment](/wiki/Programme_for_International_Student_Assessment), coordinated by the [Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development](/wiki/Organisation_for_Economic_Co-operation_and_Development), placed Poland's educational output as one of the highest in the OECD, ranking 5th by student attainment and 6th by student performance in 2022.

The framework for primary, secondary and higher tertiary education are established by the [Ministry of Education and Science](/wiki/Ministry_of_National_Education_(Poland)). One year of kindergarten is [compulsory](/wiki/Compulsory_education) for six-year-olds. Primary education traditionally begins at the age of seven, although children aged six can attend at the request of their parents or guardians. Elementary school spans eight grades and secondary schooling is dependent on student preference – a four-year high school (*[liceum](/wiki/General_education_liceum)*), a five-year technical school (*[technikum](/wiki/Technikum_(Polish_education))*) or various [vocational studies](/wiki/Vocational_school) (*szkoła branżowa*) can be pursued by individual pupils. A liceum or technikum is concluded with a maturity exit exam (*[matura](/wiki/Matura)*), which must be passed in order to apply for a university or other institutions of higher learning.

In Poland, there are over 500 university-level institutions, with numerous faculties. The [University of Warsaw](/wiki/University_of_Warsaw) and [Warsaw Polytechnic](/wiki/Warsaw_Polytechnic), the [University of Wrocław](/wiki/University_of_Wroc%C5%82aw), [Adam Mickiewicz University in Poznań](/wiki/Adam_Mickiewicz_University_in_Pozna%C5%84) and the [University of Technology in Gdańsk](/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk_University_of_Technology) are among the most prominent. There are three conventional [academic degrees](/wiki/Academic_degree) in Poland – *[licencjat](/wiki/Licentiate_(degree))* or *[inżynier](/wiki/Bachelor%27s_degree)* (first cycle), *[magister](/wiki/Magister_(degree))* (second cycle) and *[doktor](/wiki/PhD)* (third cycle qualification).

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Poland](/wiki/Culture_of_Poland)

The [Polish White Eagle](/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Poland) is Poland's enduring [national and cultural symbol](/wiki/National_symbols_of_Poland).

The culture of Poland is closely connected with its intricate 1,000-year [history](/wiki/History_of_Poland), and forms an important constituent in the [Western civilisation](/wiki/Western_world). The Poles take great pride in their national identity which is often associated with the colours white and red, and exuded by the expression *biało-czerwoni* ("whitereds"). National symbols, chiefly the crowned [white-tailed eagle](/wiki/White-tailed_eagle), are often visible on clothing, insignia and emblems. The architectural monuments of great importance are protected by the [National Heritage Board of Poland](/wiki/National_Heritage_Board_of_Poland). Over 100 of the country's most significant tangible wonders were enlisted onto the [Historic Monuments Register](/wiki/List_of_Historic_Monuments_(Poland)), with further 17 being recognised by [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) as World Heritage Sites.

### Holidays and traditions

See also: [Christmas in Poland](/wiki/Christmas_in_Poland)

[All Saints' Day](/wiki/All_Saints%27_Day) on 1 November is one of the most important public holidays in Poland.

There are 14 government-approved annual public holidays – New Year on 1 January, [Three Kings' Day](/wiki/Epiphany_(holiday)) on 6 January, [Easter Sunday](/wiki/Easter_Sunday) and [Easter Monday](/wiki/Easter_Monday), [Labour Day](/wiki/International_Workers%27_Day) on 1 May, [Constitution Day](/wiki/3_May_Constitution_Day) on 3 May, [Pentecost](/wiki/Pentecost), [Corpus Christi](/wiki/Feast_of_Corpus_Christi), [Feast of the Assumption](/wiki/Assumption_of_Mary) on 15 August, [All Saints' Day](/wiki/All_Saints%27_Day) on 1 November, [Independence Day](/wiki/Independence_Day_(Poland)) on 11 November and Christmastide on 24, 25 and 26 December.

Particular traditions and superstitious customs observed in Poland are not found elsewhere in Europe. Though Christmas Eve (*[Wigilia](/wiki/Wigilia)*) had not been a public holiday until 2025, it remains the most memorable day of the entire year. [Trees](/wiki/Christmas_trees) are decorated on 24 December, hay is placed under the tablecloth to resemble Jesus' [manger](/wiki/Manger), [Christmas wafers](/wiki/Christmas_wafer) (*opłatek*) are shared between gathered guests and a [twelve-dish meatless supper](/wiki/Twelve-dish_Christmas_Eve_supper) is served that same evening when the [first star](/wiki/Star_of_Bethlehem) appears. An empty plate and seat are symbolically left at the table for an unexpected guest. On occasion, [carolers](/wiki/Christmas_carols) journey around smaller towns with a folk [Turoń](/wiki/Turo%C5%84) creature until the [Lent](/wiki/Lent) period.

A widely popular [doughnut](/wiki/P%C4%85czki) and sweet pastry feast occurs on [Fat Thursday](/wiki/Fat_Thursday), usually 52 days prior to Easter. [Eggs](/wiki/Easter_egg) for [Holy Sunday](/wiki/Easter_Sunday) are painted and placed in decorated [baskets](/wiki/%C5%9Awi%C4%99conka) that are previously blessed by clergymen in churches on [Easter Saturday](/wiki/Easter_Saturday). Easter Monday is celebrated with pagan *[dyngus](/wiki/%C5%9Amigus-dyngus)* festivities, where the youth is engaged in water fights. Cemeteries and graves of the deceased are annually visited by family members on All Saints' Day; tombstones are cleaned as a sign of respect and candles are lit to honour the dead on an unprecedented scale.

### Music and dance

Main article: [Music of Poland](/wiki/Music_of_Poland)

[Frédéric Chopin](/wiki/Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric_Chopin) was a renowned classical composer and virtuoso pianist.

[Artur Rubinstein](/wiki/Arthur_Rubinstein) was one of the greatest concert pianists of the 20th century.

Polish [classical music](/wiki/Classical_music) encompasses medieval and early-modern [liturgical tradition](/wiki/Liturgical_music), regional [folk tunes](/wiki/Folk_music), and a world-renowned [piano](/wiki/Piano) legacy. Among the leading classical artists were [Frédéric Chopin](/wiki/Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric_Chopin), [Artur Rubinstein](/wiki/Artur_Rubinstein), [Ignacy Jan Paderewski](/wiki/Ignacy_Jan_Paderewski), [Krzysztof Penderecki](/wiki/Krzysztof_Penderecki), [Henryk Wieniawski](/wiki/Henryk_Wieniawski), [Karol Szymanowski](/wiki/Karol_Szymanowski), [Witold Lutosławski](/wiki/Witold_Lutos%C5%82awski), and [Stanisław Moniuszko](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Moniuszko). Poland maintains a lively and diverse music scene which recognises its own music and entertainment genres, such as [sung poetry](/wiki/Sung_poetry) and [disco polo](/wiki/Disco_polo). [Jazz](/wiki/Jazz) and [metal](/wiki/Metal_(music_genre)) genres are also particularly popular, and the country has become a major venue for large-scale music festivals, chief among which are the [Pol'and'Rock Festival](/wiki/Pol%27and%27Rock_Festival), [Open'er Festival](/wiki/Open%27er_Festival), [Opole Festival](/wiki/Opole_Festival) and [Sopot Festival](/wiki/Sopot_Festival).

The origins of Polish music can be traced to 13th-century [polyphonic](/wiki/Polyphony) compositions associated with the [Notre Dame School](/wiki/Notre_Dame_School). The anthemic melodies of *[Bogurodzica](/wiki/Bogurodzica)* and *[God Is Born](/wiki/God_Is_Born)* may also date back to this period, however, the first notable composer, [Nicholas of Radom](/wiki/Miko%C5%82aj_z_Radomia), lived in the 15th century. In the 16th century, [Venceslaus Samotulinus](/wiki/Venceslaus_Samotulinus) and [Mikołaj Gomółka](/wiki/Miko%C5%82aj_Gom%C3%B3%C5%82ka) gained recognition, and [Diomedes Cato](/wiki/Diomedes_Cato), an Italian [lutenist](/wiki/Lute) at the court of Sigismund III, imported musical styles from [southern Europe](/wiki/Southern_Europe). At the end of the 18th century, Polish classical music evolved into national forms, and [Wojciech Bogusławski](/wiki/Wojciech_Bogus%C5%82awski) is credited for composing the first Polish national opera in 1794. *[Halka](/wiki/Halka)* and *[The Haunted Manor](/wiki/The_Haunted_Manor_(opera))* are canons of 19th-century [Polish opera](/wiki/Polish_opera). The [interwar period](/wiki/Interwar_period) of 1918–1939 saw the rise of [jazz](/wiki/Jazz), [foxtrot](/wiki/Foxtrot), Polish [tango](/wiki/Tango) and a strong [cabaret](/wiki/Cabaret) culture.

Polish choreography is a sophisticated ethnographic system comprising five [national folk dances](/wiki/Polish_folk_dances); the [polonaise](/wiki/Polonaise), [mazurka](/wiki/Mazurka), [krakowiak](/wiki/Krakowiak) (cracovienne), [kujawiak](/wiki/Kujawiak), and [oberek](/wiki/Oberek) as well as various types of [polkas](/wiki/Polka). These are performed in national costumes for larger cultural events and accompanied by instruments such as the [fiddle](/wiki/Fiddle), [bass](/wiki/Double_bass), [clarinet](/wiki/Clarinet) and [accordion](/wiki/Accordion) across much of Poland, and by the highlander [bagpipes](/wiki/Bagpipes) in the southern mountainous areas. Since 2023, the polonaise has been inscribed in the [UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage List](/wiki/UNESCO_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage_List). Polish dances also spread across much of the [Nordic countries](/wiki/Nordic_countries) and developed into the [polska](/wiki/Polska_(dance)) family of music and dance.

### Art

Main articles: [Art in Poland](/wiki/Art_in_Poland) and [Young Poland](/wiki/Young_Poland)

[Jan Matejko](/wiki/Jan_Matejko), leading Polish [history painter](/wiki/History_painting) whose works depict Poland's heritage and key historical events

*[Lady with an Ermine](/wiki/Lady_with_an_Ermine)* (1490) by [Leonardo da Vinci](/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) is displayed in the [Czartoryski Museum](/wiki/Czartoryski_Museum) in Kraków.

Art in Poland has invariably reflected [European](/wiki/Art_of_Europe) trends, with Polish painting pivoted on [folklore](/wiki/Folklore), [Catholic themes](/wiki/Catholic_art), [historicism](/wiki/History_painting) and [realism](/wiki/Realism_(arts)), but also on [Impressionism](/wiki/Impressionism) and [romanticism](/wiki/Romanticism_in_Poland). An important art movement was [Young Poland](/wiki/Young_Poland), developed in the late 19th century for promoting [decadence](/wiki/Decadent_movement), [symbolism](/wiki/Symbolism_(arts)) and [Art Nouveau](/wiki/Art_Nouveau). Since the 20th century Polish documentary art and photography has enjoyed worldwide fame, especially the [Polish School of Posters](/wiki/Polish_School_of_Posters). One of the most distinguished paintings in Poland is *[Lady with an Ermine](/wiki/Lady_with_an_Ermine)* (1490) by [Leonardo da Vinci](/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci).

Internationally renowned Polish artists include [Jan Matejko](/wiki/Jan_Matejko) (historicism), [Jacek Malczewski](/wiki/Jacek_Malczewski) (symbolism), [Stanisław Wyspiański](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Wyspia%C5%84ski) (art nouveau), [Henryk Siemiradzki](/wiki/Henryk_Siemiradzki) (Roman [academic art](/wiki/Academic_art)), [Tamara de Lempicka](/wiki/Tamara_de_Lempicka) ([art deco](/wiki/Art_deco)), and [Zdzisław Beksiński](/wiki/Zdzis%C5%82aw_Beksi%C5%84ski) (dystopian [surrealism](/wiki/Surrealism)). Several Polish artists and sculptors were also acclaimed representatives of [avant-garde](/wiki/Avant-garde), [constructivist](/wiki/Constructivism_(art)), [minimalist](/wiki/Minimalism) and contemporary art movements, including [Katarzyna Kobro](/wiki/Katarzyna_Kobro), [Władysław Strzemiński](/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_Strzemi%C5%84ski), [Magdalena Abakanowicz](/wiki/Magdalena_Abakanowicz), [Alina Szapocznikow](/wiki/Alina_Szapocznikow), [Igor Mitoraj](/wiki/Igor_Mitoraj) and [Wilhelm Sasnal](/wiki/Wilhelm_Sasnal).

Notable art academies in Poland include the [Kraków Academy of Fine Arts](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w_Academy_of_Fine_Arts), [Academy of Fine Arts in Warsaw](/wiki/Academy_of_Fine_Arts_in_Warsaw), [Art Academy of Szczecin](/wiki/Art_Academy_of_Szczecin), [University of Fine Arts in Poznań](/wiki/University_of_Fine_Arts_in_Pozna%C5%84) and the [Geppert Academy of Fine Arts in Wrocław](/wiki/Eugeniusz_Geppert_Academy_of_Fine_Arts). Contemporary works are exhibited at [Zachęta](/wiki/Zach%C4%99ta), [Ujazdów](/wiki/Ujazd%C3%B3w_Castle), and [MOCAK](/wiki/MOCAK,_Museum_of_Contemporary_Art_in_Krak%C3%B3w) art galleries.

### Architecture

Main article: [Architecture of Poland](/wiki/Architecture_of_Poland)

[St. Mary's Basilica](/wiki/St._Mary%27s_Basilica,_Krak%C3%B3w) on the [Main Market Square](/wiki/Main_Market_Square,_Krak%C3%B3w) in [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w) is an example of [Brick Gothic](/wiki/Brick_Gothic) architecture.

The 16th-century [City Hall](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84_Town_Hall) of [Poznań](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84) illustrates the [Renaissance](/wiki/Renaissance_in_Poland) style.

The [architecture of Poland](/wiki/Architecture_of_Poland) reflects [European architectural](/wiki/Architecture_of_Europe) styles, with strong historical influences derived from [Italy](/wiki/Architecture_of_Italy), [Germany](/wiki/Architecture_of_Germany), and the [Low Countries](/wiki/Low_Countries). Settlements founded on [Magdeburg Law](/wiki/Magdeburg_rights) evolved around [central marketplaces](/wiki/Town_square) (*plac*, *rynek*), encircled by a grid or [concentric](/wiki/Concentric_objects) network of streets forming an [old town](/wiki/Old_town) (*stare miasto*). Poland's traditional landscape is characterised by ornate churches, [city tenements](/wiki/Kamienica_(architecture)) and [town halls](/wiki/Ratusz). [Cloth hall markets](/wiki/Cloth_hall) (*sukiennice*) were once an abundant feature of Polish urban architecture. The mountainous south is known for its [Zakopane chalet style](/wiki/Zakopane_Style), which originated in Poland.

The earliest architectonic trend was [Romanesque](/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) (c. 11th century), but its traces in the form of [circular rotundas](/wiki/Rotunda_(architecture)) are scarce. The arrival of [brick Gothic](/wiki/Brick_Gothic) (c. 13th century) defined Poland's most distinguishable medieval style, exuded by the castles of [Malbork](/wiki/Malbork_Castle), [Lidzbark](/wiki/Lidzbark_Castle), [Gniew](/wiki/Gniew_Castle) and [Kwidzyn](/wiki/Kwidzyn_Castle) as well as the cathedrals of [Gniezno](/wiki/Gniezno_Cathedral), [Gdańsk](/wiki/St._Mary%27s_Church,_Gda%C5%84sk), [Wrocław](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Cathedral), [Frombork](/wiki/Frombork_Cathedral) and [Kraków](/wiki/St._Mary%27s_Basilica,_Krak%C3%B3w). The [Renaissance](/wiki/Renaissance_in_Poland) (16th century) gave rise to Italianate courtyards, defensive [palazzos](/wiki/Palazzo) and [mausoleums](/wiki/Sigismund%27s_Chapel). Decorative [attics](/wiki/Attic_(architecture)) with [pinnacles](/wiki/Pinnacle) and [arcade](/wiki/Arcade_(architecture)) [loggias](/wiki/Loggia) are elements of [Polish Mannerism](/wiki/Mannerist_architecture), found in [Poznań](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84), [Lublin](/wiki/Lublin) and [Zamość](/wiki/Zamo%C5%9B%C4%87). Foreign artisans often came at the expense of kings or nobles, whose palaces were built thereafter in the [Baroque](/wiki/Baroque_in_Poland), [Neoclassical](/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture_in_Poland) and [Revivalist](/wiki/Revivalism_(architecture)) styles (17th–19th century).

Primary building materials [timber](/wiki/Lumber) and [red brick](/wiki/Brick) were used extensively in Polish folk architecture, and the concept of a [fortified church](/wiki/Fortified_church) was commonplace. Secular structures such as [*dworek* manor houses](/wiki/Dw%C3%B3r_(manor_house)), [farmsteads](/wiki/Farmhouses), [granaries](/wiki/Granary), [mills](/wiki/Gristmill) and country [inns](/wiki/Inn) are still present in some regions or in open air museums (*[skansen](/wiki/Open-Air_Museum)*). However, traditional construction methods faded in the early-mid 20th century due to urbanisation and the construction of [functionalist](/wiki/Functionalist_architecture) [housing estates](/wiki/Housing_estate) and [residential areas](/wiki/Residential_architecture_in_Poland).

### Literature

Main articles: [Polish literature](/wiki/Polish_literature) and [History of philosophy in Poland](/wiki/History_of_philosophy_in_Poland)

[Adam Mickiewicz](/wiki/Adam_Mickiewicz), whose [national epic poem](/wiki/Epic_poetry) *[Pan Tadeusz](/wiki/Pan_Tadeusz)* (1834) is considered a masterpiece of [Polish literature](/wiki/Polish_literature)

[Joseph Conrad](/wiki/Joseph_Conrad), author of popular books such as *[Heart of Darkness](/wiki/Heart_of_Darkness)* (1899) and *[Nostromo](/wiki/Nostromo)* (1904)

The [literary works of Poland](/wiki/Polish_literature) have traditionally concentrated around the themes of patriotism, [spirituality](/wiki/Spirituality), social [allegories](/wiki/Allegory) and moral narratives. The earliest examples of Polish literature, written in [Latin](/wiki/Latin), date to the 12th century. The first [Polish](/wiki/Polish_language) phrase *Day ut ia pobrusa, a ti poziwai* (officially translated as "Let me, I shall grind, and you take a rest") was documented in the [Book of Henryków](/wiki/Book_of_Henryk%C3%B3w) and reflected the use of a [quern-stone](/wiki/Quern-stone). It has been since included in [UNESCO's Memory of World Register](/wiki/Memory_of_the_World_Programme). The oldest extant manuscripts of fine [prose](/wiki/Prose) in [Old Polish](/wiki/Old_Polish_language) are the [Holy Cross Sermons](/wiki/Holy_Cross_Sermons) and the [Bible of Queen Sophia](/wiki/Bible_of_Queen_Sophia), and [Calendarium cracoviense](/wiki/Almanach_cracoviense_ad_annum_1474) (1474) is Poland's oldest surviving [print](/wiki/Printing_press).

The poets [Jan Kochanowski](/wiki/Jan_Kochanowski) and [Nicholas Rey](/wiki/Nicholas_Rey) became the first [Renaissance](/wiki/Renaissance_in_Poland) authors to write in Polish. Prime literarians of the period included [Dantiscus](/wiki/Johannes_Dantiscus), [Modrevius](/wiki/Andreus_Fricius_Modrevius), [Goslicius](/wiki/Wawrzyniec_Go%C5%9Blicki), [Sarbievius](/wiki/Matthias_Sarbievius) and theologian [John Laski](/wiki/John_Laski). In the [Baroque](/wiki/Polish_Baroque) era, [Jesuit philosophy](/wiki/Jesuits) and local culture greatly influenced the literary techniques of [Jan Andrzej Morsztyn](/wiki/Jan_Andrzej_Morsztyn) ([Marinism](/wiki/Marinism)) and [Jan Chryzostom Pasek](/wiki/Jan_Chryzostom_Pasek) ([sarmatian](/wiki/Sarmatism) memoirs). During the [Enlightenment](/wiki/Polish_Enlightenment), playwright [Ignacy Krasicki](/wiki/Ignacy_Krasicki) composed the first Polish-language [novel](/wiki/The_Adventures_of_Mr._Nicholas_Wisdom). Poland's leading 19th-century [romantic poets](/wiki/Romanticism_in_Poland) were the [Three Bards](/wiki/Three_Bards) – [Juliusz Słowacki](/wiki/Juliusz_S%C5%82owacki), [Zygmunt Krasiński](/wiki/Zygmunt_Krasi%C5%84ski) and [Adam Mickiewicz](/wiki/Adam_Mickiewicz), whose epic poem *[Pan Tadeusz](/wiki/Pan_Tadeusz)* (1834) is a national classic. In the 20th century, the English [impressionist](/wiki/Impressionism_(literature)) and early [modernist](/wiki/Literary_modernism) writings of [Joseph Conrad](/wiki/Joseph_Conrad) made him one of the most eminent novelists of all time.

Contemporary Polish literature is versatile, with its fantasy genre having been particularly praised. The philosophical [sci-fi](/wiki/Science-fiction) novel *[Solaris](/wiki/Solaris_(novel))* by [Stanisław Lem](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem) and *[The Witcher](/wiki/The_Witcher)* series by [Andrzej Sapkowski](/wiki/Andrzej_Sapkowski) are celebrated works of world fiction. Poland has six [Nobel-Prize winning](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature) authors – [Henryk Sienkiewicz](/wiki/Henryk_Sienkiewicz) (*[Quo Vadis](/wiki/Quo_Vadis_(novel))*; 1905), [Władysław Reymont](/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_Reymont) (*[The Peasants](/wiki/The_Peasants)*; 1924), [Isaac Bashevis Singer](/wiki/Isaac_Bashevis_Singer) (1978), [Czesław Miłosz](/wiki/Czes%C5%82aw_Mi%C5%82osz) (1980), [Wisława Szymborska](/wiki/Wis%C5%82awa_Szymborska) (1996), and [Olga Tokarczuk](/wiki/Olga_Tokarczuk) (2018).

### Cuisine

Main article: [Polish cuisine](/wiki/Polish_cuisine)

[Kielbasa](/wiki/Kielbasa) sausage, a staple of Polish cuisine, with [pickled cucumbers](/wiki/Pickled_cucumber) and [rye bread](/wiki/Rye_bread)

The cuisine of Poland is eclectic, with many regional varieties, sharing some similarity with other neighbouring cuisines. Among the staple or regional dishes are [pierogi](/wiki/Pierogi) (filled dumplings), [kielbasa](/wiki/Kielbasa) (sausage), [bigos](/wiki/Bigos) (hunter's stew), [kotlet schabowy](/wiki/Kotlet_schabowy) (breaded cutlet), [gołąbki](/wiki/Go%C5%82%C4%85bki) (cabbage rolls), [barszcz](/wiki/Borscht) (borscht), [żurek](/wiki/%C5%BBurek) (soured rye soup), [oscypek](/wiki/Oscypek) (smoked cheese), and [tomato soup](/wiki/Tomato_soup). [Bagels](/wiki/Bagel), a type of [bread roll](/wiki/Bread_roll), also originated in Poland.

Traditional dishes are hearty and abundant in pork, potatoes, eggs, cream, mushrooms, regional herbs, and sauce. Polish food is characteristic for its various kinds of [kluski](/wiki/Kluski) (soft dumplings), [soups](/wiki/Soup), cereals and a variety of breads and [open sandwiches](/wiki/Open_sandwich). Salads, including [mizeria](/wiki/Mizeria) (cucumber salad), [coleslaw](/wiki/Coleslaw), [sauerkraut](/wiki/Sauerkraut), carrot and [seared beets](/wiki/Searing), are common. Meals conclude with a dessert such as [sernik](/wiki/Cheesecake) (cheesecake), [makowiec](/wiki/Makowiec_(pastry)) (poppy seed roll), or [napoleonka](/wiki/Napoleonka) ([mille-feuille](/wiki/Mille-feuille)) cream pie.

Traditional alcoholic beverages include honey [mead](/wiki/Mead_in_Poland), widespread since the 13th century, [beer](/wiki/Beer_in_Poland), wine and [vodka](/wiki/Vodka). The world's first written mention of vodka originates from Poland. The most popular alcoholic drinks at present are beer and wine which took over from vodka more popular in the years 1980–1998. [Grodziskie](/wiki/Grodziskie), sometimes referred to as "Polish Champagne", is an example of a historical beer style from Poland. Tea remains common in Polish society since the 19th century, whilst coffee is drunk widely since the 18th century.

### Fashion and design

Further information: [Category:Polish fashion](/wiki/Category:Polish_fashion)

Traditional *[polonaise](/wiki/Polonaise_(clothing))* dresses, 1780–1785

Several Polish designers and stylists left a legacy of beauty inventions and cosmetics; including [Helena Rubinstein](/wiki/Helena_Rubinstein) and [Maksymilian Faktorowicz](/wiki/Maksymilian_Faktorowicz), who created a line of cosmetics company in California known as [Max Factor](/wiki/Max_Factor) and formulated the term "make-up" which is now widely used as an alternative for describing cosmetics. Faktorowicz is also credited with inventing modern [eyelash extensions](/wiki/Eyelash_extensions). As of 2020, Poland possesses the sixth-largest cosmetic market in Europe. [Inglot Cosmetics](/wiki/Inglot_Cosmetics) is the country's largest beauty products manufacturer, and the retail store [Reserved](/wiki/Reserved) is the country's most successful clothing store chain.

Historically, fashion has been an important aspect of Poland's national consciousness or [cultural manifestation](/wiki/Culture_of_Poland), beginning with the [poulaine](/wiki/Poulaine) pointed footwear in the 14th century, to developing an endemic style known as [Sarmatism](/wiki/Sarmatism) at the turn of the 17th century. The national dress and etiquette of Poland reached the court at [Versailles](/wiki/Versailles), where French dresses inspired by Polish garments included *[robe à la polonaise](/wiki/Polonaise_(clothing))* and the [witzchoura](/wiki/Witzchoura). The scope of influence also entailed furniture; rococo [Polish beds](/wiki/Polish_bed) with [canopies](/wiki/Canopy_bed) became fashionable in châteaus. Sarmatism eventually faded in the wake of the 18th century.

### Cinema

Main article: [Cinema of Poland](/wiki/Cinema_of_Poland)

[Andrzej Wajda](/wiki/Andrzej_Wajda) (1926–2016), renowned Polish film director

The [cinema of Poland](/wiki/Cinema_of_Poland) traces its origins to 1894, when inventor [Kazimierz Prószyński](/wiki/Kazimierz_Pr%C3%B3szy%C5%84ski) patented the [Pleograph](/wiki/Pleograph) and subsequently the [Aeroscope](/wiki/Aeroscope), the first successful hand-held operated film camera. In 1897, [Jan Szczepanik](/wiki/Jan_Szczepanik) constructed the [Telectroscope](/wiki/Telectroscope), a prototype of television transmitting images and sounds. They are both recognised as pioneers of [cinematography](/wiki/Cinematography). Poland has also produced influential directors, film producers and actors, many of whom were active in [Hollywood](/wiki/Hollywood,_Los_Angeles), chiefly [Roman Polański](/wiki/Roman_Pola%C5%84ski), [Andrzej Wajda](/wiki/Andrzej_Wajda), [Pola Negri](/wiki/Pola_Negri), [Samuel Goldwyn](/wiki/Samuel_Goldwyn), the [Warner brothers](/wiki/Warner_Brothers), [Max Fleischer](/wiki/Max_Fleischer), [Agnieszka Holland](/wiki/Agnieszka_Holland), [Krzysztof Zanussi](/wiki/Krzysztof_Zanussi) and [Krzysztof Kieślowski](/wiki/Krzysztof_Kie%C5%9Blowski).

The [themes](/wiki/Film_genre) commonly explored in Polish cinema include [history](/wiki/Historical_film), [drama](/wiki/Drama_(film_and_television)), war, culture and black realism ([film noir](/wiki/Film_noir)). In the 21st-century, two Polish productions won the [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Awards) – *[The Pianist](/wiki/The_Pianist_(2002_film))* (2002) by Roman Polański and *[Ida](/wiki/Ida_(film))* (2013) by [Paweł Pawlikowski](/wiki/Pawe%C5%82_Pawlikowski). Polish cinematography also created many well-received comedies. The most known of them were made by [Stanisław Bareja](/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Bareja) and [Juliusz Machulski](/wiki/Juliusz_Machulski).

### Media

Main article: [Mass media in Poland](/wiki/Mass_media_in_Poland)

Headquarters of the publicly funded national television network [TVP](/wiki/Telewizja_Polska) in Warsaw

According to the [Eurobarometer Report](/wiki/Eurobarometer) (2015), 78 percent of Poles watch the [television](/wiki/Television_in_Poland) daily. In 2020, 79 percent of the population read the news more than once a day, placing it second behind Sweden. Poland has a number of major domestic media outlets, chiefly the [public broadcasting](/wiki/Public_broadcasting) corporation [TVP](/wiki/Telewizja_Polska), [free-to-air](/wiki/Free-to-air) channels [TVN](/wiki/TVN_(Polish_TV_channel)) and [Polsat](/wiki/Polsat) as well as 24-hour news channels [TVP Info](/wiki/TVP_Info), [TVN 24](/wiki/TVN_24) and [Polsat News](/wiki/Polsat_News). Public television extends its operations to genre-specific programmes such as [TVP Sport](/wiki/TVP_Sport), [TVP Historia](/wiki/TVP_Historia), [TVP Kultura](/wiki/TVP_Kultura), [TVP Rozrywka](/wiki/TVP_Rozrywka), TVP Seriale and [TVP Polonia](/wiki/TVP_Polonia), the latter a state-run channel dedicated to the transmission of Polish-language telecasts for the [Polish diaspora](/wiki/Polish_diaspora). In 2020, the most popular types of newspapers were [tabloids](/wiki/Tabloid_(newspaper_format)) and socio-political news dailies.

Poland is a major European hub for video game developers and among the most successful companies are [CD Projekt](/wiki/CD_Projekt), [Techland](/wiki/Techland), [The Farm 51](/wiki/The_Farm_51), [CI Games](/wiki/CI_Games) and [People Can Fly](/wiki/People_Can_Fly). Some of the popular video games developed in Poland include *[The Witcher](/wiki/The_Witcher_(video_game_series))* trilogy and *[Cyberpunk 2077](/wiki/Cyberpunk_2077)*. The Polish city of [Katowice](/wiki/Katowice) also hosts [Intel Extreme Masters](/wiki/Intel_Extreme_Masters), one of the biggest [esports](/wiki/Esports) events in the world.

### Sports

Main articles: [Sport in Poland](/wiki/Sport_in_Poland) and [Poland at the Olympics](/wiki/Poland_at_the_Olympics)

The [Kazimierz Górski National Stadium](/wiki/Kazimierz_G%C3%B3rski_National_Stadium) in Warsaw, home of the [national football team](/wiki/Poland_national_football_team)

[Motorcycle speedway](/wiki/Motorcycle_speedway), volleyball and association football are among the country's most popular sports, with a rich history of international competitions. [Track and field](/wiki/Track_and_field_athletics), basketball, [handball](/wiki/Handball), boxing, [MMA](/wiki/Mixed_martial_arts), [ski jumping](/wiki/Ski_jumping), [cross-country skiing](/wiki/Cross-country_skiing_(sport)), [ice hockey](/wiki/Ice_hockey), tennis, fencing, swimming, and [weightlifting](/wiki/Olympic_weightlifting) are other popular sports.
The golden era of [football in Poland](/wiki/Football_in_Poland) occurred throughout the 1970s and went on until the early 1980s when the [Polish national football team](/wiki/Poland_national_football_team) achieved their best results in any FIFA World Cup competitions finishing third place in [the 1974](/wiki/1974_FIFA_World_Cup) and [the 1982](/wiki/1982_FIFA_World_Cup) tournaments. The team won a gold medal [in football](/wiki/Football_at_the_Summer_Olympics) at the [1972 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1972_Summer_Olympics) and two silver medals, [in 1976](/wiki/1976_Summer_Olympics) and [in 1992](/wiki/1992_Summer_Olympics). In 2012, Poland co-hosted the [UEFA European Football Championship](/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2012).

As of September 2024, the [Polish men's national volleyball team](/wiki/Poland_men%27s_national_volleyball_team) is ranked [as first](/wiki/FIVB_World_Rankings) in the world. The team won a gold medal at the [1976 Summer Olympics](/wiki/Volleyball_at_the_1976_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Men%27s_tournament) and the gold medal at the [FIVB World Championship](/wiki/FIVB_Volleyball_Men%27s_World_Championship) [1974](/wiki/1974_FIVB_Volleyball_Men%27s_World_Championship), [2014](/wiki/2014_FIVB_Volleyball_Men%27s_World_Championship) and [2018](/wiki/2018_FIVB_Volleyball_Men%27s_World_Championship).
[Mariusz Pudzianowski](/wiki/Mariusz_Pudzianowski) is a highly successful strongman competitor and has won more [World's Strongest Man](/wiki/World%27s_Strongest_Man) titles than any other competitor in the world, winning the event in 2008 for the fifth time.

Poland has made a distinctive mark [in motorcycle speedway racing](/wiki/Speedway_in_Poland). The top [Ekstraliga](/wiki/Speedway_Ekstraliga) division has one of [the highest average attendances](/wiki/List_of_attendance_figures_at_domestic_professional_sports_leagues#Outdoor_sports) for any sport in Poland. The [national speedway team of Poland](/wiki/Poland_national_speedway_team) is one of the major teams in international speedway. Individually, Poland has three [Speedway Grand Prix](/wiki/Speedway_Grand_Prix) World Champions, with the most successful being five-time World Champion [Bartosz Zmarzlik](/wiki/Bartosz_Zmarzlik) who won back-to-back championships in 2019 and 2020 as well as 2022, 2023 and 2024. In 2021, Poland finished runners-up in the Speedway of Nations world championship final, held in [Manchester](/wiki/Manchester), England in 2021.

In the 21st century, the country has seen a growth of popularity of tennis and produced a number of successful tennis players including World No. 1 [Iga Świątek](/wiki/Iga_%C5%9Awi%C4%85tek), winner of five [Grand Slam](/wiki/Grand_Slam_(tennis)) singles titles; former World No. 2 [Agnieszka Radwanska](/wiki/Agnieszka_Radwanska), winner of 20 WTA career singles titles including [2015 WTA Finals](/wiki/2015_WTA_Finals_%E2%80%93_Singles); Top 10 ATP player [Hubert Hurkacz](/wiki/Hubert_Hurkacz); former World No. 1 doubles player [Łukasz Kubot](/wiki/%C5%81ukasz_Kubot), winner of two Grand Slam doubles titles and [Jan Zieliński](/wiki/Jan_Zieli%C5%84ski), winner of two Grand Slam mixed doubles titles. Poland also won the [2015 Hopman Cup](/wiki/2015_Hopman_Cup) with Agnieszka Radwańska and [Jerzy Janowicz](/wiki/Jerzy_Janowicz) representing the country.

Poles made significant achievements in mountaineering, in particular, in the [Himalayas](/wiki/Himalayas) and the winter ascending of the [eight-thousanders](/wiki/Eight-thousander) (e.g. [Jerzy Kukuczka](/wiki/Jerzy_Kukuczka), [Krzysztof Wielicki](/wiki/Krzysztof_Wielicki), [Wanda Rutkiewicz](/wiki/Wanda_Rutkiewicz)). Polish mountains are one of the tourist attractions of the country. Hiking, climbing, skiing and mountain biking and attract numerous tourists every year from all over the world. Water sports are the most popular summer recreation activities, with ample locations for fishing, canoeing, kayaking, sailing and windsurfing especially in the northern regions of the country.
