# Shakshouka

## Infobox

- **Alternative names:** Shakshuka, chakchouka
- **Type:** Main dish
- **Place of origin:** Ottoman North Africa
- **Main ingredients:** Tomatoes, harissa, eggs, olive oil

For the shredded flatbread and chickpea dish, see [Chakhchoukha](/wiki/Chakhchoukha). For the Turkish eggplant dish, see [Şakşuka](/wiki/%C5%9Eak%C5%9Fuka). For the documentary film, see [The Shakshuka System](/wiki/The_Shakshuka_System).

Individual portion of shakshouka

**Shakshouka** is a [Maghrebi](/wiki/Maghreb) dish of eggs [poached](/wiki/Poaching_(cooking)) in a sauce of tomatoes, olive oil, peppers, onion, and garlic, commonly spiced with [cumin](/wiki/Cumin), [paprika](/wiki/Paprika), and [cayenne pepper](/wiki/Cayenne_pepper). Shakshouka is a popular dish throughout [North Africa](/wiki/North_Africa) and the [Middle East](/wiki/Middle_East).

## Etymology

*Shakshuka* is a word for "mixture" in [Algerian Arabic](/wiki/Algerian_Arabic), and "mixed" in [Tunisian Arabic](/wiki/Tunisian_Arabic). The *[Oxford English Dictionary](/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)* describes the English version of the word as being borrowed from more than one origin: an [onomatopoeic](/wiki/Onomatopoeia) Maghribi Arabic word, related to the verb *shakshaka* meaning "to bubble, to sizzle, to be mixed up, to be beaten together", and the [French](/wiki/French_language) word *Chakchouka*, which was borrowed into English in the nineteenth century, and which itself had been borrowed into French from Algerian Arabic.

## History

[Gil Marks](/wiki/Gil_Marks), while noting some similarities with the Ottoman dish [menemen](/wiki/Menemen_(food)), suggests that shakshouka evolved from [şakşuka](/wiki/%C5%9Eak%C5%9Fuka) which spread to the Maghreb through the influence of the Ottoman Empire. Anthony Buccini noted similarities between a wider range of vegetable stews. He and [Noam Sienna](/wiki/Noam_Sienna) conclude that both shakshouka and menemen, among other dishes like [piperade](/wiki/Piperade) and [ratatouille](/wiki/Ratatouille), are members of a wider family of vegetable stews of common ancestry appearing throughout the western Mediterranean.

The migration of [Maghrebi Jews](/wiki/Maghrebi_Jews) in the 1950s brought the dish to [Israel](/wiki/Israel), where it was subsequently widely adopted. The dish was not previously associated with [Palestinian](/wiki/Palestinian_cuisine) or [Levantine cuisine](/wiki/Levantine_cuisine). Shakshouka began appearing in Israeli restaurants in the 1990s.

## Variations

[Merguez](/wiki/Merguez) shakshuka

Vegan shakshouka, with [falafel](/wiki/Falafel) in place of eggs

Many variations of the basic sauce are possible, varying in spice and sweetness. Some cooks add [preserved lemon](/wiki/Preserved_lemon), salty [sheep milk cheeses](/wiki/Sheep_milk_cheese), olives, [harissa](/wiki/Harissa) or a spicy sausage such as [chorizo](/wiki/Chorizo) or [merguez](/wiki/Merguez). Shakshouka is made with eggs, which are commonly poached but can also be scrambled, like in the Turkish *[menemen](/wiki/Menemen_(food))*.

In [Algeria](/wiki/Algeria), shakshouka is commonly eaten as a side dish, and there are countless variations of it, each with their own unique blend of ingredients. One such variation is [hmiss](/wiki/Hmiss), which is often served alongside traditional [kesra bread](/wiki/Kesra_(bread)). Hmiss typically includes grilled peppers, tomatoes, and garlic. In [Tunisia](/wiki/Tunisia), a similar dish called [slata meshouia](/wiki/Mechouia_salad) is enjoyed, but it differs from hmiss with the addition of onions, cumin and tuna.

In [Syria](/wiki/Syria), jaz maz is a similar eggs-and-tomatoes dish, it is sometimes referred to as *shakshouka*.

In Morocco, there is a dish referred to as *bīḍ w-maṭiša* (بيض ومطيشة "egg and tomato").

In [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt), the dish features the typical poached eggs in a spiced [tomato](/wiki/Tomato)-based sauce but with some variations in ingredients. The base consists of sautéed [onions](/wiki/Onions), [garlic](/wiki/Garlic), and chopped [bell peppers](/wiki/Bell_peppers) cooked until softened. Chopped tomatoes or tomato paste is then added, along with spices such as [cumin](/wiki/Cumin), [paprika](/wiki/Paprika), and [chili powder](/wiki/Chili_powder). Once the sauce thickens, eggs are cracked directly into the mixture and poached until the whites are set but the yolks remain runny. It is garnished with fresh herbs like [parsley](/wiki/Parsley) and [cilantro](/wiki/Cilantro), sometimes also a sprinkle of [domiati cheese](/wiki/Domiati_cheese). The dish is commonly enjoyed with warm [eish baladi](/wiki/Eish_baladi), and is a popular choice for breakfast in the country.

Some variations of shakshouka can be made with [lamb mince](/wiki/Lamb_mince), toasted whole spices, yogurt and fresh herbs. Spices can include ground [coriander](/wiki/Coriander), [caraway](/wiki/Caraway), [paprika](/wiki/Paprika), [cumin](/wiki/Cumin) and [cayenne pepper](/wiki/Cayenne_pepper). Tunisian cooks may add potatoes, broad beans, artichoke hearts or courgettes to the dish. The [North African](/wiki/North_African_cuisine) dish *[matbukha](/wiki/Matbukha)* can be used as a base for shakshouka.

Because eggs are the main ingredient, it often appears on breakfast menus in English-speaking countries, but in the Arab world as well as Israel, it is also a popular evening meal, and, like [hummus](/wiki/Hummus) and [falafel](/wiki/Falafel), is a Levantine regional favorite. On the side, pickled vegetables and North African sausage called merguez might be served, or simply bread, with mint tea.

In Jewish culture, a large batch of tomato stew may be made on Friday for the [Sabbath](/wiki/Sabbath) dinner and the leftovers used on Sunday morning to make a breakfast shakshouka with eggs. In [Andalusian cuisine](/wiki/Andalusian_cuisine), the dish is known as *huevos a la flamenca*; this version includes chorizo and [serrano ham](/wiki/Serrano_ham). In [Italian cuisine](/wiki/Italian_cuisine), there is a version of this dish called *uova in purgatorio* (eggs in purgatory) that adds garlic, basil or parsley.
