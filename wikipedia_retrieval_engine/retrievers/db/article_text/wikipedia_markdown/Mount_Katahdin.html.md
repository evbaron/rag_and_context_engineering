**Mount Katahdin** ([/kəˈtɑːdɪn/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [*kə-TAH-din*](/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key)) is the highest mountain in the [U.S. state](/wiki/U.S._state) of [Maine](/wiki/Maine) at 5,269 feet (1,606 m). Named **Katahdin**, which means 'Great Mountain', by the [Penobscot](/wiki/Penobscot) Native Americans, it is within [Northeast Piscataquis](/wiki/Northeast_Piscataquis,_Maine), [Piscataquis County](/wiki/Piscataquis_County,_Maine), and is the centerpiece of [Baxter State Park](/wiki/Baxter_State_Park). It is a steep, tall [massif](/wiki/Massif) formed from a [granite intrusion](/wiki/Laccolith) weathered to the surface. The flora and fauna on the mountain are typical of those found in northern [New England](/wiki/New_England), with the summit hosting fragile and endangered [alpine tundra](/wiki/Alpine_tundra).

Katahdin has been known to the [Native Americans](/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) in the region for thousands of years and was known to Europeans since at least 1689. It has inspired hikes, climbs, journal narratives, paintings, and a piano sonata. The area around the peak was protected starting in the 1930s by Maine Governor [Percival Baxter](/wiki/Percival_P._Baxter), who spent much of his personal fortune purchasing the mountain and surrounding lands after being unable to convince the state legislature of the wisdom of protecting it. Katahdin is the northern terminus of the [Appalachian Trail](/wiki/Appalachian_Trail) and is near a stretch known as the [Hundred-Mile Wilderness](/wiki/Hundred-Mile_Wilderness). Though part of the Appalachian Mountain system, Katahdin is isolated from the range, and sits largely on its own as a [monadnock](/wiki/Inselberg). The nearest higher mountains in any direction are the high peaks of the [Presidential Range](/wiki/Presidential_Range), around 170 miles (270 km) to the south-west.

In 1967, Mount Katahdin was designated as a [National Natural Landmark](/wiki/National_Natural_Landmark) by the [National Park Service](/wiki/National_Park_Service).

The mountain is commonly called just "Katahdin", though the official name is "Mount Katahdin" as decided by the [US Board on Geographic Names](/wiki/United_States_Board_on_Geographic_Names) in 1893.

## Geology

Katahdin is part of a [laccolith](/wiki/Laccolith) that formed in the [Acadian orogeny](/wiki/Acadian_orogeny) when an [island arc](/wiki/Island_arc) collided with eastern [North America](/wiki/North_America) approximately 400 million years ago. On the sides of Katahdin are four glacial [cirques](/wiki/Cirque_(landform)) carved into the granite by alpine [glaciers](/wiki/Glacier) and in these cirques behind [moraines](/wiki/Moraine) and [eskers](/wiki/Esker) are several ponds.

In Baxter State Park, many outcrops of [sedimentary rocks](/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) have [striations](/wiki/Glacial_striation), whereas the [Granite](/wiki/Granite) of Katahdin and the [Rhyolite](/wiki/Rhyolite) of [Traveler Mountain](/wiki/Traveler_Mountain) have weathered surfaces on which striations are commonly not preserved. Bedrock surfaces of [igneous rocks](/wiki/Igneous_rock) which were buried by glacial sediments and only recently exposed have well preserved striations, as in the vicinity of [Ripogenus](/wiki/Ripogenus_Gorge) Dam. Several outcrops of sedimentary rocks along the Patten Road show striations, especially on the north side of the road at Hurricane Deck. A few outcrops near the Patten Road just north of Horse Mountain are striated, as are several outcrops of sedimentary rocks along the road from Trout Brook Farm northward to [Second Lake Matagamon](/wiki/Second_Lake_Matagamon).

## Fauna

[Fauna](/wiki/Fauna) include [black bear](/wiki/American_black_bear), [deer](/wiki/Deer), and [moose](/wiki/Moose) as well as [black flies](/wiki/Black_fly) and [mosquitos](/wiki/Mosquito) in the spring. A subspecies of Arctic butterfly, known as the Katahdin Arctic (*[Oeneis polixenes](/wiki/Oeneis_polixenes) katahdin*) is specific to the area, and is currently listed as endangered.
Among the birds are [Bicknell's thrush](/wiki/Bicknell%27s_thrush) and various songbirds and raptors. A study of the animal communities was published by Irving H. Blake in 1926. The flora includes [pine](/wiki/Pine), [spruce](/wiki/Spruce), [fir](/wiki/Fir), [hemlock](/wiki/Eastern_hemlock), [beech](/wiki/Beech), [maple](/wiki/Maple), [birch](/wiki/Birch), [aspen](/wiki/Aspen), and [pincushion plant](/wiki/Diapensia_lapponica) (*Diapensia lapponica*).

## Geography

Katahdin is in Baxter State Park, which is in east central Piscataquis County, about 25 mi (40 km) northwest of [Millinocket](/wiki/Millinocket,_Maine). It is on the [drainage divide](/wiki/Drainage_divide) between the [East](/wiki/East_Branch_Penobscot_River) and [West branches](/wiki/West_Branch_Penobscot_River) of the [Penobscot River](/wiki/Penobscot_River).

A view of the Knife Edge from summit of Katahdin

The mountain massif itself consists of multiple peaks. Baxter Peak is the tallest, and is the official northern terminus of the Appalachian Trail. South Peak and [Pamola](/wiki/Pamola) Peak are southeast and east of Baxter Peak, respectively, along the Knife Edge ridgeline, while [Hamlin Peak](/wiki/Hamlin_Peak) lies to the north.

There is low lake country to the south and west of Katahdin, and lowlands extending east to the [Atlantic](/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean) and north to the [Saint Lawrence River](/wiki/Saint_Lawrence_River) in [Canada](/wiki/Canada).

The summit of Katahdin offers some of the longest unbroken lines of sight in the United States, and on clear days can be seen all the way from the [White Mountains](/wiki/White_Mountains_(New_England)) of neighboring [New Hampshire](/wiki/New_Hampshire); a distance of 170 miles (270 km).

Katahdin's height and isolation earns it significant coverage in indigenous and post-colonial Maine culture and literature. Katahdin's profile is distinctive and the indisputable centerpiece of [Baxter State Park](/wiki/Baxter_State_Park). Katahdin and nearby Hamlin Peak are the only two areas to host a subarctic climate in [Maine](/wiki/Maine). In winter, the snowcapped east and west faces of Katahdin resemble "the [Kilimanjaro](/wiki/Kilimanjaro) of New England", and it dominates the otherwise flat and endless forests of the [North Maine Woods](/wiki/North_Maine_Woods). Katahdin is also the northernmost mountain in the eastern United States with an elevation over 4,000 feet (1,200 m).

## Human history

*Mount Katahdin from Millinocket Camp*, by [Frederic Edwin Church](/wiki/Frederic_Edwin_Church), 1895

Katahdin is referred to 60 years after Field's climb of Agiokochuk ([Mount Washington](/wiki/Mount_Washington_(New_Hampshire))) in the writings of [John Gyles](/wiki/John_Gyles), a teenage colonist who was captured near [Portland, Maine](/wiki/Portland,_Maine), in 1689 by the [Abenaki](/wiki/Abenaki). While in the company of Abenaki hunting parties, he traveled up and down several Maine rivers including both branches of the [Penobscot](/wiki/Penobscot_River), passing close to "Teddon". He remarked that it was higher than the [White Hills](/wiki/White_Mountains_(New_Hampshire)) above the [Saco River](/wiki/Saco_River).

Among some [Native Americans](/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States), Katahdin was believed to be the home of the storm god [Pamola](/wiki/Pamola), and thus an area to be avoided.

The first recorded climb of "Catahrdin" was by Massachusetts surveyors Zackery Adley and [Charles Turner, Jr.](/wiki/Charles_Turner,_Jr.) in August 1804. The letter describing the ascent of Charles Turner Jr. states that they began at the (West Branch Penobscot) at 8:00 a.m. and arrived on the summit at 5:00 p.m. guided by two Native Americans who were initially cautious but when the "cold part of the mountain" was reached and sensing the determination of the others became ambitious to reach the top first. Turner lists his party as: William Howe, Amos Patten, Joseph Treat, Samuel Call, William Rice, Richard Winslow, Charles Turner, Jr. In the 1930s [Governor](/wiki/Governor_of_Maine) [Percival Baxter](/wiki/Percival_P._Baxter) began to acquire land and finally deeded more than 200,000 [acres](/wiki/Acre) (810 km2) to the State of Maine for a park, named [Baxter State Park](/wiki/Baxter_State_Park) after him. The summit was officially recognized by the [US Board on Geographic Names](/wiki/United_States_Board_on_Geographic_Names) as "Baxter Peak" in 1931.

In the 1840s [Henry David Thoreau](/wiki/Henry_David_Thoreau) climbed Katahdin, which he spelled "Ktaadn"; his ascent is recorded in a well-known chapter of *The Maine Woods*. A few years later [Theodore Winthrop](/wiki/Theodore_Winthrop) wrote about his visit in *Life in the Open Air*. Painters [Frederic Edwin Church](/wiki/Frederic_Edwin_Church) and [Marsden Hartley](/wiki/Marsden_Hartley) are well-known artists who created landscapes of Katahdin. On 30 November 2011, Christie's auctioned Church's 1860 painting *Twilight (Katahdin)* for $3.1 million.

[Elizabeth Oakes Smith](/wiki/Elizabeth_Oakes_Smith) "climbed Mount Katahdin in 1849—reportedly the first white woman to do so".

## Recreation opportunities

As the northern terminus of the [Appalachian Trail](/wiki/Appalachian_Trail) and southern terminus of the [International Appalachian Trail](/wiki/International_Appalachian_Trail), Katahdin is a popular hiking and backpacking destination and the centerpiece of [Baxter State Park](/wiki/Baxter_State_Park). Baxter State Park is open year-round, though strictly regulated in winter. The overnight camping season is, weather permitting, from May 15 to October 15 each year, with some campgrounds staying open until October 22. Capacity limits have been placed on day-use parking at the trailheads to minimize the overuse of trails.

Katahdin has several trails leading up to Baxter, Pamola, or Hamlin Peaks. These trails average eight to twelve hours round-trip depending on ability. Katahdin is often considered one of the most challenging hikes in the entire [Appalachian Mountain](/wiki/Appalachian_Mountain) range.

Katahdin has three primary trailheads. Katahdin Stream, Abol, and Roaring Brook. Katahdin Stream is the trailhead for the Hunt trail, the final leg of the Appalachian Trail. Abol is the trailhead for the Abol trail. Roaring Brook is the trailhead for the Helon Taylor trail, and the Chimney Pond trail which later branches into the Saddle, Cathedral, Dudley, and Hamlin Ridge trails.

All trails are maintained by the Baxter State Park Authority, which runs the State Park. Any hike that starts at a trailhead and summits the mountain is considered very strenuous, the highest classification the Park Authority gives. Some individual trails are given lower classifications, as they are only part of a full summit hike. These trails include Saddle (strenuous), Hamlin Ridge trail (moderate), and Chimney Pond (moderate).

The most famous hike to the summit goes along Knife Edge, a glacial [arête](/wiki/Ar%C3%AAte) which traverses the ridge between Pamola Peak and Baxter Peak. There have been more than 60 deaths on Katahdin since 1933 including October 8 and 9, 2020. The park as a whole typically sees a need for roughly 40 search-and-rescue events per year (1 in 2,000 hikers), with a peak of 70 in 2013. The vast majority of incidents occur in the summer months, and the primary causes are leg injury, exhaustion, dehydration and disorientation. Because of this, Katahdin and New Hampshire's [Mount Washington](/wiki/Mount_Washington) are routinely near the top of the most deadly "low mountains" on Earth, with more fatalities per foot of elevation gained than some much higher mountains in the [Himalayas](/wiki/Himalaya).

## Namesakes

- Two [US Navy](/wiki/US_Navy) ships have been named [USS *Katahdin*](/wiki/USS_Katahdin). [*Katahdin*](/wiki/Katahdin_(Lake_Boat)) is also the name of a 1914 [steamboat](/wiki/Steamboat) (later converted to [diesel](/wiki/Diesel_engine)) owned by the Moosehead Marine Museum that plies the waters of [Moosehead Lake](/wiki/Moosehead_Lake) in northern [Maine](/wiki/Maine).
- The Katahdin potato, which was certified by the [U.S. Department of Agriculture](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) in 1932, and is still popular in the Northeastern United States.
- The [Katahdin sheep](/wiki/Katahdin_sheep).
- Composer [Alan Hovhaness](/wiki/Alan_Hovhaness) composed a sonata for piano titled *Mount Katahdin* in 1987.
- Katahdin is a monster in the 1979 horror film *[Prophecy](/wiki/Prophecy_(film))*.
