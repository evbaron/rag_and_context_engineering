This article is about the bodhisattva. For the film, see [Avalokitesvara (film)](/wiki/Avalokitesvara_(film)).

In [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism), **Avalokiteśvara** (meaning "the Lord who looks down", [IPA](/wiki/International_Phonetic_Alphabet): [/ˌʌvəloʊkɪˈteɪʃvərə/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English)), also known as **Lokeśvara** ("Lord of the World") and **Chenrezig** (in Tibetan), is a [Bodhisattva](/wiki/Bodhisattva) associated with Great [Compassion](/wiki/Compassion) (*[mahākaruṇā](/wiki/Karu%E1%B9%87%C4%81)*). Avalokiteśvara has a vast number of manifestations (e.g., the 108 forms of Avalokiteśvara) and is depicted in various forms and styles across Buddhist traditions of different cultures. In some texts, he is considered to be the source and divine creator of all [Hindu deities](/wiki/Hindu_deities) (such as [Vishnu](/wiki/Vishnu), [Shiva](/wiki/Shiva), [Brahma](/wiki/Brahma), [Saraswati](/wiki/Saraswati), [Bhudevi](/wiki/Bhumi_(goddess)), [Varuna](/wiki/Varuna), etc.). In [Mahayana](/wiki/Mahayana) and [Vajrayana](/wiki/Vajrayana) Buddhism, Avalokiteśvara is also considered a manifestation of [Amitabha Buddha](/wiki/Amitabha_Buddha) for the purpose of Dharma teaching, and an emanation from [Vairocana Buddha](/wiki/Vairocana_Buddha) as an embodiment of the Miraculous Observing Wisdom (妙觀察智).

In [East Asian Buddhism](/wiki/East_Asian_Buddhism), Avalokiteśvara is known as [觀音](/wiki/%E8%A7%80%E9%9F%B3) (an abbreviation for 觀世音), pronounced [**Gwoon Yaam**](/wiki/Guanyin) in Cantonese, **[Guanyin](/wiki/Guanyin)** in Mandarin Chinese, **[Kannon](/wiki/Guanyin)** in Japanese, [**Gwaneum**](/wiki/Guanyin)  in Korean, and [**Quan Âm**](/wiki/Guanyin) in Vietnamese. In the traditional cultures of these Asian countries, there is a female form of Avalokiteśvara depicted as a divine mother in a white robe, called White-Robed Avalokiteśvara or Southern Sea Avalokiteśvara. This female form of Avalokiteśvara is worshiped widely in [East Asian religions](/wiki/East_Asian_religions) including [Taoism](/wiki/Taoism) and [Chinese folk religion](/wiki/Chinese_folk_religion).

Avalokiteśvara is also known for his popular [mantra](/wiki/Mantra), *[Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ](/wiki/Om_mani_padme_hum)*, which is the most popular mantra in [Tibetan Buddhism](/wiki/Tibetan_Buddhism).

## Etymology

The name *Avalokiteśvara* combines the verbal prefix *ava* "down", *lokita*, a past participle of the verb *lok* "to look, notice, behold, observe", here used in an active sense, and finally *[īśvara](/wiki/Ishvara)*, "lord", "ruler", "sovereign", or "master". In accordance with [sandhi](/wiki/Sandhi) (Sanskrit rules of sound combination), *a*+*īśvara* becomes *eśvara*. Combined, the parts mean "lord who gazed down (at the world)". The word *[loka](/wiki/Loka)* ("world") is absent from the name, but the phrase is implied. It does appear in the Cambodian form of the name, *Lokesvarak*.

The earliest translation of the name *Avalokiteśvara* into Chinese by authors such as [Xuanzang](/wiki/Xuanzang) was as *Guānzìzài* ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 觀自在; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Guān zìzài*), not the form used in [East Asian Buddhism](/wiki/East_Asian_Buddhism) today, which is ***[Guanyin](/wiki/Guanyin)*** ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 觀音; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Guānyīn*). It was initially thought that early translators, lacking fluency in [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit), mistook *Avalokiteśvara* for *Avalokitasvara* ("who looked down upon sound", *i.e.*, the cries of [sentient beings](/wiki/Sentient_beings_(Buddhism)) who need help) and thus mistranslated *Avalokiteśvara* as *Guānyīn*. It is now understood that *Avalokitasvara* was the original form and is also the origin of *Guanyin* "perceiving sound, cries". This translation was favored by the tendency of some Chinese translators, notably [Kumārajīva](/wiki/Kum%C4%81raj%C4%ABva), to use the variant *Guānshìyīn* [Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 觀世音; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Guānshìyīn* "who perceives the world's lamentations"—wherein *lok* was read as simultaneously meaning both "to look" and "world" (Sanskrit *loka*; [Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 世; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *shì*). The original form of Guanyin's name appears in Sanskrit fragments from the fifth century.

The original meaning of the name fits the Buddhist understanding of the role of a bodhisattva. The reinterpretation presenting him as an *īśvara* shows a strong influence of [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism), as the term *īśvara* was usually connected to the Hindu notion of [Vishnu](/wiki/Vishnu) (in [Vaishnavism](/wiki/Vaishnavism)) or [Shiva](/wiki/Shiva) (in [Shaivism](/wiki/Shaivism)) as the [Supreme Lord](/wiki/God), Creator, and Ruler of the world. Some attributes of such a god were transmitted to the bodhisattva, but the mainstream of those who venerated Avalokiteśvara upheld the Buddhist rejection of the doctrine of any creator god.

In Sanskrit, Avalokiteśvara is also referred to as *Lokeśvara* ("Lord of the World"). In [Tibetan](/wiki/Classical_Tibetan), Avalokiteśvara is *Chenrézig* ([Tibetan](/wiki/Tibetan_script): སྤྱན་རས་གཟིགས་). The etymology of the Tibetan name Chenrézik is *spyan* "eye", *ras* "continuity", and *gzig* "to look". This gives the meaning of one who always looks upon all beings (with the eye of compassion).

## Origin

### Mahayana account

Avalokiteśvara painting from a [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit) [palm-leaf manuscript](/wiki/Palm-leaf_manuscript). [Nalanda](/wiki/Nalanda_mahavihara), India, 12th century.

These are found in Chapter 25 of the *Lotus Sutra:* The Universal Gate of Bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 觀世音菩薩普門品; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Guānshìyīn púsà pǔ mén pǐn*). This chapter is devoted to Avalokiteśvara, describing him as a compassionate bodhisattva who hears the cries of sentient beings and who works tirelessly to help those who call upon his name. A total of 33 different manifestations of Avalokiteśvara are described, including female manifestations, all to suit the minds of various beings. The chapter consists of both a prose and a verse section. This earliest source often circulates separately as its own sutra, called the *Avalokiteśvara Sūtra* ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 觀世音經; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Guānshìyīn jīng*), and is commonly recited or chanted at Buddhist temples in East Asia.

Four-armed [Tibetan](/wiki/Tibet) form of Avalokiteśvara.

When the Chinese monk [Faxian](/wiki/Faxian) traveled to [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura) in India around 400 CE, he wrote about monks presenting offerings to Avalokiteśvara. When [Xuanzang](/wiki/Xuanzang) traveled to India in the 7th century, he provided eyewitness accounts of Avalokiteśvara statues being venerated by devotees from all walks of life, from kings to monks to laypeople.

Avalokiteśvara / Padmapani, [Ajanta Caves](/wiki/Ajanta_Caves), India

In [Chinese Buddhism](/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism) and East Asia, [Tangmi](/wiki/Tangmi) practices for the 18-armed form of Avalokiteśvara called [Cundī](/wiki/Cundi_(Buddhism)) are very popular. The popularity of Cundī is attested by the three extant translations of the *Cundī Dhāraṇī Sūtra* from Sanskrit to Chinese, made from the end of the seventh century to the beginning of the eighth century. In [late imperial China](/wiki/Late_Imperial_China), these early esoteric traditions still thrived in Buddhist communities. Robert Gimello has also observed that in these communities, the esoteric practices of Cundī were extremely popular among both the populace and the elite.

In the [Tiantai](/wiki/Tiantai) school, [six forms of Avalokiteśvara](/wiki/Six_Guanyin) are defined. Each of the bodhisattva's six qualities is said to break the hindrances in one of the six realms of existence: hell-beings, [pretas](/wiki/Preta), animals, humans, [asuras](/wiki/Asura_(Buddhism)), and [devas](/wiki/Deva_(Buddhism)).

According to Nīlakaṇṭha Dhāraṇī Sūtra, [Gautama Buddha](/wiki/Gautama_Buddha) told his disciple Ānanda that Avalokiteśvara had become a Buddha countless eons ago, with the name Samyaka Dharma-Vidya Tathāgata meaning "Tathāgata who clearly understands the right Dharma". Out of great compassion, he wants to help all other Bodhisattvas to achieve the highest Awakenment, and bring happiness and peacefulness to all sentient beings, therefore he appears as a Bodhisattva, taking the name Avalokiteshvara and often abides in the [Sahā world](/wiki/Sah%C4%81).

Another Mahayana Sutra, Tathagata's Unimaginable State Sutra, reaffirms that Avalokiteśvara is actually a Buddha. In the Sutra it is written that when [Sakyamuni Buddha](/wiki/Sakyamuni_Buddha) attained the highest Awakenment, countless Buddhas from other worlds, appearing as Bodhisattvas, came to our world to congratulate him and assist his Dharma-teaching work, and Avalokiteśvara was one of those Buddhas who appeared as Bodhisattvas.

In Mahayana Buddhism, Avalokiteśvara is one of the **Four Great Bodhisattvas** (四大菩薩) who are [Mañjuśrī](/wiki/Ma%C3%B1ju%C5%9Br%C4%AB), [Samantabhadra](/wiki/Samantabhadra_(Bodhisattva)), Avalokiteśvara, and [Kṣitigarbha](/wiki/K%E1%B9%A3itigarbha). Avalokiteśvara is also a close assistant of [Amitabha Buddha](/wiki/Amitabha_Buddha), helping Amitabha Buddha to preach the Dharma of the Pure Land.

### Theravāda account

Bronze statue of Avalokiteśvara from [Sri Lanka](/wiki/Sri_Lanka), c. 750 CE

Veneration of Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva has continued to the present day in [Sri Lanka](/wiki/Sri_Lanka).

In times past, both *Tantrayana* and *Mahayana* have been found in some of the *Theravada* countries, but today the Buddhism of [Sri Lanka](/wiki/Sri_Lanka) (formerly, Ceylon), [Myanmar](/wiki/Myanmar) (formerly, Burma), Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia is almost exclusively *Theravada*, based on the Pali Canon. The only *Mahayana* deity that has entered the worship of ordinary Buddhists in Theravada Buddhism is *Bodhisattva Avalokitesvara*. In Sri Lanka, he is known as *Natha-deva* and is believed by the majority to be the Buddha yet to come, *Bodhisattva Maitreya*. The figure of *Avalokitesvara* is usually found in the shrine room near the Buddha image.

In more recent times, some western-educated Theravādins have attempted to identify Nātha with Maitreya Bodhisattva; however, traditions and basic iconography (including an image of Amitābha Buddha on the front of the crown) identify Nātha as Avalokiteśvara. Andrew Skilton writes:

> ... It is clear from sculptural evidence alone that the Mahāyāna was fairly widespread throughout Sri Lanka, although the modern account of the history of Buddhism on the island presents an unbroken and pure lineage of Theravāda. (One can only assume that similar trends were transmitted to other parts of Southeast Asia with Sri Lankan ordination lineages.) Relics of an extensive cult of Avalokiteśvara can be seen in the present-day figure of Nātha.

Avalokiteśvara is popularly worshipped in [Myanmar](/wiki/Myanmar), where he is called *Lokanat* or lokabyuharnat, and [Thailand](/wiki/Thailand), where he is called *Lokesvara*. The bodhisattva goes by many other names. In Indochina and Thailand, he is *Lokesvara*, "The Lord of the World". In Tibet, he is *Chenrezig*, also spelled Spyan-ras gzigs, "With a Pitying Look". In China, the bodhisattva takes a female form and is called *Guanyin* (also spelled Kwan Yin, Kuanyin, or Kwun Yum), "Hearing the Sounds of the World". In Japan, Guanyin is *Kannon* or Kanzeon; in Korea, *Gwaneum*; and in Vietnam, *Quan Am*.

Wood carving of Lokanat at [Shwenandaw Monastery](/wiki/Shwenandaw_Monastery), [Mandalay](/wiki/Mandalay), [Burma](/wiki/Burma)

### Modern scholarship

Avalokiteśvara is worshipped as Nātha in Sri Lanka. The [Tamil Buddhist](/wiki/Buddhism_amongst_Tamils) tradition developed in [Chola literature](/wiki/Chola_literature), such as Buddamitra's *Virasoliyam*, states that the [Vedic](/wiki/Vedic) sage [Agastya](/wiki/Agastya) learned Tamil from Avalokiteśvara. The earlier Chinese traveler [Xuanzang](/wiki/Xuanzang) recorded a temple dedicated to Avalokitesvara in the south Indian [Mount Potalaka](/wiki/Mount_Potalaka), a Sanskritization of [Pothigai](/wiki/Pothigai), where Tamil Hindu tradition places Agastya as having learned the Tamil language from [Shiva](/wiki/Shiva). Avalokitesvara worship gained popularity with the growth of the [Abhayagiri vihāra](/wiki/Abhayagiri_vih%C4%81ra)'s [Tamraparniyan](/wiki/Tamraparni) Mahayana sect.

[Pothigai Malai](/wiki/Pothigai) in [Tamil Nadu](/wiki/Tamil_Nadu) is proposed as the original [Mount Potalaka](/wiki/Mount_Potalaka) in [India](/wiki/India).

Western scholars have not reached a consensus on the origin of the reverence for Avalokiteśvara. Some have suggested that Avalokiteśvara, along with many other supernatural beings in Buddhism, was a borrowing or absorption by [Mahayana Buddhism](/wiki/Mahayana) of one or more deities from [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism), in particular Shiva or [Vishnu](/wiki/Vishnu). This seems to be based on the name Avalokiteśvara.

On the basis of Buddhist scriptures, ancient [Tamil](/wiki/Tamil_language) literary sources, and field surveys, Japanese scholar Shu Hikosaka proposes the hypothesis that ancient Mount Potalaka, the residence of Avalokiteśvara described in the *[Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra](/wiki/Gandavyuha)* and Xuanzang's *[Great Tang Records on the Western Regions](/wiki/Great_Tang_Records_on_the_Western_Regions)*, is [Mount Potigai](/wiki/Pothigai) in [Ambasamudram](/wiki/Ambasamudram), [Tirunelveli](/wiki/Tirunelveli), at the [Tamil Nadu](/wiki/Tamil_Nadu)-[Kerala](/wiki/Kerala) border. Shu also said that Mount Potalaka has been a sacred place for the people of [South India](/wiki/South_India) since time immemorial. It is the traditional residence of [Siddhar](/wiki/Siddhar) [Agastya](/wiki/Agastya) at [Agastya Mala](/wiki/Agastya_Mala). With the spread of Buddhism in the region beginning at the time of the great king [Aśoka](/wiki/Ashoka) in the third century BCE, it became a holy place also for Buddhists, who gradually became dominant as a number of their hermits settled there. The local people, though, mainly remained followers of the Tamil animist religion. The mixed Tamil-Buddhist cult culminated in the formation of the figure of Avalokiteśvara.

The name Lokeśvara should not be confused with that of [Lokeśvararāja](/wiki/Loke%C5%9Bvarar%C4%81ja), the Buddha under whom Dharmakara became a monk and made forty-eight vows before becoming [Amitābha](/wiki/Amit%C4%81bha).

Avalokiteśvara's six armed manifestation as [Cintāmaṇicakra](/wiki/Cint%C4%81ma%E1%B9%87icakra) is also widely venerated in East Asia. The *Cintāmaṇicakra Dharani* ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 如意寶輪王陀羅尼; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Rúyì Bǎolún Wáng Tuóluóní*) is another popular dharani associated with the bodhisattva.

## Manifestations

Avalokiteśvara has an extraordinarily large number of forms, emanations or manifestations, including wisdom goddesses (*vidyās*) directly associated with him in images and texts.

Furthermore, at least two separate female Buddhist deities, [Cundī](/wiki/Cundi_(Buddhism)) and [Tara](/wiki/Tara_(Buddhism)) also later came to be associated with Avalokiteśvara (and were even seen as manifestations of him).

According to [Śūraṅgama Sūtra](/wiki/%C5%9A%C5%ABra%E1%B9%85gama_S%C5%ABtra), Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva had achieved perfect mastery of Buddhist Dharma and hence can manifest countless forms with different numbers of heads, arms and eyes.

### Thousand-armed Avalokiteśvara

Colossal bronze [Song dynasty](/wiki/Song_dynasty) (960–1279) statue of Qianshou [Guanyin](/wiki/Guanyin) (Sahrasasbhuja Avalokiteśvara) located at the [Tower of Great Compassion](/wiki/Guanyin_Dian) of [Longxing Temple](/wiki/Longxing_Temple) in [Hebei](/wiki/Hebei), [China](/wiki/China).

One prominent Buddhist story tells of Avalokiteśvara vowing never to rest until he had freed all sentient beings from [saṃsāra](/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra). Despite strenuous effort, he realizes that many unhappy beings were yet to be saved. After struggling to comprehend the needs of so many, his head splits into eleven pieces. [Amitābha](/wiki/Amit%C4%81bha), seeing his plight, gives him eleven heads with which to hear the cries of the suffering. Upon hearing these cries and comprehending them, Avalokiteśvara tries to reach out to all those who needed aid, but found that his two arms shattered into pieces. Once more, Amitābha comes to his aid and invests him with a thousand arms with which to aid the suffering multitudes.

This manifestation of Avalokiteśvara is known as Sahrasasbhuja Avalokiteśvara ("Thousand-armed Avalokiteśvara), and is among the bodhisattva's most popular iconographic forms across China, Japan and Korea.

### Avalokiteśvara as a cosmic maheśvara ("Great Lord of Creation")

Sṛṣṭikartā Lokeśvara (Avalokiteshvara in the process of creation), in which the bodhisattva takes on the form of Sṛṣṭikartā (creator) and emanates all the [Hindu gods](/wiki/Hindu_deities) for the benefit of sentient beings

According to various Mahayana sources, numerous [Hindu deities](/wiki/Hindu_deities) are considered to be emanations of Avalokiteshvara. For example, in the *[Kāraṇḍavyūhasūtra](/wiki/K%C4%81ra%E1%B9%87%E1%B8%8Davy%C5%ABha_S%C5%ABtra)* (4th–5th century CE), Great universal deities called [Vishnu](/wiki/Vishnu), [Shiva](/wiki/Shiva), [Brahma](/wiki/Brahma) and [Saraswati](/wiki/Saraswati) are all said to have emerged from Avalokiteshvara bodhisattva's body. The passage states:

> [Āditya](/wiki/Adityas) and [Candra](/wiki/Chandra) came from his eyes, [Maheśvara](/wiki/Shiva) came from his forehead, [Brahmā](/wiki/Brahma) came from his shoulders, [Nārāyaṇa](/wiki/Narayana) came from his heart, Devi [Sarasvatī](/wiki/Saraswati) came from his canines, [Vāyu](/wiki/Vayu) came from his mouth, [Dharaṇī](/wiki/Dhara_(deity)) came from his feet, and [Varuṇa](/wiki/Varuna) came from his stomach.

In a similar manner, Hindu deities like [Nīlakaṇṭha](/wiki/Nilkanth) and [Harihara](/wiki/Harihara) are cited in the *[Nīlakaṇṭha Dhāraṇī](/wiki/N%C4%ABlaka%E1%B9%87%E1%B9%ADha_Dh%C4%81ra%E1%B9%87%C4%AB)*, possibly as forms of Avalokiteshvara or as associated bodhisattvas (the text is not clear, though traditionally these have been interpreted as various names or forms of Avalokiteshvara).

Alexander Studholme writes that these sources are influenced by [Puranic](/wiki/Puranas) [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism), and its concepts of an [Īśvara](/wiki/Ishvara) ("lord") and Maheśvara ("great lord"), both of which are terms that refer to a transcendent and all pervasive being. The name Maheśvara is also applied to Avalokiteshvara three times in the Kāraṇḍavyūhasūtra, and some passages he is described as a [cosmic man](/wiki/Macranthropy), similar to how the Puranas depict Vishnu or Shiva. However, this Buddhist myth only focuses on how Avalokiteshvara gives birth to all the gods (devas), and he is not depicted as a true [Creator God](/wiki/Creator_deity) (who creates the cosmos, like the Hindu [Īśvara](/wiki/Ishvara)), instead he is depicted as a great cosmic being that manifests in myriad ways as a [skillful means](/wiki/Upaya) to guide living beings to Buddhahood.

## Mantras and Dharanis

OṂ MAŅI PADME HǕṂ. The six syllable [mantra](/wiki/Mantra) of Avalokiteśvara written in the [Tibetan alphabet](/wiki/Tibetan_alphabet).

There are various [mantras](/wiki/Mantra) and [dharanis](/wiki/Dharani) associated with Avalokiteśvara.

### Mani mantra

In [Tibetan Buddhism](/wiki/Tibetan_Buddhism), the central mantra is the six-syllable [mantra](/wiki/Mantra) *[Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ](/wiki/Om_mani_padme_hum)* ([Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit_language): ॐ मणि पद्मे हूँ, also called the Mani mantra. Due to his association with this mantra, one form of Avalokiteśvara is called *Ṣaḍākṣarī* ("Lord of the Six Syllables") in [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit). The Mani mantra is also popular in [East Asian Mahayana](/wiki/East_Asian_Buddhism), such as [Chinese Buddhism](/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism). There are also different variations of the mani mantra, the most common which is *Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ hrīḥ.* Hrīḥ is the seed syllable of the Lotus [Buddha family](/wiki/Five_Tath%C4%81gatas) and the Buddha [Amitabha](/wiki/Amit%C4%81bha).

Recitation of this mantra while using [prayer beads](/wiki/Buddhist_prayer_beads) is the most popular religious practice in Tibetan Buddhism. Another popular religious practice associated with om mani padme hum is the spinning of [prayer wheels](/wiki/Prayer_wheels) clockwise, which contains numerous repetitions of this [mantra](/wiki/Mantra) and effectively benefits everyone within the vicinity of the practitioner.

The connection between this famous mantra and Avalokiteśvara is documented for the first time in the **[Kāraṇḍavyūhasūtra](/wiki/K%C4%81ra%E1%B9%87%E1%B8%8Davy%C5%ABhas%C5%ABtra)**. This text is dated to around the late 4th century CE to the early 5th century CE. In this sūtra, a bodhisattva is told by the Buddha that recitation of this mantra while focusing on the sound can lead to the attainment of eight hundred [samādhis](/wiki/Samadhi).

### Ārolik mantra

Another mantra for Avalokiteśvara commonly recited in East Asian Buddhism is "three and a half syllables" (ardhacaturthākṣara) heart-mantra: "*oṃ ārolik svāha*" (or sometimes just *Ārolik* or *oṁ ārolik*), which is found (in many forms and variations like *ārolika*, *arulika*, etc.) in numerous pre-tenth-century Indian texts, including the 7th century Chinese translation of the *Dhāraṇīsaṁgraha*, the *[Susiddhikarasūtra](/wiki/Susiddhikara_S%C5%ABtra),* [the *Mañjuśriyamūlakalpa*](/wiki/Ma%C3%B1ju%C5%9Br%C4%AB-m%C5%ABla-kalpa)*,* and the *[Guhyasamājatantra](/wiki/Guhyasam%C4%81ja_Tantra).*

In [Chinese Buddhism](/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism), this mantra is known as "Avalokiteśvara's mantra for eradicating karmic obstructions" ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 觀世音菩薩滅業障真言, [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *[Guānshìyīn](/wiki/Guan_Sh%C3%AC_Yin) púsà miè yèzhàng zhēnyán*) and is typically recited everyday as part of the standard liturgy during the daily Mengshan Shishi ritual ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 蒙山施食; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Méngshān shīshí*; lit. 'Mengshan food-bestowal') carried out in all Chinese Buddhist monasteries. In [Shingon Buddhism](/wiki/Shingon_Buddhism), this mantra is the main mantra for Avalokiteśvara, and it is also considered to be the main mantra of the [Lotus Buddha family](/wiki/Five_Tath%C4%81gatas).

One text (Taisho Tripitaka no. 1031) describes a visualization practice done after reciting oṁ ārolik svāhā seven times which includes meditating on the meanings of the four letters of ārolik which are:

1. a: all dharmas are originally unborn (ādyanutpanna);
2. ra: all dharmas are dissociated from defilement (rajas);
3. la: characteristics (lakṣaṇa) are inapprehensible in all dharmas;
4. ka: all dharmas are without action (kārya).

The Ārolik mantra has also been found engraved on a few sculptures found in north India*.* One of these begins with "ārolik oṁ hrīḥ". Another one of these found in [Bihar](/wiki/Bihar) also included other mantras, including [*ye dharma hetu*](/wiki/Prat%C4%ABtyasamutp%C4%81da_g%C4%81th%C4%81)*,* followed by *"namo ratnatrayāya namo Āryāvalokiteśvarāya bodhisatvāya mahāsatvāya mahākāruṇikāya Ārolok Oṁ hriḥ hriḥ".*

Another longer mantra appears in a translation by [Amoghavajra](/wiki/Amoghavajra) (T. 1033, 20: 9b1–7):

> namoratnatrayāya | nama āryāvalokiteśvarāya bodhisattvāya mahāsattvāya mahākāruṇikāya | tadyathā
> padmapāṇi sara sara ehy ehi bhagavann āryāvalokiteśvara ārolik |

In Chinese, *oṃ ārolik svāha* is pronounced *Ǎn ālǔlēi jì suōpóhē* (唵 阿嚕勒繼 娑婆訶). In Korean, it is pronounced *Om aroreuk Ge Sabaha* (옴 아로늑계 사바하). In Japanese, it is pronounced *On arori kya sowa ka* (おん あろりきゃ そわか). In the [Siddham script](/wiki/Siddham_script) it is written as 𑖄𑖼 𑖁𑖨𑖺𑖩𑖰𑖎𑖿 𑖭𑖿𑖪𑖯𑖮𑖯𑗃.

### Dharanis

The *[Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra](/wiki/K%C4%81ra%E1%B9%87%E1%B8%8Davy%C5%ABha_S%C5%ABtra)* also features the first appearance of the [dhāraṇī](/wiki/Dh%C4%81ra%E1%B9%87%C4%AB) of [Cundī](/wiki/Cundi_(Buddhism)), which occurs at the end of the sūtra text. After the bodhisattva finally attains samādhi with the mantra "oṃ maṇipadme hūṃ", he is able to observe 77 koṭīs of fully enlightened buddhas replying to him in one voice with the Cundī Dhāraṇī: *namaḥ saptānāṃ samyaksaṃbuddha koṭīnāṃ tadyathā, oṃ cale cule cunde svāhā.*

The *[Nīlakaṇṭha Dhāraṇī](/wiki/N%C4%ABlaka%E1%B9%87%E1%B9%ADha_Dh%C4%81ra%E1%B9%87%C4%AB)* is an 82-syllable dhāraṇī for Avalokiteśvara also known as the Great Compassion Mantra. It is very popular in [East Asian Buddhism](/wiki/East_Asian_Buddhism). Another popular Avalokiteśvara dharani in East Asian Buddhism is *[Eleven-Faced Avalokitesvara Heart Dharani](/wiki/Eleven-Faced_Avalokitesvara_Heart_Dharani_Sutra).* This dharani is associated with Avalokiteśvara's eleven face form, known as [Ekādaśamukha](/wiki/Ek%C4%81da%C5%9Bamukha), one of [the six forms of Guanyin.](/wiki/Six_Guanyin)

## Tibetan Buddhist beliefs

Avalokiteśvara is an important deity in [Tibetan Buddhism](/wiki/Tibetan_Buddhism). He is regarded in the [Vajrayana](/wiki/Vajrayana) teachings as a Buddha.

In Tibetan Buddhism, [Tãrã](/wiki/Tara_(Buddhism)) came into existence from a single tear shed by Avalokiteśvara. When the tear fell to the ground it created a lake, and a lotus opening in the lake revealed Tara. In another version of this story, Tara emerges from the heart of Avalokiteśvara. In either version, it is Avalokiteśvara's outpouring of compassion which manifests Tãrã as a being.

Certain living [tulku](/wiki/Tulku) lineages, including the [Dalai Lamas](/wiki/Dalai_Lama) and the [Karmapas](/wiki/Karmapa), are considered by many Tibetan Buddhists to also be manifestations of Avalokiteśvara.

## Iconography

### Deer-skin (tinasara)

krishnasara deer ([Antilope cervicapra](/wiki/Antilope_cervicapra))

In Tibetan iconography, Avalokiteśvara (Chenrezig) is occasionally depicted wearing a deer-skin—*tinasara* or *krishnasara* कृष्णसार (kṛṣṇasāra) in Sanskrit, and *ཀྲཱིཤྣ་སཱ་ར (krī-shna-sā-ra)* or *ཁྲི་སྙན་སཱ་ར།* in Tibetan—draped over his left shoulder. This attribute serves as a layered symbol of renunciation, deep compassion, and yogic practice. Historically, Indian ascetics used deer-skins as meditation seats or garments to support austere retreat and concentration.

The iconographic tradition may trace back to the *Ruru Jātaka* (Jātaka tale No. 482), also known as the 'Golden Deer' story, in which the bodhisattva was once born as a splendid golden deer endowed with compassion and the ability to speak human languages. He saved a man from drowning and, when the king later hunted him, he offered himself to protect his followers. His act of selflessness moved the king to prohibit hunting throughout the realm—thus embodying compassion that transforms society.

This convergence of yogic renunciation, mythic compassion, and visual representation makes the tinasara a powerful emblem in Chenrezig's devotional art.
