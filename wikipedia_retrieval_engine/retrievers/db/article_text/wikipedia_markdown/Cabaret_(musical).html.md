***Cabaret*** is an American [musical](/wiki/Musical_theater) with music by [John Kander](/wiki/John_Kander), lyrics by [Fred Ebb](/wiki/Fred_Ebb), and a book by [Joe Masteroff](/wiki/Joe_Masteroff). It is based on the play *[I Am a Camera](/wiki/I_Am_a_Camera)* by [John Van Druten](/wiki/John_Van_Druten), premiered in 1951, which in turn was based on the 1939 novel *[Goodbye to Berlin](/wiki/Goodbye_to_Berlin)* by [Christopher Isherwood](/wiki/Christopher_Isherwood).

Set in 1929–1930 [Berlin](/wiki/Berlin) during the twilight of the [Jazz Age](/wiki/Jazz_Age) as the [Nazis](/wiki/Nazi_Party) rise to power, the musical focuses on the hedonistic nightlife at the seedy Kit Kat Klub and revolves around American writer Clifford Bradshaw's relations with English [cabaret](/wiki/Cabaret) performer [Sally Bowles](/wiki/Sally_Bowles). A subplot involves the doomed romance between German boarding house owner Fräulein Schneider and her elderly suitor Herr Schultz, a [Jewish](/wiki/Anti-Jewish_legislation_in_prewar_Nazi_Germany) fruit vendor. Overseeing the action is the [Master of Ceremonies](/wiki/Master_of_Ceremonies) at the Kit Kat Klub, and the club itself serves as a metaphor for ominous political developments in late [Weimar Germany](/wiki/Weimar_Germany).

The original [Broadway](/wiki/Broadway_theatre) production opened on November 20, 1966, at the [Broadhurst Theatre](/wiki/Broadhurst_Theatre) in New York City and became a box office hit that ran for 1,166 performances. The production won eight [Tony Awards](/wiki/Tony_Award) and inspired numerous subsequent productions around the world as well as the [1972 film of the same name](/wiki/Cabaret_(1972_film)).

## Background

### Historical basis

Further information: [Christopher Isherwood](/wiki/Christopher_Isherwood) and [Jean Ross](/wiki/Jean_Ross)

Writer [Christopher Isherwood](/wiki/Christopher_Isherwood) (left) and chanteuse [Jean Ross](/wiki/Jean_Ross) (right) photographed in the 1930s.

The events depicted in the 1966 musical are derived from Anglo-American writer Christopher Isherwood's autobiographical tales of his colorful escapades in the [Weimar Republic](/wiki/Weimar_Republic). In 1929, Isherwood visited [Weimar-era Berlin](/wiki/Weimar_culture) during the final months of the [Golden Twenties](/wiki/Golden_Twenties). He relocated to Berlin to avail himself of boy prostitutes and to enjoy the city's orgiastic [Jazz Age](/wiki/Jazz_Age) cabarets. He socialized with a [coterie of gay writers](/wiki/Auden_Group) that included [Stephen Spender](/wiki/Stephen_Spender), [Paul Bowles](/wiki/Paul_Bowles), and [W.H. Auden](/wiki/W.H._Auden). At the time, Isherwood viewed the rise of [Nazism](/wiki/Nazism) in Germany with political indifference and instead focused on writing a novel.

In Berlin, Isherwood shared modest lodgings with 19-year-old British [flapper](/wiki/Flapper) [Jean Ross](/wiki/Jean_Ross), an aspiring film actress who earned her living as a [chanteuse](/wiki/Chanteuse) in [lesbian bars](/wiki/Lesbian_bar) and second-rate cabarets. While room-mates at [Nollendorfstrasse 17](/wiki/Nollendorfplatz) in [Schöneberg](/wiki/Sch%C3%B6neberg), a 27-year-old Isherwood settled into a sexual relationship with a 16-year-old German boy, and Ross became pregnant after engaging in a series of sexual liaisons. She believed the father of the child to be jazz pianist and later film actor [Peter van Eyck](/wiki/Peter_van_Eyck).

As a favor to Ross, Isherwood pretended to be her heterosexual impregnator in order to facilitate an [abortion](/wiki/Abortion). Ross nearly died from the procedure due to the doctor's incompetence. Visiting Ross during her recovery in a Berlin hospital, Isherwood perceived hostility from the hospital staff who believed that he had forced her to undergo the abortion. These events inspired Isherwood to write his 1937 [novella](/wiki/Novella) *Sally Bowles*, in which the abortion serves as the narrative climax.

While Ross recovered from the botched abortion, the political situation [rapidly deteriorated](/wiki/Adolf_Hitler%27s_rise_to_power) in Weimar Germany as the incipient [Nazi Party](/wiki/Nazi_Party) grew stronger day by day. "There was a sensation of doom to be felt in the Berlin streets", Stephen Spender recalled. As Berlin's daily scenes increasingly featured "poverty, unemployment, political demonstrations and [street fighting](/wiki/Street_fighting) between the forces of the [extreme left](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Germany) and the [extreme right](/wiki/Nazi_Party)", Isherwood, Ross, Spender, and other British nationals realized that they must leave the politically volatile country as soon as possible.

Two weeks after the [Enabling Act](/wiki/Enabling_Act_of_1933) cemented [Adolf Hitler](/wiki/Adolf_Hitler)'s dictatorship, Isherwood fled Germany on May 13, 1933. Afterwards, the Nazis shuttered most of Berlin's seedy cabarets, and many of Isherwood's cabaret acquaintances fled abroad or perished in [concentration camps](/wiki/Concentration_camp). These events served as the genesis for Isherwood's [Berlin stories](/wiki/The_Berlin_Stories). In 1951, playwright [John Van Druten](/wiki/John_Van_Druten) adapted Isherwood's 1939 novel *[Goodbye to Berlin](/wiki/Goodbye_to_Berlin)* into the Broadway play *[I Am a Camera](/wiki/I_Am_a_Camera)* which in turn became a [1955 film](/wiki/I_Am_a_Camera_(film)) starring [Laurence Harvey](/wiki/Laurence_Harvey) and [Julie Harris](/wiki/Julie_Harris).

### Musical development

Further information: [Harold Prince](/wiki/Harold_Prince) and [Kander and Ebb](/wiki/Kander_and_Ebb)

[Julie Andrews](/wiki/Julie_Andrews) was offered the role of [Sally Bowles](/wiki/Sally_Bowles), but her manager refused due to the character's immorality.

In early 1963, producer David Black commissioned English composer and lyricist [Sandy Wilson](/wiki/Sandy_Wilson) to undertake a musical adaptation of Van Druten's 1951 play *[I Am a Camera](/wiki/I_Am_a_Camera)*. Black hoped that singer [Julie Andrews](/wiki/Julie_Andrews) would agree to star in the adaptation, but Andrews' manager refused to allow her to accept the role of Sally Bowles due to the character's immorality. By the time Wilson completed his work, however, Black's option on both the 1951 Van Druten play and its source material by Isherwood had lapsed and been acquired by rival Broadway producer [Harold Prince](/wiki/Harold_Prince). Prince wished to create a gritty adaptation of Isherwood's stories that drew parallels between the spiritual bankruptcy of Germany in the 1920s and contemporary social problems in the United States at a time "when the struggle for civil rights for black Americans was heating up as a result of nonviolent but bold demonstrations being held in the Deep South."

Prince hired playwright [Joe Masteroff](/wiki/Joe_Masteroff) to work on the adaptation. Both men believed that Wilson's score failed to capture the carefree hedonism of the Jazz Age in late [1920s Berlin](/wiki/1920s_Berlin). They wanted a score that "evoked the Berlin of [Kurt Weill](/wiki/Kurt_Weill) and [Lotte Lenya](/wiki/Lotte_Lenya)." Consequently, Prince invited the songwriting team of [John Kander](/wiki/John_Kander) and [Fred Ebb](/wiki/Fred_Ebb) to join the project. Kander and Ebb envisioned the work as a dramatic play preceded by a prologue of songs describing the Berlin atmosphere from various points of view. As the composers distributed the songs between scenes, they realized the story could be told in the structure of a more traditional [book musical](/wiki/Book_musical), and they replaced several songs with tunes more relevant to the plot.

For the musical adaptation, playwright Joe Masteroff significantly altered Isherwood's original characters. He transformed the English protagonist into an American writer named Clifford Bradshaw; the [antisemitic](/wiki/Antisemitic) landlady became a tolerant woman with a Jewish beau who owned a fruit store; they cut various supporting characters and added new characters such as the Nazi [smuggler](/wiki/Smuggler) Ernst Ludwig for dramatic purposes. The musical ultimately expressed two stories in one: the first, a [revue](/wiki/Revue) centered on the decadence of the Kit Kat Klub, for which Hal Prince created the Master of Ceremonies (Emcee) character played by Joel Grey; the second, a story set in the society outside the club, thus juxtaposing the lives of the characters based on Isherwood's real-life associates and acquaintances with the seedy club.

In fall 1966, the musical entered rehearsals. After viewing one of the last rehearsals before the company headed to [Boston](/wiki/Boston) for the pre-Broadway run, Prince's friend [Jerome Robbins](/wiki/Jerome_Robbins) suggested cutting the songs outside the cabaret, but Prince ignored his advice. In Boston, lead actress [Jill Haworth](/wiki/Jill_Haworth) struggled with her characterization of Sally Bowles. Critics thought Sally's blonde hair and white dress suggested a [debutante](/wiki/Debutante) at a senior [prom](/wiki/Prom) instead of a cabaret singer, so Sally became a brunette before the show opened on Broadway.

Prince staged the show in an unusual way for the time. As the audience entered the theater, they saw the curtain raised, exposing a stage with only a large mirror that reflected the auditorium. Instead of an overture, a drum roll and cymbal crash introduced the opening number. The show mixed dialogue scenes with expository songs and standalone cabaret numbers that provided social commentary. This innovative concept initially surprised audiences. Over time, they discerned the distinction between the two and appreciated the rationale behind them.

## Synopsis

### Act I

{{{annotations}}}

The Kit Kat Klub is intended to evoke the many [Weimar-era cabarets](/wiki/Weimar_culture) in Berlin.

At the twilight of the Jazz Age in Berlin, the incipient [Nazi Party](/wiki/Nazi_Party) is growing stronger. The Kit Kat Klub is a seedy cabaret – a place of decadent celebration. The club's [Master of Ceremonies](/wiki/Master_of_Ceremonies) (Emcee) together with the cabaret girls and waiters, warm up the audience ("[Willkommen](/wiki/Willkommen)"). Meanwhile, a young American writer named Clifford Bradshaw arrives via a railway train in Berlin. He has journeyed to the city to work on a new novel. Cliff encounters Ernst Ludwig, a German [smuggler](/wiki/Smuggler) who offers him [black market](/wiki/Black_market) work and recommends a boarding house. At the boarding house, the proprietress [Fräulein](/wiki/Fr%C3%A4ulein) Schneider offers Cliff a room for one hundred [reichsmarks](/wiki/Reichsmark), but he can only pay fifty. After a brief debate, she relents and allows Cliff to live there for fifty marks. Fräulein Schneider observes that she has learned to take whatever life offers ("So What?").

When Cliff visits the Kit Kat Klub, the Emcee introduces an English [chanteuse](/wiki/Chanteuse), Sally Bowles, who performs a flirtatious number ("Don't Tell Mama"). Afterward, she asks Cliff to recite poetry for her, and he recites [Ernest Thayer](/wiki/Ernest_Thayer)'s mock-heroic poem "[Casey at the Bat](/wiki/Casey_at_the_Bat)". Cliff offers to escort Sally home, but she says that her boyfriend Max, the club's owner, is too jealous. Sally performs her final number at the Kit Kat Klub aided by a female ensemble of jazz babies ("Mein Herr"). The cabaret ensemble performs a song and dance, calling each other on [inter-table phones](/wiki/Chat_line) and inviting each other for dances and drinks ("The Telephone Song").

The next day at the boarding house, Cliff has just finished giving an English lesson to Ernst when Sally arrives. Max has fired her and thrown her out, and now she has no place to live. Sally asks Cliff if she can live in his room. At first he resists, but she persuades him to take her in ("Perfectly Marvelous"). The Emcee and two female companions sing a song ("Two Ladies") that comments on Cliff and Sally's new living arrangement. Herr Schultz, an elderly Jewish fruit-shop owner who lives in the boarding house, gives a [pineapple](/wiki/Pineapple) to Fräulein Schneider as a romantic gesture ("It Couldn't Please Me More"). In the Kit Kat Klub, a young waiter starts to sing a song – a patriotic anthem to the [Fatherland](/wiki/Fatherland) that slowly descends into a darker, [Nazi-inspired](/wiki/Nazi_songs) [marching song](/wiki/Marching_song) ("[Tomorrow Belongs to Me](/wiki/Tomorrow_Belongs_to_Me)"). He initially sings *[a cappella](/wiki/A_cappella)*, before the customers and the band join in.

Months later, Cliff and Sally are still living together and have grown intimate. Cliff knows that he is in a "dream", but he enjoys living with Sally too much to come to his senses ("Why Should I Wake Up?"). Sally reveals that she is pregnant, but she does not know who the father is and decides to have an abortion. Cliff reminds her that it could be his child and tries to persuade her to have the baby ("[Maybe This Time](/wiki/Maybe_This_Time_(Kander_and_Ebb_song))"). Ernst enters and offers Cliff a chance to earn easy money – picking up a suitcase in Paris and delivering it to a client in Berlin. The Emcee comments on this with the song "Sitting Pretty" (or, in later versions, "Money").

Meanwhile, Fräulein Schneider has caught one of her boarders, the prostitute Fräulein Kost, bringing sailors into her room. Fräulein Schneider forbids her from doing so again, but Kost threatens to leave. Kost reveals that she has seen Fräulein Schneider with Herr Schultz in her room. Herr Schultz saves Fräulein Schneider's reputation by telling Fräulein Kost that he and Fräulein Schneider are to be married in three weeks. After Fräulein Kost departs, Fräulein Schneider thanks Herr Schultz for lying to Fräulein Kost. Herr Schultz says that he still wishes to marry Fräulein Schneider ("Married").

At Fräulein Schneider and Herr Schultz's engagement party, Cliff arrives and delivers the suitcase of [contraband](/wiki/Contraband) to Ernst. Sally and Cliff gift the couple a crystal fruit bowl. A tipsy Schultz sings "Meeskite" (*meeskite*, he explains, is [Yiddish](/wiki/Yiddish) for ugly or funny-looking), a song with a moral ("Anyone responsible for loveliness, large or small/Is not a *meeskite* at all"). Afterward, seeking revenge on Fräulein Schneider, Kost tells Ernst, who now sports a [Nazi armband](/wiki/Nazi_armband), that Schultz is a Jew. Ernst warns Schneider that marrying a Jew is unwise. Kost and company reprise "Tomorrow Belongs to Me", with more overtly Nazi overtones, as Cliff, Sally, Schneider, Schultz, and the Emcee look on.

### Act II

{{{annotations}}}

The musical is set against the backdrop of [Nazism's rise in Germany](/wiki/Adolf_Hitler%27s_rise_to_power) which reaches a climax at the end of the second act.

The cabaret girls – along with the Emcee in [drag](/wiki/Drag_(clothing)) – perform a [kickline](/wiki/Kickline) routine which eventually becomes a [goose step](/wiki/Goose_step). Fräulein Schneider expresses her concerns about her impending nuptials to Herr Schultz, who assures her that everything will be all right ("Married" (reprise)). They are interrupted by the crash of a brick being thrown [through the glass window](/wiki/Kristallnacht) of Herr Schultz's fruit shop. Schultz tries to reassure her that it is merely rowdy children making trouble, but Fräulein Schneider is now afraid.

Back at the Kit Kat Klub, the Emcee performs a song-and-dance routine with a woman in a [gorilla suit](/wiki/Gorilla_suit), singing that their love has been met with universal disapproval ("[If You Could See Her](/wiki/If_You_Could_See_Her)"). Encouraging the audience to be more open-minded, he defends his [ape-woman](/wiki/Untermensch), concluding with, "if you could see her through my eyes... she wouldn't look Jewish at all." Fräulein Schneider goes to Cliff and Sally's room and returns their engagement present, explaining that her marriage has been called off. When Cliff protests and states that she can't just give up this way, she asks him what other choice she has ("What Would You Do?").

Cliff begs Sally to leave Germany with him so that they can raise their child together in America. Sally protests and claims that their life in Berlin is wonderful. Cliff urges her to "wake up" and to notice the growing social upheaval around them. Sally retorts that politics have nothing to do with them and returns to the Kit Kat Klub ("I Don't Care Much"). At the club, after another heated argument with Sally, Cliff is accosted by Ernst, who has another delivery job for him. Cliff tries to brush him off. When Ernst inquires if Cliff's attitude towards him is because of "that Jew at the party", Cliff attacks him – only to be beaten by Ernst's bodyguards and ejected from the club. On stage, the Emcee introduces Sally, who enters to perform again, singing that "life is a cabaret, old chum," cementing her decision to live in carefree ignorance ("[Cabaret](/wiki/Cabaret_(Cabaret_song))").

The next morning, a bruised Cliff is packing his clothes in his room when Herr Schultz visits. He informs Cliff that he is moving to another boarding house, but he is confident that these difficult times will soon pass. He understands the German people, he declares, because he is a German too. When Sally returns, she announces that she has had an abortion, and Cliff slaps her. She chides him for his previous insistence on keeping the baby, pointing out it would be a "terrible burden" for a child knowing it was the only reason the parents were together. Cliff still hopes that she will join him in France, but Sally retorts that she has "always hated Paris." She hopes that, when Cliff finally writes his novel, he will dedicate the work to her. Cliff leaves, heartbroken.

> There was a cabaret, and there was a master of ceremonies and there was a city called Berlin, in a country called Germany – and it was the end of the world.

— Cliff Bradshaw, *Cabaret*, Act II

On the railway train to Paris, Cliff begins to compose his novel, reflecting on his experiences: "There was a cabaret, and there was a master of ceremonies ... and there was a city called Berlin, in a country called Germany – and it was the end of the world and I was dancing with Sally Bowles – and we were both fast asleep" ("Willkommen" (reprise)). In the Kit Kat Klub, the Emcee welcomes the audience once again as the ensemble reprises "Willkommen" but the song is now harsh and discordant. The Emcee sings, "Auf Wiedersehen... à bientôt..." followed by a drum roll crescendo and a cymbal crash.

### Song modifications

Many songs planned for the 1966 production were cut. Three excised songs – "Good Time Charlie", "It'll All Blow Over", and "Roommates" – were recorded by Kander and Ebb, and the sheet music published in a collector's book. Sally sang "Good Time Charlie" to Cliff as they walked to Fräulein Schneider and Herr Schultz's engagement party, mocking Cliff for his gloominess. At the end of the first act, Fräulein Schneider sang "It'll All Blow Over," expressing her concerns about marrying a Jew, while Cliff voiced his worries about Germany's emerging Nazism. In the song, Sally declares that all will turn out well in the end. "Perfectly Marvelous" replaced "Roommates" and serves the same plot function of Sally convincing Cliff to let her move in with him.

The 1972 film added several songs, notably "*Mein Herr*" and "Maybe This Time" which were included in later productions. The latter song had been written by Kander and Ebb for the unproduced musical *Golden Gate*. The later 1987 and 1998 Broadway revivals also added new songs such as "I Don't Care Much". In the 1987 revival, Kander and Ebb wrote a new song for Cliff titled "Don't Go". In the 1998 revival, "Mein Herr" replaced "The Telephone Song", and "Maybe This Time" replaced "Why Should I Wake Up?".

Originally, the Emcee sang "Sitting Pretty" accompanied by the cabaret girls in international costumes with their units of currency representing [Russian rubles](/wiki/Soviet_ruble), [Japanese yen](/wiki/Japanese_yen), [French francs](/wiki/French_franc), [American dollars](/wiki/American_dollar), and [German reichsmarks](/wiki/Reichsmark). In the 1972 film, the Emcee and Sally Bowles sang "Money, Money" instead of "Sitting Pretty." The film soundtrack briefly played "Sitting Pretty" as orchestral background music. In the 1987 revival, they presented a special version that combined a medley of both money songs, and they incorporated motifs from the later song into the "international" dance that featured "Sitting Pretty." In the 1998 revival, they used only the later song written for the film. This version included the cabaret girls and carried a darker undertone.

## Productions

### Original Broadway production

The original production featured [Jill Haworth](/wiki/Jill_Haworth) (left) as Sally Bowles, [Joel Grey](/wiki/Joel_Grey) (center) as the Emcee, and [Lotte Lenya](/wiki/Lotte_Lenya) (right) as Fräulein Schneider.

The musical opened on Broadway on November 20, 1966, at the [Broadhurst Theatre](/wiki/Broadhurst_Theatre), transferred to the [Imperial Theatre](/wiki/Imperial_Theatre) and then the [Broadway Theatre](/wiki/The_Broadway_Theatre) before closing on September 6, 1969, after 1,166 performances and 21 previews. Directed by [Harold Prince](/wiki/Harold_Prince) and choreographed by [Ron Field](/wiki/Ron_Field), the cast featured [Jill Haworth](/wiki/Jill_Haworth) as Sally, [Bert Convy](/wiki/Bert_Convy) as Cliff, [Lotte Lenya](/wiki/Lotte_Lenya) as Fräulein Schneider, [Jack Gilford](/wiki/Jack_Gilford) as Herr Schultz, [Joel Grey](/wiki/Joel_Grey) as the Emcee, [Edward Winter](/wiki/Edward_Winter_(actor)) as Ernst, and [Peg Murray](/wiki/Peg_Murray) as Fräulein Kost. Replacements later in the run included [Anita Gillette](/wiki/Anita_Gillette) and [Melissa Hart](/wiki/Melissa_Hart_(actress)) as Sally, [Ken Kercheval](/wiki/Ken_Kercheval) and [Larry Kert](/wiki/Larry_Kert) as Cliff, and Martin Ross as the Emcee. In addition, [John Serry Sr.](/wiki/John_Serry_Sr.) performed as the orchestral accordionist.

The original Broadway production was not an instant success according to playwright Joe Masteroff due to its perceived immoral content. "When the show opened in Boston," Masteroff recalled, "there were a lot of walkouts. Once the reviews came out, the public came back." At the time, actor Joel Grey was merely fifth-billed in the show. Nevertheless, audiences were hypnotized by Grey's sinister performance as the Emcee.

In contrast, Jill Haworth's performance as Sally was less well-received and was criticized for its blandness. Emory Lewis, the reviewer for *[The Morning Call](/wiki/The_Morning_Call)*, wrote that "Jill Haworth, the lovely English actress who played Sally Bowles on opening night, was personable, but she was not sufficiently trained for so pivotal a role. And her voice was small and undramatic. Her performance threw 'Cabaret' out of kilter."

The 1967–68 US national tour featured Melissa Hart as Sally, [Signe Hasso](/wiki/Signe_Hasso) as Fräulein Schneider, and [Leo Fuchs](/wiki/Leo_Fuchs) as Herr Schultz. The tour included the [Shubert Theatre](/wiki/Shubert_Theatre_(New_Haven)) in [New Haven, Connecticut](/wiki/New_Haven,_Connecticut) in December 1967, the [Ahmanson Theatre](/wiki/Ahmanson_Theatre) in Los Angeles in May 1968, the [Curran Theatre](/wiki/Curran_Theatre) in San Francisco in September 1968, and many others.

### Original West End production

The musical premiered in the [West End](/wiki/West_End_theatre) on February 28, 1968, at the [Palace Theatre](/wiki/Palace_Theatre,_London) with [Judi Dench](/wiki/Judi_Dench) as Sally, [Kevin Colson](/wiki/Kevin_Colson) as Cliff, [Barry Dennen](/wiki/Barry_Dennen) as the Emcee, [Lila Kedrova](/wiki/Lila_Kedrova) as Fräulein Schneider and [Peter Sallis](/wiki/Peter_Sallis) as Herr Schultz. It ran for 336 performances. Critics such as Ken Mandelbaum have asserted that "Judi Dench was the finest of all the Sallys that appeared in Hal Prince's original staging, and if she's obviously not much of a singer, her Sally is a perfect example of how one can give a thrilling musical theatre performance without a great singing voice."

### 1986 West End revival

[Kelly Hunter](/wiki/Kelly_Hunter) as [Sally Bowles](/wiki/Sally_Bowles), 1986

In 1986, the show was revived in London at the [Strand Theatre](/wiki/Novello_Theatre) starring [Kelly Hunter](/wiki/Kelly_Hunter) as Sally, [Peter Land](/wiki/Peter_Land) as Cliff and [Wayne Sleep](/wiki/Wayne_Sleep) as the Emcee, directed and choreographed by [Gillian Lynne](/wiki/Gillian_Lynne).

### 1987 Broadway revival

The first Broadway revival opened on October 22, 1987, with direction and choreography by Prince and Field. The revival opened at the [Imperial Theatre](/wiki/Imperial_Theatre), and then transferred to the [Minskoff Theatre](/wiki/Minskoff_Theatre) to complete its 261-performance run. [Joel Grey](/wiki/Joel_Grey) received star billing as the Emcee, with [Alyson Reed](/wiki/Alyson_Reed) as Sally, [Gregg Edelman](/wiki/Gregg_Edelman) as Cliff, [Regina Resnik](/wiki/Regina_Resnik) as Fräulein Schneider, [Werner Klemperer](/wiki/Werner_Klemperer) as Herr Schultz, and [David Staller](/wiki/David_Staller) as Ernst Ludwig. The song "Don't Go" was added for Cliff's character.

### 1993 London revival

[Sam Mendes](/wiki/Sam_Mendes) (left) directed the 1993 London revival of *Cabaret* with [Alan Cumming](/wiki/Alan_Cumming) (right) as the Emcee. Cumming would return as the Emcee for the 1998 and 2014 Broadway revivals.

In 1993, [Sam Mendes](/wiki/Sam_Mendes) directed a new production for the [Donmar Warehouse](/wiki/Donmar_Warehouse) in London. The revival starred [Jane Horrocks](/wiki/Jane_Horrocks) as Sally, [Adam Godley](/wiki/Adam_Godley) as Cliff, [Alan Cumming](/wiki/Alan_Cumming) as the Emcee and [Sara Kestelman](/wiki/Sara_Kestelman) as Fräulein Schneider. Kestelman won the [Olivier Award](/wiki/Olivier_Award) for Best Supporting Performance in a Musical, and Cumming was nominated for an Olivier. Mendes' concept was different from either the original production or the conventional first revival, particularly with respect to the character of the Emcee. The role, as played by [Joel Grey](/wiki/Joel_Grey) in both prior productions, was a sexually aloof, edgy character with rouged cheeks dressed in a [tuxedo](/wiki/Tuxedo). [Alan Cumming](/wiki/Alan_Cumming)'s portrayal was highly sexualized, as he wore suspenders around his crotch and red paint on his nipples. Staging details differed as well. Instead of "Tomorrow Belongs to Me" being performed by a male choir of waiting staff, the Emcee plays a recording of a [boy soprano](/wiki/Boy_soprano) singing it. In the final scene, the Emcee removes his outer clothes to reveal a striped uniform of the type worn by the internees in [concentration camps](/wiki/Concentration_camp); on it are pinned a [yellow badge](/wiki/Yellow_badge) (identifying Jews), a [red star](/wiki/Red_star) (marking Communists and socialists), and a [pink triangle](/wiki/Pink_triangle) (denoting homosexuals). Other changes included added references to Cliff's bisexuality, including a brief scene where he kisses one of the Cabaret boys. "I Don't Care Much," which was added for the 1987 Broadway revival, was maintained for this production, and "Mein Herr" was added from the film.

This production was filmed by [Channel Four Film](/wiki/Channel_Four_Television_Corporation) for airing on UK television.

### 1998 Broadway revival

[Natasha Richardson](/wiki/Natasha_Richardson) portrayed Sally Bowles in the 1998 revival, and [Neil Patrick Harris](/wiki/Neil_Patrick_Harris) later starred as the Emcee after Alan Cumming departed the role.

The second Broadway revival, by the [Roundabout Theatre Company](/wiki/Roundabout_Theatre_Company), was based on the 1993 Mendes-Donmar Warehouse production. For the Broadway transfer, [Rob Marshall](/wiki/Rob_Marshall) was co-director and choreographer. The production opened after 37 previews on March 19, 1998, at the Kit Kat Klub, housed in what previously had been known as [Henry Miller's Theatre](/wiki/Henry_Miller%27s_Theatre). Later that year it transferred to [Studio 54](/wiki/Studio_54), where it remained for the rest of its 2,377-performance run, becoming the [third longest-running revival](/wiki/List_of_the_longest-running_Broadway_shows) in Broadway musical history, third only to *[Oh! Calcutta!](/wiki/Oh!_Calcutta!)* and *[Chicago](/wiki/Chicago_(musical))*. Cumming reprised his role as the Emcee, opposite newcomers [Natasha Richardson](/wiki/Natasha_Richardson) as Sally, [John Benjamin Hickey](/wiki/John_Benjamin_Hickey) as Cliff, [Ron Rifkin](/wiki/Ron_Rifkin) as Herr Schultz, [Denis O'Hare](/wiki/Denis_O%27Hare) as Ernst Ludwig, [Michele Pawk](/wiki/Michele_Pawk) as Fräulein Kost, and [Mary Louise Wilson](/wiki/Mary_Louise_Wilson) as Fräulein Schneider.

The Broadway production was nominated for ten [Tony Awards](/wiki/Tony_Awards), winning four for Cumming, Richardson and Rifkin, as well as the Tony for Best Revival of a Musical. This production featured a number of notable replacements later in the run: [Susan Egan](/wiki/Susan_Egan), [Joely Fisher](/wiki/Joely_Fisher), [Gina Gershon](/wiki/Gina_Gershon), [Debbie Gibson](/wiki/Debbie_Gibson), [Milena Govich](/wiki/Milena_Govich), [Jennifer Jason Leigh](/wiki/Jennifer_Jason_Leigh), [Melina Kanakaredes](/wiki/Melina_Kanakaredes), [Jane Leeves](/wiki/Jane_Leeves), [Molly Ringwald](/wiki/Molly_Ringwald), [Brooke Shields](/wiki/Brooke_Shields), and [Lea Thompson](/wiki/Lea_Thompson) as Sally; [Michael C. Hall](/wiki/Michael_C._Hall), [Raúl Esparza](/wiki/Ra%C3%BAl_Esparza), [Neil Patrick Harris](/wiki/Neil_Patrick_Harris), [Adam Pascal](/wiki/Adam_Pascal), [Jon Secada](/wiki/Jon_Secada), and [John Stamos](/wiki/John_Stamos) as the Emcee; [Boyd Gaines](/wiki/Boyd_Gaines), [Michael Hayden](/wiki/Michael_Hayden_(actor)), and [Rick Holmes](/wiki/Rick_Holmes) as Cliff; [Tom Bosley](/wiki/Tom_Bosley), [Dick Latessa](/wiki/Dick_Latessa), [Hal Linden](/wiki/Hal_Linden), [Laurence Luckinbill](/wiki/Laurence_Luckinbill), and [Tony Roberts](/wiki/Tony_Roberts_(actor)) as Herr Schultz; and [Blair Brown](/wiki/Blair_Brown), [Carole Shelley](/wiki/Carole_Shelley), [Polly Bergen](/wiki/Polly_Bergen), [Alma Cuervo](/wiki/Alma_Cuervo), [Mariette Hartley](/wiki/Mariette_Hartley) as Fräulein Schneider, and [Martin Moran](/wiki/Martin_Moran_(actor)) as Ernst Ludwig.

There were a number of changes made between the 1993 and 1998 revivals, despite the similarities in creative team. The cabaret number "Two Ladies" was staged with the Emcee, a cabaret girl, and a cabaret boy in [drag](/wiki/Drag_(clothing)) and included a [shadow play](/wiki/Shadow_play) simulating various sexual positions. The score was re-orchestrated using synthesizer effects and expanding the stage band, with all the instruments now being played by the cabaret girls and boys. The satiric "Sitting Pretty", with its mocking references to deprivation, despair and hunger, was eliminated, as it had been in the film version, and where in the 1993 revival it had been combined with "Money" (as it had been in 1987 London production), "Money" was now performed on its own. "Maybe This Time", from the film adaptation, was added to the score.

### 2006 West End revival

In September 2006, a new production presented by [Bill Kenwright](/wiki/Bill_Kenwright) opened at the [Lyric Theatre](/wiki/Lyric_Theatre_(London)), directed by Rufus Norris, and starring [Anna Maxwell Martin](/wiki/Anna_Maxwell_Martin) as Sally, [James Dreyfus](/wiki/James_Dreyfus) as the Emcee, [Harriet Thorpe](/wiki/Harriet_Thorpe) as Fräulein Kost, [Michael Hayden](/wiki/Michael_Hayden_(actor)) as Cliff, and [Sheila Hancock](/wiki/Sheila_Hancock) as Fräulein Schneider. Hancock won the Olivier Award for Best Supporting Performance in a Musical. Replacements later in the run included [Kim Medcalf](/wiki/Kim_Medcalf) and [Amy Nuttall](/wiki/Amy_Nuttall) as Sally, [Honor Blackman](/wiki/Honor_Blackman) and [Angela Richards](/wiki/Angela_Richards) as Fräulein Schneider, and [Julian Clary](/wiki/Julian_Clary) and [Alistair McGowan](/wiki/Alistair_McGowan) as the Emcee. This production closed in June 2008 and toured the UK for two years opening at the [Birmingham Repertory Theatre](/wiki/Birmingham_Repertory_Theatre) with a cast that included [Wayne Sleep](/wiki/Wayne_Sleep) as the Emcee and [Samantha Barks](/wiki/Samantha_Barks) as Sally, before [Siobhan Dillon](/wiki/Siobhan_Dillon) took over the role.

### 2012 West End revival

A revival opened in the West End at the [Savoy Theatre](/wiki/Savoy_Theatre) on October 3, 2012, following a four-week tour of the UK, including Bromley, Southampton, Nottingham, Norwich and Salford, with [Will Young](/wiki/Will_Young) as the Emcee and [Michelle Ryan](/wiki/Michelle_Ryan) as [Sally Bowles](/wiki/Sally_Bowles). [Siân Phillips](/wiki/Si%C3%A2n_Phillips), [Harriet Thorpe](/wiki/Harriet_Thorpe) and [Matt Rawle](/wiki/Matt_Rawle) also joined the cast. The production was made by the creative team behind the 2006 London revival, but with new sets, lighting, costumes, choreography and direction.

In August 2013 the show went on tour in the UK, again with Young as the Emcee, [Siobhan Dillon](/wiki/Siobhan_Dillon) reprising her role of Sally and [Lyn Paul](/wiki/Lyn_Paul) joining the cast as Fräulein Schneider. The same production toured the UK again in autumn 2017 with Young as the Emcee and [Louise Redknapp](/wiki/Louise_Redknapp) as Sally. Another UK tour began in autumn 2019 starring [John Partridge](/wiki/John_Partridge_(actor)) as the Emcee, Kara Lily Hayworth as Sally Bowles and [Anita Harris](/wiki/Anita_Harris) as Fräulein Schneider.

### 2014 Broadway revival

[Michelle Williams](/wiki/Michelle_Williams_(actress)), [Emma Stone](/wiki/Emma_Stone), and [Sienna Miller](/wiki/Sienna_Miller) each portrayed Sally Bowles in the 2014 Broadway revival.

In September 2013 [Roundabout Theatre Company](/wiki/Roundabout_Theatre_Company) announced plans to return the company's acclaimed 1998 production to [Studio 54](/wiki/Studio_54) in New York. For this, the show's third Broadway revival, [Sam Mendes](/wiki/Sam_Mendes) and [Rob Marshall](/wiki/Rob_Marshall) reprised their respective roles as director and co-director/choreographer to recreate their work from the earlier production. [Alan Cumming](/wiki/Alan_Cumming) starred again as the Emcee while [Academy Award](/wiki/Academy_Award)-nominee [Michelle Williams](/wiki/Michelle_Williams_(actress)) made her Broadway debut as [Sally Bowles](/wiki/Sally_Bowles). On October 7, 2013, [Tony Award](/wiki/Tony_Award) nominees [Danny Burstein](/wiki/Danny_Burstein) and [Linda Emond](/wiki/Linda_Emond) joined the cast as Herr Schultz and Fräulein Schneider. The production began a 24-week limited engagement with previews from March 21, 2014, with opening night on April 24, 2014, but the engagement was extended. [Emma Stone](/wiki/Emma_Stone) replaced Michelle Williams as Sally from November 2014 to February 2015. Critics praised Stone's performance for her interpretation of the hard-drinking [sybarite](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sybarite) Sally Bowles "as a flaming [flapper](/wiki/Flapper), the kind hymned by [F. Scott Fitzgerald](/wiki/F._Scott_Fitzgerald) and embodied by the young [Joan Crawford](/wiki/Joan_Crawford) in [silent movies](/wiki/Silent_movies)." [Sienna Miller](/wiki/Sienna_Miller) took over the role on March 29, 2015, remaining through to the show's closing. [Alan Cumming](/wiki/Alan_Cumming) continued in the role of the Emcee until the show's final curtain.

The production toured the US from January 2016 with [Randy Harrison](/wiki/Randy_Harrison) as the Emcee and Andrea Goss (who played Frenchie in the Broadway production). They were later replaced by Jon Peterson and [Leigh Ann Larkin](/wiki/Leigh_Ann_Larkin).

### 2021 West End revival

Kit Kat Club (Playhouse Theatre) Marquee in London

[Eddie Redmayne](/wiki/Eddie_Redmayne) and [Jessie Buckley](/wiki/Jessie_Buckley) starred as the Emcee and Sally Bowles in a West End production directed by [Rebecca Frecknall](/wiki/Rebecca_Frecknall), designed by [Tom Scutt](/wiki/Tom_Scutt), choreographed by [Julia Cheng](/wiki/Julia_Cheng), with lighting design by [Isabella Byrd](/wiki/Isabella_Byrd) and sound design by Nick Lidster. The production also featured [Omari Douglas](/wiki/Omari_Douglas) as Cliff, [Liza Sadovy](/wiki/Liza_Sadovy) as Fräulein Schneider, [Elliot Levey](/wiki/Elliot_Levey) as Herr Schultz, Stewart Clarke as Ernst and [Anna-Jane Casey](/wiki/Anna-Jane_Casey) as Fräulein Kost. Produced by [Underbelly](/wiki/Underbelly_(events_promoter)) and [Ambassador Theatre Group](/wiki/Ambassador_Theatre_Group), and billed as *Cabaret at the Kit Kat Club*, the production began previews November 15, 2021 at [Playhouse Theatre](/wiki/Playhouse_Theatre), which was reduced to a 550-seat capacity with an intimate in-the-round stage and table seating for some audience members, in effect transforming the theater into a Weimar-era nightclub. The production led the 2022 Olivier Award nominations with 11 nods, including [Best Musical Revival](/wiki/Laurence_Olivier_Award_for_Best_Musical_Revival), [Best Actor in a Musical](/wiki/Laurence_Olivier_Award_for_Best_Actor_in_a_Musical) for Redmayne and [Best Actress in a Musical](/wiki/Laurence_Olivier_Award_for_Best_Actress_in_a_Musical) for Buckley. The production won seven awards and set a record as the most award-winning revival in Olivier history and the first production to receive awards in all four eligible acting categories.

Following the departure of Redmayne and Buckley, notable players as the Emcee and Sally Bowles have been [Fra Fee](/wiki/Fra_Fee) and [Amy Lennox](/wiki/Amy_Lennox); [Callum Scott Howells](/wiki/Callum_Scott_Howells) and [Madeline Brewer](/wiki/Madeline_Brewer); [John McCrea](/wiki/John_McCrea_(actor)) and [Aimee Lou Wood](/wiki/Aimee_Lou_Wood); [Mason Alexander Park](/wiki/Mason_Alexander_Park) and [Maude Apatow](/wiki/Maude_Apatow); [Jake Shears](/wiki/Jake_Shears) and [Rebecca Lucy Taylor (aka Self Esteem)](/wiki/Rebecca_Lucy_Taylor); [Luke Treadaway](/wiki/Luke_Treadaway) and [Cara Delevingne](/wiki/Cara_Delevingne); [Layton Williams](/wiki/Layton_Williams) and [Rhea Norwood](/wiki/Rhea_Norwood); [Adam Gillen](/wiki/Adam_Gillen) and [Katherine Langford](/wiki/Katherine_Langford); [Billy Porter](/wiki/Billy_Porter) and [Marisha Wallace](/wiki/Marisha_Wallace); [Rob Madge](/wiki/Robert_Madge_(actor)) and [Hannah Dodd](/wiki/Hannah_Dodd_(actress)); [Reeve Carney](/wiki/Reeve_Carney) and [Eva Noblezada](/wiki/Eva_Noblezada); and [Matt Willis](/wiki/Matt_Willis) and [Katie Hall](/wiki/Katie_Hall_(actress)).

### 2024 Broadway revival

The 2021 West End production transferred to the [August Wilson Theatre](/wiki/August_Wilson_Theatre) on Broadway, with previews from April 1, 2024, and the opening on April 21. As in the West End production, the August Wilson Theatre was refurbished as the "Kit Kat Club" with an intimate in-the-round staging. Redmayne reprised his role as the Emcee with [Gayle Rankin](/wiki/Gayle_Rankin) and Ato Blankson-Wood co-starring as Sally and Cliff. [Bebe Neuwirth](/wiki/Bebe_Neuwirth), [Steven Skybell](/wiki/Steven_Skybell), Natascia Diaz, and Henry Gottfried played Fräulein Schneider, Herr Schultz, Fräulein Kost, and Ernst Ludwig, respectively. The production was nominated for 9 Tony Awards, winning one for [scenic design](/wiki/Tony_Award_for_Best_Scenic_Design_of_a_Musical). Replacements in this revival included [Adam Lambert](/wiki/Adam_Lambert), [Orville Peck](/wiki/Orville_Peck), and [Billy Porter](/wiki/Billy_Porter) as the Emcee and [Auliʻi Cravalho](/wiki/Auli%CA%BBi_Cravalho), [Eva Noblezada](/wiki/Eva_Noblezada), and [Marisha Wallace](/wiki/Marisha_Wallace) as Sally Bowles. The production closed on September 21, 2025, having played 18 previews and 592 performances.

### Other productions

A [BBC Radio 2](/wiki/BBC_Radio_2) radio broadcast in 1996 from the [Golders Green Hippodrome](/wiki/Golders_Green_Hippodrome) starred [Clare Burt](/wiki/Clare_Burt) as Sally Bowles, [Steven Berkoff](/wiki/Steven_Berkoff) as the Emcee, [Alexander Hanson](/wiki/Alexander_Hanson_(actor)) as Clifford Bradshaw, [Keith Michell](/wiki/Keith_Michell) as Herr Schultz, and [Rosemary Leach](/wiki/Rosemary_Leach) as Fräulein Schneider.

Since 2003, international stagings of the show, many influenced by Mendes' concept, have included productions in Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Colombia, Costa Rica, France, Portugal, Greece, Israel, Malaysia, Mexico, Peru, Puerto Rico, Serbia, South Africa, Spain and Venezuela. A 2008 production at the [Stratford Shakespeare Festival](/wiki/Stratford_Shakespeare_Festival)'s Avon Theatre in Canada, designed by Douglas Paraschuk and directed by [Amanda Dehnert](/wiki/Amanda_Dehnert), featured [Bruce Dow](/wiki/Bruce_Dow) as the Emcee, Trish Lindström as Sally, Sean Arbuckle as Cliff, Nora McClellan as Fräulein Schneider and [Frank Moore](/wiki/Frank_Moore_(Canadian_actor)) as Herr Schultz. The [Shaw Festival](/wiki/Shaw_Festival) at [Niagara-on-the-Lake](/wiki/Niagara-on-the-Lake), Ontario, included *Cabaret* in its 2014 season. The production, which ran from April 10 – October 26, 2014 at the Festival Theatre, was directed by [Peter Hinton-Davis](/wiki/Peter_Hinton-Davis) with choreography by Denise Clarke. It featured [Juan Chioran](/wiki/Juan_Chioran) as the Emcee, Deborah Hay as Sally, Gray Powell as Cliff, [Benedict Campbell](/wiki/Benedict_Campbell) as Herr Schultz, and [Corrine Koslo](/wiki/Corrine_Koslo) as Fräulein Schneider.

A 2017 revival played in [Sydney](/wiki/Sydney) and [Melbourne](/wiki/Melbourne), Australia, starred [Paul Capsis](/wiki/Paul_Capsis) as the Emcee and Chelsea Gibb as Sally. The production mixed elements of the Mendes production, such as its version of "Two Ladies" and its portrayal of a gay Cliff, with the colorful art design of the original (the Emcee is in full makeup and clothed) and most of the additional songs from the 1972 film (with the exception of "Mein Herr").

## Recordings

The first recording of *Cabaret* was the original Broadway [cast album](/wiki/Cast_album) with a number of the songs either truncated (e.g., "Sitting Pretty"/"The Money Song") or outright cut to conserve disk space. When this album was released on compact disc, Kander and Ebb's voice-and-piano recordings of songs cut from the musical were added as bonus material. According to Mandelbaum, the 1968 London cast recording features "a more accurate rendering of the score" and includes the Act One finale "Tomorrow Belongs to Me" reprise, the second-act finale as performed in the theatre, and a number of other previously unrecorded bits and pieces." It was released in the UK and reissued on the [CBS Embassy](/wiki/Embassy_Records) label in 1973.

The 1972 movie soundtrack with [Liza Minnelli](/wiki/Liza_Minnelli) is much re-written and eliminates all but six of the original songs from the stage production.

Both the 1986 London and 1998 Broadway revival casts were recorded. A 1993 two-CD studio recording contains nearly the entire score, including songs written for the movie and for later productions and much of the incidental music. This recording features [Jonathan Pryce](/wiki/Jonathan_Pryce) as the Emcee, [Maria Friedman](/wiki/Maria_Friedman) as Sally, [Gregg Edelman](/wiki/Gregg_Edelman) as Cliff, [Judi Dench](/wiki/Judi_Dench) as Fräulein Schneider, and [Fred Ebb](/wiki/Fred_Ebb) as Herr Schultz. The cast recording of the 2006 London revival at the Lyric Theatre includes James Dreyfus as the Emcee and Anna Maxwell Martin as Sally.

The 2021 London cast recording featuring [Eddie Redmayne](/wiki/Eddie_Redmayne) and [Jessie Buckley](/wiki/Jessie_Buckley) was recorded live at the [Playhouse Theatre](/wiki/Playhouse_Theatre), London, and released in January 2023. *Cabaret: The Maida Vale Session* is an [EP](/wiki/EP) that was released in March 2024 with four songs from the revival at the Playhouse Theatre, including "Willkommen", "Don't Tell Mama", "I Don't Care Much" and the title song. It was recorded for a [BBC Radio 2](/wiki/BBC_Radio_2) show with [Jo Whiley](/wiki/Jo_Whiley) at the [BBC](/wiki/BBC)'s [Maida Vale Studios](/wiki/Maida_Vale_Studios) with [Jake Shears](/wiki/Jake_Shears) as Emcee, [Rebecca Lucy Taylor](/wiki/Rebecca_Lucy_Taylor) as Sally and the 2023 London cast and orchestra.

In addition to these recordings, cast albums for French, Spanish, Greek, Hebrew, Italian, Austrian, Dutch, Mexican, and German productions have been released.
