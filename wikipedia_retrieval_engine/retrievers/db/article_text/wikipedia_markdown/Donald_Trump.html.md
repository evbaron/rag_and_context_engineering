**Donald John Trump** (born June 14, 1946) is an American politician, media personality, and businessman who is the 47th [president of the United States](/wiki/President_of_the_United_States). A member of the [Republican Party](/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States)), he served as the 45th president from 2017 to 2021.

Born into a wealthy New York City family, Trump graduated from the [University of Pennsylvania](/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania) in 1968 with a bachelor's degree in economics. He became the president of his family's real estate business in 1971, renamed it [the Trump Organization](/wiki/The_Trump_Organization), and began acquiring and building skyscrapers, hotels, casinos, and golf courses. He launched side ventures, many licensing the Trump name, and filed for six business bankruptcies in the 1990s and 2000s. From 2004 to 2015, he hosted the reality television show *[The Apprentice](/wiki/The_Apprentice_(American_TV_series))*, bolstering his fame as a billionaire. Presenting himself as a political outsider, Trump won the [2016 presidential election](/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election) against [Democratic Party](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)) nominee [Hillary Clinton](/wiki/Hillary_Clinton).

During [his first presidency](/wiki/First_presidency_of_Donald_Trump), Trump imposed [a travel ban](/wiki/Executive_Order_13769) on seven [Muslim-majority countries](/wiki/Islam_by_country#Countries), expanded the [Mexico–United States border wall](/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall), and enforced [a family separation policy](/wiki/Trump_administration_family_separation_policy) on the border. He rolled back environmental and business regulations, signed the [Tax Cuts and Jobs Act](/wiki/Tax_Cuts_and_Jobs_Act), and appointed three Supreme Court justices. He withdrew the U.S. from agreements on climate and trade, and started [a trade war with China](/wiki/China%E2%80%93United_States_trade_war). In response to the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_States) in 2020, he downplayed its severity, contradicted health officials, and signed the [CARES Act](/wiki/CARES_Act). After losing the [2020 presidential election](/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election) to [Joe Biden](/wiki/Joe_Biden), Trump [attempted to overturn the result](/wiki/Attempts_to_overturn_the_2020_United_States_presidential_election), culminating in the [January 6 Capitol attack](/wiki/January_6_United_States_Capitol_attack) in 2021. He was impeached twice—[in 2019](/wiki/First_impeachment_of_Donald_Trump) for abuse of power and obstruction of Congress and [in 2021](/wiki/Second_impeachment_of_Donald_Trump) for incitement of insurrection—and acquitted by the Senate both times.

In 2023, Trump was found liable in New York state civil cases [for sexual abuse and defamation](/wiki/E._Jean_Carroll_v._Donald_J._Trump) and [for business fraud](/wiki/New_York_business_fraud_lawsuit_against_the_Trump_Organization). In May 2024, he was found guilty in a New York state court on 34 counts [of falsifying business records](/wiki/Prosecution_of_Donald_Trump_in_New_York), making him the first U.S. president convicted of a felony. After winning the [2024 presidential election](/wiki/2024_United_States_presidential_election) against Vice President [Kamala Harris](/wiki/Kamala_Harris), he was given a no-penalty sentence, and two federal felony indictments for [retention of classified documents](/wiki/Federal_prosecution_of_Donald_Trump_(classified_documents_case)) and [obstruction of the 2020 election](/wiki/Federal_prosecution_of_Donald_Trump_(election_obstruction_case)) were [dismissed without prejudice](/wiki/Prejudice_(legal_term)).

Trump began [his second presidency](/wiki/Second_presidency_of_Donald_Trump) by initiating [mass layoffs of federal workers](/wiki/2025_United_States_federal_mass_layoffs). He [imposed tariffs](/wiki/Tariffs_in_the_second_Trump_administration) on nearly all countries at the highest level since the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression) and signed the [One Big Beautiful Bill Act](/wiki/One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act). His administration's actions—including the [targeting of political opponents and civil society](/wiki/Targeting_of_political_opponents_and_civil_society_under_the_second_Trump_administration), restriction of transgender rights, mass [deportation of undocumented immigrants](/wiki/Deportation_in_the_second_Trump_administration), and extensive use of executive orders—have drawn over 550 [lawsuits challenging their legality](/wiki/Legal_affairs_of_the_second_Trump_presidency). In Latin America, he pursued a legally contested campaign to [attack alleged drug traffickers](/wiki/United_States_strikes_on_alleged_drug_traffickers_during_Operation_Southern_Spear), and ordered [a military raid into Venezuela](/wiki/2026_United_States_intervention_in_Venezuela) to capture the country's president. In February 2026, he authorized joint U.S.–Israeli strikes on Iran that resulted in the [2026 Iran war](/wiki/2026_Iran_war).

Since 2015, Trump's leadership style and political agenda—often referred to as [Trumpism](/wiki/Trumpism)—have reshaped the Republican Party's identity. Many of his comments and actions have been [characterized as racist](/wiki/Racial_views_of_Donald_Trump) or misogynistic. He has [made many false or misleading statements](/wiki/False_or_misleading_statements_by_Donald_Trump) during his campaigns and presidency, to a degree unprecedented in American politics, and he [promotes conspiracy theories](/wiki/List_of_conspiracy_theories_promoted_by_Donald_Trump). Trump's actions have been described by researchers as authoritarian and contributing to [democratic backsliding](/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_the_United_States). After his first term, scholars and historians [ranked him](#Scholarly_rankings) as one of the worst presidents in American history.

## Early life and education

Main article: [Early life and education of Donald Trump](/wiki/Early_life_and_education_of_Donald_Trump)

1964 yearbook photo

Donald John Trump was born on June 14, 1946, at [Jamaica Hospital](/wiki/Jamaica_Hospital) in the New York City borough of [Queens](/wiki/Queens), the fourth child of [Fred Trump](/wiki/Fred_Trump) and [Mary Anne MacLeod Trump](/wiki/Mary_Anne_MacLeod_Trump). He is of German and Scottish descent. He grew up with his older siblings, [Maryanne](/wiki/Maryanne_Trump_Barry), [Fred Jr.](/wiki/Fred_Trump_Jr.), and Elizabeth, and his younger brother, [Robert](/wiki/Robert_Trump), in a 23-room mansion in the [Jamaica Estates](/wiki/Jamaica_Estates) neighborhood of Queens. Fred Trump paid his children each about $20,000 a year, equivalent to $265,000 a year in 2024. Trump was a millionaire in inflation-adjusted dollars by age eight.

Trump attended the private [Kew-Forest School](/wiki/Kew-Forest_School) through seventh grade. His father enrolled him in the [New York Military Academy](/wiki/New_York_Military_Academy), a private boarding school, from eighth to twelfth grade. The academy pushed students into sports and taught the imperative of winning.

Trump considered a show business career but, to be closer to home, enrolled at [Fordham University](/wiki/Fordham_University) in 1964. He participated in the [Reserve Officers' Training Corps](/wiki/Reserve_Officers%27_Training_Corps) during his first year, attending classes in a military uniform every Wednesday, but dropped it in his second year. In his junior year, he transferred to the [Wharton School](/wiki/Wharton_School) of the [University of Pennsylvania](/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania), most often commuting to his father's office on weekends, and graduated in May 1968 with a [Bachelor of Science](/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science) in economics. Contrary to his statements that he was top of his class with the highest grades possible, Wharton's published academic honors and dean's list do not include his name. By the time he went to Wharton he was eyeing a career in real estate. He was exempted from the draft during the Vietnam War due to a claim of [bone spurs](/wiki/Bone_spurs) in his heels.

## Business career

Main article: [Business career of Donald Trump](/wiki/Business_career_of_Donald_Trump)

Further information: [Business projects of Donald Trump in Russia](/wiki/Business_projects_of_Donald_Trump_in_Russia), [Tax returns of Donald Trump](/wiki/Tax_returns_of_Donald_Trump), and [The Trump Organization](/wiki/The_Trump_Organization)

### Real estate

See also: [The Trump Organization § Real estate](/wiki/The_Trump_Organization#Real_estate)

Starting in 1968, Trump was employed at Trump Management, his father's real estate company, which managed the middle-class apartment complexes Fred had built in Queens, [Staten Island](/wiki/Staten_Island), and [Brooklyn](/wiki/Brooklyn). His main tasks were collecting rent and making repairs for about five years. Trump asked his father to expand to [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan) where prices were higher, but his father was content in the outer boroughs. In 1971, he moved to Manhattan, where he planned to move the business and commuted to his father's office. That year, his father made himself chairman and Trump president. Trump began using the Trump Organization as an umbrella for the corporate names of his father's businesses.

[Roy Cohn](/wiki/Roy_Cohn), Trump's most important early influence after his father, was his [fixer](/wiki/Fixer_(person)), lawyer, and mentor for 13 years in the 1970s and 1980s. Cohn taught Trump that life is transactional. In 1973, Cohn helped Trump countersue the U.S. government for $100 million (equivalent to $725 million in 2025) over its charges that Trump's properties had discriminated against Black applicants and tenants. The case was settled in a consent decree agreeing to desegregate, which the Trumps ended up in court for violating four years later. Helping Trump projects, Cohn was a [consigliere](/wiki/Consigliere) whose Mafia connections controlled construction unions. In 1979, Cohn introduced political consultant [Roger Stone](/wiki/Roger_Stone) to Trump, who enlisted Stone's services to deal with the federal government.

Trump showed a propensity for litigation, no matter the outcome and cost; even when he lost, he described the case as a win. By 2018, Trump had been involved in more than 4,000 lawsuits, liens, and other filings, often filed for nonpayment against him by employees, contractors, real estate brokers, and his own attorneys. Between 1991 and 2009, Trump filed for [Chapter 11](/wiki/Chapter_11) bankruptcy protection for six of his businesses: the [Plaza Hotel](/wiki/Plaza_Hotel) in Manhattan, the casinos in [Atlantic City, New Jersey](/wiki/Atlantic_City,_New_Jersey), and the [Trump Hotels & Casino Resorts](/wiki/Trump_Hotels_%26_Casino_Resorts) company.

In 1992 and 1994, Trump, working with several relatives, formed a shell company for paying the vendors providing services and supplies for Trump's rental units, then billing those services and supplies to Trump Management with significant markups; the increased costs were used to get state approval for increasing the rents of his rent-stabilized units. Besides inflating rents, the schemes served to transfer assets from Fred Trump to his children and nephew and lower their tax burden.

#### Manhattan and Chicago developments

In 1985 with a model of one of his aborted Manhattan development projects

Trump gained public attention in 1978 with the launch of his family's first Manhattan venture: the renovation of the derelict [Commodore Hotel](/wiki/Hyatt_Grand_Central_New_York#Commodore_Hotel), adjacent to Grand Central Terminal. The financing was facilitated by a $400 million city property tax abatement arranged for him by his father who also, jointly with [Hyatt](/wiki/Hyatt), guaranteed a $70 million bank construction loan. The hotel reopened in 1980 as the [Grand Hyatt Hotel](/wiki/Hyatt_Grand_Central_New_York), and that same year, he obtained rights to develop [Trump Tower](/wiki/Trump_Tower), a mixed-use skyscraper in Midtown Manhattan. The building houses the headquarters of the Trump Corporation and Trump's [PAC](/wiki/Political_action_committee) and was his primary residence until 2019. In 1988, Trump acquired the Plaza Hotel with a loan from a consortium of 16 banks. The hotel filed for bankruptcy protection in 1992, and a reorganization plan was approved a month later, with the banks taking control of the property.

In 1995, Trump defaulted on over $3 billion of bank loans, and the lenders seized the Plaza Hotel along with most of his other properties in a "vast and humiliating restructuring" that allowed him to avoid personal bankruptcy. Trump's last major construction project was the 92-story mixed-use [Trump International Hotel and Tower](/wiki/Trump_International_Hotel_and_Tower_(Chicago)) in Chicago, which opened in 2008. In 2024, [*The New York Times* and ProPublica reported](/wiki/Trump_International_Hotel_and_Tower_(Chicago)#Tax_deductions) that the Internal Revenue Service was investigating whether he had twice written off losses incurred through construction cost overruns and lagging sales of residential units in the building he had declared to be worthless on his 2008 tax return.

#### Atlantic City casinos

Entrance of the Trump Taj Mahal (now the [Hard Rock Hotel & Casino](/wiki/Hard_Rock_Hotel_%26_Casino_Atlantic_City)) in [Atlantic City](/wiki/Atlantic_City)

In 1984, Trump opened [Harrah's at Trump Plaza](/wiki/Harrah%27s_at_Trump_Plaza), a hotel and casino, with financing and management help from the [Holiday Corporation](/wiki/Holiday_Corporation). It was unprofitable, and he paid Holiday $70 million in May 1986 to take sole control. In 1985, he bought the unopened Atlantic City Hilton Hotel and renamed it [Trump's Castle](/wiki/Trump%27s_Castle). Both casinos filed for [Chapter 11 bankruptcy](/wiki/Chapter_11_bankruptcy) protection in 1992. Trump bought a third Atlantic City venue in 1988, the [Trump Taj Mahal](/wiki/Trump_Taj_Mahal). It was financed with $675 million in [junk bonds](/wiki/Junk_bonds) and completed for $1.1 billion, opening in April 1990. He filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in 1991. Under the provisions of the restructuring agreement, Trump gave up half his initial stake and personally guaranteed future performance. To reduce his $900 million of personal debt, he sold the [Trump Shuttle](/wiki/Trump_Shuttle) airline; his megayacht, the *[Trump Princess](/wiki/Trump_Princess)*, which had been leased to his casinos and kept docked; and other businesses. In 1995, Trump founded Trump Hotels & Casino Resorts (THCR), which assumed ownership of the Trump Plaza. THCR purchased the Taj Mahal and the Trump Castle in 1996 and went bankrupt in 2004 and 2009, leaving him with 10 percent ownership. He remained chairman until 2009.

#### Golf clubs

In 1985, Trump acquired the [Mar-a-Lago](/wiki/Mar-a-Lago) estate in Palm Beach, Florida. In 1995, he converted the estate into a private club with an initiation fee and annual dues. Trump continued to use a wing of the house as a private residence. He declared the club his primary residence in 2019. Trump began [building and buying golf courses](/wiki/Donald_Trump_and_golf) in 1999, owning 17 golf courses globally by 2016.

### Licensing the Trump name

See also: [List of things named after Donald Trump](/wiki/List_of_things_named_after_Donald_Trump)

[The Trump Organization](/wiki/The_Trump_Organization) often licensed the Trump name for consumer products and services, including foodstuffs, apparel, learning courses, and home furnishings. Over 50 licensing or management deals involved his name, generating at least $59 million for his companies. By 2018, only two consumer goods companies continued to license Trump's name. During the 2000s, he licensed his name to real estate developments. Forty of the projects he announced were not completed.

### Side ventures

See also: [Donald Trump and American football](/wiki/Donald_Trump_and_American_football)

1985 New Jersey Generals press conference in [Trump Tower](/wiki/Trump_Tower)

In 1970, Trump invested $70,000 of his father's wealth to receive billing as coproducer of a Broadway comedy—and lost the money. After making low-ball bids for the [New York Mets](/wiki/New_York_Mets) and the [Cleveland Indians](/wiki/Cleveland_Indians) baseball teams, in 1983 for about $6 million, he purchased the [New Jersey Generals](/wiki/New_Jersey_Generals), a team in the [United States Football League](/wiki/United_States_Football_League). The league folded after the 1985 season, largely due to his attempt to move to a fall schedule (when it would have competed with the [National Football League](/wiki/National_Football_League) for audience) and his attempt to force a merger with the NFL by bringing an antitrust suit. In 1989 and 1990, he lent his name to the [Tour de Trump](/wiki/Tour_de_Trump) cycling stage race, an attempt to create an American equivalent of European races such as the [Tour de France](/wiki/Tour_de_France) or the [Giro d'Italia](/wiki/Giro_d%27Italia).

From 1986 to 1988, he purchased significant blocks of shares in various public companies while suggesting that he intended to take over the company and then sold his shares for a profit, leading some observers to think he was engaged in [greenmail](/wiki/Greenmail). *The New York Times* found that he initially made millions of dollars in such stock transactions, but "lost most, if not all, of those gains after investors stopped taking his takeover talk seriously".

Trump's star on the [Hollywood Walk of Fame](/wiki/Hollywood_Walk_of_Fame)

In 1988, Trump purchased the [Eastern Air Lines Shuttle](/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines_Shuttle), financing the purchase with $380 million (equivalent to $1.03 billion in 2025) in loans from a syndicate of 22 banks. He renamed the airline [Trump Shuttle](/wiki/Trump_Shuttle) and operated it until 1992. He defaulted on his loans in 1991, and ownership passed to the banks. In 1996, he purchased the [Miss Universe](/wiki/Miss_Universe) pageants, including [Miss USA](/wiki/Miss_USA) and [Miss Teen USA](/wiki/Miss_Teen_USA). Due to disagreements with [CBS](/wiki/CBS) about scheduling, he took both pageants to [NBC](/wiki/NBC) in 2002. In 2007, he received a star on the [Hollywood Walk of Fame](/wiki/Hollywood_Walk_of_Fame) for his work as producer of Miss Universe. NBC and Univision dropped the pageants in June 2015 in reaction to his comments about Mexican immigrants.

#### Trump University

In 2005, Trump cofounded [Trump University](/wiki/Trump_University), a company that sold real estate seminars for up to $35,000. After New York State authorities notified the company that its use of "university" violated state law because it was not an academic institution, its name was changed to the Trump Entrepreneur Initiative in 2010. In 2013, the State of New York filed a $40 million civil suit against Trump University, alleging that the company made false statements and defrauded consumers. Additionally, two class actions were filed in federal court against Trump and his companies. Internal documents revealed that employees were instructed to use a hard-sell approach, and former employees testified that Trump University had defrauded or lied to its students. Shortly after he won the 2016 presidential election, he agreed to pay a total of $25 million to settle the three cases.

### Foundation

Main article: [Donald J. Trump Foundation](/wiki/Donald_J._Trump_Foundation)

The Donald J. Trump Foundation was a [private foundation](/wiki/Private_foundation_(United_States)) established in 1988. From 1987 to 2006, Trump gave his foundation $5.4 million, which had been spent by the end of 2006. After donating a total of $65,000 in 2007–2008, he stopped donating any personal funds to the charity, which received millions from other donors, including $5 million from [Vince McMahon](/wiki/Vince_McMahon). The foundation gave to health and sports-related charities, conservative groups, and charities that held events at Trump properties. In 2016, *[The Washington Post](/wiki/The_Washington_Post)* reported that the charity had committed several potential legal and ethical violations, including self-dealing and [tax evasion](/wiki/Tax_evasion). Also in 2016, the New York attorney general stated the foundation had violated state law by soliciting donations without submitting to required annual external audits and ordered it to cease its fundraising activities in New York immediately. Trump's team announced in December 2016 that the foundation would be dissolved.

In June 2018, the New York attorney general's office filed a civil suit against the foundation, Trump, and his adult children, seeking $2.8 million in restitution and additional penalties. In December 2018, the foundation ceased operation and disbursed its assets to other charities. In November 2019, a New York state judge ordered Trump to pay $2 million to a group of charities for misusing the foundation's funds, in part to finance his presidential campaign.

### Legal affairs and bankruptcies

Main article: [Personal and business legal affairs of Donald Trump](/wiki/Personal_and_business_legal_affairs_of_Donald_Trump)

According to a review of state and federal court files conducted by *[USA Today](/wiki/USA_Today)* in 2018, Trump and his businesses had been involved in more than 4,000 state and federal legal actions. While Trump has not filed for [personal bankruptcy](/wiki/Personal_bankruptcy), his over-leveraged hotel and casino businesses in Atlantic City and New York filed for [Chapter 11 bankruptcy](/wiki/Chapter_11_bankruptcy) protection six times between 1991 and 2009. They continued to operate while the banks restructured debt and reduced his shares in the properties. During the 1980s, more than 70 banks had lent Trump $4 billion. After his corporate bankruptcies of the early 1990s, most major banks, with the exception of [Deutsche Bank](/wiki/Deutsche_Bank), declined to lend to him. After the [January 6 Capitol attack](/wiki/January_6_United_States_Capitol_attack), Deutsche Bank also decided not to do business with him or his affiliated company in the future.

## Media career

Main article: [Media career of Donald Trump](/wiki/Media_career_of_Donald_Trump)

Trump has [published 19 books under his name](/wiki/Bibliography_of_Donald_Trump), most written or cowritten by [ghostwriters](/wiki/Ghostwriters). His first book, *[The Art of the Deal](/wiki/The_Art_of_the_Deal)* (1987), was a [*New York Times* Best Seller](/wiki/The_New_York_Times_Best_Seller_list), and was credited by *[The New Yorker](/wiki/The_New_Yorker)* with making Trump famous as an "emblem of the successful tycoon". The book was ghostwritten by [Tony Schwartz](/wiki/Tony_Schwartz_(author)), who is credited as a coauthor. Trump had cameos in many films and television shows from 1985 to 2001. Trump acquired his style of politics from [professional wrestling](/wiki/Professional_wrestling). From the late 1980s, he sporadically played himself as a super-rich boss at events staged by professional wrestling promotion [WWE](/wiki/WWE), including [WrestleMania 23](/wiki/WrestleMania_23) in 2007. Starting in the 1990s, Trump appeared 24 times as a guest on the nationally syndicated *[Howard Stern Show](/wiki/Howard_Stern_Show)*. He had his own short-form talk radio program, *[Trumped!](/wiki/Trumped!)*, from 2004 to 2008. From 2011 until 2015, he was a guest commentator on *[Fox & Friends](/wiki/Fox_%26_Friends)*. In 2021, Trump, who had been a member since 1989, resigned from [SAG-AFTRA](/wiki/SAG-AFTRA) to avoid a disciplinary hearing regarding the January 6 attack; two days later, the union permanently barred him.

Producer [Mark Burnett](/wiki/Mark_Burnett) made Trump a television star when he created the reality show [*The Apprentice*](/wiki/The_Apprentice_(American_TV_series)), which Trump hosted from 2004 to 2015 (including variant *[The Celebrity Apprentice](/wiki/The_Celebrity_Apprentice)*). On the shows, he was a superrich chief executive who eliminated contestants with the [catchphrase](/wiki/Catchphrase) "you're fired". *The New York Times* called his portrayal "a highly flattering, highly fictionalized version" of himself. The shows remade Trump's image for millions of viewers nationwide. With the related licensing agreements, they earned him more than $400 million.

## Early political aspirations

Further information: [Political career of Donald Trump](/wiki/Political_career_of_Donald_Trump)

Trump registered as a Republican in Queens in 1969 and in Manhattan in 1987; a member of the [Independence Party](/wiki/Independence_Party_of_New_York), the New York state affiliate of the [Reform Party](/wiki/Reform_Party_of_the_United_States_of_America), in 1999; a [Democrat](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)) in 2001; a Republican in 2009; [unaffiliated](/wiki/Independent_politician) in 2011; and a Republican in 2012.

Speaking at [CPAC](/wiki/Conservative_Political_Action_Conference), February 2011

In 1987, Trump placed full-page advertisements in major newspapers, expressing his views on foreign policy and how to eliminate the federal budget deficit. In 1988, he approached [Lee Atwater](/wiki/Lee_Atwater), asking to be put into consideration to be Republican nominee [George H. W. Bush](/wiki/George_H._W._Bush)'s running mate. Bush found the request "strange and unbelievable". Trump [was a candidate](/wiki/Donald_Trump_2000_presidential_campaign) in the [2000 Reform Party presidential primaries](/wiki/2000_Reform_Party_presidential_primaries) for three months before he withdrew in February 2000. In 2011, Trump considered challenging President [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama) in [the 2012 election](/wiki/2012_United_States_presidential_election). He spoke at the [Conservative Political Action Conference](/wiki/Conservative_Political_Action_Conference) in February and gave speeches in states with early primaries. In May 2011, he announced that he would not run.

## 2016 presidential election

Main article: [2016 United States presidential election](/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election)

Further information: [Donald Trump 2016 presidential campaign](/wiki/Donald_Trump_2016_presidential_campaign), [2016 Republican Party presidential primaries](/wiki/2016_Republican_Party_presidential_primaries), and [First presidential transition of Donald Trump](/wiki/First_presidential_transition_of_Donald_Trump)

Trump announced his candidacy for the 2016 election in June 2015. Using the slogan "[Make America Great Again](/wiki/Make_America_Great_Again)" he had trademarked in 2012, he campaigned as a rich, successful businessman and an outsider without political experience, and claimed [media bias](/wiki/Media_bias) against him. Trump's campaign statements were often opaque and suggestive; a record number were false. He became the Republican front-runner in March 2016 and was declared the presumptive Republican nominee in May.

Campaigning in Arizona, March 2016

Trump described [NATO](/wiki/NATO) as "obsolete" and espoused views described by *The Washington Post* as [noninterventionist](/wiki/Noninterventionist) and [protectionist](/wiki/Protectionist). His campaign platform emphasized renegotiating [U.S.–China relations](/wiki/U.S.%E2%80%93China_relations) and [free trade agreements](/wiki/Free_trade_agreements) such as [NAFTA](/wiki/NAFTA) and strongly enforcing immigration laws. Other campaign positions included pursuing [energy independence](/wiki/Energy_independence) while opposing climate change regulations, modernizing [services for veterans](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Veterans_Affairs#Veterans_Benefits_Administration), repealing and replacing the [Affordable Care Act](/wiki/Affordable_Care_Act), abolishing [Common Core](/wiki/Common_Core) education standards, [investing in infrastructure](/wiki/Infrastructure-based_development), simplifying the [tax code](/wiki/Internal_Revenue_Code) while reducing taxes, and imposing [tariffs](/wiki/Tariff) on imports by companies that offshore jobs. He advocated increasing military spending and extreme vetting or banning of immigrants from Muslim-majority countries. He promised to build a [wall on the Mexico–U.S. border](/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall) and vowed that Mexico would pay for it. He pledged to deport millions of [illegal immigrants residing in the U.S.](/wiki/Undocumented_immigrant_population_of_the_United_States), and criticized [birthright citizenship](/wiki/Birthright_citizenship_in_the_United_States) for incentivizing "[anchor babies](/wiki/Anchor_babies)". According to an analysis in *[Political Science Quarterly](/wiki/Political_Science_Quarterly)*, Trump made "explicitly racist and sexist appeals to win over white voters" during his 2016 presidential campaign. In particular, his campaign launch speech drew criticism for claiming Mexican immigrants were "bringing drugs, they're bringing crime, they're rapists"; in response, NBC fired him from *Celebrity Apprentice*.

Trump's FEC-required reports listed assets above $1.4 billion and outstanding debts of at least $265 million. He did not release [his tax returns](/wiki/Tax_returns_of_Donald_Trump), contrary to the practice of every major candidate since 1976 and to promises he made in 2014 and 2015 to release them if he ran for office. He said his tax returns were being [audited](/wiki/Income_tax_audit), and that his lawyers had advised him against releasing them. After a lengthy court battle to block release of his tax returns and other records to the [Manhattan district attorney](/wiki/Manhattan_district_attorney) for a criminal investigation, including two appeals by Trump to the [U.S. Supreme Court](/wiki/U.S._Supreme_Court), in February 2021 the high court allowed the records to be released to the prosecutor for review by a grand jury. In October 2016, portions of Trump's state filings for 1995 were leaked to a reporter from *The New York Times*. They show that he had declared a loss of $916 million that year, which could have let him avoid taxes for up to 18 years.

Trump won the election with 304 [electoral votes](/wiki/Electoral_College_(United_States)) versus 227 for Democratic candidate [Hillary Clinton](/wiki/Hillary_Clinton). The fifth person to be elected president [despite losing the popular vote](/wiki/List_of_United_States_presidential_elections_in_which_the_winner_lost_the_popular_vote), he received about 2.87 million fewer votes than Clinton, 46.1% to her 48.2%. He was the only president who neither served in the military nor held any government office prior to becoming president. His election marked the return of a Republican [undivided government](/wiki/Divided_government_in_the_United_States). Trump's victory [sparked protests](/wiki/Protests_against_Donald_Trump#Before_first_presidency) in major U.S. cities.

## First presidency (2017–2021)

Main article: [First presidency of Donald Trump](/wiki/First_presidency_of_Donald_Trump)

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of the Donald Trump presidencies § First presidency (2017–2021)](/wiki/Timeline_of_the_Donald_Trump_presidencies#First_presidency_(2017–2021)).

Taking the [presidential oath of office](/wiki/Oath_of_office_of_the_President_of_the_United_States), administered by Chief Justice [John Roberts](/wiki/John_Roberts), on January 20, 2017

Trump [was inaugurated](/wiki/First_inauguration_of_Donald_Trump) on January 20, 2017. The day after his inauguration, an estimated 2.6 million people worldwide, including 500,000 in Washington, D.C., protested against him in the [Women's Marches](/wiki/2017_Women%27s_March). During his first two weeks in office, Trump signed [eighteen executive orders](/wiki/List_of_executive_orders_in_the_first_Trump_presidency), including authorizing procedures for repealing the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act ("Obamacare"), withdrawal from the Trans-Pacific Partnership negotiations, advancement of the [Keystone XL](/wiki/Keystone_Pipeline) and [Dakota Access Pipeline](/wiki/Dakota_Access_Pipeline) projects, and planning for a wall along the U.S. border with Mexico.

### Conflicts of interest

See also: [First presidency of Donald Trump § Ethics](/wiki/First_presidency_of_Donald_Trump#Ethics)

Trump continued to profit from his businesses during his first presidency and knew how his administration's policies affected them. Although he said he would eschew "new foreign deals", the Trump Organization pursued operational expansions in Scotland, Dubai, and the Dominican Republic. Lobbyists, foreign government officials, and Trump donors and allies generated hundreds of millions of dollars for his resorts and hotels.

### Domestic policy

Main articles: [Domestic policy of the first Trump administration](/wiki/Domestic_policy_of_the_first_Trump_administration), [Economic policy of the first Trump administration](/wiki/Economic_policy_of_the_first_Trump_administration), [Environmental policy of the first Trump administration](/wiki/Environmental_policy_of_the_first_Trump_administration), and [Social policy of the first Trump administration](/wiki/Social_policy_of_the_first_Trump_administration)

Trump took office at the height of the longest [economic expansion](/wiki/Economic_expansion) in American history, which began in 2009 and continued until February 2020, when the [COVID-19 recession](/wiki/COVID-19_recession) began. In December 2017, he signed the [Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017](/wiki/Tax_Cuts_and_Jobs_Act_of_2017), which reduced tax rates for businesses and individuals and eliminated the penalty associated with the [Affordable Care Act](/wiki/Affordable_Care_Act)'s individual mandate. Under Trump, the federal budget deficit increased by almost 50 percent, to nearly $1 trillion in 2019. By the end of his term, the [U.S. national debt](/wiki/U.S._national_debt) increased by 39 percent, reaching $27.75 trillion, and the U.S. [debt-to-GDP ratio](/wiki/Debt-to-GDP_ratio) hit a post-World War II high.

Trump rejects the [scientific consensus on climate change](/wiki/Scientific_consensus_on_climate_change).
He reduced the budget for [renewable energy](/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_the_United_States#Renewable_energy_research) research by 40 percent and reversed Obama-era policies directed at curbing [climate change](/wiki/Climate_change). He [withdrew from the Paris Agreement](/wiki/United_States_and_the_Paris_Agreement), making the U.S. the only nation to not ratify it.

Trump aimed and aims to boost the production and exports of [fossil fuels](/wiki/Fossil_fuel). [Natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas) expanded under Trump, but [coal continued to decline](/wiki/Coal_mining_in_the_United_States#Production).
He [rolled back](/wiki/Rollback) more than 100 federal environmental regulations, including those that curbed [greenhouse gas emissions](/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions), air and [water pollution](/wiki/Water_pollution), and the use of toxic substances. He weakened protections for animals and [environmental standards](/wiki/Environmental_standard#United_States) for federal infrastructure projects, and expanded permitted areas for drilling and [resource extraction](/wiki/Resource_extraction), such as allowing [drilling in the Arctic Refuge](/wiki/Arctic_Refuge_drilling_controversy).

Trump dismantled federal regulations on health, labor, the environment, and other areas, including a bill that revoked the Obama-era regulation restricting the sale of firearms to severely mentally ill people. During his first six weeks in office, he delayed, suspended, or reversed ninety federal regulations, often "after requests by the regulated industries".

Trump vowed to repeal and replace the [Affordable Care Act](/wiki/Affordable_Care_Act). He scaled back the act's implementation through executive orders. He expressed a desire to "let Obamacare fail"; his administration halved the [enrollment period](/wiki/Annual_enrollment) and drastically reduced funding for enrollment promotion.

In response to the [opioid epidemic](/wiki/Opioid_epidemic_in_the_United_States), Trump signed legislation in 2018 to increase funding for drug treatments, but was widely criticized for failing to make a concrete strategy. He barred organizations that provide abortions or abortion referrals from receiving federal funds. His administration rolled back key components of the Obama administration's workplace protections against [discrimination of LGBTQ people](/wiki/Discrimination_against_LGBT_people). His attempted rollback of anti-discrimination protections for [transgender](/wiki/Transgender) patients in August 2020 was halted by a federal judge after a Supreme Court ruling extended employees' civil rights protections to [gender identity](/wiki/Gender_identity) and sexual orientation. His administration [took an anti-marijuana position](/wiki/Cannabis_policy_of_the_Donald_Trump_administration), revoking [Obama-era policies](/wiki/Cole_Memorandum) that provided protections for states that legalized marijuana. Trump is a long-time advocate of capital punishment, and his administration oversaw the [federal government execute](/wiki/Capital_punishment_by_the_United_States_federal_government) 13 prisoners, more than in the previous 56 years combined, ending a 17-year moratorium.

### Pardons and commutations

Further information: [List of people granted executive clemency in the first Trump presidency](/wiki/List_of_people_granted_executive_clemency_in_the_first_Trump_presidency)

During his first term, Trump granted 237 requests for clemency, fewer than all presidents since 1900 with the exception of [George H. W. Bush](/wiki/George_H._W._Bush) and [George W. Bush](/wiki/George_W._Bush). Only 25 of them had been vetted by the Justice Department's [Office of the Pardon Attorney](/wiki/Office_of_the_Pardon_Attorney); the others were granted to people with personal or political connections to him, his family, and his allies, or recommended by celebrities. In his last full day in office, he granted 73 pardons and commuted 70 sentences. The pardons of three military service members convicted of or charged with violent crimes were opposed by military leaders.

### Immigration

Main article: [Immigration policy of the first Trump administration](/wiki/Immigration_policy_of_the_first_Trump_administration)

Further information: [Mexico–United States border crisis § First Trump administration (2017–2021)](/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_crisis#First_Trump_administration_(2017–2021))

As president, Trump described illegal immigration as an "invasion" of the United States and drastically escalated immigration enforcement. He implemented harsh policies against asylum seekers and [deployed nearly 6,000 troops](/wiki/Operation_Faithful_Patriot) to the [U.S.–Mexico border](/wiki/U.S.%E2%80%93Mexico_border) to stop illegal crossings. He reduced the number of [refugees admitted](/wiki/United_States_Refugee_Admissions_Program) to record lows, from an annual limit of 110,000 before he took office to 15,000 in 2021. Trump also increased restrictions on granting [permanent residency](/wiki/Permanent_residency_in_the_United_States) to [immigrants needing public benefits](/wiki/Public_charge_rule). One of his central campaign promises was to build a [wall along the U.S.–Mexico border](/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall); during his first term, the U.S. built 73 miles (117 km) of wall in areas without barriers and 365 miles (587 km) to replace older barriers. In 2018, Trump's refusal to sign any [spending bill](/wiki/Spending_bill) unless it allocated funding for the border wall resulted in [the longest-ever federal government shutdown](/wiki/2018%E2%80%932019_United_States_federal_government_shutdown), for 35 days from December 2018 to January 2019. The shutdown ended after he agreed to fund the government without any funds for the wall. To avoid another shutdown, Congress passed a funding bill with $1.4 billion for border fencing in February. Trump later declared a [national emergency on the southern border](/wiki/National_Emergency_Concerning_the_Southern_Border_of_the_United_States) to divert $6.1 billion of funding to the border wall despite congressional disagreement.

In January 2017, Trump signed [an executive order](/wiki/Executive_Order_13769) that [denied entry to citizens from six Muslim-majority countries](/wiki/Trump_travel_ban) for four months and from Syria indefinitely. The order caused [many protests](/wiki/Protests_against_Executive_Order_13769) and [legal challenges](/wiki/Legal_challenges_to_the_Trump_travel_ban) that resulted in [nationwide injunctions](/wiki/Nationwide_injunction). [A revised order](/wiki/Executive_Order_13780) giving some exceptions was also blocked by courts, but [the Supreme Court ruled in June](/wiki/International_Refugee_Assistance_Project_v._Trump) that the ban could be enforced on those lacking "a *bona fide* relationship with a person or entity" in the U.S. Trump replaced the ban in September with [a presidential proclamation](/wiki/Presidential_Proclamation_9645) extending travel bans to North Koreans, Chadians, and some Venezuelan officials, but excluded Iraq and Sudan. The Supreme Court allowed that version to go into effect in December 2017, and ultimately upheld the ban in 2019.

Children, sleeping mats, and foil blankets in wire mesh compartment, [Ursula detention facility](/wiki/Ursula_detention_facility), June 2018

From 2017 to 2018, the Trump administration had [a policy of family separation](/wiki/Trump_administration_family_separation_policy) that separated over 4,400 children, some as young as four months old, from migrant parents at the U.S.–Mexico border. The unprecedented policy sparked public outrage in the country. Despite Trump initially blaming Democrats and insisting he could not stop the policy with an executive order, he acceded to public pressure in June 2018 and mandated that illegal immigrant families be detained together unless "there is a concern" of risk for the child. A judge later ordered that the families be reunited and further separations stopped except in limited circumstances, though over 1,000 additional children were separated from their families after the order. By April 2024, 1,360 children had not been confirmed as reunified with their families.

### Foreign policy

Main articles: [Foreign policy of the first Trump administration](/wiki/Foreign_policy_of_the_first_Trump_administration) and [Tariffs in the first Trump administration](/wiki/Tariffs_in_the_first_Trump_administration)

Further information: [Russia–United States relations § First Trump administration (2017–2021)](/wiki/Russia%E2%80%93United_States_relations#First_Trump_administration_(2017–2021)), [China–United States relations § First Trump administration (2017–2021)](/wiki/China%E2%80%93United_States_relations#First_Trump_administration_(2017–2021)), [2017–2018 North Korea crisis](/wiki/2017%E2%80%932018_North_Korea_crisis), and [2018–19 Korean peace process](/wiki/2018%E2%80%9319_Korean_peace_process)

[G7](/wiki/Group_of_Seven) leaders at the [45th summit](/wiki/45th_G7_summit) in France, 2019

Trump describes himself as a "nationalist" and his foreign policy as "[America First](/wiki/America_First)". He supported [populist](/wiki/Populist), [neo-nationalist](/wiki/Neo-nationalist), and authoritarian governments. Unpredictability, uncertainty, and inconsistency characterized foreign relations during his tenure. Relations between the U.S. and its European allies were strained under Trump. He criticized [NATO allies](/wiki/Member_states_of_NATO) and privately suggested that the U.S. should [withdraw from NATO](/wiki/Withdrawal_from_NATO#United_States).

Trump supported many of the policies of Israeli prime minister [Benjamin Netanyahu](/wiki/Benjamin_Netanyahu). In 2020, Trump hosted the signing of the [Abraham Accords](/wiki/Abraham_Accords) between Israel and the [United Arab Emirates](/wiki/United_Arab_Emirates) and [Bahrain](/wiki/Bahrain) to normalize their foreign relations.

Shaking hands with Russian president Vladimir Putin during the [2018 summit](/wiki/2018_Russia%E2%80%93United_States_Summit) in [Helsinki](/wiki/Helsinki), Finland

Trump began [a trade war with China](/wiki/China%E2%80%93United_States_trade_war) in 2018 after imposing tariffs and other trade barriers he said would force China to end longstanding unfair trade practice and [intellectual property infringement](/wiki/Allegations_of_intellectual_property_infringement_by_China). Trump weakened the toughest U.S. sanctions imposed after the [2014 Russian annexation of Crimea](/wiki/2014_Russian_annexation_of_Crimea). Trump praised and, according to some critics, rarely criticized Russian president [Vladimir Putin](/wiki/Vladimir_Putin), though he opposed some actions of Russia's government. He withdrew the U.S. from the [Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty](/wiki/Intermediate-Range_Nuclear_Forces_Treaty), citing alleged Russian noncompliance, and supported Russia's possible return to the [G7](/wiki/G7).

As [North Korea's nuclear weapons](/wiki/North_Korea%27s_nuclear_weapons) were increasingly seen as a serious threat, Trump became the first sitting U.S. president to meet a North Korean leader, meeting [Kim Jong Un](/wiki/Kim_Jong_Un) three times between June 2018 and June 2019. Talks on North Korean [denuclearization](/wiki/Denuclearization) broke down in October 2019, and no agreement was reached.

### Personnel

Main articles: [Political appointments of the first Trump administration](/wiki/Political_appointments_of_the_first_Trump_administration) and [First cabinet of Donald Trump](/wiki/First_cabinet_of_Donald_Trump)

Further information: [Government hiring and personnel of Donald Trump](/wiki/Government_hiring_and_personnel_of_Donald_Trump)

By the end of Trump's first year in office, 34 percent of his original staff had resigned, been fired, or been reassigned. By July 2018, 61 percent of his senior aides had left and 141 staffers had left in the previous year. Both figures set a record for recent presidents. Various close personal aides to Trump stopped working for him or were forced to leave. Trump publicly disparaged several of his former top officials.

Trump had four [White House chiefs of staff](/wiki/White_House_chiefs_of_staff), marginalizing or pushing out several. In May 2017, he [dismissed Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) director James Comey](/wiki/Dismissal_of_James_Comey), saying a few days later that he was concerned about Comey's role in the Trump–Russia investigations. Three of Trump's 15 original cabinet members left or were forced to resign within his first year. Trump was slow to appoint second-tier officials in the executive branch, saying many of the positions are unnecessary. In October 2017, there were hundreds of sub-cabinet positions without a nominee. By January 8, 2019, of 706 key positions, 433 had been filled and he had no nominee for 264.

### Judiciary

Further information: [List of federal judges appointed by Donald Trump](/wiki/List_of_federal_judges_appointed_by_Donald_Trump) and [Donald Trump judicial appointment controversies](/wiki/Donald_Trump_judicial_appointment_controversies)

Trump appointed 234 [federal judges](/wiki/United_States_federal_judge), including 54 to the [courts of appeals](/wiki/United_States_courts_of_appeals) and [three](/wiki/Donald_Trump_Supreme_Court_candidates) to the [Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States): [Neil Gorsuch](/wiki/Neil_Gorsuch), [Brett Kavanaugh](/wiki/Brett_Kavanaugh), and [Amy Coney Barrett](/wiki/Amy_Coney_Barrett), which politically shifted the Court to the right. In the 2016 campaign, he pledged that *[Roe v. Wade](/wiki/Roe_v._Wade)* would be overturned "automatically" if he were elected and given the opportunity to appoint two or three anti-abortion justices. He later took credit when *Roe* was overturned by *[Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization](/wiki/Dobbs_v._Jackson_Women%27s_Health_Organization)* in 2022; all three of his Supreme Court nominees voted with the majority. Trump disparaged courts and judges he disagreed with, often in personal terms, and questioned the judiciary's constitutional authority. His attacks on courts drew rebukes from observers, including sitting federal judges, concerned about the effect of his statements on the [judicial independence](/wiki/Judicial_independence) and public confidence in the judiciary.

### COVID-19 pandemic

Main article: [COVID-19 pandemic in the United States](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_States)

Further information: [U.S. federal government response to the COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/U.S._federal_government_response_to_the_COVID-19_pandemic) and [Communication of the Trump administration during the COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/Communication_of_the_Trump_administration_during_the_COVID-19_pandemic)

See also: [Economic impact of the COVID-19 pandemic in the United States](/wiki/Economic_impact_of_the_COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_States)

Conducting a COVID-19 press briefing with members of the [White House Coronavirus Task Force](/wiki/White_House_Coronavirus_Task_Force) on March 15, 2020

After ignoring public health warnings and calls for action from health officials within his administration for weeks, Trump established the [White House Coronavirus Task Force](/wiki/White_House_Coronavirus_Task_Force) in late January. In March, he signed into law the [CARES Act](/wiki/CARES_Act)—a $2.2 trillion bipartisan [economic stimulus](/wiki/Economic_stimulus) bill—the largest stimulus in U.S. history. After weeks of attacks to draw attention away from his slow response, Trump halted funding of the [World Health Organization](/wiki/World_Health_Organization) in April. He encouraged right-wing [protests against social-distancing policies](/wiki/COVID-19_protests_in_the_United_States) in states with stay-at-home orders and repeatedly pressured federal health agencies to take actions he favored, such as approving unproven treatments.

In October, Trump was [hospitalized for three days](/wiki/White_House_COVID-19_outbreak#Presidential_hospitalization_and_early_release) with a severe case of [COVID-19](/wiki/COVID-19) while his medical team downplayed the severity of his condition.

### Investigations

Further information: [Russian interference in the 2016 United States elections](/wiki/Russian_interference_in_the_2016_United_States_elections), [Mueller special counsel investigation](/wiki/Mueller_special_counsel_investigation), and [Mueller report](/wiki/Mueller_report)

After he assumed office, Trump was the subject of increasing [Justice Department](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice) and congressional scrutiny, with investigations covering his election campaign, transition, and inauguration, actions taken during his presidency, [his private businesses](/wiki/The_Trump_Organization), personal taxes, and [his charitable foundation](/wiki/Donald_J._Trump_Foundation). There were ten federal criminal investigations, eight state and local investigations, and twelve congressional investigations.

In July 2016, the [FBI](/wiki/FBI) launched [Crossfire Hurricane](/wiki/Crossfire_Hurricane_(FBI_investigation)), an investigation into possible links between Russia and Trump's 2016 campaign. After Trump fired Comey in May 2017, the FBI opened a second investigation into Trump's personal and [business dealings with Russia](/wiki/Business_projects_of_Donald_Trump_in_Russia). In January 2017, three U.S. intelligence agencies jointly stated with "high confidence" that [Russia interfered in the 2016 presidential election](/wiki/Russian_interference_in_the_2016_United_States_elections) to favor Trump. Crossfire Hurricane was later transferred to [Robert Mueller](/wiki/Robert_Mueller)'s [special counsel investigation](/wiki/Mueller_special_counsel_investigation); the investigation into Trump's ties to Russia was ended by Deputy Attorney General [Rod Rosenstein](/wiki/Rod_Rosenstein) after he told the FBI that Mueller would pursue the matter. At the request of Rosenstein, the Mueller investigation examined criminal matters "in connection with Russia's 2016 election interference". [Mueller's final report](/wiki/Mueller_report) in March 2019 found that Russia interfered in 2016 to favor Trump and that Trump and his campaign welcomed and encouraged the effort, but that the evidence "did not establish" that Trump campaign members conspired or coordinated with Russia. Trump claimed the report exonerated him despite Mueller writing that it did not. The report also detailed potential obstruction of justice by Trump but "did not draw ultimate conclusions" and left the decision to charge the laws to Congress.

### Impeachments

Main articles: [First impeachment of Donald Trump](/wiki/First_impeachment_of_Donald_Trump) and [Second impeachment of Donald Trump](/wiki/Second_impeachment_of_Donald_Trump)

Displaying the headline "Trump acquitted" in 2020

Trump was [impeached](/wiki/Impeachment_in_the_United_States) twice by the House of Representatives, though acquitted by the Senate on both occasions. The [first impeachment](/wiki/First_impeachment_of_Donald_Trump) arose from a whistleblower complaint that in July 2019 Trump [had pressured](/wiki/2019_Trump%E2%80%93Ukraine_scandal) Ukrainian president [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](/wiki/Volodymyr_Zelenskyy) to investigate Joe and Hunter Biden, in an attempt to gain an advantage in the [2020 presidential election](/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election). In December 2019, the House voted to impeach Trump for [abuse of power](/wiki/Abuse_of_power) and [obstruction of Congress](/wiki/Obstruction_of_Congress), and the Senate acquitted him in February 2020.

The [second impeachment](/wiki/Second_impeachment_of_Donald_Trump) came after the January 6 Capitol attack, for which the House charged Trump with [incitement of insurrection](/wiki/Incitement_of_insurrection) on January 13, 2021. Trump left office on January 20, and was acquitted on February 13. Seven Republican senators voted for conviction.

### 2020 presidential election and rejection of outcome

Further information: [2020 United States presidential election](/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election), [Donald Trump 2020 presidential campaign](/wiki/Donald_Trump_2020_presidential_campaign), and [Attempts to overturn the 2020 United States presidential election](/wiki/Attempts_to_overturn_the_2020_United_States_presidential_election)

Trump filed to run for reelection only a few hours after becoming president in 2017. He held his first reelection rally less than a month after taking office and officially became the [Republican nominee](/wiki/2020_Republican_Party_presidential_primaries) in August 2020. Trump's campaign focused on crime, claiming that cities would descend into lawlessness if Democratic nominee [Joe Biden](/wiki/Joe_Biden) won. Starting in early 2020, Trump sowed doubts about the election, claiming without evidence that it would be rigged and that widespread use of mail balloting would produce massive election fraud. He repeatedly refused to say whether he would accept the results if he lost and commit to a [peaceful transition of power](/wiki/Peaceful_transition_of_power).

Joe Biden won the November 2020 election, receiving 81.3 million votes (51.3 percent) to Trump's 74.2 million (46.8 percent) and 306 electoral votes to Trump's 232, with the [Electoral College](/wiki/United_States_Electoral_College) formalizing Biden's victory on December 14. Trump declared victory before the results were known on the morning after the election. Days later, when Biden was projected the winner, Trump baselessly alleged election fraud. As part of [an effort to overturn the results](/wiki/Attempts_to_overturn_the_2020_United_States_presidential_election), Trump and his allies filed many [lawsuits challenging the results](/wiki/Post-election_lawsuits_related_to_the_2020_U.S._presidential_election), which were rejected by at least 86 judges in both state and federal courts for having no factual or legal basis.

Trump's allegations were also refuted by state election officials, and the Supreme Court declined to hear [a case asking it to overturn the results](/wiki/Texas_v._Pennsylvania) in four states won by Biden. Trump repeatedly sought help to overturn the results, personally pressuring Republican local and state office-holders, Republican legislators, the Justice Department, and Vice President Pence, urging actions such as [replacing presidential electors](/wiki/Trump_fake_electors_plot), or that Georgia officials ["find" votes and announce](/wiki/Trump%E2%80%93Raffensperger_phone_call) a "recalculated" result. Trump did not attend [Biden's inauguration](/wiki/Biden%27s_inauguration) on January 20.

A crowd of Trump supporters during the attack

### January 6 attack

Further information: [January 6 United States Capitol attack](/wiki/January_6_United_States_Capitol_attack)

On January 6, 2021, while [Congress was certifying the presidential election results](/wiki/2021_United_States_Electoral_College_vote_count), [Trump held a rally](/wiki/January_6_Trump_rally) at which he called for the election to be overturned and urged his supporters to "fight like hell" and "take back our country" by marching to the Capitol. His supporters then formed a mob that broke into the building, disrupting certification and causing the evacuation of Congress. More than 140 police officers were injured, and five people died during or after the attack. The event has been described as an attempted [self-coup](/wiki/Self-coup) by Trump. Congress later reconvened and confirmed Biden's victory in the early hours of January 7.

## Between presidencies (2021–2025)

Upon leaving the White House, Trump began living at Mar-a-Lago, establishing an office there as provided for by the [Former Presidents Act](/wiki/Former_Presidents_Act). His continuing [false claims concerning the 2020 election](/wiki/Big_lie#Donald_Trump's_false_claims_of_a_stolen_election) were commonly referred to as the "[big lie](/wiki/Big_lie)" by his critics, although in May 2021, he and many of his supporters began using the term to refer to the election itself. The Republican Party used his false claims about the election to justify [imposing new voting restrictions](/wiki/Republican_efforts_to_restrict_voting_following_the_2020_United_States_presidential_election) in its favor. He continued to pressure state legislators to overturn the election. Unlike other former presidents, Trump continued to dominate his party; a 2022 profile in *The New York Times* described him as a modern [party boss](/wiki/Party_boss). He continued fundraising, raising a [war chest](/wiki/War_chest) containing more than twice that of the Republican Party, and profited from fundraisers many Republican candidates held at Mar-a-Lago. Much of his focus was on party governance and installing in key posts officials loyal to him. In the [2022 midterm elections](/wiki/2022_midterm_elections), Trump endorsed over 200 candidates for various offices. In February 2022, his [Trump Media & Technology Group](/wiki/Trump_Media_%26_Technology_Group) (TMTG) launched a pro-Trump social media platform called [Truth Social](/wiki/Truth_Social). In March 2024, TMTG became a [public company](/wiki/Public_company).

### Legal issues

See also: [Personal and business legal affairs of Donald Trump](/wiki/Personal_and_business_legal_affairs_of_Donald_Trump) and [Legal affairs of the first Trump presidency](/wiki/Legal_affairs_of_the_first_Trump_presidency)

In 2019, journalist [E. Jean Carroll](/wiki/E._Jean_Carroll) accused Trump of raping her in the 1990s and [sued him for defamation](/wiki/E._Jean_Carroll_v._Donald_J._Trump) over his denial. Carroll sued him again in 2022 for [battery](/wiki/Battery_(crime)) and further defamation. He was [found liable](/wiki/Legal_liability) for sexual abuse and defamation and ordered to pay $5 million in one case and $83.3 million in the other. Federal appeals courts upheld both findings and awards in December 2024 and September 2025, respectively.

In 2022, [New York filed a civil lawsuit](/wiki/New_York_business_fraud_lawsuit_against_the_Trump_Organization) against Trump accusing him of inflating the Trump Organization's value to gain an advantage with lenders and banks. He was found liable and ordered to pay nearly $355 million plus interest. In August 2025, the appeals court upheld his liability and nonmonetary penalties, but voided the monetary penalty as excessive.

In connection with Trump's efforts to overturn the 2020 election and his involvement in the January 6 attack, in December 2022 the [U.S. House committee on the attack](/wiki/United_States_House_Select_Committee_on_the_January_6_Attack) recommended criminal charges against him for [obstructing an official proceeding](/wiki/Obstructing_an_official_proceeding), inciting or assisting an insurrection, and [conspiracy to defraud the United States](/wiki/Conspiracy_to_defraud_the_United_States). In August 2023, a grand jury in [Fulton County, Georgia](/wiki/Fulton_County,_Georgia), indicted him on [13 charges, including racketeering](/wiki/Georgia_election_racketeering_prosecution), for his efforts to subvert the 2020 election in the state. The case was dismissed in November 2025 after the new prosecutor declined to pursue the charges.

Classified intelligence material found during search of Mar-a-Lago

In January 2022, the [National Archives and Records Administration](/wiki/National_Archives_and_Records_Administration) retrieved [15 boxes of documents](/wiki/Federal_prosecution_of_Donald_Trump_(classified_documents_case)) Trump had taken to [Mar-a-Lago](/wiki/Mar-a-Lago) after leaving the White House, some of which were classified. In the ensuing Justice Department investigation, officials retrieved more classified documents from his lawyers. On August 8, 2022, [FBI agents searched Mar-a-Lago](/wiki/FBI_search_of_Mar-a-Lago) for illegally held documents, including those in breach of the [Espionage Act](/wiki/Espionage_Act), collecting 11 sets of classified documents, some marked top secret. A [federal grand jury](/wiki/Federal_grand_jury) constituted by Special Counsel [Jack Smith](/wiki/Jack_Smith_(lawyer)) indicted Trump in June 2023 on 31 counts of "willfully retaining national defense information" under the Espionage Act, among other charges. Trump pleaded not guilty. In July 2024, judge [Aileen Cannon](/wiki/Aileen_Cannon) dismissed the case, ruling Smith's appointment as special prosecutor was unconstitutional. After his reelection, the [2020 election obstruction case](/wiki/Federal_prosecution_of_Donald_Trump_(election_obstruction_case)) and the classified documents case were dismissed without [prejudice](/wiki/Prejudice_(legal_term)) (a legal term meaning charges could be refiled after the end of Trump's second term) due to Justice Department policy against prosecuting sitting presidents; *[Politico](/wiki/Politico)* described those dismissals as "in some sense a formality".

In May 2024, Trump [was convicted on 34 felony counts](/wiki/Prosecution_of_Donald_Trump_in_New_York) of [falsifying business records](/wiki/Falsifying_business_records). The case stemmed from evidence that he booked [Michael Cohen](/wiki/Michael_Cohen_(lawyer))'s hush-money payments to adult film actress [Stormy Daniels](/wiki/Stormy_Daniels) as business expenses to cover up his alleged 2006–2007 affair with Daniels during the 2016 election. On January 10, 2025, the judge gave Trump a no-penalty sentence known as an unconditional discharge, saying that punitive requirements would have interfered with presidential immunity.

### 2024 presidential election

Main article: [2024 United States presidential election](/wiki/2024_United_States_presidential_election)

Further information: [Donald Trump 2024 presidential campaign](/wiki/Donald_Trump_2024_presidential_campaign), [2024 Republican Party presidential primaries](/wiki/2024_Republican_Party_presidential_primaries), and [Second presidential transition of Donald Trump](/wiki/Second_presidential_transition_of_Donald_Trump)

In November 2022, Trump announced his candidacy for the 2024 presidential election and created a fundraising account. In March 2023, the campaign began diverting ten percent of the donations to his [leadership PAC](/wiki/Leadership_PAC). His campaign had paid $100 million towards his legal bills by March 2024.

Trump's policy platform, named [Agenda 47](/wiki/Agenda_47), was vague and aimed at Republican primaries. He occasionally disavowed knowledge of [Project 2025](/wiki/Project_2025), an outline for consolidating power in the executive branch, despite personnel overlap with his first administration. During the campaign, he made increasingly violent and authoritarian statements.
He said that he would weaponize the FBI and the Justice Department against his political opponents and use the military to target Democratic politicians and those not supporting his candidacy. He used harsher and more dehumanizing anti-immigrant rhetoric than during his presidency.
His rhetoric, calling his political opponents "the enemy", vermin, and fascists, has been described by some historians and scholars as authoritarian, [fascist](/wiki/Donald_Trump_and_fascism),
and unlike anything a political candidate has ever said in American history. [Age and health concerns](/wiki/Age_and_health_concerns_about_Donald_Trump) also arose during the campaign, with several medical experts highlighting an increase in rambling, [tangential speech](/wiki/Tangential_speech) and [behavioral disinhibition](/wiki/Disinhibition).

Trump mentioned "rigged election" and "election interference" earlier and more frequently than in the 2016 and 2020 campaigns and refused to commit to accepting the 2024 election results. On July 13, 2024, Trump was shot in the ear in [an assassination attempt](/wiki/Attempted_assassination_of_Donald_Trump_in_Pennsylvania) at a campaign rally in [Butler Township, Pennsylvania](/wiki/Butler_Township,_Butler_County,_Pennsylvania). Two days later, the [2024 Republican National Convention](/wiki/2024_Republican_National_Convention) nominated him as their presidential candidate. In September he was targeted but unharmed in [another assassination attempt](/wiki/Attempted_assassination_of_Donald_Trump_in_Florida) in Florida. Trump won the election in November 2024 with 312 electoral votes to incumbent vice president Kamala Harris's 226. He also won the popular vote with 49.8% to Harris's 48.3%. His victory in 2024 was in large part due to the [2021–2023 inflation surge](/wiki/2021%E2%80%932023_inflation_surge). Several outlets described his reelection as an extraordinary comeback.

## Second presidency (2025–present)

Main article: [Second presidency of Donald Trump](/wiki/Second_presidency_of_Donald_Trump)

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of the Donald Trump presidencies § Second presidency (2025–present)](/wiki/Timeline_of_the_Donald_Trump_presidencies#Second_presidency_(2025–present)).

Taking the [presidential oath of office](/wiki/Oath_of_office_of_the_President_of_the_United_States), administered by Chief Justice [John Roberts](/wiki/John_Roberts), on January 20, 2025

Trump began his second term upon [his inauguration](/wiki/Second_inauguration_of_Donald_Trump) on January 20, 2025. He became the [oldest to assume the presidency](/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_United_States_by_age), the first president with a felony conviction, and the second person to serve two nonconsecutive terms as president.

Trump chose several Project 2025 authors for his second administration; after the first year of his second term about half of the project's agenda had been implemented. Emphasizing his conflicts with judicial authority, *The New York Times* said his attempts to expand presidential power are a defining characteristic of his second term. Trump's second term saw frequent policy announcements, delays, and reversals leading to the emergence of the term "[TACO](/wiki/Trump_Always_Chickens_Out)" to describe it.

### Early actions

See also: [First 100 days of the second Trump presidency](/wiki/First_100_days_of_the_second_Trump_presidency)

More than a month before the 100-day mark of Trump's second term, he had [issued more executive orders](/wiki/List_of_executive_orders_in_the_second_Trump_presidency) than any other U.S. president's first 100 days. As of mid-January 2026, his orders and actions had been challenged in over 550 lawsuits nationwide. Of these, plaintiffs had prevailed in 195 cases, the government had prevailed in 109 cases, there were split rulings in 16 cases, 228 cases were pending, and 25 cases were closed. Both Republican and Democratic judicial appointees have found numerous constitutional and statutory flaws with Trump administration policies.

Following legal setbacks, Trump increased his criticism of the judiciary and called for impeachment of federal judges who ruled against him. By mid-July 2025, a *[Washington Post](/wiki/Washington_Post)* analysis found he defied judges and the courts in roughly one third of all cases against him, actions which were described by legal experts as unprecedented for any presidential administration.

His administration asserted a constitutional right to ignore federal law in its justification of several actions such as in his refusal to enforce a [federal ban on TikTok](/wiki/Protecting_Americans_from_Foreign_Adversary_Controlled_Applications_Act), with legal experts describing it as claiming a "constitutional power to immunize private parties to commit otherwise illegal acts with impunity".

### Conflicts of interest (2025–present)

Further information: [Second presidency of Donald Trump § Ethics](/wiki/Second_presidency_of_Donald_Trump#Ethics), and [Cryptocurrency in the second Trump presidency](/wiki/Cryptocurrency_in_the_second_Trump_presidency)

Trump's second presidency has been described as having fewer guardrails against conflicts of interest than his first, breaking with decades of ethical norms, and raising substantial [corruption](/wiki/Corruption) concerns. He maintained a publicly traded company in [Trump Media & Technology Group](/wiki/Trump_Media_%26_Technology_Group), and diversified it into financial services. He pursued new overseas real estate deals involving state-affiliated entities, and had several branding and licensing deals selling Trump-branded merchandise. He profited from events held at his hotels and golf courses and did not place his assets in a blind trust, as previous presidents had done.

Trump launched, promoted, and personally benefited from [two cryptocurrency tokens](/wiki/The_Trump_Organization#Other_ventures_and_investments) ("[meme coins](/wiki/Meme_coin)"), [$Trump](/wiki/$Trump) and $Melania. He also directly benefited from his family's cryptocurrency company [World Liberty Financial](/wiki/World_Liberty_Financial), which engaged in an unprecedented mixing of private enterprise and government policy.

### Mass terminations of federal employees

Main article: [2025 United States federal mass layoffs](/wiki/2025_United_States_federal_mass_layoffs)

Trump implemented a hiring freeze across the federal government and ordered telework of federal employees to be discontinued within 30 days. He ordered a review of many career civil service positions with the intention of [reclassifying them into at-will positions](/wiki/Policy/Career_appointment) without job protections. He initiated mass job terminations of federal employees, which were described by legal experts as unprecedented or in violation of federal law, with the intent of replacing them with workers more aligned with his agenda. By late February 2025, the administration had fired more than 30,000 people. He ordered an end to [diversity, equity, and inclusion](/wiki/Diversity,_equity,_and_inclusion) (DEI) projects in the federal government and rescinded [Lyndon B. Johnson](/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson)'s 1965 [Executive Order 11246](/wiki/Executive_Order_11246), which had mandated that federal contractors take affirmative action to end racial discrimination.

Trump and [Elon Musk](/wiki/Elon_Musk)'s [Department of Government Efficiency](/wiki/Department_of_Government_Efficiency) largely dismantled several federal agencies including [USAID](/wiki/USAID) and the [Department of Education](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Education), unilaterally fired several thousand staff, and reduced administrative functions to statutory minimums.

### Targeting political opponents

Main article: [Targeting of political opponents and civil society under the second Trump administration](/wiki/Targeting_of_political_opponents_and_civil_society_under_the_second_Trump_administration)

During his second presidency, the Trump administration took a series of actions using the government to target political opponents and [civil society](/wiki/Civil_society). He threatened, signed executive actions, and ordered investigations into his political opponents, critics, and organizations aligned with the Democratic Party. He ended the post-[Watergate](/wiki/Watergate) norm of [Justice Department](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice) independence, weaponizing it and agencies across the federal government to target his political enemies. Trump's actions against civil society were described by hundreds of legal experts and political scientists as [authoritarian](/wiki/Authoritarian), contributing to [democratic backsliding](/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_the_United_States), and negatively impacting the [rule of law](/wiki/Rule_of_law).

### Pardons and commutations (2025–present)

Further information: [List of people granted executive clemency in the second Trump presidency](/wiki/List_of_people_granted_executive_clemency_in_the_second_Trump_presidency)

Trump's pardons and grants of clemency favored political allies and loyalists, and disproportionately pardoned "the powerful, famous, well-connected and wealthy" accused of white-collar crime. Trump [granted clemency to all January 6 rioters](/wiki/Pardon_of_January_6_United_States_Capitol_attack_defendants) convicted or charged on his first day in office, including those who violently attacked police, by pardoning more than 1,500 and commuting the sentences of 14.

### Domestic policy (2025–present)

Main articles: [Domestic policy of the second Trump administration](/wiki/Domestic_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration), [Economic policy of the second Trump administration](/wiki/Economic_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration), [Education policy of the second Trump administration](/wiki/Education_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration), [Science policy of the second Trump administration](/wiki/Science_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration), and [Persecution of transgender people under the second Trump administration](/wiki/Persecution_of_transgender_people_under_the_second_Trump_administration)

Trump ordered agencies to stop enforcement of disfavored rules in an attempt at large-scale deregulation that legal experts described as illegal and contrary to decades of federal law. He [branded government buildings, programs and money](/wiki/Branding_of_United_States_government_programs_and_facilities_after_Donald_Trump) with his name or likeness, the first sitting president to do so. He sought greater government control over private businesses and shifted away from traditional conservative [free market](/wiki/Free_market) orthodoxy, engaging in [state capitalism](/wiki/State_capitalism) by taking direct government equity stakes in multiple U.S. corporations. His economic policies and frequent tariff announcements and pauses spurred market volatility and the emergence of the "[Sell America](/wiki/Sell_America)" investment trend on Wall Street.

#### Science

Trump [canceled and paused federal grants](/wiki/2025_United_States_federal_government_grant_pause) and made large cuts to scientific research, several of which were found by judges and the [Government Accountability Office](/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office) as being illegal and unconstitutional. Trump and his administration's [Make America Healthy Again](/wiki/Make_America_Healthy_Again) agenda promoted [anti-science](/wiki/Anti-science) and [anti-vaccine activism](/wiki/Anti-vaccine_activism), resulting in a resurgence of whooping cough and measles.

#### National emergencies/health policy

Trump relied on declaring national emergencies to justify hundreds of actions and bypass congressional approval or regulatory review. For instance, he declared a national energy emergency, allowing the suspension of environmental regulations, loosening the rules for fossil fuel extraction and limiting renewable energy projects. Trump withdrew the U.S. from the [Paris Agreement](/wiki/Paris_Agreement) on climate change during his first presidency, [Biden](/wiki/Presidency_of_Joe_Biden) reentered the agreement in 2021, and Trump again withdrew from the agreement during his second presidency.

#### Social

Trump attributed societal problems to [diversity, equity, and inclusion](/wiki/Diversity,_equity,_and_inclusion) (DEI) initiatives and [wokeness](/wiki/Woke#United_States). Equating diversity with incompetence, he reversed pro-diversity policies in the federal government. He threatened cultural institutions on DEI grounds and sixty universities on accusations of antisemitism, and forced law firms to capitulate to his political agenda.

Trump expanded the domestic use of the military and ordered [military deployments to several Democratic Party-led cities](/wiki/2025_deployment_of_federal_forces_in_the_United_States), and threatened to expand his deployments further.

Through [a series of executive orders](/wiki/Persecution_of_transgender_people_under_the_second_Trump_administration#Executive_Orders) and other actions, Trump banned transgender people from the military; restricted or defunded gender-affirming healthcare; opposed [inclusive language](/wiki/Inclusive_language); censored research and education materials; targeted schools, universities, and cultural institutions accused of promoting what his government calls "gender ideology"; barred transgender athletes from sports; and required U.S. passports to state transgender people's sex assigned at birth.

#### One Big Beautiful Bill Act

Main article: [One Big Beautiful Bill Act](/wiki/One_Big_Beautiful_Bill_Act)

In July 2025, Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act into law. The bill made the temporary tax cuts of the 2017 [Tax Cuts and Jobs Act](/wiki/Tax_Cuts_and_Jobs_Act) permanent and added additional tax deductions for a total of around $4.5 trillion, mostly benefiting the highest income brackets and costing people in the lowest income bracket $1,600 per year. It increased funding for national defense, deportations, the border wall, and Trump's proposed [Golden Dome](/wiki/Golden_Dome_(missile_defense_system)) missile defense system. It removed tax credits for renewable energy sources such as wind and solar power and for buyers of electric vehicles. The bill cut funding for [Medicaid](/wiki/Medicaid) and [SNAP](/wiki/Supplemental_Nutrition_Assistance_Program) and added additional work requirements for eligibility and a $35 co-payment for some Medicaid services; the cuts and additional requirements will take effect after the 2026 general election. The bill was projected by the [Congressional Budget Office](/wiki/Congressional_Budget_Office) to increase the budget deficit by $3.4 trillion by 2034, cause 11.8 million people to lose Medicaid coverage, and eliminate SNAP benefits for three million people.

### Immigration (2025–present)

Main articles: [Immigration policy of the second Trump administration](/wiki/Immigration_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration) and [Deportation in the second Trump administration](/wiki/Deportation_in_the_second_Trump_administration)

Further information: [Mexico–United States border crisis § Second Trump administration (2025–present)](/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_crisis#Second_Trump_administration_(2025–present)), [Mexico–United States border wall § Second Trump administration (2025–present)](/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall#Second_Trump_administration_(2025–present)), and [Visa and deportation controversies in the second Trump administration](/wiki/Visa_and_deportation_controversies_in_the_second_Trump_administration)

During Trump's first days in office, he instructed Border Patrol agents to summarily deport [illegal immigrants](/wiki/Illegal_immigration_to_the_United_States) crossing the border and disabled the [CBP One](/wiki/CBP_One) app that was being used to schedule border crossings. Trump resumed the [Remain in Mexico](/wiki/Remain_in_Mexico) policy, designated drug cartels as terrorist groups, and ordered construction to be resumed on a border wall. He also revoked the parole status of immigrants who entered the U.S. under CBP One and ended [humanitarian parole for Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans](/wiki/Parole_for_Cubans,_Haitians,_Nicaraguans,_and_Venezuelans). In March 2025, he used the [Alien Enemies Act](/wiki/Alien_Enemies_Act) of 1798 to [deport migrants without trial](/wiki/March_2025_American_deportations_of_Venezuelans) to be imprisoned at the [Terrorism Confinement Center](/wiki/Terrorism_Confinement_Center) in El Salvador. Trump targeted activists, legal immigrants, tourists, and students with visas who expressed criticism of his policies or pro-Palestinian advocacy. Several [American citizens were detained and deported](/wiki/Detention_and_deportation_of_American_citizens_in_the_second_Trump_administration). His aggressive and hardline deportation campaign led to large nationwide protests and violent confrontations with migrants and protesters which increased in intensity following high-profile killings of U.S. citizens by federal agents during [Operation Metro Surge](/wiki/Operation_Metro_Surge) in January 2026.

### Foreign policy (2025–present)

Main article: [Foreign policy of the second Trump administration](/wiki/Foreign_policy_of_the_second_Trump_administration)

Further information: [Tariffs in the second Trump administration](/wiki/Tariffs_in_the_second_Trump_administration), [China–United States trade war](/wiki/China%E2%80%93United_States_trade_war), [2025–2026 United States trade war with Canada and Mexico](/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_United_States_trade_war_with_Canada_and_Mexico), [American expansionism under Donald Trump](/wiki/American_expansionism_under_Donald_Trump), and [2026 Iran war](/wiki/2026_Iran_war)

Trump's second-term foreign policy was described as [expansionist](/wiki/American_expansionism_under_Donald_Trump) and [imperialist](/wiki/American_imperialism). His administration favored [hard power](/wiki/Hard_power) to achieve its [America First](/wiki/America_First) foreign policy goals, and dismantled or withdrew support from domestic and international organizations dedicated to advancing American [soft power](/wiki/Soft_power). Trump's relations with U.S. allies have been transactional and ranged from indifference to hostility, while he has sought friendlier relations with certain U.S. adversaries.

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/President_Trump_and_Ukrainian_President_Zelenskyy_in_Oval_Office%2C_Feb._28%2C_2025.webm/250px-seek%3D2594-President_Trump_and_Ukrainian_President_Zelenskyy_in_Oval_Office%2C_Feb._28%2C_2025.webm.jpg)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/d/db/President_Trump_and_Ukrainian_President_Zelenskyy_in_Oval_Office%2C_Feb._28%2C_2025.webm/President_Trump_and_Ukrainian_President_Zelenskyy_in_Oval_Office%2C_Feb._28%2C_2025.webm.480p.vp9.webm)

Meeting with Zelenskyy, February 2025

In February 2025, Trump and Vice President Vance [berated Ukrainian president Volodymyr Zelenskyy](/wiki/2025_Trump%E2%80%93Zelenskyy_Oval_Office_meeting) in a highly contentious televised meeting. Media outlets described it as an unprecedented public confrontation between an American president and a foreign [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state).

Trump's economic policies have been described as [protectionist](/wiki/Protectionism_in_the_United_States), with him imposing tariffs on most countries, including large tariffs on major trading partners China, Canada, and Mexico. He started a global trade war, imposing tariffs at the highest level since the 1930 [Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act](/wiki/Smoot%E2%80%93Hawley_Tariff_Act) at the onset of the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression). In February 2026, the Supreme Court ruled the tariffs he imposed under the [International Emergency Economic Powers Act](/wiki/International_Emergency_Economic_Powers_Act) to be illegal. Trump frequently threatened and enacted tariffs against treaty allies for opposing his political objectives.

In Latin America, Trump pursued legally controversial military strikes against [alleged drug boats](/wiki/United_States_strikes_on_alleged_drug_traffickers_during_Operation_Southern_Spear) in international waters, and ordered [a large military buildup](/wiki/United_States_military_buildup_in_the_Caribbean_during_Operation_Southern_Spear). In November 2025, Trump pardoned former Honduran president [Juan Orlando Hernández](/wiki/Juan_Orlando_Hern%C3%A1ndez), who had been extradited to the U.S. in 2022 and sentenced to 45 years in prison for drug trafficking. In January 2026, the U.S. captured Venezuelan president [Nicolás Maduro](/wiki/Nicol%C3%A1s_Maduro) in [a military raid in Venezuela](/wiki/2026_United_States_strikes_in_Venezuela#Capture_of_Maduro) and took him to New York, where he was charged with drug trafficking.

In 2025, Trump [threatened the annexation of Greenland](/wiki/Greenland_crisis), including by military force or the imposition of tariffs on European allies. In January 2026, he appeared to withdraw both threats, stating that he had reached "the framework of a future deal" with NATO.

#### Middle East wars

Trump has taken a pro-Israel stance and continued [support for Israel in the Gaza war](/wiki/United_States_support_for_Israel_in_the_Gaza_war). From March to May 2025, Trump [launched an extensive aerial campaign](/wiki/March%E2%80%93May_2025_United_States_attacks_in_Yemen) against [Houthi](/wiki/Houthis) targets in Yemen—his first major military operation in the Middle East during his second term. In June 2025, Trump joined Israel in its [Twelve-Day War](/wiki/Twelve-Day_War) with Iran, ordering the [bombing of Iranian nuclear sites](/wiki/2025_United_States_strikes_on_Iranian_nuclear_sites) by U.S. planes. His [plan for a Gaza ceasefire deal](/wiki/Gaza_peace_plan) between Israel and [Hamas](/wiki/Hamas) was signed in October 2025, leading to the creation of the [Board of Peace](/wiki/Board_of_Peace).

In February 2026, Trump authorized joint U.S.–Israeli air strikes on Iran, sparking the [2026 Iran war](/wiki/2026_Iran_war) and killing Iranian supreme leader [Ali Khamenei](/wiki/Ali_Khamenei). During the conflict, Trump and his administration made diverse and inconsistent [explanations for starting the war](/wiki/Rationale_for_the_2026_Iran_war). He repeatedly threatened to destroy Iranian civilian infrastructure, and his April 7 comment that "A whole civilization will die tonight, never to be brought back again" if Iran did not yield to his demands sparked widespread bipartisan and international rebuke.

### Personnel (2025–present)

Main articles: [Political appointments of the second Trump administration](/wiki/Political_appointments_of_the_second_Trump_administration) and [Second cabinet of Donald Trump](/wiki/Second_cabinet_of_Donald_Trump)

In his second term, Trump selected [cabinet](/wiki/Second_cabinet_of_Donald_Trump) members with personal loyalty to him, with the "focus on loyalty over subject-matter expertise". In February 2025, the White House stated that [Elon Musk](/wiki/Elon_Musk) was a [special government employee](/wiki/Special_government_employee). Trump gave Musk's [Department of Government Efficiency](/wiki/Department_of_Government_Efficiency) (DOGE) access to many federal government agencies. Musk's teams operated in eighteen departments and agencies in the administration's first month, including in the Treasury Department's $5 trillion payment system, the [Small Business Administration](/wiki/Small_Business_Administration), the [Office of Personnel Management](/wiki/Office_of_Personnel_Management), and the [General Services Administration](/wiki/General_Services_Administration).

## Rhetoric, behavior, and political practice

Main articles: [Political positions of Donald Trump](/wiki/Political_positions_of_Donald_Trump) and [Rhetoric of Donald Trump](/wiki/Rhetoric_of_Donald_Trump)

Starting with his advocacy of [birther](/wiki/Birther) conspiracy theories in the 2010s, Trump helped bring once-fringe far-right ideas and organizations into the mainstream.
The [alt-right](/wiki/Alt-right) movement coalesced around and supported his candidacy due in part to [opposition to multiculturalism](/wiki/Opposition_to_multiculturalism) and [immigration](/wiki/Opposition_to_immigration).
During his 2016 campaign, Trump's politics and rhetoric led to the formation of a [political movement](/wiki/Political_movement) known as [Trumpism](/wiki/Trumpism), which has been [compared to a cult of personality](/wiki/Trumpism#Cult_of_personality).
His political positions have been described as [populist](/wiki/Populist) or [right-wing populist](/wiki/Right-wing_populist), and favoring an expansion of presidential power under a maximalist interpretation of the [unitary executive theory](/wiki/Unitary_executive_theory).

Many of his actions and rhetoric have been described as [authoritarian](/wiki/Authoritarian) and contributing to [democratic backsliding](/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_the_United_States), as well as establishing an ["us versus them" narrative](/wiki/In-group_and_out-group). Trump's rhetoric has been described as using [fearmongering](/wiki/Fearmongering) and [demagogy](/wiki/Demagogy) which [intensified during his 2024 presidential campaign](/wiki/Donald_Trump_2024_presidential_campaign#Fearmongering). He appeals to [evangelical Christians](/wiki/Trumpism#Christian_Trumpism) and [Christian nationalists](/wiki/Christian_nationalism_in_the_United_States), and regularly calls his political opponents "evil".

### Race and gender

Main articles: [Racial views of Donald Trump](/wiki/Racial_views_of_Donald_Trump) and [Donald Trump sexual misconduct allegations](/wiki/Donald_Trump_sexual_misconduct_allegations)

Many of Trump's comments and actions have been characterized as racist. Several studies and surveys found that racist attitudes fueled Trump's political ascent and were more important than economic factors in determining the allegiance of Trump voters. He explicitly and routinely disparages racial, religious, and ethnic minorities, and scholars consistently find that racist and [Islamophobic](/wiki/Islamophobic) attitudes regarding blacks, immigrants, and Muslims are strong indicators of support for Trump.

Trump has been accused of racism for running full-page newspaper advertisements and insisting that a group of five black and Latino teenagers were guilty of raping a white woman in the 1989 [Central Park jogger case](/wiki/Central_Park_jogger_case), even after they were cleared of suspicion in 2002. Trump's comments on the 2017 [Unite the Right rally](/wiki/Unite_the_Right_rally), condemning "this egregious display of hatred, bigotry and violence on many sides" and stating that there were "very fine people on both sides", were criticized as implying a [moral equivalence](/wiki/Moral_equivalence) between the [white supremacist](/wiki/White_supremacist) demonstrators and the counter-protesters. In a January 2018 discussion of immigration legislation, he reportedly referred to El Salvador, Haiti, Honduras, and African nations as "shithole countries", remarks condemned as racist. In July 2019, a House of Representatives [resolution condemned Trump for racist remarks](/wiki/Racial_views_of_Donald_Trump#Democratic_congresswomen_should_"go_back"_to_their_countries) about four minority Democratic congresswomen. His 2024 campaign made extensive use of [dehumanizing language](/wiki/Dehumanization) and [racial stereotypes](/wiki/Racial_stereotypes).

With a group of officials and advisors walking from the White House to St. John's Church, following the [forced removal of protesters at Lafayette Square](/wiki/Donald_Trump_photo_op_at_St._John%27s_Church#Clearing_Lafayette_Square_and_St._John's)

In June 2020, during the [George Floyd protests](/wiki/George_Floyd_protests), federal law-enforcement officials used tear gas and other crowd control tactics to remove a largely peaceful crowd of lawful protesters from [Lafayette Square](/wiki/Lafayette_Square,_Washington,_D.C.), outside the [White House](/wiki/White_House). Trump then [posed with a Bible for a photo op](/wiki/Donald_Trump_photo_op_at_St._John%27s_Church) at the nearby [St. John's Episcopal Church](/wiki/St._John%27s_Episcopal_Church,_Lafayette_Square), with religious leaders condemning both the treatment of protesters and the photo opportunity itself.

Trump has a history of belittling women when speaking to the media and on social media. He made lewd comments, disparaged women's physical appearances, and referred to them using derogatory epithets. In October 2016, a 2005 "[hot mic](/wiki/Hot_mic)" recording surfaced in which [he bragged](/wiki/Donald_Trump_Access_Hollywood_tape) about kissing and groping women without their consent, saying that, "when you're a star, they let you do it. You can do anything. ... Grab 'em by the [pussy](/wiki/Pussy#Female_genitalia)." He characterized the comments as "locker-room talk". After widespread public outrage, including among Republicans, Trump issued a video with a rare public apology and an attack on Hillary Clinton. As of October 2024, since the 1970s, at least 28 women have accused Trump of various acts of sexual misconduct, including rape, sex with minors, sexual assault, physical abuse, kissing and groping without consent, looking under women's skirts, and walking in on naked pageant contestants.

### Link to violence and hate crimes

Further information: [Rhetoric of Donald Trump § Violence](/wiki/Rhetoric_of_Donald_Trump#Violence)

Trump has been identified as a key figure in increasing political violence in the U.S., both for and against him. He is described as embracing extremism, [conspiracy theories](/wiki/List_of_conspiracy_theories_promoted_by_Donald_Trump) such as [QAnon](/wiki/QAnon), and far-right [militia movements](/wiki/American_militia_movement) more than any other modern American president.

Research suggests that Trump's rhetoric is associated with an increased incidence of hate crimes, and that he has an emboldening effect on expressing prejudicial attitudes due to his normalization of explicit racial rhetoric. Researchers have also argued that Trump's "negative characterisations of, and false claims made about, those who became the targets" of the mob at the January 6 riot was a case of [stochastic terrorism](/wiki/Stochastic_terrorism). Numerous defendants investigated or prosecuted for violent acts and hate crimes cited his rhetoric in arguing that they were not culpable or deserved leniency. A nationwide review by [ABC News](/wiki/ABC_News_(United_States)) in May 2020 identified at least 54 criminal cases, from August 2015 to April 2020, in which he was invoked in direct connection with violence or threats of violence mostly by white men and primarily against minorities. Trump's refusal to condemn the white supremacist [Proud Boys](/wiki/Proud_Boys) during a 2020 presidential debate and his comment, "Proud Boys, stand back and stand by", were said to have led to increased recruitment for the pro-Trump group. Counterterrorism researchers described his normalization and [revisionist history](/wiki/Historical_revisionism) of the January 6 Capitol attack, and [grant of clemency to all January 6 rioters](/wiki/Pardon_of_January_6_United_States_Capitol_attack_defendants), as encouraging future political violence.

### False or misleading statements and conspiracy theories

Main articles: [False or misleading statements by Donald Trump](/wiki/False_or_misleading_statements_by_Donald_Trump) and [List of conspiracy theories promoted by Donald Trump](/wiki/List_of_conspiracy_theories_promoted_by_Donald_Trump)

[Fact-checkers](/wiki/Fact-checkers) from *The Washington Post*, the *Toronto Star*, and CNN compiled data on "false or misleading claims" (orange background) and "false claims" (violet foreground).

Trump frequently makes false statements in public remarks, to an extent that was unprecedented in American politics. His false and misleading statements were documented by [fact-checkers](/wiki/Fact-checker), including at *The Washington Post*, which tallied 30,573 false or misleading statements made by him during his first presidency, increasing in frequency over time. His falsehoods are a distinctive part of his political identity and have been described as [firehosing](/wiki/Firehosing).

During his second term, Trump relied on false, misleading, and exaggerated claims to justify his executive actions and policies. He continued to make untrue statements as frequently as before but with less variety while increasing the frequency of repeating a "core set" of falsehoods.

Some of Trump's falsehoods were inconsequential, while others had more far-reaching effects, such as his unproven promotion of antimalarial drugs as a treatment for COVID-19, contributing to a U.S. shortage of these drugs and [panic buying](/wiki/Panic_buying) in Africa and South Asia. His attacks on mail-in ballots and other election practices weakened public faith in the integrity of the 2020 presidential election, while his disinformation about the COVID-19 pandemic delayed and weakened the national response to it. He habitually does not apologize for his falsehoods. Until 2018, the media rarely referred to his falsehoods as lies, including when he repeated demonstrably false statements.

Since before his first presidency, Trump has [promoted numerous conspiracy theories](/wiki/List_of_conspiracy_theories_promoted_by_Donald_Trump). In 2011, Trump became the leading proponent of the racist ["birther" conspiracy theory](/wiki/Barack_Obama_citizenship_conspiracy_theories) that Barack Obama, the first black U.S. president, was not born in the United States, and he claimed credit for pressuring the government to publish Obama's birth certificate, which he considered fraudulent. In September 2016, he publicly acknowledged [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama)'s birthplace and falsely claimed that the rumors had been started by [Hillary Clinton](/wiki/Hillary_Clinton) during her [2008 presidential campaign](/wiki/Hillary_Clinton_2008_presidential_campaign). Trump often prefaced his conspiracies by claiming he heard them from someone else, and promoted conspiracies such as [climate change denial](/wiki/Climate_change_denial), [alleged Ukrainian interference in U.S. elections](/wiki/Conspiracy_theories_related_to_the_2019_Trump%E2%80%93Ukraine_scandal), and [vaccines and autism](/wiki/Vaccines_and_autism). After the 2020 presidential election, he promoted conspiracy theories for his defeat that were characterized as "[the big lie](/wiki/Big_lie_(2020_United_States_presidential_election))".

### Relationship with news media

Main article: [Donald Trump's conflict with the news media](/wiki/Donald_Trump%27s_conflict_with_the_news_media)

In the 2016 campaign, Trump benefited from a record amount of free media coverage, estimated at $2 billion. As a candidate and as president, he frequently accused the press of bias, calling it the "fake news media" and "the [enemy of the people](/wiki/Enemy_of_the_people)".

The first Trump presidency reduced formal press briefings from about one hundred in 2017 to two by 2019 and revoked the press passes of two White House reporters, which were restored by the courts. By 2024, Trump repeatedly voiced support for [outlawing political dissent](/wiki/Criminalization_of_dissent) and criticism, and said that reporters should be prosecuted for not divulging confidential sources and media companies should possibly lose their broadcast licenses for unfavorable coverage of him.

In his second term, Trump's actions against the media were unprecedented in modern American history, and historians described them as mirroring actions by authoritarian leaders to censor political opponents and negatively impacting the [freedom of speech](/wiki/Freedom_of_speech) and [free press](/wiki/Free_press). The campaign to police speech drew comparisons to [cancel culture](/wiki/Cancel_culture), government [censorship](/wiki/Censorship), and [McCarthyism](/wiki/McCarthyism). The [Federal Communications Commission](/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission) launched investigations into media outlets accused of bias against him. As a result of Trump's threats, media executives instructed journalists and their staff to [self-censor](/wiki/Self-censor) and reduce criticism of Trump. Many have characterized Trump as causing a significant decline in [freedom of the press](/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press), including journalist advocacy groups and academic sources.

### Social media

Main articles: [Social media use by Donald Trump](/wiki/Social_media_use_by_Donald_Trump) and [Use of X by Donald Trump](/wiki/Use_of_X_by_Donald_Trump)

From 2009 until he was banned in January 2021, Trump posted on [Twitter](/wiki/Twitter) more than 57,000 times. After years of criticism for allowing Trump to post misinformation and falsehoods, Twitter began to tag some of his tweets with fact-checks in May 2020. He was banned from [Facebook](/wiki/Facebook), [Instagram](/wiki/Instagram), Twitter, and other platforms after the January 6 attack. The loss of his social media presence diminished his ability to shape events and correlated with a dramatic decrease in the volume of misinformation on Twitter. In February 2022, he launched social media platform [Truth Social](/wiki/Truth_Social) where he attracted a fraction of his Twitter following. Twitter reinstated his account in November 2022. The two-year ban at [Meta Platforms](/wiki/Meta_Platforms) lapsed in January 2023, allowing him to return to Facebook and Instagram.

## Assessments

### Public image

Main articles: [Public image of Donald Trump](/wiki/Public_image_of_Donald_Trump) and [Opinion polling on the first Trump presidency](/wiki/Opinion_polling_on_the_first_Trump_presidency)

In Trump's first term, from 2017 to 2021, international approval ratings of U.S. leadership dropped from about 22 percent in a [Gallup](/wiki/Gallup,_Inc.) poll of 134 countries to 16 percent in a [Pew Research](/wiki/Pew_Research) poll of 13 countries. In 2017, estimation of U.S. leadership declined most among allies. Domestically, Trump had chiefly partisan support: 88 percent among Republicans and 7 percent among Democrats. In a 2021 Gallup poll, he was the only president never to reach a 50 percent approval rating, and he was the first not to be named most admired in his first year in office.

### Scholarly rankings

Further information: [Historical rankings of presidents of the United States](/wiki/Historical_rankings_of_presidents_of_the_United_States)

After Trump's first term, [historians ranked Trump](/wiki/Historical_rankings_of_presidents_of_the_United_States#2021_C-SPAN) as the fourth-worst president in [C-SPAN](/wiki/C-SPAN)'s 2021 survey of presidential historians. He rated lowest in the leadership characteristics categories for moral authority and administrative skills. The [Siena College Research Institute](/wiki/Siena_College_Research_Institute)'s 2022 survey [ranked him](/wiki/Historical_rankings_of_presidents_of_the_United_States#2022_Siena_College) third-worst. He was ranked near the bottom in all categories except for luck, willingness to take risks, and party leadership, and ranked last in several categories. In 2018 and 2024, the [American Political Science Association](/wiki/American_Political_Science_Association) ranked him the worst president.

## Personal life

### Family

Main article: [Trump family](/wiki/Trump_family)

Trump (rightmost) and his wife Ivana at a 1985 state dinner for King [Fahd of Saudi Arabia](/wiki/Fahd_of_Saudi_Arabia) with President [Ronald Reagan](/wiki/Ronald_Reagan) and First Lady [Nancy Reagan](/wiki/Nancy_Reagan)

In 1977, Trump married [Ivana Zelníčková](/wiki/Ivana_Zeln%C3%AD%C4%8Dkov%C3%A1). They had three children: [Donald Jr.](/wiki/Donald_Jr.) (b. 1977), [Ivanka](/wiki/Ivanka_Trump) (b. 1981), and [Eric](/wiki/Eric_Trump) (b. 1984). Ivana played a crucial role in generating the "fawning press coverage" that shaped Trump's public image as a real estate mogul. The couple divorced in 1990, following his affair with model and actress [Marla Maples](/wiki/Marla_Maples). He and Maples married in 1993 and divorced in 1999. They have one daughter, [Tiffany](/wiki/Tiffany_Trump) (b. 1993), whom Maples raised in California. In 2005, he married Slovenian model [Melania Knauss](/wiki/Melania_Knauss). Their son [Barron](/wiki/Barron_Trump) was born in 2006.

### Wealth

Main article: [Wealth of Donald Trump](/wiki/Wealth_of_Donald_Trump)

Trump's net worth over time, as estimated by *[Forbes](/wiki/Forbes)*

Trump has said he began his career with "a small loan of a million dollars" from his father and that he had to pay it back with interest. He borrowed at least $60 million from his father, did not repay many of the loans, and received $413 million (2018 equivalent, adjusted for inflation) from his father's real estate company. Posing as a Trump Organization official named "[John Barron](/wiki/John_Barron_(pseudonym))", Trump called journalist Jonathan Greenberg in 1984, trying to get a higher ranking on the [*Forbes* 400](/wiki/Forbes_400) list of wealthiest Americans.

Trump self-reported his net worth over a wide range, from minus $900 million in 1990, to $10 billion in 2015. After his business bankruptcies in the 1990s, his lenders forgave more than 80% of his personal debt and allowed him to keep some of his real estate. In 2015, *[Forbes](/wiki/Forbes)* estimated his net worth at $4.5 billion, based on interviews with more than 80 sources. In 2026, the magazine set that number at $6.5 billion and ranked him the 645th wealthiest person in the world.

### Relationship with Jeffrey Epstein

Main article: [Relationship of Donald Trump and Jeffrey Epstein](/wiki/Relationship_of_Donald_Trump_and_Jeffrey_Epstein)

Trump had a 15-year friendship with convicted sex offender [Jeffrey Epstein](/wiki/Jeffrey_Epstein). Persons who knew them at the time said they frequently "hit on" and competed for women. Media attention and public pressure mounted in 2025, when his administration did not release [files relating to Epstein](/wiki/Epstein_files), despite Trump's promise to do so during the 2024 campaign. Some of the files were released in December 2025, mostly heavily redacted. Roughly three million additional pages released in 2026 included approximately 38,000 references to Trump, his wife, Mar-a-Lago, and other Trump-related terms. References to Trump included "salacious information" and uncorroborated statements about him from witnesses in transcripts made since the early 2000s.

### Health

Main article: [Age and health concerns about Donald Trump](/wiki/Age_and_health_concerns_about_Donald_Trump)

Trump says he has never drunk alcohol, smoked cigarettes, or used drugs, and that he sleeps about four or five hours a night. He has called golfing his "primary form of exercise", but usually does not walk the course. He considers exercise a waste of energy, believing the body is "like a battery, with a finite amount of energy" which is depleted by exercise.

In 2015, his campaign released a letter from his longtime personal physician, [Harold Bornstein](/wiki/Harold_Bornstein), stating that he would "be the healthiest individual ever elected to the presidency". In 2018, Bornstein said Trump had dictated the contents of the letter and that three of Trump's agents had seized his medical records in a February 2017 raid on Bornstein's office.

### Religion

Main articles: [Donald Trump and religion](/wiki/Donald_Trump_and_religion) and [Christian support of Donald Trump](/wiki/Christian_support_of_Donald_Trump)

Trump's April 2026 post

Trump called himself a [Presbyterian](/wiki/Presbyterian) and a [Protestant](/wiki/Protestant) in 2016. In 2020, he said he was a [nondenominational Christian](/wiki/Nondenominational_Christian). In his first term, Trump appointed his personal pastor and spiritual advisor, televangelist [Paula White-Cain](/wiki/Paula_White-Cain), to the White House [Office of Public Liaison](/wiki/Office_of_Public_Liaison). During his second term, he appointed her as senior advisor of the newly created [White House Faith Office](/wiki/White_House_Faith_Office).

Many have questioned the depth of Trump's religious affiliations. A survey during his first presidency showed that 63 percent of Americans did not believe that he was religious, despite professing a Christian affiliation, and that only 44 percent believed he was a Christian. Some of Trump's comments on the Bible or Christian practice have led critical observers to suggest that his knowledge of Christianity is superficial or erroneous, and few biographers have described him as deeply or even particularly religious.

In April 2026, Trump posted an image depicting him as Jesus Christ. The post was deleted after criticism. "I thought it was me as a doctor," Trump said.
