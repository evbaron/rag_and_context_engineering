# List of ancient great powers

See also: [Ancient history](/wiki/Ancient_history)

The exterior of the [Colosseum](/wiki/Colosseum) at night, showing the partially intact outer wall (left) and the mostly intact inner wall (right), one of the best-known symbols of the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire)

**Recognized [great powers](/wiki/Great_power)** came about first in [Europe](/wiki/Europe) during the post-[Napoleonic](/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars) era. The formalization of the division between [small powers](/wiki/Small_powers) and great powers came with the signing of the [Treaty of Chaumont](/wiki/Treaty_of_Chaumont) in 1814. A great power is a [nation](/wiki/Nation) or [state](/wiki/State_(polity)) that, through [economic](/wiki/Economic), [political](/wiki/Politics) and [military](/wiki/Military) strength, is able to exert [power](/wiki/Power_(philosophy)) and [influence](/wiki/Sphere_of_influence) over not only its own region, but beyond to others.

The historical terms "**Great Nation**", a distinguished aggregate of people inhabiting a particular country or territory, and "**Great Empire**", a considerable group of states or countries under a single supreme authority, are [colloquial](/wiki/Colloquial) conversations (historical [jargon](/wiki/Jargon)).

### Sumer and Akkad

Main articles: [Sumer](/wiki/Sumer) and [Akkadian Empire](/wiki/Akkadian_Empire)

**Sumer** (or *Šumer*) was one of the early civilizations of the [Ancient Near East](/wiki/Ancient_Near_East), located in the southern part of [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia) (modern-day [Iraq](/wiki/Iraq)) from the time of the earliest records in the mid [4th millennium BC](/wiki/4th_millennium_BC) until the rise of [Babylonia](/wiki/Babylonia) in the late 3rd millennium BC. The term "Sumerian" applies to all speakers of the [Sumerian language](/wiki/Sumerian_language). Sumer (together with [Ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) and the [Indus Valley civilization](/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilization)) is considered the first settled society in the world to have manifested all the features needed to qualify fully as a "[civilization](/wiki/Civilization)", eventually expanding into the first [empire](/wiki/Empire) in history, the [Akkadian Empire](/wiki/Akkadian_Empire).

### Hurrians

Main article: [Hurrians](/wiki/Hurrians)

| [Hurrians](/wiki/Hurrians) |
| --- |
| Hurrian kingdom in 2300 BC    ---     Akkad     Elam |

The [Hurrians](/wiki/Hurrians) refer to a people who inhabited northern Mesopotamia beginning approximately 2500 BC. The *Hurrian kingdoms* of the Ancient Near East was in Northern Mesopotamia and its people lived in the adjacent regions during the [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age). The largest and most influential Hurrian nation was the [kingdom of Mitanni](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Mitanni). The population of the [Hittite Empire](/wiki/Hittite_Empire) in Anatolia to a large part consisted of Hurrians, and there is significant Hurrian influence in Hittite mythology.

By the Early Iron Age, the Hurrians had been assimilated with other peoples, except perhaps in the [kingdom of Urartu](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Urartu). The Hurrian peoples were not incredibly united, existing as quasi-feudal kingdoms. The kingdom of Mitanni was at its height towards the close of the 14th century BC. By the 13th century BC, the Hurrian kingdoms had been conquered by foreign powers, chiefly the Assyrians.

### Babylonia

Main article: [Babylon](/wiki/Babylon)

The profound political, social, and cultural influence imposed upon the Near East by the civilization known as the [Ancient Babylonian Empire](/wiki/Babylonia) was the most pervasive in this historical period. The city itself, [Babylon](/wiki/Babylon), positioned itself as a center of pivotal historical developments for centuries. There were 3 major Babylonian Dynasties: Amorite, Kassite, and Chaldean. This political entity was most predominate within the southern portion of Mesopotamia. It existed as an unremitting rival of the northern Assyrian Mesopotamians. Although it was assaulted and militarily overcome on several occasions, it did exist as a stalwart presence from the later 3rd millennium BC to the middle of the 6th century BC. After the fall of the Assyrian Empire in 612 BC, the Babylonian Empire was the most powerful state in the ancient world. Its capital, Babylon, was beautifully adorned by King Nebuchadnezzar, who erected several famous buildings. Even after the Babylonian Empire had been overthrown by the Persian king Cyrus the Great in 539 BC, the city itself remained an important cultural center. This period would be considered the zenith of Babylon's dominance in its two-and-a-half millennium history. Ancient Babylon was officially conquered by the Achaemenid Persian Empire in the late 6th century BC.

### Assyria

Main article: [Assyria](/wiki/Assyria)

| [Neo-Assyrian Empire](/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire) |
| --- |
| ---   *Neo-Assyrian Empire*    [Assyrian Empire](/wiki/Assyrian_Empire) - 824 BC    [Assyrian Empire](/wiki/Assyrian_Empire) - 671 BC    ---   *Other*    [Judah](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah)    [Phrygian Kingdom](/wiki/Phrygia)    [Lydian Kingdom](/wiki/Lydia)    [Greek City States](/wiki/Ancient_Greece) |

In early times, the term [Assyria](/wiki/Assyria) referred to a region on the Upper [Tigris](/wiki/Tigris) river, named for its original capital, the ancient city of [Assur](/wiki/Assur). Later, as a nation and empire that came to control all of the [Fertile Crescent](/wiki/Fertile_Crescent), [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt) and much of [Anatolia](/wiki/Anatolia), the term "Assyria proper" referred to roughly the northern half of [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia) (the southern half being [Babylonia](/wiki/Babylonia)), with [Nineveh](/wiki/Nineveh) as its capital. The Assyrian homeland was located near a mountainous region, extending along the Tigris as far as the high Gordiaean or Carduchian mountain range of
[Armenia](/wiki/Armenia), sometimes known as the "Mountains of Ashur". The Assyrian kings controlled a large kingdom at three different times in history. These are called the Old, Middle, and Neo-Assyrian kingdoms, or periods. The most powerful and best-known nation of these periods is the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire), 934–609 BC.

[Shalmaneser III](/wiki/Shalmaneser_III) (858–823 BC) attacked and reduced [Babylonia](/wiki/Babylonia) to vassalage, and defeated [Aramea](/wiki/Aramea), [Israel](/wiki/Israel), [Urartu](/wiki/Urartu), [Phoenicia](/wiki/Phoenicia) and the [Neo-Hittite](/wiki/Neo-Hittite) states, forcing all of these to pay tribute to Assyria. [Shamshi-Adad V](/wiki/Shamshi-Adad_V) (822–811 BC) inherited an empire beset by civil war which he took most of his reign to quell. He was succeeded by [Adad-nirari III](/wiki/Adad-nirari_III) who was merely a boy. The Empire was thus ruled by the famed queen [Semiramis](/wiki/Semiramis) until 806 BC. In that year Adad-nirari III took the reins of power. After his premature death, Assyria failed to expand further during the reigns of [Shalmaneser IV](/wiki/Shalmaneser_IV) (782–773 BC), [Ashur-dan III](/wiki/Ashur-dan_III) (772–755 BC) and [Ashur-nirari V](/wiki/Ashur-nirari_V) (754–746 BC).

Under [Ashurbanipal](/wiki/Ashurbanipal) (669–627 BC) its domination spanned from the [Caucasus Mountains](/wiki/Caucasus_Mountains) in the north to [Nubia](/wiki/Nubia), [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt) and [Arabia](/wiki/Arabian_Peninsula) in the south, and from [Cyprus](/wiki/Cyprus) and [Antioch](/wiki/Antioch) in the west to [Persia](/wiki/Persia) in the east. Ashurbanipal destroyed [Elam](/wiki/Elam) and smashed a rebellion led by his own brother Shamash-shum-ukim who was the Assyrian king of [Babylon](/wiki/Babylon), exacting savage revenge on the [Chaldeans](/wiki/Chaldea), [Nabateans](/wiki/Nabateans), [Arabs](/wiki/Arabs) and [Elamites](/wiki/Elamites) who had supported him. Persia and [Media](/wiki/Median_Empire) were regarded as vassals of Ashurbanipal. He built vast libraries and initiated a surge in the building of temples and palaces.

### Hittites

Main article: [Hittites](/wiki/Hittites)

| [Hittite Empire](/wiki/Hittite_Empire) |
| --- |
| Approximate extent of Hittite rule, c. 1350–1300 BC; [Arzawa](/wiki/Arzawa) and the [Lukka](/wiki/Lukka) are to the west, with [Mitanni](/wiki/Mitanni) to the southeast. |

The [Hittites](/wiki/Hittites) were an ancient people who spoke an [Indo-European language](/wiki/Indo-European_language), and established a kingdom centered at [Hattusa](/wiki/Hattusa) in north-central [Anatolia](/wiki/Anatolia) from the 18th century BC. In the 14th century BC, the Hittite Empire was at its height, encompassing central Anatolia, northwestern [Syria](/wiki/Syria_(region)) as far as [Ugarit](/wiki/Ugarit), and upper [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia). After 1180 BC, the empire disintegrated into several independent "[Neo-Hittite](/wiki/Neo-Hittite)" city-states, some surviving until as late as the 8th century BC.

[Tarḫunna](/wiki/Tar%E1%B8%ABunna), the [storm god](/wiki/Storm_god) and the [god of thunder](/wiki/God_of_thunder) in the [Hittite religion](/wiki/Hittite_religion) (8th century BC)

The Hittites were also famous for their skill in building and using [chariots](/wiki/Chariot#Hittites), as the [Battle of Kadesh](/wiki/Battle_of_Kadesh) demonstrated. The Hittites were pioneers of the [Iron Age](/wiki/Iron_Age), manufacturing [iron](/wiki/Iron) artifacts from as early as the 14th century BC, making them possibly even the first to do so. The Hittites passed much knowledge and lore from the [Ancient Near East](/wiki/Ancient_Near_East) to the newly arrived [Greeks](/wiki/Greeks) in [Europe](/wiki/Europe).

Hittite prosperity was mostly dependent on control of the trade routes and metal sources. Because of the importance of northern Syria to the vital routes linking the [Cilician Gates](/wiki/Cilician_Gates) with Mesopotamia, defense of this area was crucial, and was soon put to the test by Egyptian expansion under Pharaoh Rameses II. The outcome of the Battle of Kadesh is uncertain, though it seems that the timely arrival of Egyptian reinforcements prevented total Hittite victory. The Egyptians forced the Hittites to take refuge in the fortress of [Kadesh](/wiki/Kadesh_(Syria)), but their own losses prevented them from sustaining a siege. This battle took place in the fifth year of Rameses II (c. 1274 BC by the most commonly used chronology).

### Phoenicia

Main article: [Phoenicia](/wiki/Phoenicia)

| [Ancient](/wiki/Ancient) [Phoenicia](/wiki/Phoenicia) |
| --- |
| Phoenicia Territory in the 3rd century BC |

[Phoenicia](/wiki/Phoenicia) was a major power over the Mediterranean between 1200 BC and 539 BC. This ancient [Semitic](/wiki/Semitic_people) [thalassocratic](/wiki/Thalassocracy) civilization was situated on the western, coastal part of the [Fertile Crescent](/wiki/Fertile_Crescent) and centered on the coastline of modern [Lebanon](/wiki/Lebanon). All major Phoenician cities were on the coastline of the Mediterranean, some colonies reaching the Western Mediterranean. It was an enterprising [maritime trading culture](/wiki/Thalassocracy) that spread across the [Mediterranean](/wiki/Mediterranean) from 1550 BC to 300 BC. The Phoenicians used the [galley](/wiki/Galley), a man-powered sailing vessel, and are credited with the invention of the [bireme](/wiki/Bireme). They were famed in Classical Greece and Rome as 'traders in purple', referring to their monopoly on the precious purple dye of the *[Murex](/wiki/Murex)* snail, used, among other things, for royal clothing, and for the spread of their [alphabets](/wiki/Semitic_alphabet), from which almost all modern phonetic alphabets are derived.

### Carthaginian Empire

Main article: [Ancient Carthage](/wiki/Ancient_Carthage)

The **Carthaginian Empire**, also known as the **Carthaginian Republic** (alternatively "**Carthaginian hegemony**", or simply "**Carthage**") was the [Phoenician](/wiki/Phoenicians) [city-state](/wiki/City-state) of [Carthage](/wiki/Carthage) and its sphere of influence, which included much of the coast of [North Africa](/wiki/North_Africa) as well as substantial parts of coastal [Iberia](/wiki/Carthaginian_Iberia) and the islands of the western [Mediterranean](/wiki/Mediterranean) from 814 to 146 BC.

### Ancient Iran

#### Elam

Main article: [Elam](/wiki/Elam)

| Elamite Empire |
| --- |
| Elamite Empire   ---   The approximate [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age) Elamite Empire extension of the [Persian Gulf](/wiki/Persian_Gulf) is shown. |

Elamite worshipper

[Elam](/wiki/Elam) was situated just to the east of [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia) and was one of the oldest recorded [civilizations](/wiki/Civilization). Elam was centered in the far west and southwest of modern-day Iran, stretching from the lowlands of [Khuzestan](/wiki/Khuzestan) and [Ilam Province](/wiki/Ilam_Province) (which takes its name from Elam), as well as a small part of southern Iraq. In the [Old Elamite period](/wiki/Old_Elamite_period) ([Middle Bronze Age](/wiki/Middle_Bronze_Age)), Elam consisted of kingdoms on the [Iranian plateau](/wiki/Iranian_plateau), centered in [Anshan](/wiki/Anshan_(Persia)), and from the mid-2nd millennium BC, it was centered in [Susa](/wiki/Susa) in the Khuzestan lowlands.

Elam was part of the early [cities of the Ancient Near East](/wiki/Cities_of_the_Ancient_Near_East) during the [Chalcolithic](/wiki/Chalcolithic) ([Copper Age](/wiki/Copper_Age)). The emergence of written records from around 3000 BC also parallels Mesopotamian history where writing was used slightly earlier. Elamite strength was based on an ability to hold various areas together under a coordinated government that permitted the maximum interchange of the natural resources unique to each region. Traditionally, this was done through a federated governmental structure.

Elamite culture played a crucial role in the [Persian Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire), especially during the [Achaemenid dynasty](/wiki/Achaemenid_dynasty) that succeeded it, when the [Elamite language](/wiki/Elamite_language) remained among those in official use. The Elamite language is generally treated as a language isolate. As such, the Elamite period is considered a starting point for the [history of Iran](/wiki/History_of_Iran).

#### Medes

Main article: [Median state](/wiki/Median_state)

| [Median Empire](/wiki/Median_Empire) |
| --- |
| A map of the Median Empire at its greatest extent (6th century BC), according to [Herodotus](/wiki/Herodotus) |

The [Median Empire](/wiki/Median_Empire) was the first empire on the territory of Persia. By the 6th century BC, after having together with the [Babylonians](/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_empire) defeated the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire). In Greek references to "Median" people there is no clear distinction between the "Persians" and the "Medians"; in fact for a Greek to become "too closely associated with Iranian culture" was "to become medianized, not persianized". The Median kingdom was a short-lived Iranian state and the textual and archaeological sources of that period are rare and little could be known from the Median culture which nevertheless made a "profound, and lasting, contribution to the greater world of Iranian culture". The Medes were able to establish their own empire, the largest of its day, lasting for about sixty years, from the [sack of Nineveh](/wiki/Battle_of_Nineveh_(612_BC)) in 612 BC until 549 BC when [Cyrus the Great](/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great) established the [Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) by defeating his overlord and grandfather, [Astyages](/wiki/Astyages), king of Media.

#### Achaemenid Empire

550 BC–330 BC

Main article: [Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire)

| [Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) |

The [Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) was the first of the [Persian Empires](/wiki/History_of_Iran) to become a world empire. At the height of its power, the Empire spanned over three continents, namely [Europe](/wiki/Europe), [Asia](/wiki/Asia), and [Africa](/wiki/Africa), and was the most powerful empire of its time. It also eventually, either quickly in its earliest days or steadily over time, incorporated the following territories: in the north and west all of [Asia Minor](/wiki/Asia_Minor) (modern [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey)), parts of the [Balkan Peninsula](/wiki/Balkans) - [Thrace](/wiki/Thracians), [Macedon](/wiki/Macedon) and [Paeonia](/wiki/Paeonia_(kingdom)), and most of the [Black Sea](/wiki/Black_Sea) coastal regions or present day southern and eastern [Bulgaria](/wiki/Bulgaria), northern Greece, and [Macedonia](/wiki/Macedonia_(region)); in the west and southwest the territories of modern [Iraq](/wiki/Iraq), northern [Saudi Arabia](/wiki/Saudi_Arabia), [Jordan](/wiki/Jordan), [Israel](/wiki/Israel_(region)), [Lebanon](/wiki/Lebanon), [Syria](/wiki/Syria), all significant population centers of ancient [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt) and as far west as portions of [Libya](/wiki/Libya); in the east modern [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan) and beyond into central Asia, and parts of [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan).

Achaemenids warriors

Encompassing approximately 5.5 million square kilometers at its height in 500 BC, the Achaemenid Empire was territorially the largest empire of [antiquity](/wiki/Ancient_history). In its time it had political power over neighboring countries, and had high cultural and economic achievements during its lengthy rule over a vast region from its picturesque capital at [Persepolis](/wiki/Persepolis).

[Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) (Alexander III of Macedon) defeated the Persian armies at Granicus (334 BC), followed by Issus (333 BC), and lastly at Gaugamela (331 BC). Afterwards, he marched on Susa and [Persepolis](/wiki/Persepolis) which surrendered in early 330 BC. From Persepolis, Alexander headed north to Pasargadae where he visited the tomb of [Cyrus the Great](/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great).

### [Armenia](/wiki/Armenia)

#### Great Armenia

Main article: [History of Armenia](/wiki/History_of_Armenia)

331 BC–428 AD

| [Kingdom of Armenia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Armenia_(antiquity)) |
| --- |

The [Kingdom of Armenia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Armenia_(antiquity)) was one of the first [Armenian Empires](/wiki/History_of_Armenia). At the height of its power, the Empire spanned over many parts of countries, namely the modern-day countries of [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey), [Iran](/wiki/Iran), [Syria](/wiki/Syria) and [Lebanon](/wiki/Lebanon).
The Kingdom of Armenia has three distinct dynasties - the [Orontid dynasty](/wiki/Orontid_dynasty), the [Artaxiad dynasty](/wiki/Artaxiad_dynasty), and the [Arsacid dynasty of Armenia](/wiki/Arsacid_dynasty_of_Armenia). During the reign of the [Orontid dynasty](/wiki/Orontid_dynasty), Armenia became independent from the [Achaemenids](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire). During the reign of the [Artaxiad dynasty](/wiki/Artaxiad_dynasty), Armenia has reached the peak of its power. During the reign of the [Arsacid dynasty](/wiki/Arsacid_dynasty_of_Armenia), Armenia became the first country in the world to adopt Christianity as an official religion.

In 387, the Kingdom of Armenia was split between the [Eastern Roman Empire](/wiki/Eastern_Roman_Empire) and the Sassanid Empire. Western Armenia first became a [province](/wiki/Roman_province) of the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire) under the name of [Armenia Minor](/wiki/Armenia_Minor), and later [Byzantine Armenia](/wiki/Byzantine_Armenia); [Eastern Armenia](/wiki/Eastern_Armenia) remained a kingdom within Persia until, in 428, the local [nobility](/wiki/Nobility) overthrew the king, and the Sassanids installed a *[marzban](/wiki/Marzban)* ([governor](/wiki/Governor)) in his place, beginning the [Marzpanate](/wiki/Marzpanate_Armenia) period over [Persian Armenia](/wiki/Sasanian_Armenia).

### [Iran](/wiki/Iran)

#### Parthian Empire

Main article: [Parthian Empire](/wiki/Parthian_Empire)

| [Parthian Empire](/wiki/Parthian_Empire) |
| --- |
| The Parthian empire at its greatest extent |

The [Parthian Empire](/wiki/Parthian_Empire) was the third [Iranian](/wiki/Iran) Empire. At the height of its power, the empire ruled most of [Greater Iran](/wiki/Greater_Iran), [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia) and [Armenia](/wiki/Armenia). But unlike most other Iranian monarchies, the Parthian followed a vassal system, which they adopted from the [Seleucids](/wiki/Seleucids). The [Arsacid](/wiki/List_of_Parthian_kings) culture was not a single coherent state, but instead made up of numerous tributary (but otherwise independent) kingdoms.

The Parthians largely adopted the art, architecture, religious beliefs, and royal insignia of their culturally heterogeneous empire, which encompassed Persian, Hellenistic, and regional cultures. For about the first half of its existence, the Arsacid court adopted elements of Greek culture, though it eventually saw a gradual revival of Iranian traditions. The Arsacid rulers were titled the 'King of Kings', as a claim to be the heirs to the Achaemenid Empire; indeed, they accepted many local kings as vassals where the Achaemenids would have had centrally appointed, albeit largely autonomous, satraps. The court did appoint a small number of satraps, largely outside Iran, but these satrapies were smaller and less powerful than the Achaemenid potentates. With the expansion of Arsacid power, the seat of central government shifted from Nisa, Turkmenistan to [Ctesiphon](/wiki/Ctesiphon) along the Tigris (south of modern Baghdad, Iraq), although several other sites also served as capitals. Although the Parthian Arsacids made way for a new Iranian dynasty, the Arsacid family continued to exist through the [Arsacid dynasty of Armenia](/wiki/Arsacid_dynasty_of_Armenia), the [Arsacid dynasty of Iberia](/wiki/Arsacid_dynasty_of_Iberia), and the [Arsacid Dynasty of Caucasian Albania](/wiki/Arsacid_Dynasty_of_Caucasian_Albania); all eponymous branches of the Parthian Arsacids.

#### Sasanian Empire

Main article: [Sasanian Empire](/wiki/Sasanian_Empire)

| [Sassanid Empire](/wiki/Sassanid_Empire) |
| --- |
| The Sassanid Empire around its greatest extent |

The [Sasanian Empire](/wiki/Sasanian_Empire) is the name used for the fourth Iranian dynasty, and the second [Persian Empire](/wiki/History_of_Iran) (226–651). The empire's territory encompassed all of today's [Iran](/wiki/Iran), [Iraq](/wiki/Iraq), [Armenia](/wiki/Armenia), [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan), eastern parts of [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey), and parts of [Syria](/wiki/Syria), [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan), and large parts of [Caucasia](/wiki/Caucasus), [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia) and [Arabia](/wiki/Arabia). During [Khosrow II](/wiki/Khosrow_II)'s rule in 590–628 [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt), [Jordan](/wiki/Jordan), [Israel](/wiki/Israel_(region)), [Lebanon](/wiki/Lebanon) were also briefly annexed to the Empire, as well as far west as western [Asia Minor](/wiki/Asia_Minor). The Sassanid era, encompassing the length of the [Late Antiquity](/wiki/Late_Antiquity) period, is considered to be one of the most important and influential historical periods in Iran. In many ways the Sassanid period witnessed the highest achievement of [Persian civilization](/wiki/Persian_culture). The empire is furthermore known for being the arch-rival of the neighboring [Roman](/wiki/Roman_Empire)–[Byzantine Empire](/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) for a period of over 400 years. As the Parthians were replaced by the Sassanids, they carried on the already century long lasting [Roman–Persian Wars](/wiki/Roman%E2%80%93Persian_Wars), which would eventually become the longest conflict in human history.

The empire constituted the last great Iranian Empire before the [Muslim conquest](/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia) and adoption of [Islam](/wiki/Islam). The climactic [Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628](/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_602%E2%80%93628) had drastically exhausted both the Byzantines as well as the Sassanids, laying the way open for an easy conquest. The Sassanids, heavily weakened, never mounted a truly effective resistance to the pressure applied by the initial Arab armies. Ctesiphon fell after a prolonged siege. Yazdegerd fled eastward from Ctesiphon, leaving behind him most of the Empire's vast treasury. The Arabs captured Ctesiphon shortly afterward, leaving the Sassanid government strapped for funds and acquiring a powerful financial resource for their own use. A number of Sassanid governors attempted to combine their forces to throw back the invaders, but the effort was crippled by the lack of a strong central authority, and the governors were defeated at the Battle of Nihawānd. The empire, with its military command structure non-existent, its non-noble troop levies decimated, its financial resources effectively destroyed, and the Asawaran knightly caste destroyed piecemeal, was utterly helpless in the face of the invaders. Upon hearing the defeat, Persian nobilities fled further inland to the eastern province of [Khorasan](/wiki/Greater_Khorasan).

### Ancient Egypt

Main article: [Ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt)

| [Ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) |
| --- |
| Major ancient Egyptian cities during the Dynastic period |

Ancient Egypt was one of the world's first civilizations, with its beginnings in the fertile Nile valley around 3150 BC. [Ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) reached the zenith of its power during the [New Kingdom](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt) (1570–1070 BC) under great pharaohs. Ancient Egypt was a great power to be contended with by both the ancient Near East, the Mediterranean and sub-Saharan Africa. The empire expanded far south into [Nubia](/wiki/Nubia) and held wide territories across the ancient [Near East](/wiki/Near_East). The combination of a fertile river valley, natural borders that made an invasion unfeasible, and a [military](/wiki/Military_history_of_Ancient_Egypt) able to [rise to the challenge when needed](/wiki/Battle_of_the_Delta), turned Egypt into a major power.

Drawing of Akhnaton Cairo Cast

It was one of the first nations to have a system of writing and large scale construction projects. However, as neighboring civilizations developed militaries capable of crossing Egypt's natural barriers, the Egyptian armies were not always able to repel them and so by 1000 BC Egyptian influence as an independent civilization waned.

#### Early Dynastic Period

Main article: [Early Dynastic Period (Egypt)](/wiki/Early_Dynastic_Period_(Egypt))

The Archaic or Early Dynastic Period of [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt) is the era immediately following the unification of [Upper and Lower Egypt](/wiki/Upper_and_Lower_Egypt) *c.* 3100 BC. It is generally taken to include the [First](/wiki/First_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and [Second Dynasties](/wiki/Second_Dynasty_of_Egypt), lasting from the end of the [Naqada III](/wiki/Naqada_III) archaeological period until about 2686 BC, or the beginning of the [Old Kingdom](/wiki/Old_Kingdom_of_Egypt). With the [First Dynasty](/wiki/First_Dynasty_of_Egypt), the capital moved from [Thinis](/wiki/Thinis) to [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt) with a unified Egypt ruled by an Egyptian [god-king](/wiki/Imperial_cult). [Abydos](/wiki/Abydos,_Egypt) remained the major holy land in the south. The hallmarks of [ancient Egyptian](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) civilization, such as [art](/wiki/Art_of_ancient_Egypt), [architecture](/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_architecture) and many aspects of [religion](/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion), took shape during the Early Dynastic period. During the Early Dynastic period, the pharaohs established the earliest Central Government in the world.

#### Old Kingdom

Main article: [Old Kingdom of Egypt](/wiki/Old_Kingdom_of_Egypt)

The Old Kingdom is the period spanning c. 2686–2181 BC. It is also known as the "Age of the Pyramids" or the "Age of the Pyramid Builders", as it encompasses the reigns of the great pyramid builders of the [Fourth Dynasty](/wiki/Fourth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)—King [Sneferu](/wiki/Sneferu) perfected the art of [pyramid](/wiki/Pyramid)-building and the [pyramids of Giza](/wiki/Pyramids_of_Giza) were constructed under the kings [Khufu](/wiki/Khufu), [Khafre](/wiki/Khafra) and [Menkaure](/wiki/Menkaure). [Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) attained its first sustained peak of civilization—the first of three so-called "Kingdom" [periods](/wiki/Egyptian_chronology) (followed by the [Middle Kingdom](/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt) and [New Kingdom](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt)) which mark the high points of civilization in the lower [Nile Valley](/wiki/Nile_Valley).

The [Pyramid of Djoser](/wiki/Pyramid_of_Djoser) at [Saqqara](/wiki/Saqqara)

During the Old Kingdom, the pyramid building in ancient Egypt began during the [Third Dynasty](/wiki/Third_Dynasty_of_Egypt) under the rule of king [Djoser](/wiki/Djoser) when he built the [Step Pyramid of Djoser](/wiki/Step_Pyramid_of_Djoser) (Egypt's first Pyramid) and peaked during [Fourth Dynasty](/wiki/Fourth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) during the construction of the [Giza Pyramids](/wiki/Giza_Pyramids).

The [Great Sphinx of Giza](/wiki/Great_Sphinx_of_Giza) in front of the [Great Pyramid](/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza) of [Giza](/wiki/Giza)

During the [Fifth Dynasty](/wiki/Fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) the pyramid building declined in Egypt. The [Pyramid Texts](/wiki/Pyramid_Texts), the oldest ritual texts from ancient Egypt first appeared in the late fifth Dynasty.

#### Middle Kingdom

Main article: [Middle Kingdom of Egypt](/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt)

The Middle Kingdom of Egypt (also known as "The Period of Reunification") is the period in the history of [ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) following a period of political division known as the [First Intermediate Period](/wiki/First_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt). The Middle Kingdom lasted from around 2050 BC to around 1710 BC, stretching from the reunification of Egypt under the reign of [Mentuhotep II](/wiki/Mentuhotep_II) of the [Eleventh Dynasty](/wiki/Eleventh_Dynasty_of_Egypt) to the end of the [Twelfth Dynasty](/wiki/Twelfth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). The Eleventh Dynasty ruled from Thebes and the Twelfth Dynasty ruled from el-Lisht.

The middle kingdom reached its peak under the pharaohs [Senusret III](/wiki/Senusret_III) and [Amenemhat III](/wiki/Amenemhat_III). [Senusret III](/wiki/Senusret_III) was a great pharaoh of the Twelfth Dynasty and is considered to be the greatest pharaoh in the middle kingdom. However, The reign of [Amenemhat](/wiki/Amenemhat_III) III was the height of Middle Kingdom economic prosperity. His reign is remarkable for the degree to which Egypt exploited its resources.

#### New Kingdom

Main article: [New Kingdom of Egypt](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt)

The maximum territorial extent of Ancient Egypt (15th century BC)

The [New Kingdom](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt) began around 1550 B.C when king [Ahmose I](/wiki/Ahmose_I) became the king of Egypt, defeated the [Hyksos](/wiki/Hyksos) and reunified Egypt. The pharaohs of the new kingdom established a period of unprecedented prosperity by securing their borders and strengthening diplomatic ties with their neighbors. Military campaigns waged under [Tuthmosis I](/wiki/Tuthmosis_I) and his grandson [Tuthmosis III](/wiki/Tuthmosis_III) extended the influence of the pharaohs to the largest empire Egypt had ever seen. [Tuthmosis III](/wiki/Tuthmosis_III) is recorded to have captured 350 cities during his rule and conquered much of the Near East from the Euphrates to Nubia during seventeen known military campaigns. During the reign of [Amenhotep III](/wiki/Amenhotep_III), Egypt entered a period of unprecedented prosperity and artistic splendour, Egypt reached the peak of its artistic and international power in his reign. [Amenhotep IV](/wiki/Amenhotep_IV) ascended the throne and instituted a series of radical and chaotic reforms. Changing his name to [Akhenaten](/wiki/Akhenaten), he touted the god [Aten](/wiki/Aten) as the [supreme deity](/wiki/Deity), suppressed the worship of other deities, and attacked the power of the priestly establishment. Moving the capital to the new city of Akhetaten, he turned a deaf ear to foreign affairs and absorbed himself in his new religion and artistic style. After his death, the religion of the Aten was quickly abandoned, and the subsequent pharaohs erased all mention of Akhenaten's Egyptian heresy, now known as the [Amarna Period](/wiki/Amarna_Period).

Ramesses II's [sea peoples](/wiki/Sea_peoples) of the [Great Karnak Inscription](/wiki/Great_Karnak_Inscription)

[Ramesses the Great](/wiki/Ramesses_II) ascended the throne, and went on to build more temples, erect more statues and obelisks, and sire more children than any other pharaoh in history. One of the greatest construction projects conducted by Ramesses was the city of [Pi-Ramesses](/wiki/Pi-Ramesses). The city covered an area of 18 km2 (as big as [Rome](/wiki/Rome)). At its peak, The city was home to a population of 160,000-300,000. This would make Pi-Ramesses 2-4 times bigger than [Yinxu](/wiki/Yinxu) (Second largest city at that time). Ramesses led his army against the Hittites in the Battle of Kadesh and, after fighting to a stalemate, finally agreed to the first recorded peace treaty. Egypt's wealth, however, made it a tempting target for invasion, particularly by the Libyans and the Sea Peoples. Initially, the military was able to repel these invasions during the reign of [Ramesses III](/wiki/Ramesses_III), but Egypt eventually lost control of Syria and Palestine. The impact of external threats was exacerbated by internal problems such as corruption, tomb robbery and civil unrest. The high priests at the temple of Amun in Thebes accumulated vast tracts of land and wealth, and their growing power splintered the country during the [Third Intermediate Period](/wiki/Third_Intermediate_Period).

#### Third Intermediate Period

Main article: [Third Intermediate Period of Egypt](/wiki/Third_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt)

The Third Intermediate Period of Ancient Egypt began with the death of Pharaoh [Ramesses XI](/wiki/Ramesses_XI) in 1070 BC, ending the New Kingdom, and was eventually followed by the Late Period. Various points are offered as the beginning for the latter era, though it is most often regarded as dating from the foundation of the [Twenty-Sixth Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-Sixth_Dynasty) by [Psamtik I](/wiki/Psamtik_I) in 664 BC.

The first Dynasty of the Ancient Egyptian [Third Intermediate Period](/wiki/Third_Intermediate_Period) is the [Twenty-first Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-first_Dynasty_of_Egypt). Its first ruler is king [Smendes](/wiki/Smendes) who ruled only in [Lower Egypt](/wiki/Lower_Egypt). The most powerful pharaohs of the Twenty-first dynasty were [psusennes I](/wiki/Psusennes_I) and [Siamun](/wiki/Siamun) who built extensively compared to the other pharaohs of the dynasty. The Pharaohs of the Twenty-first Dynasty transported all the old Ramesside temples, obelisks, stelae, statues and sphinxes from [Pi-Ramesses](/wiki/Pi-Ramesses) to the new capital [Tanis](/wiki/Tanis). The obelisks and statues, the largest weighing over 200 tons, were transported in one piece while major buildings were dismantled into sections and reassembled at Tanis.

The country was firmly reunited by the Twenty-Second Dynasty founded by [Shoshenq I](/wiki/Shoshenq_I) in 945 BC (or 943 BC), who descended from Meshwesh immigrants, originally from Ancient Libya. Upon unifying Egypt, king [Shoshenq I](/wiki/Shoshenq_I) started campaigning in the [Levante](/wiki/Levant) . This brought stability to the country for well over a century and made Egypt a superpower again, but after the reign of [Osorkon II](/wiki/Osorkon_II), particularly, the country had effectively split into two states, with Shoshenq III of the Twenty-Second Dynasty controlling Lower Egypt by 818 BC while [Takelot II](/wiki/Takelot_II) and his son Osorkon (the future [Osorkon III](/wiki/Osorkon_III)) ruled Middle and Upper Egypt.

#### Late Period

Main article: [Late Period of ancient Egypt](/wiki/Late_Period_of_ancient_Egypt)

The Late Period of ancient Egypt refers to the last flowering of native Egyptian rulers after the Third Intermediate Period beginning with the 26th Saite Dynasty founded by [Psamtik I](/wiki/Psamtik_I). The 26th Dynasty of Egypt managed to regain Egypt's power for a short time before the [Achaemenid conquest of Egypt](/wiki/First_Achaemenid_conquest_of_Egypt) lad by [Cambyses II](/wiki/Cambyses_II) in 525 BC.

However, the Egyptians managed to gain independence from the Persians during a rebellion led by the rebel Pharaoh [Amyrtaeus](/wiki/Amyrtaeus) around 404 B.C who established the [Twenty-eighth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Twenty-eighth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). Egypt remained independent during the [Twenty-ninth Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-ninth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and [Thirtieth Dynasty](/wiki/Thirtieth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) until the Persians invaded it again in 343 B.C. During that time, the Egyptians managed to repel several attacks from the [Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire). The most famous attack of them occurred in around 351 BC, when [Artaxerxes III](/wiki/Artaxerxes_III) embarked on a campaign to recover Egypt, which had revolted under his father, [Artaxerxes II](/wiki/Artaxerxes_II). Levying a vast army, Artaxerxes marched into Egypt, and engaged [Nectanebo II](/wiki/Nectanebo_II) the founder of the [Thirtieth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Thirtieth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). After a year of fighting the Egyptian Pharaoh, Nectanebo inflicted a crushing defeat on the Persians with the support of mercenaries led by the Greek generals Diophantus and Lamius. Artaxerxes [successfully reconquered Egypt](/wiki/Second_Achaemenid_conquest_of_Egypt) in 340 or 339 B.C.

The Late period of ancient Egypt ends when [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) took Egypt from the Persians without war around 332 B.C.

#### Legacy

The many achievements of the ancient Egyptians include the quarrying, surveying and construction techniques that facilitated the building of monumental pyramids, temples, and obelisks; a system of mathematics, a practical and effective system of medicine, irrigation systems and agricultural production techniques, the first known ships, Egyptian faience and glass technology, new forms of literature, and the earliest known peace treaty. Egypt left a lasting legacy. Its art and architecture were widely copied, and its antiquities carried off to far corners of the world. Its monumental ruins have inspired the imaginations of travellers and writers for centuries. A new-found respect for antiquities and excavations in the early modern period led to the scientific investigation of Egyptian civilization and a greater appreciation of its cultural legacy, for Egypt and the world.

### Kerma

Main article: [Kerma culture](/wiki/Kerma_culture)

The Kerma culture was an early civilization which flourished from around 2500 BC to about 1600 BC in Nubia, present day Sudan, centered at Kerma. It seems to have been one of a number of Sudanese states during the Middle Kingdom period of Ancient Egypt. In its latest phase, lasting from about 1700–1500 BC, it absorbed the Sudanese kingdom of Sai and became a sizable, populous empire rivaling Egypt. Around 1500 BC, it was absorbed into the Egyptian Empire, but rebellions continued for centuries. By the 11th century BC, the more 'Egyptianized' Kingdom of Kush emerged, apparently from Kerma, and regained the region's independence from Egypt.

### Kush

Main article: [Kingdom of Kush](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush)

| [Kingdom of Kush](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush) |
| --- |
| The maximum territorial extent of Kush circa 700 BC |

The Kingdom of Kush was the earliest of the Subsaharan states in Africa as well as the first to implement iron weapons. It was heavily influenced by Egyptian colonists, but in 1070 BC it became not only independent of Egypt but a fierce rival. It successfully fought off attempts by Egypt to reconquer it, and it began to extend influence over Upper Egypt. By the end of King [Kashta's](/wiki/Kashta) reign in 752 BC, Thebes was under Kushite control.

A slew of able successors took the rest of Egypt and reigned as the [Twenty-fifth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Twenty-fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) stretching Kushite control from central Sudan to modern-day Israel. The Kushites did not maintain this empire for long and were beaten back by the Assyrians in 653 BC. However, Kush remained a powerful entity in the region. It continued to meddle in Egyptian affairs and control trade resources originating in Subsaharan Africa. It waged a hard-fought campaign against the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire) (27 BC - 22 BC) under the leadership of Queen [Amanirenas](/wiki/Amanirenas), and achieved a more than amicable peace with the young [Augustus Caesar](/wiki/Augustus_Caesar). The two states worked as allies, with Kush lending cavalry support to Rome in its conquest of Jerusalem in 70 AD.

The kingdom of Kush maintained its status as a regional power until its conquest by the [Aksumite Empire](/wiki/Aksumite_Empire) in 350.

### Macrobia

Reconstruction of the [Oikumene](/wiki/Oikumene) (inhabited world) ancient map from Herodotus, c. 450 BC

The [Macrobians](/wiki/Macrobians) were an ancient people and kingdom situated in the [Horn of Africa](/wiki/Horn_of_Africa) (Somalia) around the 1st millennium BC. According to Herodotus, the Macrobians practiced an elaborate form of [embalming](/wiki/Embalming). This, in turn, suggested a knowledge on their part of [anatomy](/wiki/Anatomy) and, at the very least, a grasp of the basics of [chemistry](/wiki/Chemistry). The Macrobians preserved the bodies of the dead by first extracting moisture from the corpses, then overlaying the bodies with a type of [plaster](/wiki/Plaster), and finally decorating the exterior in vivid colors in order to imitate the deceased as realistically as possible. They then placed the body in a hollow [crystal](/wiki/Crystal) pillar, which they kept in their homes for a period of about a year. Macrobia was also noted for its [gold](/wiki/Gold), which was so plentiful that the Macrobians shackled their prisoners in golden chains.

The Ancient city-states located in northern [Somalia](/wiki/Somalia), had a steady trade link with the [Ancient Egyptians](/wiki/Ancient_Egyptians) and exported precious natural resources such as [myrrh](/wiki/Myrrh), [frankincense](/wiki/Frankincense) and [gum](/wiki/Natural_gum). This trade network continued all the way into the [classical era](/wiki/Classical_era). The city states of [Mossylon](/wiki/Bosaso), [Malao](/wiki/Berbera), [Mundus](/wiki/Zeila) and [Tabae](/wiki/Tabae) in Somalia engaged in a lucrative trade network connecting Somali merchants with [Phoenicia](/wiki/Phoenicia), [Ptolemaic Egypt](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom), [Greece](/wiki/Greece), [Parthian Persia](/wiki/Parthia), [Saba](/wiki/Sabaeans), [Nabataea](/wiki/Nabataea) and the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire). Somali sailors used the ancient Somali maritime vessel known as the '[beden](/wiki/Beden)' to transport their cargo.

### Kingdom of Aksum

Main article: [Kingdom of Aksum](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum)

Aksumite Empire based in Ethiopia

The [Kingdom of Aksum](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum) was an important trading nation originating from Northern Ethiopia in northeastern [Africa](/wiki/Africa), growing from the proto-Aksumite period ca. 4th century BC to achieve prominence by the 1st century AD. It was a major player in the commerce between the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire) and [Ancient India](/wiki/History_of_India) and the Aksumite rulers facilitated trade by minting their own [currency](/wiki/Aksumite_currency). The state established its [hegemony](/wiki/Hegemony) over the declining [Kingdom of Kush](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush) and regularly entered the politics of the kingdoms on the [Arabian peninsula](/wiki/Arabian_peninsula), and would eventually extend its rule over the region with the conquest of the [Himyarite Kingdom](/wiki/Himyarite_Kingdom). At its peak it controlled Much of [Ethiopia](/wiki/Ethiopia), [Eritrea](/wiki/Eritrea), [Djibouti](/wiki/Djibouti), [Sudan](/wiki/Sudan), [Somalia](/wiki/Somalia), [Yemen](/wiki/Yemen), and [Saudi Arabia](/wiki/Saudi_Arabia). It was considered by historians as one of the most powerful military powers in the world.

## Ancient India

Main article: [History of India](/wiki/History_of_India)

Several Indian empires including [Maurya Empire](/wiki/Maurya_Empire) and [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire) were able to expand across southern Asia, incorporating much of the region, as well as beyond. The subcontinent was conquered by the Mauryan Empire during the 4th and 3rd centuries BC. Various parts of India was ruled by numerous Middle kingdoms for the next 1,500 years, among which the Gupta Empire united India in the 4th and 5th century CE . Southern India saw the rule of the [Chalukyas](/wiki/Chalukyas), [Cholas](/wiki/Cholas), [Pallavas](/wiki/Pallavas), [Pandyas](/wiki/Pandyas) and [Cheras](/wiki/Cheras). This period, witnessing a [Hindu](/wiki/Hindu) religious and intellectual resurgence, is known as the classical or "Golden Age of India". During this period, aspects of Indian civilisation, administration, culture, and religion (Hinduism and Buddhism) spread to much of Asia, while kingdoms in Southern India had maritime business links with the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire) from around 77 AD.

### Maurya Empire

Main article: [Maurya Empire](/wiki/Maurya_Empire)

| [Maurya Empire](/wiki/Maurya_Empire) |
| --- |
| [Maurya Empire](/wiki/Maurya_Empire) at its greatest extent under [Ashoka the Great](/wiki/Ashoka_the_Great), conceptualized as a network of core regions connected by networks of communication and trade, with large areas with peripheral or no Maurya control. |

The [Mauryan Empire](/wiki/Mauryan_Empire) was the first political entity to unite India and expand into Central Asia. Its cultural influence also extended west into [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt) and [Syria](/wiki/Syria), and east into [Thailand](/wiki/Thailand), [China](/wiki/China) and [Burma](/wiki/Burma).

The Empire was founded in 322 BC by [Chandragupta Maurya](/wiki/Chandragupta_Maurya). Chandragupta waged a war against the nearby [Greek](/wiki/Greeks) powers and won, forcing the Greeks to surrender large amounts of land. Under the reign of [Ashoka the Great](/wiki/Ashoka_the_Great), the empire turned to spreading its soft power in the form of [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism).

Spread of [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism) to [the West](/wiki/Western_world) during the reign of Maurya Empire

The Empire was divided into four provinces, which one of the four, look like a giant crescents. with the imperial capital at [Pataliputra](/wiki/Pataliputra). From Ashokan edicts, the names of the four provincial capitals are [Tosali](/wiki/Tosali) (in the east), [Ujjain](/wiki/Ujjain) in the west, [Suvarnagiri](/wiki/Suvarnagiri) (in the south), and [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila) (in the north). The head of the provincial administration was the *Kumara* (royal prince), who governed the provinces as king's representative. The *kumara* was assisted by Mahamatyas and council of ministers. This organizational structure was reflected at the imperial level with the Emperor and his *Mantriparishad* (Council of Ministers).

[Chanakya](/wiki/Chanakya), semi-legendary adviser and prime minister of [Chandragupta Maurya](/wiki/Chandragupta_Maurya)

Historians theorize that the organization of the Empire was in line with the extensive bureaucracy described by [Kautilya](/wiki/Kautilya) in the [Arthashastra](/wiki/Arthashastra): a sophisticated civil service governed everything from municipal hygiene to international trade. According to [Megasthenes](/wiki/Megasthenes), the empire wielded a military of 600,000 infantry, 30,000 cavalry, and 9,000 [war elephants](/wiki/War_elephant). A vast [espionage](/wiki/Espionage) system collected intelligence for both internal and external security purposes. Having renounced offensive warfare and expansionism, Ashoka nevertheless continued to maintain this large army, to protect the Empire and instill stability and peace across South Asia.

### Gupta Empire

Main article: [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire)

See also: [Origin of the Gupta dynasty](/wiki/Origin_of_the_Gupta_dynasty)

Gupta Empire and its tributaries

In the 4th and 5th centuries, the [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire) unified India. This period is called the [Golden Age of India](/wiki/Golden_Age_of_India) and was marked by extensive achievements in [science, technology](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_ancient_India), [engineering](/wiki/List_of_Indian_inventions_and_discoveries), [art](/wiki/Indian_art), [literature](/wiki/Indian_literature), [logic](/wiki/Indian_logic), [mathematics](/wiki/Indian_mathematics), [astronomy](/wiki/Indian_astronomy), [religion](/wiki/Indian_religion) and [philosophy](/wiki/Indian_philosophy) that crystallized the elements of what is generally known as Indian culture. [Chandragupta I](/wiki/Chandragupta_I), [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta), and [Chandragupta II](/wiki/Chandragupta_II) were the most notable rulers of the Gupta dynasty.

The high points of this cultural creativity are magnificent architectures, sculptures and paintings. The Gupta period produced scholars such as [Kalidasa](/wiki/Kalidasa), [Aryabhata](/wiki/Aryabhata), [Varahamihira](/wiki/Varahamihira), [Vishnu Sharma](/wiki/Vishnu_Sharma), and [Vatsyayana](/wiki/Vatsyayana) who made great advancements in many academic fields. Science and political administration reached new heights during the Gupta era. Strong trade ties also made the region an important cultural center and set the region up as a base that would influence nearby kingdoms and regions in [Burma](/wiki/Burma), [Sri Lanka](/wiki/Sri_Lanka), [Indonesian Archipelago](/wiki/Indonesian_Archipelago) and [Indochina](/wiki/Indochina).

## Ancient China

Main article: [History of China](/wiki/History_of_China)

### Shang dynasty

Main article: [Shang dynasty](/wiki/Shang_dynasty)

The **Shang dynasty** ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): [商](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%95%86)[朝](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9C%9D); [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Shāng cháo*) or **Yin dynasty** (Chinese: [殷](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%AE%B7)[代](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BB%A3); pinyin: *Yīn dài*), according to [traditional historiography](/wiki/Chinese_historiography), ruled in the [Yellow River](/wiki/Yellow_River) valley in the second millennium BC, succeeding the [Xia dynasty](/wiki/Xia_dynasty) and followed by the [Zhou dynasty](/wiki/Zhou_dynasty). The classic account of the Shang comes from texts such as the *[Classic of History](/wiki/Classic_of_History)*, *[Bamboo Annals](/wiki/Bamboo_Annals)* and *[Records of the Grand Historian](/wiki/Records_of_the_Grand_Historian)*. According to the traditional chronology based upon calculations made approximately 2,000 years ago by [Liu Xin](/wiki/Liu_Xin_(scholar)), the Shang ruled from 1766 BC to 1122 BC, but according to the chronology based upon the "current text" of *Bamboo Annals*, they ruled from 1556 BC to 1046 BC. The [Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project](/wiki/Xia%E2%80%93Shang%E2%80%93Zhou_Chronology_Project) dated them from c. 1600 BC to 1046 BC. At its peak 1122 BC it covered an area of 1,250,000 km2.

### Zhou dynasty

Main article: [Zhou dynasty](/wiki/Zhou_dynasty)

The **Zhou dynasty** (c. 1046–256 BC; [Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 周朝; [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Zhōu Cháo*; [Wade–Giles](/wiki/Wade%E2%80%93Giles): *Chou1 Ch'ao2* [[tʂóʊ tʂʰɑ̌ʊ]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Mandarin)) was a [Chinese dynasty](/wiki/Dynasties_in_Chinese_history) that followed the [Shang dynasty](/wiki/Shang_dynasty) and preceded the [Qin dynasty](/wiki/Qin_dynasty). Although the Zhou dynasty lasted longer than any other dynasty in [Chinese history](/wiki/History_of_China), the actual political and military control of [China](/wiki/China) by the dynasty, surnamed [Ji](/wiki/J%C4%AB_(surname)) ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 姬), lasted only until 771 BC, a period known as the [Western Zhou](/wiki/Western_Zhou).

This period of Chinese history produced what many consider the zenith of Chinese [bronze](/wiki/Bronze)-ware making. The dynasty also spans the period in which the written script evolved into its modern form with the use of an archaic clerical script that emerged during the late [Warring States period](/wiki/Warring_States_period).

### Qin dynasty

Main article: [Qin dynasty](/wiki/Qin_dynasty)

| Qin Dynasty |
| --- |
| Qin Empire in 210 BC   ---     Qin region    Outlying regions |

The Qin dynasty was preceded by the [feudal](/wiki/Feudal) [Zhou dynasty](/wiki/Zhou_dynasty) and followed by the [Han dynasty](/wiki/Han_dynasty) in China. The unification of China in 221 BC under the [First Emperor Qin Shi Huang](/wiki/Qin_Shi_Huang) marked the beginning of Imperial China, a period which lasted until the fall of the [Qing dynasty](/wiki/Qing_dynasty) in 1912.

[Epang Palace](/wiki/Epang_Palace)

In 214 BC Qin Shihuang secured his boundaries to the north with a fraction (100,000 men) of his large army, and sent the majority (500,000 men) south to seize still more land. Prior to the events leading to Qin dominance over China, they had gained possession of much of Sichuan to the southwest. The Qin army was unfamiliar with the jungle terrain, and it was defeated by the southern tribes' guerrilla warfare tactics with over 100,000 men lost. However, in the defeat Qin was successful in building a canal to the south, which they used heavily for supplying and reinforcing their troops during their second attack to the south. Building on these gains, the Qin armies conquered the coastal lands surrounding Guangzhou, and took the provinces of Fuzhou and Guilin. They struck as far south as Hanoi. After these victories in the south, Qin Shihuang moved over 100,000 prisoners and exiles to colonize the newly conquered area. In terms of extending the boundaries of his empire, the First Emperor was extremely successful in the south.

Despite its military strength, the Qin dynasty did not last long. When the first emperor died in 210 BC, his son was placed on the throne by two of the previous emperor's advisers, in an attempt to influence and control the administration of the entire country through him. The advisors squabbled among themselves, however, which resulted in both their deaths and that of the second Qin emperor. Popular revolt broke out a few years later, and the weakened empire soon fell to a Chu lieutenant, who went on to found the Han dynasty. Despite its rapid end, the Qin dynasty influenced future Chinese regimes, particularly the Han, and the European name for China is derived from it.

### Han dynasty

Main article: [Han dynasty](/wiki/Han_dynasty)

| [Han Dynasty](/wiki/Han_Dynasty) |
| --- |
| Han Empire in 87 BC   ---     Han region    Outlying regions |

The Han dynasty (202 BC – AD 220), lasting 400 years, is commonly considered within China to be one of the greatest periods in the [history of China](/wiki/History_of_China). At its height, the Han empire extended over a vast territory of 6 million km2 and housed a population of approximately 55 million. During this time period, [China](/wiki/China) became a military, economic, and cultural powerhouse. The empire extended its political and [cultural influence](/wiki/Chinese_culture) over [Korea](/wiki/Korea), [Japan](/wiki/Japan), [Mongolia](/wiki/Mongolia), [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam), and [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia) before it finally collapsed under a combination of domestic and external pressures.

Servant (Shinü Yong). Terra Cotta statue.

The Han Empire was divided into areas directly controlled by the central government, known as commanderies, and a number of semi-autonomous kingdoms. These kingdoms gradually lost all vestiges of their independence, particularly following the [Rebellion of the Seven States](/wiki/Rebellion_of_the_Seven_States). The [Xiongnu](/wiki/Xiongnu), a nomadic confederation which dominated the eastern Eurasian Steppe, defeated the Han army in battle in 200 BC. Following the defeat, a political marriage alliance was negotiated in which the Han became the de facto inferior partner. When, despite the treaty, the Xiongnu continued to raid Han borders, [Emperor Wu of Han](/wiki/Emperor_Wu_of_Han) (r. 141–87 BC) launched several military campaigns against them. The ultimate Han victory in these wars eventually forced the Xiongnu to accept vassal status as Han tributaries. These campaigns expanded Han sovereignty into the Tarim Basin of Central Asia and helped establish the vast trade network known as the [Silk Road](/wiki/Silk_Road), which reached as far as the Mediterranean world. Han forces managed to divide the Xiongnu into two competing nations, the Southern and Northern Xiongnu, and forced the Northern Xiongnu across the Ili River. Despite these victories, the territories north of Han's borders were quickly overrun by the nomadic [Xianbei confederation](/wiki/Xianbei).

### Jin dynasty

Main article: [Jin dynasty (266–420)](/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(266%E2%80%93420))

The Jin dynasty ([simplified Chinese](/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters): [晋](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%99%8B)[朝](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9C%9D); [traditional Chinese](/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters): [晉](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%99%89)[朝](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9C%9D); [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Jìn Cháo*; [Wade–Giles](/wiki/Wade%E2%80%93Giles): *Chin⁴-ch'ao²*, IPA: [[tɕîn tʂʰɑ̌ʊ]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Mandarin);), was a dynasty in Chinese history, lasting between the years 265 and 420 [AD](/wiki/Common_Era). There are two main divisions in the history of the dynasty, the first being [Western Jin](/wiki/Western_Jin) (西晉, 265–316) and the second [Eastern Jin](/wiki/Eastern_Jin) (東晉, 317–420). Western Jin was founded by [Sima Yan](/wiki/Emperor_Wu_of_Jin), with its capital at [Luoyang](/wiki/Luoyang), while Eastern Jin was begun by [Sima Rui](/wiki/Emperor_Yuan_of_Jin), with its capital at [Jiankang](/wiki/Jiankang). The two periods are also known as *Liang Jin* (兩晉; literally: two Jin) and *Sima Jin* (司馬晉) by scholars, to distinguish this dynasty from other dynasties that use the same Chinese character, such as the [Later Jin dynasty](/wiki/Later_Jin_(Five_Dynasties)) (後晉).

## Ancient Europe

### Ancient Greece

Main article: [Ancient Greece](/wiki/Ancient_Greece)

Magna Graecia ancient colonies

Ancient Greece is the civilization belonging to the period of Greek history lasting from the Archaic period of the 8th to 6th centuries BC to 146 BC and the Roman conquest of Greece after the Battle of Corinth. At the center of this time period is Classical Greece, which flourished during the 5th to 4th centuries BC, at first under Athenian leadership successfully repelling the military threat of Persian invasion. The [Athenian Golden Age](/wiki/Athenian_Golden_Age) ends with the defeat of Athens at the hands of Sparta in the [Peloponnesian War](/wiki/Peloponnesian_War) in 404 BC. Following the conquests of [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great), Hellenistic civilization flourished from Central Asia to the western end of the Mediterranean Sea. Classical Greek culture had a powerful influence on the Roman Empire, which carried a version of it to many parts of the Mediterranean region and Europe, for which reason Classical Greece is generally considered to be the seminal culture which provided the foundation of Western civilization.

Athens, after a tyranny in the second half of the 6th century, established ancient Europe's first democracy as a radical solution to prevent the aristocracy from regaining power. A citizens' assembly (the Ecclesia), for the discussion of city policy, had existed since the reforms of Draco; all citizens were permitted to attend after the reforms of Solon, but the poorest citizens could not address the assembly or run for office. With the establishment of the democracy, the assembly became the de jure mechanism of government; all citizens had equal privileges in the assembly. However, non-citizens, foreigners living in Athens, slaves and women had no political rights at all. After the rise of the democracy in Athens, other city-states founded democracies. However, many retained more traditional forms of government. As so often in other matters, Sparta was a notable exception to the rest of Greece, ruled through the whole period by not one, but two hereditary monarchs. This was a form of diarchy.

#### Athens

Main article: [Ancient Athens](/wiki/Ancient_Athens)

| [Ancient Athens](/wiki/Ancient_Athens) |

| Map of Ancient Greece in 431 BC |

[Ancient Athens](/wiki/Ancient_Athens) was inhabited around 3,000 years ago. [Athens](/wiki/Athens) has one of the longest histories of any city in [Europe](/wiki/Europe) and in the world. It became the leading city of Ancient Greece in the first millennium BC. Its cultural achievements during the 5th century BC laid the foundations of [western civilization](/wiki/Western_world). During the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages), Athens experienced decline and then a recovery under the [Byzantine Empire](/wiki/Byzantine_Empire). Athens was relatively prosperous during the [Crusades](/wiki/Crusades), benefiting from medieval Italian trade.

[Acropolis of Athens](/wiki/Acropolis_of_Athens) (architectural model)

[Fifth-century Athens](/wiki/Fifth-century_Athens) refers to the Greek city-state Athens in the period of roughly 480 BC-404 BC. This was a period of Athenian political hegemony, economic growth and cultural flourishing known as the [Golden Age of Athens](/wiki/Golden_Age_of_Athens) or [Age of Pericles](/wiki/Age_of_Pericles). The period began in 480 BC when an Athenian-led coalition of city-states, known as the Delian League, defeated the Persians at Salamis. As the fifth century wore on, what started as an alliance of independent city-states gradually became an Athenian empire. Eventually, Athens abandoned the pretense of parity among its allies and relocated the Delian League treasury from Delos to Athens, where it funded the building of the Athenian Acropolis. With its enemies under its feet and its political fortunes guided by legendary statesman and orator Pericles, Athens as a center of literature, philosophy (see [Greek philosophy](/wiki/Greek_philosophy)) and the arts (see [Greek theatre](/wiki/Greek_theatre)). Some of the most important figures of Western cultural and intellectual history lived in Athens during this period: the dramatists [Aeschylus](/wiki/Aeschylus), [Aristophanes](/wiki/Aristophanes), [Euripides](/wiki/Euripides) and [Sophocles](/wiki/Sophocles), the philosophers [Aristotle](/wiki/Aristotle), [Plato](/wiki/Plato) and [Socrates](/wiki/Socrates).

#### Sparta

Main article: [Sparta](/wiki/Sparta)

| [Lacedaemon](/wiki/Lacedaemon) |
| --- |
| Territory of ancient Sparta |

[Sparta](/wiki/Sparta) was a [Dorian](/wiki/Dorian_Greeks) Greek [military](/wiki/Militarist) state, originally centered in [Laconia](/wiki/Laconia). As a [city-state](/wiki/City-state) devoted to military training, Sparta possessed the most formidable army in the Greek world, and after achieving notable victories over the [Athenian](/wiki/Athenian_Empire) and [Persian](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) Empires, regarded itself as the natural protector of Greece. [Laconia](/wiki/Laconia) or [Lacedaemon](/wiki/Lacedaemon) (Λακεδαίμων) was the name of the wider [city-state](/wiki/City-state) centered at the city of Sparta, though the name "Sparta" is now used for both.

Following the victories in the Messenian Wars (631 BC), Sparta's reputation as a land-fighting force was unequaled. In 480 BC a small Spartan unit under King [Leonidas](/wiki/Leonidas_I) made a legendary [last stand](/wiki/Last_stand) against a massive, invading Persian army at the [Battle of Thermopylae](/wiki/Battle_of_Thermopylae). One year later, Sparta assembled at full strength and led a Greek alliance against the Persians at [Plataea](/wiki/Battle_of_Plataea). There, a decisive Greek victory put an end to the [Greco-Persian War](/wiki/Greco-Persian_War) along with Persian ambition of expanding into Europe. Even though this war was won by a pan-Hellenic army, credit was given to Sparta, who, besides being the protagonist at Thermopylae and Plataea, had been the nominal leader of the entire Greek expedition.

In later Classical times, Sparta along with [Athens](/wiki/Athens), [Thebes](/wiki/Thebes,_Greece) and [Persia](/wiki/Persia) had been the main regional powers fighting for supremacy against each other. As a result of the [Peloponnesian War](/wiki/Peloponnesian_War), Sparta, a traditionally continental culture, became a naval power. At the peak of her power, she subdued many of the key Greek states and even managed to overpower the powerful Athenian navy. By the end of the 5th century, she stood out as a state which had defeated at war both the [Persian](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) and [Athenian Empires](/wiki/Athenian_Empire), a period which marks the [Spartan Hegemony](/wiki/Spartan_hegemony).

Sparta was, above all, a militarist state, and emphasis on military fitness began virtually at birth.

### Macedonia

Main article: [Macedonia (ancient kingdom)](/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom))

| [Macedon](/wiki/Macedon) |
| --- |
| Map of Alexander the Great's empire |

[Macedonia](/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom)) was the name of an ancient kingdom in the northernmost part of [ancient Greece](/wiki/Ancient_Greece), bordering [Epirus](/wiki/Epirus) to the west and the ancient thracian [Odrysian kingdom](/wiki/Odrysian_kingdom) to the east. For a brief period it became the most powerful state in the world after [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) conquered most of the known world, including the entire Achaemenid Empire, inaugurating the [Hellenistic period](/wiki/Hellenistic_civilization) of [Greek history](/wiki/Greek_history).

The [rise of Macedon](/wiki/Rise_of_Macedon), from a small kingdom at the periphery of Classical Greek affairs, to one which came to dominate the entire Hellenic world (and beyond), occurred in the space of just 25 years, between 359 and 336 BC. This ascendancy is largely attributable to the personality and policies of [Philip II of Macedon](/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon). Philip's military skills and expansionist vision of Macedonian greatness brought him early success. He had however first to re-establish a situation which had been greatly worsened by the defeat against the Illyrians in which King Perdiccas himself had died. The Paionians and the [Thracians](/wiki/Odrysian_kingdom) had sacked and invaded the eastern regions of the country, while the Athenians had landed, at Methoni on the coast, a contingent under a Macedonian pretender called Argeus. Using diplomacy, Philip pushed back Paionians and [Thracians](/wiki/Thracians) promising tributes, and crushed the 3,000 Athenian hoplites (359). Momentarily free from his opponents, he concentrated on strengthening his internal position and, above all, his army. His most important innovation was doubtless the introduction of the phalanx infantry corps, armed with the famous sarissa, an exceedingly long spear, at the time the most important army corps in Macedonia.

Polychrome decoration of the [Alexander Sarcophagus](/wiki/Alexander_Sarcophagus)

Philip's son, [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great), managed to briefly extend Macedonian power not only over the central Greek city-states, but also to the Persian empire, including Egypt and lands as far east as the fringes of India. Alexander's adoption of the styles of government of the conquered territories was accompanied by the spread of Greek culture and learning through his vast empire. Although the empire fractured into multiple Hellenic regimes shortly after his death, his conquests left a lasting legacy, not least in the new Greek-speaking cities founded across Persia's western territories, heralding the Hellenistic period. In the partition of Alexander's empire among the Diadochi, Macedonia fell to the Antipatrid dynasty, which was overthrown by the Antigonid dynasty after only a few years.

Alexander had made no special preparations for his succession in his newly founded empire and the Apocrypha of his death state that on his death-bed he willed it to those that performed actions well and powerfully. The result was the [wars of the Diadochi](/wiki/Wars_of_the_Diadochi) between his generals (the [Diadochi](/wiki/Diadochi), or 'Successors'), which lasted for forty years before a more-or-less stable arrangement was established, consisting of four major domains:

- The [Antigonid dynasty](/wiki/Antigonid_dynasty) in [Macedon](/wiki/Macedon) and central Greece;
- The [Ptolemaic dynasty](/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty) in [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt) based at [Alexandria](/wiki/Alexandria);
- The [Seleucid dynasty](/wiki/Seleucid_dynasty) in [Syria](/wiki/Syria) and [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia) based at [Antioch](/wiki/Antioch);
- The [Attalid dynasty](/wiki/Attalid_dynasty) in [Anatolia](/wiki/Anatolia) based at [Pergamum](/wiki/Pergamon).

A further two kingdoms later emerged, the so-called [Greco-Bactrian](/wiki/Greco-Bactrian_kingdom) and [Indo-Greek kingdom](/wiki/Indo-Greek_kingdom). Hellenistic culture thrived in its preservation of the past. The states of the Hellenistic period were deeply fixated with the past and its seemingly lost glories. Athens retained its position as the most prestigious seat of higher education, especially in the domains of philosophy and rhetoric, with considerable libraries. Alexandria was a center of Greek learning and the Library of Alexandria had 700,000 volumes. The city of Pergamon became a major center of book production, possessing a library of some 200,000 volumes, second only to Alexandria's. The island of Rhodes boasted a famous finishing school for politics and diplomacy. Antioch was founded as a metropolis and center of Greek learning which retained its status into the era of Christianity. Seleucia replaced Babylon as the metropolis of the lower Tigris.

### Ancient Rome and the Roman Empire

Main articles: [Ancient Rome](/wiki/Ancient_Rome), [Roman Kingdom](/wiki/Roman_Kingdom), [Roman Republic](/wiki/Roman_Republic), and [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire)

| [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire) |
| --- |
| The [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire) under [Trajan](/wiki/Trajan) in 117 AD, at the peak of its territorial power |

[Ancient Rome](/wiki/Ancient_Rome) is widely known as ancient [Europe](/wiki/Europe)'s largest and most powerful [civilization](/wiki/Civilization). After the [second Punic war](/wiki/Second_Punic_War) Rome was already one of the biggest empires on the planet but its expansion continued with the invasions of Greece, Asia Minor and later Gaul. By 27 BC Rome had control over half of [Europe](/wiki/Europe) as well as Northern Africa and large amounts of the Middle East. Rome also had a developed culture, building on the earlier [Greek culture](/wiki/Greek_culture). From the time of [Augustus](/wiki/Augustus) to the [Fall of the Western Empire](/wiki/Decline_of_the_Roman_Empire), Rome dominated [Western Eurasia](/wiki/Europe), comprising the majority of its population.

Roman expansion began long before the state was transformed into a de facto monarchy and reached its zenith under emperor [Trajan](/wiki/Trajan) with the conquest of [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia) and [Armenia](/wiki/Armenia) from 113 to 117. The period of the "Five Good Emperors" saw a successions of peaceful years and the Empire was prosperous. Each emperor of this period was adopted by his predecessor. The [Nerva–Antonine dynasty](/wiki/Nerva%E2%80%93Antonine_dynasty) was a dynasty of seven consecutive Roman Emperors who ruled over the Roman Empire from 96 to 192. These Emperors are Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius, Lucius Verus, and Commodus.

Roman army soldiers (historical reenactment)

The last two of the "[Five Good Emperors](/wiki/Five_Good_Emperors)" and [Commodus](/wiki/Commodus) are also called [Antonines](/wiki/Antonines). After his accession, Nerva, who succeeded Domitian, set a new tone: he restored much confiscated property and involved the [Roman Senate](/wiki/Roman_Senate) in his rule. Starting in 101, [Trajan](/wiki/Trajan) undertook two military campaigns against the gold rich Dacia, which he finally conquered in 106 (see [Trajan's Dacian Wars](/wiki/Trajan%27s_Dacian_Wars)). In 112, Trajan marched on Armenia and annexed it to the Roman Empire. Then he turned south into Parthia, taking several cities before declaring Mesopotamia a new province of the empire, and lamenting that he was too old to follow in the steps of Alexander the Great. During his rule, the Roman Empire expanded to its largest extent, and would never again advance so far to the east. Hadrian's reign was marked by a general lack of major military conflicts, but he decided to defend the vast territories that Trajan had acquired.

At its territorial peak in the year 117, the Roman Empire controlled approximately 5,000,000 km2 (1,900,000 mi2) of land surface. After Trajans reign the Empire continued to grow stronger until the reign of [Marcus Aurelius](/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius). After the death of [Marcus Aurelius](/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius) his son, [Commodus](/wiki/Commodus) would take power. The reign of Commodus marked the beginning of the decline of the Roman Empire . [Ancient Rome](/wiki/Ancient_Rome)'s influence upon the culture, law, technology, arts, language, religion, government, military, and architecture of [Western civilization](/wiki/Western_culture) continues to this day even outside [latin countries](/wiki/Romance_languages) . It would fall to [Odoacer](/wiki/Odoacer) in 476, but its [eastern half](/wiki/Tetrarchy) will still be alive until it [fell in 1453](/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople).

### Thracian States

Main articles: [Odrysian kingdom](/wiki/Odrysian_kingdom), [Thracians](/wiki/Thracians), [Getae](/wiki/Getae), [Seuthes III](/wiki/Seuthes_III), [Seuthopolis](/wiki/Seuthopolis), [Dacians](/wiki/Dacians), [Dacia](/wiki/Dacia), [Burebista](/wiki/Burebista), and [Sapaean kingdom](/wiki/Sapaean_kingdom)

The [Odrysian Kingdom](/wiki/Odrysian_Kingdom) proper was a [Thracian](/wiki/Thracians) kingdom that existed from the early 5th century BC at least until the mid-3rd century BC. It consisted mainly of present-day [Bulgaria](/wiki/Bulgaria) and parts of Southeastern [Romania](/wiki/Romania) ([Northern Dobruja](/wiki/Northern_Dobruja)), [Northern Greece](/wiki/Northern_Greece) and [European Turkey](/wiki/Eastern_Thrace). Dominated by the eponymous Odrysian people, it was the largest and most powerful Thracian realm and the first larger political entity of the eastern [Balkans](/wiki/Balkans). Before the foundation of [Seuthopolis](/wiki/Seuthopolis) in the late 4th century it had no fixed capital.

The Odrysian kingdom was founded by king [Teres I](/wiki/Teres_I), exploiting the collapse of the [Persian](/wiki/Achaemenid_Persia) presence in Europe due to [failed invasion of Greece in 480-79](/wiki/Second_Persian_invasion_of_Greece). Teres and his son [Sitalces](/wiki/Sitalces) pursued a policy of expansion, making the kingdom one of the most powerful of its time. Throughout much of its early history it remained an ally of [Athens](/wiki/Athens) and even joined the [Peloponnesian War](/wiki/Peloponnesian_War) on its side. By 400 the state showed first signs of fatigue, although the skilled [Cotys I](/wiki/Cotys_I_(Odrysian)) initiated a brief renaissance that lasted until his murder in 360.

The [Getae](/wiki/Getae), a northern Thracian people located between the northeastern foothills of the Haemus range and the lower Danube and the Black Sea, had been part of the Odrysian realm since Teres I, even though it is not clear how tightly they were actually incorporated into the state. When and how the Getae became independent is not discussed in the available sources. Perhaps they became independent during the rule of Cotys I or after his death in 360. Rich funeral treasures from the second half of the 4th century, like those of [Agighiol](/wiki/Agighiol), [Peretu](/wiki/Peretu) or [Borovo](/wiki/Borovo_Treasure), attest to the increasing wealth of the Getic elite. Several artefacts seem to have originated in the Odrysian kingdom and may well have been prestige gifts.

By the middle of the 4th century, there existed a Getic kingdom that was to thrive for a century. The Getic capital was Helis, which has been identified with the archaeological site of [Sboryanovo](/wiki/Thracian_Tomb_of_Sveshtari), which was founded in the 330s or early 320s and housed around 10.000 inhabitants. It seems that the Getae also became active in [Muntenia](/wiki/Muntenia) north of the Danube, a region that would come to constitute a part of the "[Dacia](/wiki/Dacia)" of imperial [Roman historiography](/wiki/Roman_historiography). The first Getic king to appear in the sources was [Cothelas](/wiki/Cothelas), who married his daughter [Meda](/wiki/Meda_of_Odessos) to Philip II, thus concluding an alliance between the two states. This probably happened during or shortly after Philip's conquest of the Odrysians. The kingdom survived two wars with [Lysimachus](/wiki/Lysimachus) and the [Celtic invasion in around 280](/wiki/Celtic_settlement_of_Southeast_Europe), but eventually disintegrated a few decades later. Helis/Sboryanovo was completely destroyed by an earthquake in the middle of the 3rd century.

With Alexander's absence in Asia, the *Strategoi* of [Thrace](/wiki/Thrace) engaged in rebellions and failed expeditions against the Getae, greatly unsettling the country in the process. At the end of the 330s or in the mid-320s (the dating is not entirely clear), a certain Seuthes, later known as [Seuthes III](/wiki/Seuthes_III), instigated a Thracian rebellion. He seems to have been an Odrysian and may have been associated with the royal house of Cersebleptes, although his social background must remain speculation. Seuthes' goal seems to have been the revival of an independent Odrysian state. Seuthes ruled in the interior, what is Thrace north of the [Rhodopes](/wiki/Rhodope_mountains), but not the coastal regions of the Aegean and Black Sea. Probably after the death of Alexander in 323, he founded the capital town of Seuthopolis, on the [Tonzos river](/wiki/Tundzha), near modern [Kazanlak](/wiki/Kazanlak), and named after himself.

The [Dacian Kingdom](/wiki/Dacia) reached its greatest territorial extent under [Burebista](/wiki/Burebista)'s rule (82BC-44BC). During his rule, he conquered territories from [Central Europe](/wiki/Central_Europe) to the [Balkans](/wiki/Balkans), reaching the [Aegean Sea](/wiki/Aegean_Sea). He is well known by the Greek scholars as the "Celtic Slayer", because he defeated and killed many Celtic tribes from the Balkans ([Scordisci](/wiki/Scordisci)) and the Central Europe ([Boii](/wiki/Boii) & [Taurisci](/wiki/Taurisci)). In 48BC Burebista tried to influence the Roman politics, during the [Roman civil war](/wiki/Caesar%27s_civil_war) by allying with [Pompey Magnus](/wiki/Pompey_Magnus) against the victorious [Julius Caesar](/wiki/Julius_Caesar). But [Pompey](/wiki/Pompey) was defeated and later killed in [Ptolemaic Egypt](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Egypt). After all of that, Julius Caesar viewed Burebista's Empire as a threat and he planned to invade it along with the [Parthian Empire](/wiki/Parthian_Empire). But he was assassinated in 44 BC. Burebista's state collapsed into various kingdoms. It will never be unified until [Decebalus](/wiki/Decebalus) becomes the last King in 87AD.

The Thracian kingdom, also known as the [Sapaean kingdom](/wiki/Sapaean_kingdom), was the continuation of the Thracian state from the middle of the 1st century BC to 46 AD. It was dominated by the [Sapaean](/wiki/Sapaeans) tribe, who ruled from their capital [Bizye](/wiki/Vize) in what is now northwestern [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey). Initially only of limited relevance, its power grew significantly after the [battle of Actium](/wiki/Battle_of_Actium) in 31 BC, when Emperor [Augustus](/wiki/Augustus) installed a new dynasty that proved to be highly loyal and expansive. Conquering and ruling much of Thrace on [Roman](/wiki/Roman_Empire) behalf, it lasted until 46 AD, when Emperor [Claudius](/wiki/Claudius) annexed the kingdom and made Thrace a Roman [province](/wiki/Thracia).

### Seleucid Empire

Main article: [Seleucid Empire](/wiki/Seleucid_Empire)

| [Seleucid Empire](/wiki/Seleucid_Empire) |
| --- |
| Seleucid Empire amongst other Hellenistic kingdoms |

The Seleucid Empire was a [Hellenistic empire](/wiki/Hellenistic_empire), and the eastern remnant of the former Achaemenid Persian Empire following its breakup after Alexander the Great's invasion. The Seleucid Empire was centered in the near East. It was a center of Hellenistic culture which maintained the Greek customs and Greek-speaking Macedonian elite.

Seleucid expansion into Greece was abruptly halted after decisive defeats at the hands of the Roman army. Much of the eastern part of the empire was conquered by the Parthians under Mithridates I of Parthia in the mid-2nd century BC, yet the Seleucid kings continued to rule a rump state from Syria until the invasion by Armenian king [Tigranes the Great](/wiki/Tigranes_the_Great)and their ultimate overthrow by the Roman general [Pompey](/wiki/Pompey).

### Ptolemaic Empire

Main articles: [Ptolemaic Kingdom](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom) and [Ptolemaic dynasty](/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty)

| [Ptolemaic empire](/wiki/Ptolemaic_empire) |
| --- |
| Ptolemaic Empire in 300 BC |

The [Ptolemaic dynasty](/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty), sometimes also known as the [Lagids](/wiki/Lagids), was a Greek royal family which ruled the [Ptolemaic Empire](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Empire) in Egypt during the Hellenistic period.

Ptolemy, one of the seven somatophylakes (bodyguards) who served as Alexander the Great's generals and deputies, was appointed satrap of Egypt after Alexander's death in 323 BC. In 305 BC, he declared himself King Ptolemy I, later known as "Soter" (saviour). The Egyptians soon accepted the Ptolemies as the successors to the pharaohs of independent Egypt. Ptolemy's family ruled Egypt until the Roman conquest of 30 BC. All the male rulers of the dynasty took the name Ptolemy.

Ptolemaic Egypt began when Ptolemy I Soter declared himself Pharaoh of Egypt in 305 BC and ended with the death of queen Cleopatra VII of Egypt and the Roman conquest in 30 BC. The Ptolemaic Kingdom was a powerful Hellenistic state, extending from southern Syria in the east, to Cyrene to the west, and south to the frontier with Nubia. Alexandria became the capital city and a center of Greek culture and trade. To gain recognition by the native Egyptian populace, they named themselves as the successors to the Pharaohs. The later Ptolemies took on Egyptian traditions, had themselves portrayed on public monuments in Egyptian style and dress, and participated in Egyptian religious life. Hellenistic culture continued to thrive in Egypt well after the Muslim conquest. The Ptolemies faced rebellions of native Egyptians often caused by an unwanted regime and were involved in foreign and civil wars that led to the decline of the kingdom and its annexation by Rome.

## Ancient Eurasian Steppe

### Scythia

Main article: [Scythians](/wiki/Scythians)

The **Scythians** ([/ˈsɪθiən/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) or [/ˈsɪðiən/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English)) or **Scyths** ([/ˈsɪθ/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English), but note **Scytho-** ([/ˈsaɪθoʊ/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English)) in composition) and sometimes also referred to as the **Pontic Scythians**, were an [ancient](/wiki/Ancient_Iranian_peoples) [Eastern](/wiki/Eastern_Iranian_languages) [Iranian](/wiki/Iranian_peoples) [equestrian](/wiki/Eurasian_nomads) [nomadic](/wiki/Nomad) people who had migrated during the 9th to 8th centuries BC from [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia) to the [Pontic Steppe](/wiki/Pontic_Steppe) in modern-day [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine) and [Southern Russia](/wiki/Southern_Russia), where they remained established from the 7th century BC until the 3rd century BC.

After the Scythians' disappearance, authors of the ancient, mediaeval, and early modern periods used their name to refer to various populations of the steppes unrelated to them.

### Sarmatia

Main article: [Sarmatians](/wiki/Sarmatians)

The **Sarmatians** (Latin: *Sarmatæ* or *[Sauromatæ](/wiki/Sauromatian_culture)*, [Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greek): Σαρμάται, Σαυρομάται) were an [Iranian people](/wiki/Iranian_peoples) during [classical antiquity](/wiki/Classical_antiquity), flourishing from about the 5th century BC to the 4th century AD. They spoke [Scythian](/wiki/Scythian_language), an [Indo-European](/wiki/Indo-European_languages) language from the [Eastern Iranian](/wiki/Eastern_Iranian_languages) family.

Originating in [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia), the Sarmatians started their westward migration around the 6th century bc, coming to dominate the closely related [Scythians](/wiki/Scythians) by the 2nd century bc. The Sarmatians differed from the Scythians in their veneration of the god of fire rather than god of nature, and their [women's prominent role](/wiki/Women_in_ancient_warfare) in warfare, which possibly served as the inspiration for the [Amazons](/wiki/Amazons). At their greatest reported extent, around the 1st century AD, these tribes ranged from the [Vistula River](/wiki/Vistula_River) to the mouth of the [Danube](/wiki/Danube) and eastward to the [Volga](/wiki/Volga), bordering the shores of the [Black](/wiki/Black_Sea) and [Caspian](/wiki/Caspian_Sea) seas as well as the [Caucasus](/wiki/Caucasus) to the south. Their territory, which was known as Sarmatia to Greco-Roman ethnographers, corresponded to the western part of greater [Scythia](/wiki/Scythia) (mostly modern [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine) and [Southern Russia](/wiki/Southern_Federal_District), also to a smaller extent north eastern [Balkans](/wiki/Balkans) around [Moldova](/wiki/Moldova)). According to authors Arrowsmith, Fellowes and Graves Hansard in their book *A Grammar of Ancient Geography* published in 1832, Sarmatia had two parts, Sarmatia Europea and Sarmatia Asiatica covering a combined area of 503,000 sq mi or 1,302,764 km2.

### Xiongnu

Main article: [Xiongnu](/wiki/Xiongnu)

See also: [Serica](/wiki/Serica)

| [Xiongnu](/wiki/Xiongnu) |
| --- |
| Xiongnu c. 174   ---     Xiongnu Region    Outlying regions |

Xiongnu (Hsiung nu) was a [nomadic empire](/wiki/Nomadic_empire) that flourished in the central Asia. Their origin is debatable, but they probably spoke either an [Iranian](/wiki/Iranian_language), [Proto-Turkic](/wiki/Proto-Turkic_language), [Proto-Mongolic](/wiki/Mongolic_languages), or [Yeniseian](/wiki/Yeniseian) language. They conquered most of modern [Mongolia](/wiki/Mongolia) under their leader [Toumen](/wiki/Toumen) (220–209 BC) in the 3rd century BC. During [Modu](/wiki/Modu)'s reign (209–174 BC) they defeated both the [Donghu](/wiki/Donghu_People) in the east and [Yuezhi](/wiki/Yuezhi) in the west and they began threatening [Han China](/wiki/Han_China).

The [Great Wall of China](/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China) had been constructed to protect Chinese towns from the Xiongnu attacks. While the Chinese were trying to bring the Xiongnu under control, something of high significance happened: cross-cultural encounters. A large variety of people (such as traders, ambassadors, hostages, parents in cross-cultural marriages, etc.) served as helpers that passed on ideas, values, and techniques across cultural boundary lines. These encounters helped cultures learn from other cultures. The Xiongnu Empire disintegrated into two parts during the 1st century; eventually the Xiongnu fell due to their defeat in the [Han–Xiongnu War](/wiki/Han%E2%80%93Xiongnu_War).

### Huns

Main article: [Huns](/wiki/Huns)

| [Hunnic Empire](/wiki/Huns#Unified_Empire_under_Attila) |
| --- |
| The [Hunnic Empire](/wiki/Huns#Unified_Empire_under_Attila)   ---     Attila's imperial capital (approximate)    Attila's empire (approximate)    Non-Hunnic Regions |

Huns were nomadic people who were known for their hordes of [mounted archers](/wiki/Mounted_archer). Their language seems to be [Turkic](/wiki/Turkic_languages); but [Mongolic](/wiki/Mongolic_languages), [Yeniseian](/wiki/Yeniseian), [Uralic](/wiki/Uralic_languages) etc., are also postulated. After 370 under a certain [Balamber](/wiki/Balamber) they founded an empire in the East Europe defeating [Alans](/wiki/Alans) and [Goths](/wiki/Goths). They triggered the [great migration](/wiki/Migration_period) which eventually caused the collapse of the [West Roman Empire](/wiki/West_Roman_Empire).

The death of Rugila in 434 left the sons of his brother Mundzuk, Attila and Bleda, in control of the united Hun tribes. [Attila the Hun](/wiki/Attila_the_Hun) ruled of the Huns from 434 until his death in 453. Under his rule and leader of the Hunnic Empire, the empire stretched from Germany to the Ural River and from the Danube River to the Baltic Sea. Hunnic khagan Atilla invaded Europe. The rise of the Huns around 370 overwhelmed the Gothic kingdoms. Many of the Goths migrated into Roman territory in the Balkans, while others remained north of the Danube under Hunnic rule.

During Attila the Hun's rule, he was one of the most fearsome enemies of the Western and Eastern Roman Empire. He invaded the Balkans twice and marched through Gaul (modern France) as far as Orléans before being defeated at the [Battle of Châlons](/wiki/Battle_of_Ch%C3%A2lons), though some scholars now believe this battle resulted in a stalemate or even a Hunnish victory. He appeared in [North Italy](/wiki/North_Italy) in the next year. After Attila's death in 453, the Hunnic Empire collapsed in 469. The Huns seem to have been absorbed by other ethnic groups such as the [Bulgars](/wiki/Bulgars). Kim, however, argues that the Huns continued under Ernak, becoming the [Kutrigur](/wiki/Kutrigurs) and [Utigur](/wiki/Utigurs) Hunno-[Bulgars](/wiki/Bulgars). This conclusion is still subject to some controversy. Some scholars also argue that another group identified in ancient sources as Huns, the [North Caucasian Huns](/wiki/North_Caucasian_Huns), were genuine Huns. The rulers of various post-Hunnic steppe peoples are known to have claimed descent from Attila in order to legitimize their right to the power, and various steppe peoples were also called "Huns" by Western and Byzantine sources from the fourth century onward.
