***Shrek*** is a 2001 American animated [fantasy comedy film](/wiki/Fantasy_comedy_film) directed by [Andrew Adamson](/wiki/Andrew_Adamson) and [Vicky Jenson](/wiki/Vicky_Jenson), and written by [Ted Elliott](/wiki/Ted_Elliott_(screenwriter)), [Terry Rossio](/wiki/Terry_Rossio), [Joe Stillman](/wiki/Joe_Stillman), and [Roger S. H. Schulman](/wiki/Roger_S._H._Schulman), loosely based on the 1990 children's picture book *[Shrek!](/wiki/Shrek!)* by [William Steig](/wiki/William_Steig). It is the first in [the *Shrek* film series](/wiki/Shrek_(franchise)), and stars [Mike Myers](/wiki/Mike_Myers), [Eddie Murphy](/wiki/Eddie_Murphy), [Cameron Diaz](/wiki/Cameron_Diaz) and [John Lithgow](/wiki/John_Lithgow). In the film, an embittered [ogre](/wiki/Ogre) named [Shrek](/wiki/Shrek_(character)) (voiced by Myers) finds his home in the swamp overrun by fairy tale creatures banished by the obsessive ruler [Lord Farquaad](/wiki/Lord_Farquaad) (Lithgow). With the help of [Donkey](/wiki/Donkey_(Shrek)) (Murphy), Shrek makes a pact with Farquaad to rescue [Princess Fiona](/wiki/Princess_Fiona) (Diaz) in exchange for regaining control of his swamp.

After purchasing the rights to Steig's book in 1991, [Steven Spielberg](/wiki/Steven_Spielberg) sought to produce a [traditionally-animated](/wiki/Traditionally-animated) film adaptation, but [John H. Williams](/wiki/John_H._Williams_(film_producer)) convinced him to bring the project to the newly founded [DreamWorks Pictures](/wiki/DreamWorks_Pictures) in 1994. [Jeffrey Katzenberg](/wiki/Jeffrey_Katzenberg), along with Williams and [Aron Warner](/wiki/Aron_Warner), began development on *Shrek* in 1995, immediately following the studio's purchase of the rights from Spielberg. [Chris Farley](/wiki/Chris_Farley) was cast as the voice for the title character, recording most of the required dialogue, but died in 1997 before his work on the film was finished; Myers was hired to replace him, and gave Shrek his characteristic [Scottish accent](/wiki/Scottish_accent). The film was initially intended to be created using [motion capture](/wiki/Motion_capture), but after poor test results, the studio hired [Pacific Data Images](/wiki/Pacific_Data_Images) to complete the final computer animation. *Shrek* [parodies](/wiki/Parody_film) other [fairy tale](/wiki/Fairy_tale) adaptations, primarily animated Disney films.

*Shrek* premiered at the [Mann Village Theatre](/wiki/Fox_Theater,_Westwood_Village) in [Westwood](/wiki/Westwood,_Los_Angeles) on April 22, 2001, and was later shown at the [2001 Cannes Film Festival](/wiki/2001_Cannes_Film_Festival), where it competed for the [Palme d'Or](/wiki/Palme_d%27Or), making it the first animated film since [Picha's](/wiki/Picha) *[The Missing Link](/wiki/The_Missing_Link_(1980_film))* (1980) to be chosen as main competition at the festival. The film was theatrically released by [DreamWorks Pictures](/wiki/DreamWorks_Pictures) in the United States on May 18, 2001, and grossed over $492 million worldwide, becoming the [fourth highest-grossing film of 2001](/wiki/2001_in_film#Highest-grossing_films). It was widely praised by critics for its animation, voice performances, soundtrack, writing and humor, which they noted catered to both adults and children. *Shrek* was named one of the [top 10 films of 2001](/wiki/American_Film_Institute_Awards#2001) by the [American Film Institute](/wiki/American_Film_Institute), the first animated film to be listed, and won numerous accolades including the [BAFTA Award for Best Adapted Screenplay](/wiki/BAFTA_Award_for_Best_Adapted_Screenplay) and the inaugural [Academy Award for Best Animated Feature](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Animated_Feature).

The film's major success helped establish [DreamWorks Animation](/wiki/DreamWorks_Animation) as a competitor to [Pixar](/wiki/Pixar) in feature film computer animation. Three sequels have been released—*[Shrek 2](/wiki/Shrek_2)* (2004), *[Shrek the Third](/wiki/Shrek_the_Third)* (2007), and *[Shrek Forever After](/wiki/Shrek_Forever_After)* (2010)—along with two spin-off films—[*Puss in Boots*](/wiki/Puss_in_Boots_(2011_film)) (2011) and *[Puss in Boots: The Last Wish](/wiki/Puss_in_Boots:_The_Last_Wish)* (2022)—with other productions, notably *[Shrek 5](/wiki/Shrek_5)*, in development. It is also regarded as one of the most influential animated films of the 2000s and [one of the greatest animated films ever made](/wiki/List_of_films_voted_the_best). The United States [Library of Congress](/wiki/Library_of_Congress) selected *Shrek* for preservation in the [National Film Registry](/wiki/National_Film_Registry) in 2020, becoming the first animated film of the 21st century to be preserved.

## Plot

[Shrek](/wiki/Shrek_(character)) is an asocial [ogre](/wiki/Ogre) who loves the solitude of his swamp and enjoys fending off mobs and intruders. One day, his life is interrupted after he inadvertently saves a talkative [Donkey](/wiki/Donkey_(Shrek)) from some soldiers, prompting Donkey to forcibly stay with him. Donkey is one of many fairy tale creatures that are being exiled by the dwarfish [Lord Farquaad](/wiki/Lord_Farquaad) of Duloc to beautify his land. However, the creatures inadvertently end up in the swamp. Angered by the intrusion, Shrek resolves to visit Farquaad and demand that he move the creatures elsewhere, reluctantly allowing Donkey to accompany him as he is the only one who knows where Duloc is.

Meanwhile, Farquaad is presented with the [Magic Mirror](/wiki/Magic_Mirror_(Snow_White)), who tells him that he must marry a princess in order to become a king. Presented with three options, Farquaad chooses [Princess Fiona](/wiki/Princess_Fiona), who is imprisoned in a castle guarded by a [Dragon](/wiki/Dragon_(Shrek)). Unwilling to rescue Fiona himself, he organizes a tournament in which the winner will receive the "privilege" of performing the task on his behalf. When Shrek and Donkey arrive at Duloc, Farquaad announces that whoever kills Shrek will win the tournament; however, Shrek and Donkey defeat Farquaad's knights with relative ease. Amused, Farquaad proclaims Shrek his champion instead, and agrees to relocate the fairy tale creatures in exchange for Shrek rescuing Fiona.

Shrek and Donkey travel to the castle and the Dragon attacks them. Shrek locates Fiona, who is bewildered by his lack of [romanticism](/wiki/Romanticism); they flee the castle after rescuing Donkey from the Dragon, who is revealed to be female and has fallen in love with him. When Shrek removes his helmet and reveals he is an ogre, Fiona stubbornly refuses to go to Duloc, demanding Farquaad arrive in person to save her, but Shrek carries Fiona against her will. That night, after setting up camp, and with Fiona alone in a cave, Shrek admits to Donkey that he is asocial because he grew frustrated over being constantly judged for his appearance. Fiona overhears this and becomes kinder to Shrek. The next day, [Robin Hood](/wiki/Robin_Hood) and his band of Merry Men harass the three, but Fiona easily defeats them in physical combat. Shrek becomes impressed with Fiona, and they begin to fall in love.

When the trio nears Duloc, Fiona takes shelter in a windmill for the evening. Donkey enters alone and discovers that Fiona has transformed into an ogress. She explains that during her childhood, she was cursed to transform into an ogress at night but retain her human form during the day. She tells Donkey that only "true love's first kiss" will break the spell and allow her to "take love's true form". Meanwhile, Shrek is about to confess his feelings to Fiona, when he overhears Fiona referring to herself as an "ugly beast". Thinking that she is talking about him, Shrek angrily leaves and returns the next morning with Farquaad. Confused and hurt by Shrek's abrupt hostility, Fiona reluctantly accepts Farquaad's marriage proposal and requests that they be married that day before sunset. Shrek dismisses Donkey and returns to his now vacated swamp, but quickly realizes that he feels miserable without Fiona. Donkey returns and lectures Shrek for jumping to conclusions and reveals that Fiona was not referring to him as an "ugly beast", although Donkey does not reveal Fiona's secret to Shrek. The two reconcile, and Donkey summons the Dragon, whom he had reunited with earlier in the day. Shrek and Donkey ride Dragon to Duloc so they can stop the wedding.

Shrek interrupts the ceremony just before it ends and expresses his feelings for Fiona. The sun sets, and Fiona transforms into an ogress in front of everyone. Disgusted and enraged, Farquaad orders Shrek to be executed and Fiona re-imprisoned, while he declares himself king. The two are saved when the Dragon, ridden by Donkey, breaks in and devours Farquaad. Shrek and Fiona kiss, and Fiona's curse is broken; though she remains an ogress, Shrek reassures her that he still finds her beautiful. They marry in the swamp with the fairy tale creatures in attendance, then leave for their honeymoon.

## Production

### Development

See also: [Shrek – I Feel Good Animation Test](/wiki/Shrek_%E2%80%93_I_Feel_Good_Animation_Test)

At the time [DreamWorks Pictures](/wiki/DreamWorks_Pictures) was founded, producer [John H. Williams](/wiki/John_H._Williams_(film_producer)) got hold of the book from his children and when he brought it to DreamWorks, it caught CEO [Jeffrey Katzenberg](/wiki/Jeffrey_Katzenberg)'s attention and the studio decided to make it into a film. Recounting the inspiration of making the film, Williams said:

> Every development deal starts with a pitch and my pitch came from my then kindergartner, in collaboration with his pre-school brother. Upon our second reading of *Shrek*, the kindergartner started quoting large segments of the book pretending he could read them. Even as an adult, I thought *Shrek* was outrageous, irreverent, iconoclastic, gross, and just a lot of fun. He was a great movie character in search of a movie.

After buying the rights to the film, Katzenberg quickly put it in active development in November 1995. [Steven Spielberg](/wiki/Steven_Spielberg) had thought about making a traditionally animated film adaptation of the book before, when he bought the rights to the book in 1991 before the founding of DreamWorks, where [Bill Murray](/wiki/Bill_Murray) would play Shrek and [Steve Martin](/wiki/Steve_Martin) would play Donkey. In the beginning of production, co-director [Andrew Adamson](/wiki/Andrew_Adamson) refused to be intimidated by Katzenberg and had an argument with him about how much the film should appeal to adults. Katzenberg wanted both audiences, but he deemed some of Adamson's ideas, such as adding sexual jokes and [Guns N' Roses](/wiki/Guns_N%27_Roses) music to the soundtrack, to be too outrageous. Adamson and [Kelly Asbury](/wiki/Kelly_Asbury) joined in 1997 to co-direct the film. However, Asbury left a year later for work on the 2002 film *[Spirit: Stallion of the Cimarron](/wiki/Spirit:_Stallion_of_the_Cimarron)*, and was replaced with story artist [Vicky Jenson](/wiki/Vicky_Jenson), although Asbury would later co-direct [the sequel](/wiki/Shrek_2). Both Adamson and Jenson decided to work on the film in half, so the crew could at least know whom to go to with specific detail questions about the film's sequences; "We both ended up doing a lot of everything," Adamson said. "We're both kinda control freaks, and we both wanted to do everything."

*Perry* in [Palo Alto](/wiki/Palo_Alto,_California) (2016)

Early development of the film from 1996–1997 involved concept art designed in [Photoshop](/wiki/Adobe_Photoshop), which depicted a significantly different aesthetic and setting for the titular character. Initial iterations placed Shrek's home within a garbage dump near a human settlement, referenced as Wart Creek. Furthermore, early conceptual drafts explored a background where Shrek resided with his parents and featured habits such as storing decaying fish in his bedroom. Donkey was modeled after Pericles (born 1994; died 2025; also known as Perry), a real miniature donkey from Barron Park in Palo Alto, California. [Raman Hui](/wiki/Raman_Hui), supervising animator of *Shrek*, stated that Fiona "wasn't based on any real person" and he did many different sketches for her. He had done over 100 sculptures of Fiona before the directors chose the final design. In early development, the art directors visited [Hearst Castle](/wiki/Hearst_Castle), [Stratford upon Avon](/wiki/Stratford_upon_Avon), and [Dordogne](/wiki/Dordogne) for inspiration. Art Director Douglas Rogers visited a magnolia plantation in [Charleston, South Carolina](/wiki/Charleston,_South_Carolina), as inspiration for Shrek's swamp. Planned characters not used in the film include [Goldilocks](/wiki/Goldilocks) and [Sleeping Beauty](/wiki/Sleeping_Beauty).

### Writing

The screenplay was written by Ted Elliott, Terry Rossio, Joe Stillman, and Roger S. H. Schulman, loosely based on the picture book by William Steig. The way Steig upended fairy tale conventions in the original book by making an ogre the protagonist formed the basis for the movie being a parody of fairy tales. Elliott and Rossio began writing for the film in 1997 and worked on the project for two years. Elliott and Rossio were also credited as co-producers for the film. They centered the four main characters' personalities on maladaptive responses to self-esteem issues; as the DreamWorks story crew developed the plot, their personalities remained the same, ensuring the "thematic unity" of the story was preserved. When the studio leaned towards Shrek being a kind person shunned for being an ogre, they pushed for Shrek to remain a "misanthropic anti-hero."

### Casting

[Nicolas Cage](/wiki/Nicolas_Cage) was initially offered the role of Shrek but he turned it down because he did not want to look like an ogre. In 2013, Cage explained: "When you're drawn, in a way it says more about how children are going to see you than anything else, and I so care about that." Alternately, [Ian Bagg](/wiki/Ian_Bagg) auditioned for the role but was rejected.

Following the death of [Chris Farley](/wiki/Chris_Farley) in late 1997, [Mike Myers](/wiki/Mike_Myers) was re-cast as the voice of Shrek for the film.

[Chris Farley](/wiki/Chris_Farley) was initially hired to voice Shrek, and he had recorded nearly all of the dialogue for the character, but died before completing the project. According to [David Spade](/wiki/David_Spade), Farley only had 5 days of voice work left to perform; Farley's brother [John](/wiki/John_Farley_(actor)) was asked if he would come in and finish the remaining lines, but he refused. A story reel featuring a sample of Farley's recorded dialogue was leaked to the public in August 2015. Katzenberg pitched the film to [Mike Myers](/wiki/Mike_Myers) at the premiere of *[Saving Private Ryan](/wiki/Saving_Private_Ryan)* (1998), after hearing the pitch he thought that *Shrek* was the "worst fucking title" he ever heard. DreamWorks then re-cast the voice role to Myers, who insisted on a complete script rewrite, to leave no traces of Farley's version of Shrek. According to Myers, he wanted to voice the character "for two reasons: I wanted the opportunity to work with Jeffrey Katzenberg; and [the book is] a great story about accepting yourself for who you are."

After Myers had completed providing the voice for the character over the course of 1999 and the film was well into production, a rough cut of the movie was shown to him in February 2000. Myers asked to re-record all of his lines with a [Scottish accent](/wiki/Scottish_English_phonology), similar to that his mother used when she told him bedtime stories and also used for his roles in other films, such as *[So I Married an Axe Murderer](/wiki/So_I_Married_an_Axe_Murderer)* and *[Austin Powers: The Spy Who Shagged Me](/wiki/Austin_Powers:_The_Spy_Who_Shagged_Me)*. According to the DVD commentary, he had also tried using a [Lothar of the Hill People](/wiki/Recurring_Saturday_Night_Live_characters_and_sketches_introduced_1988%E2%80%9389#Lothar_of_the_Hill_People) accent and a Canadian accent. After hearing the alternative, Katzenberg agreed to redo scenes in the film, saying, "It was so good we took $4M worth of animation out and did it again." Myers disputed the cost, saying "it didn't cost the studio 'millions of dollars'," as rumored. "What it meant is instead of me going in for ten sessions, I went in for twenty sessions. I got paid the same." Because of Myers voicing the character, more ideas began to come. There were clearer story points, fresher gags and comedy bits. "I got a letter from Spielberg thanking me so much for caring about the character," Myers said. "And he said the Scottish accent had improved the movie."

Another person planned to voice a character in the film was [Janeane Garofalo](/wiki/Janeane_Garofalo), who was set to star alongside Farley as Princess Fiona. However, she was fired from the project for unexplained reasons. Years later, Garofalo stated "I was never told why [I was fired]. I assume because I sound like a man sometimes? I don't know why. Nobody told me ... But, you know, the movie didn't do anything, so who cares?"

### Animation

*Shrek* was originally set up to be a [live-action animation](/wiki/Live-action_animation) hybrid with background plate miniature sets and the main characters composited into the scene as motion-captured computer graphics, using an ExpertVision Hires Falcon 10 camera system to capture and apply realistic human movement to the characters. [Softimage](/wiki/Autodesk_Softimage) was used, as well as [Mental Ray](/wiki/Mental_Ray). A sizable crew was hired to run a test, and after a year and a half of R & D, the test was finally screened in May 1997. The results were not satisfactory, with Katzenberg stating "It looked terrible, it didn't work, it wasn't funny, and we didn't like it." The animation, known as *[Shrek – I Feel Good Animation Test](/wiki/Shrek_%E2%80%93_I_Feel_Good_Animation_Test)*, was not released publicly until 2023 when it was posted by production designer [Barry E. Jackson](/wiki/Barry_E._Jackson) on his [YouTube](/wiki/YouTube) channel. The studio then turned to its production partners at [Pacific Data Images](/wiki/Pacific_Data_Images) (PDI), who began production with the studio in 1998 and helped *Shrek* get to its final, computer-animated look. At this time, *[Antz](/wiki/Antz)* was still in production at the studio and effects supervisor Ken Bielenberg was asked by [Aron Warner](/wiki/Aron_Warner) "to start development for *Shrek*". Similar to previous PDI films, PDI used its own proprietary software (like the Fluid Animation System) for its animated movies. For some elements, however, it also took advantage of some of the powerhouse animation software on the market. This is particularly true with [Maya](/wiki/Autodesk_Maya), which PDI used for most of its dynamic cloth animation and for the hair of Fiona and Farquaad.

"We did a lot of work on character and set-up, and then kept changing the set up while we were doing the animation," Hui noted. "In *Antz*, we had a facial system that gave us all the facial muscles under the skin. In *Shrek*, we applied that to whole body. So, if you pay attention to Shrek when he talks, you see that when he opens his jaw, he forms a double chin, because we have the fat and the muscles underneath. That kind of detail took us a long time to get right." One of the most difficult parts of creating the film was making Donkey's fur flow smoothly so that it did not look like that of a [Chia Pet](/wiki/Chia_Pet). This fell into the hands of the surfacing animators, who used flow controls within a complex shader to provide the fur with many attributes (ability to change directions, lie flat, swirl, etc.). It was then the job of the visual effects group, led by Ken Bielenberg, to make the fur react to environment conditions. Once the technology was mastered, it could be applied to many aspects of the movie, including grass, moss, beards, eyebrows, and even threads on Shrek's tunic. Making human hair realistic was different from Donkey's fur, requiring a separate rendering system and much attention from the [lighting](/wiki/Lighting) and [visual effects](/wiki/Visual_effects) teams.

*Shrek* has 31 sequences, with 1,288 total shots. Aron Warner said that the creators "envisioned a magical environment that you could immerse yourself into". Shrek includes 36 separate in-film locations to make the world of the film, which DreamWorks claimed was more than any previous computer-animated feature. In-film locations were finalized and, as demonstrated by past DreamWorks animated movies, color and mood was of the utmost importance. The animation was completed in 2000.

### Music

Main article: [Shrek: Music from the Original Motion Picture](/wiki/Shrek:_Music_from_the_Original_Motion_Picture)

*Shrek* is the third DreamWorks animated film (and the only film in the [*Shrek* series](/wiki/Shrek_(franchise))) to have [Harry Gregson-Williams](/wiki/Harry_Gregson-Williams) team up with [John Powell](/wiki/John_Powell_(composer)) to compose the score following *[Antz](/wiki/Antz)* (1998) and *[Chicken Run](/wiki/Chicken_Run)* (2000). Powell was left out to compose scores for later Shrek films with Gregson-Williams due to a conflict. The score was recorded at [Abbey Road Studios](/wiki/Abbey_Road_Studios) by Nick Wollage and Slamm Andrews, with the latter mixing it at Media Ventures and Patricia Sullivan-Fourstar handling mastering.

*Shrek* introduced a new element to give the film a unique feel. The film used [pop music](/wiki/Pop_music) and other [oldies](/wiki/Oldies) to make the story more forward. Covers of songs like "[On the Road Again](/wiki/On_the_Road_Again_(Willie_Nelson_song))" and "[Try a Little Tenderness](/wiki/Try_a_Little_Tenderness)" were integrated in the film's score. The band [Smash Mouth](/wiki/Smash_Mouth)'s song "[All Star](/wiki/All_Star_(song))" gained massive popularity after its original release two years earlier, due to its usage in the film's opening credits. The filmmakers for *Shrek* had originally used the song as a placeholder for the opening credits and intended to replace it with an original composition by [Matt Mahaffey](/wiki/Matt_Mahaffey)'s band [Self](/wiki/Self_(band)) that would mimic the feel of "All Star", named "[Stay Home](/wiki/Stay_Home)". However, DreamWorks executive [Jeffrey Katzenberg](/wiki/Jeffrey_Katzenberg) suggested for them to use "All Star" over the sequence instead, with *Stay Home* ultimately playing over the credits sequence instead. As the film was about to be completed, Katzenberg suggested to the filmmakers to redo the film's ending to "go out with a big laugh"; instead of ending the film with just a storybook closing over Shrek and Fiona as they ride off into the sunset, they decided to add a cover of [The Monkees](/wiki/The_Monkees)' "[I'm a Believer](/wiki/I%27m_a_Believer)" by Smash Mouth and show all the fairytale creatures in the film.

Although [Rufus Wainwright](/wiki/Rufus_Wainwright)'s version of the song ["Hallelujah"](/wiki/Hallelujah_(Leonard_Cohen_song)) appeared in the soundtrack album, it was [John Cale](/wiki/John_Cale)'s version that appeared in the film; in a radio interview, Rufus Wainwright suggested that his version of "Hallelujah" did not appear in the film due to the "glass ceiling" he was hitting because of his sexuality. An alternative explanation is that, although the filmmakers wanted Cale's version for the film, licensing issues prevented its use in the soundtrack album, because Wainwright was an artist for [DreamWorks](/wiki/DreamWorks_Records) but Cale was not.

The film's [1996 animation test](/wiki/Shrek_%E2%80%93_I_Feel_Good_Animation_Test), made public in 2023, used the song "[I Got You (I Feel Good)](/wiki/I_Got_You_(I_Feel_Good))".

## Release

### Marketing

In 2000, [IMAX](/wiki/IMAX) released *[CyberWorld](/wiki/CyberWorld)* onto its branded large-screen theaters. It was a compilation film that featured stereoscopic conversions of various animated shorts and sequences, including the bar sequence in *[Antz](/wiki/Antz)*. DreamWorks was so impressed by the technology used for the sequence's "stereoscopic translation", that the studio and IMAX decided to plan a big-screen 3D version of *Shrek*. The film would have been re-released during the Christmas season of 2001, or the following summer, after its conventional 2D release. The re-release would have also included new sequences and an alternate ending. Plans for this was dropped due to "creative changes" instituted by DreamWorks and resulted in a loss of $1.18 million, down from IMAX's profit of $3.24 million.

[Radio Disney](/wiki/Radio_Disney) was told not to allow any ads for the film to air on the station, stating, "Due to recent initiatives with [The Walt Disney Company](/wiki/The_Walt_Disney_Company), we are being asked not to align ourselves promotionally with this new release *Shrek*. Stations may accept spot dollars only in individual markets." The restriction was later relaxed to allow ads for the film's soundtrack album onto the network.

On May 7, 2001, [Burger King](/wiki/Burger_King) began promotions for the film, giving out a selection of nine exclusive Candy Caddies based on the *Shrek* characters, in Big Kids Meal and Kids Meal orders. [Ice cream](/wiki/Ice_cream) chain [Baskin-Robbins](/wiki/Baskin-Robbins) also ran an 8-week promotion of the film, selling products such as Shrek's Hot Sludge Sundae, a combination of [Oreo](/wiki/Oreo) Cookies 'n Cream ice cream, [hot fudge](/wiki/Hot_fudge), crushed chocolate cookies, [whipped cream](/wiki/Whipped_cream) and squiggly [gummy worms](/wiki/Gummy_worms), and Shrek Freeze Frame Cake, featuring an image of Shrek and Donkey framed by [sunflowers](/wiki/Sunflowers). This was to support the film's DVD/VHS release.

### Home media

*Shrek* was released by DreamWorks Home Entertainment on [VHS](/wiki/VHS) and [DVD](/wiki/DVD) on November 2, 2001. It surpassed *[Star Wars: Episode I – The Phantom Menace](/wiki/Star_Wars:_Episode_I_%E2%80%93_The_Phantom_Menace)*'s short-lived record for being the fastest-selling DVD title of all time, selling 2.5 million copies within the first three days of release. Another 4.5 million copies were sold on VHS in the same timespan, making it the biggest opening weekend in retail video since *[The Lion King](/wiki/The_Lion_King)* in 1995. Both releases included *[Shrek in the Swamp Karaoke Dance Party](/wiki/Shrek_in_the_Swamp_Karaoke_Dance_Party)*, a 3-minute musical short film, that takes up right after *Shrek*'s ending, with film's characters performing a medley of modern pop songs. As for the DVD release, this two-disc set features a fullscreen format on the first disc and a widescreen format on the second disc, with the latter featuring a [DTS](/wiki/DTS,_Inc.) audio track.

Coincidentally, the film was released on video the same day that Pixar's *[Monsters, Inc.](/wiki/Monsters,_Inc.)* hit theaters. Since videos were traditionally released on Tuesdays, Disney's executives did not receive this well, saying that the move "seemed like an underhanded attempt to siphon off some of their film's steam". DreamWorks responded that it "simply shifted the release to a Friday to make it more of an event and predicted that it and other studios would do so more frequently with important films." *Monsters, Inc.* earned that weekend more than $62 million, breaking the record for an animated film, while *Shrek*'s video release made more than $100 million, and eventually became the biggest selling DVD at the time with over 5.5 million sales. This broke the record previously held by *[Gladiator](/wiki/Gladiator_(2000_film))*, another DreamWorks film. *Shrek* generated more than $420 million in revenue for DreamWorks on DVD and VHS, and has sold more than 21 million copies of the 23 million shipped by January 2002. Worldwide, more than 10 million *Shrek* DVDs have been sold by that point.

In February 2006, [Paramount Pictures](/wiki/Paramount_Pictures) acquired the rights to all live-action films DreamWorks had released between 1997 and 2005, following [Viacom](/wiki/Viacom_(1952%E2%80%932005))'s $1.6 billion acquisition of the company's live-action film assets and television assets. Additionally, Paramount signed a six-year distribution agreement for past and future [DreamWorks Animation](/wiki/DreamWorks_Animation) films, with DreamWorks Animation having spun off into a separate company from the live-action division in 2004. On August 16, 2010, [Paramount Home Entertainment](/wiki/Paramount_Home_Entertainment) released a 10 film box set titled the "DreamWorks Animation Ultimate Box Set", which included *Shrek*, *[Shrek 2](/wiki/Shrek_2)*, *[Shrek the Third](/wiki/Shrek_the_Third)* and 7 other DreamWorks Animation films. A 3D version of the film was released on [Blu-ray 3D](/wiki/Blu-ray_3D) by Paramount Home Entertainment on December 1, 2010, along with its sequels, and a regular 2D [Blu-ray](/wiki/Blu-ray) boxset of the series was released six days later.

On December 31, 2012, DreamWorks Animation's distribution agreement with Paramount officially ended, and in July 2014, DreamWorks Animation announced they had reacquired the distribution rights to all of their films from Paramount, transferring these rights to their new distribution partner [20th Century Fox](/wiki/20th_Century_Fox). On April 28, 2016, DreamWorks Animation was purchased by [NBCUniversal](/wiki/NBCUniversal) for $3.8 billion. In commemoration of the film's 20th anniversary, an [Ultra HD Blu-ray](/wiki/Ultra_HD_Blu-ray) edition was released on May 11, 2021, by [Universal Pictures Home Entertainment](/wiki/Universal_Pictures_Home_Entertainment).

## Reception

### Box office

*Shrek* opened on around 6,000 screens across 3,587 theaters, at that point the second highest number of locations in history behind only *[Mission: Impossible 2](/wiki/Mission:_Impossible_2)*. Eleven locations screened the film digitally, the first time that DreamWorks had shown one of its films in this format. The film earned $11.6 million on its first day and $42.3 million on its opening weekend, topping the box office for the weekend and averaging $11,805 from 3,587 theaters. In its [second weekend](/wiki/Second_weekend_in_box_office_performance#Second_weekend_increase), due to the Memorial Day Weekend holiday, the film gained 0.3 percent to $42.5 million and $55.2 million over the four-day weekend, resulting in an overall 30 percent gain. Despite this, the film finished in second place behind *[Pearl Harbor](/wiki/Pearl_Harbor_(film))* and had an average of $15,240 from expanding to 3,623 sites. In its third weekend, the film retreated 34 percent to $28.2 million for a $7,695 average from expanding to 3,661 theaters. By mid-June 2001, *Shrek* became the top-grossing movie of the year domestically, defeating *[The Mummy Returns](/wiki/The_Mummy_Returns)*. This achievement would last until that December when it was beaten by *[Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone](/wiki/Harry_Potter_and_the_Philosopher%27s_Stone_(film))*. The film closed on December 6, 2001, after grossing $267.7 million domestically, along with $216.7 million overseas, for a worldwide total of $484.4 million. Produced on a $60 million budget, the film was a huge box office smash and is the [fourth highest-grossing film of 2001](/wiki/2001_in_film#Highest-grossing_films).

*Shrek* became the highest-grossing animated film ever to be released in [Australia](/wiki/Australia), passing the mark set by *[The Lion King](/wiki/The_Lion_King)* in 1994. In the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom), *Shrek* regained the top spot at the British box office after being beaten out the previous week by *[Lara Croft: Tomb Raider](/wiki/Lara_Croft:_Tomb_Raider)*, earning a $20.3 million since its opening in the UK.

### Critical response

In addition to being an instant box office success, *Shrek* received wide critical acclaim. 88% of 212 professional reviews of the film on the [review aggregator](/wiki/Review_aggregator) website [Rotten Tomatoes](/wiki/Rotten_Tomatoes) are positive, with an average rating of 7.8/10. The website's critical consensus reads, "While simultaneously embracing and subverting fairy tales, the irreverent *Shrek* also manages to tweak Disney's nose, provide a moral message to children, and offer viewers a funny, fast-paced ride." [Metacritic](/wiki/Metacritic) assigned *Shrek* a weighted average score of 84 out of 100 based on 34 critics, indicating "universal acclaim". Audiences polled by [CinemaScore](/wiki/CinemaScore) gave the film an average grade of "A" on an A+ to F scale.

Murphy was particularly praised by reviewers for his performance as Donkey.

[Roger Ebert](/wiki/Roger_Ebert) praised the film, giving it four stars out of a possible four and describing it as "jolly and wicked, filled with sly in-jokes and yet somehow possessing a heart". *[USA Today](/wiki/USA_Today)*'s Susan Wloszczyna praised [Eddie Murphy](/wiki/Eddie_Murphy)'s performance, stating it "gives the comic performance of his career, aided by sensational digital artistry, as he brays for the slightly neurotic motormouth". [Richard Schickel](/wiki/Richard_Schickel) of *[Time](/wiki/Time_(magazine))* also enjoyed Murphy's role, stating "No one has ever made a funnier jackass of himself than Murphy." Peter Rainer of *[New York](/wiki/New_York_(magazine))* magazine liked the script, also stating "The animation, directed by Andrew Adamson and Vicky Jenson, is often on the same wriggly, giggly level as the script, although the more "human" characters, such as Princess Fiona and Lord Farquaad, are less interesting than the animals and creatures—a common pitfall in animated films of all types." [Peter Travers](/wiki/Peter_Travers) of *[Rolling Stone](/wiki/Rolling_Stone)* wrote "*Shrek* is a world-class charmer that could even seduce the academy when it hands out the first official animation Oscar next year." James Berardinelli of *[ReelViews](/wiki/ReelViews)* gave the film three and a half stars out of four, saying "*Shrek* is not a guilty pleasure for sophisticated movie-goers; it is, purely and simply, a pleasure." Kenneth Turan of the *[Los Angeles Times](/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)* wrote "The witty, fractured fairy tale *Shrek* has a solid base of clever writing." Lisa Schwarzbaum of *[Entertainment Weekly](/wiki/Entertainment_Weekly)* gave the film an A−, saying "A kind of palace coup, a shout of defiance, and a coming of age for DreamWorks." Jay Boyar of the *[Orlando Sentinel](/wiki/Orlando_Sentinel)* wrote "It's a pleasure to be able to report that the movie both captures and expands upon the book's playful spirit of deconstruction."

Steven Rosen of *[The Denver Post](/wiki/The_Denver_Post)* wrote "DreamWorks Pictures again proves a name to trust for imaginative, funny animated movies that delight kids and adults equally." Susan Stark of *[The Detroit News](/wiki/The_Detroit_News)* gave the film four out of four stars, saying "Swift, sweet, irreverent, rangy and as spirited in the writing and voice work as it is splendid in design." Jami Bernard of the *[New York Daily News](/wiki/New_York_Daily_News)* gave the film four out of four stars, saying "The brilliance of the voice work, script, direction and animation all serve to make *Shrek* an adorable, infectious work of true sophistication." Rene Rodriguez gave the film three out of four stars, calling it "a gleefully fractured fairy tale that never becomes cynical or crass". Elvis Mitchell of *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* gave the film four out of five stars, saying "Beating up on the irritatingly dainty Disney trademarks is nothing new; it's just that it has rarely been done with the demolition-derby zest of *Shrek*." [William Steig](/wiki/William_Steig), the author of the original book, and his wife Jeanne Steig also enjoyed the film, stating "We all went sort of expecting to hate it, thinking, 'What has Hollywood done to it?' But we loved it. We were afraid it would be too sickeningly cute and, instead, Bill just thought they did a wonderful, witty job of it."

John Anderson of *[Newsday](/wiki/Newsday)* wrote "The kind of movie that will entertain everyone of every age and probably for ages to come." Jay Carr of *[The Boston Globe](/wiki/The_Boston_Globe)* wrote "In an era when much on film seems old, *Shrek* seems new and fresh and clever." Stephen Hunter of *[The Washington Post](/wiki/The_Washington_Post)* gave the film five out of five stars, saying "Despite all its high-tech weirdness, it is really that most perdurable of human constructions, a tale told well and true." Joe Baltake of *[The Sacramento Bee](/wiki/The_Sacramento_Bee)* wrote that it "isn't so much a fractured spoof of everything Disney, but actually a Monty Python flick for kids – kids of all ages". Andrew Sarris of *[The New York Observer](/wiki/The_New_York_Observer)* wrote "What gives *Shrek* its special artistic distinction is its witty and knowingly sassy dialogue, delivered by vocally charismatic performers whose voices remind us of their stellar screen personae in live-action movies." Lisa Alspector of the *[Chicago Reader](/wiki/Chicago_Reader)* wrote "This romantic fantasy complicates the roles of beauty and beast, making it hard to guess what form a sensitive resolution will take." Joe Morgenstern of *[The Wall Street Journal](/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal)* wrote "The charms of *Shrek*, which is based on the children's book by William Steig, go far beyond in-jokes for adults." John Zebrowski of *[The Seattle Times](/wiki/The_Seattle_Times)* gave the film three out of four stars, saying "The movie is helped immensely by its cast, who carry it through some of the early, sluggish scenes. But this is Murphy's movie. Donkey gets most of the good lines, and Murphy hits every one."

A mixed review came from Mark Caro of the *[Chicago Tribune](/wiki/Chicago_Tribune)*, who gave the film two and a half stars out of four and compared it to *[Toy Story 2](/wiki/Toy_Story_2)*, which he said "had a higher in-jokes/laughs ratio without straining to demonstrate its hipness or to evoke heartfelt emotions". On the more negative side, Michael Atkinson of *[The Village Voice](/wiki/The_Village_Voice)* said he was "desperately avoiding the risk of even a half-second of boredom", and said "the movie is wall-to-window-to-door noise, babbling, and jokes (the first minute sees the first fart gag), and demographically it's a hard-sell shotgun spray." Christy Lemire of the *[Associated Press](/wiki/Associated_Press)* described *Shrek* as a "90-minute onslaught of in-jokes", and said while it "strives to have a heart" with "a message about beauty coming from within", "somehow [the message] rings hollow". Anthony Lane of *[The New Yorker](/wiki/The_New_Yorker)* said, despite the film "cunning the rendering of surfaces, there's still something flat and charmless in the digital look, and most of the pleasure rises not from the main romance but from the quick, incidental gags."

## Legacy

### Rank

*[Entertainment Weekly](/wiki/Entertainment_Weekly)* put *Shrek* on its end-of-the-decade "best-of" list for the 2000s, saying, "Prince Charming? So last millennium. This decade, fairy-tale fans – and Princess Fiona – fell for a fat and flatulent Ogre. Now, that's progress." In June 2008, the [American Film Institute](/wiki/American_Film_Institute) revealed its "[Ten top Ten](/wiki/AFI%27s_10_Top_10)"; the best ten films in ten "classic" American film genres—after polling over 1,500 people from the creative community *Shrek* was acknowledged as the eighth best film in the animated genre, and the only non-Disney·[Pixar](/wiki/Pixar) film in the Top 10. *Shrek* was also ranked second in a [Channel 4](/wiki/Channel_4) poll of the "100 Greatest Family Films", losing out on the top spot to *[E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial](/wiki/E.T._the_Extra-Terrestrial)*. In 2005, *Shrek* came sixth in Channel 4's 100 Greatest Cartoons poll behind *[The Simpsons](/wiki/The_Simpsons)*, *[Tom and Jerry](/wiki/Tom_and_Jerry)*, *[South Park](/wiki/South_Park)*, *[Toy Story](/wiki/Toy_Story)* and *[Family Guy](/wiki/Family_Guy)*. In November 2009, the character, Lord Farquaad, was listed No. 14 in [IGN UK](/wiki/IGN_UK)'s "Top 15 Fantasy Villains". In 2006, it was ranked third on [Bravo](/wiki/Bravo_(U.S._TV_channel))'s 100 funniest films list. The film's title character was awarded his own star on the [Hollywood Walk of Fame](/wiki/Hollywood_Walk_of_Fame) in May 2010.

- [AFI's 100 Years...100 Songs](/wiki/AFI%27s_100_Years...100_Songs):

### Cultural impact

*The New York Times* retrospectively described the film as "a beloved, offbeat fairy tale whose characters and jokes continue to permeate [pop culture](/wiki/Pop_culture), reaching another generation of fans." Previous films and television shows, such as *[Fractured Fairy Tales](/wiki/Fractured_Fairy_Tales)* and *[The Princess Bride](/wiki/The_Princess_Bride_(film))*, had parodied the traditional fairy tale. *Shrek* had a significant impact that influenced a later generation of mainstream animated films, receiving recognition for being one of the most influential from the 2000s. Animated films such as *[Ice Age](/wiki/Ice_Age_(2002_film))* and *[Despicable Me](/wiki/Despicable_Me_(film))* began to incorporate more pop culture references. It also inspired a number of computer animated films which also spoofed fairy tales, or other related story genres, often including adult-oriented humor, such as *[Happily N'Ever After](/wiki/Happily_N%27Ever_After)*, *[Hoodwinked!](/wiki/Hoodwinked!)*, and *[Enchanted](/wiki/Enchanted_(film))*. In 2020, the United States [Library of Congress](/wiki/Library_of_Congress) selected *Shrek* to be preserved in the [National Film Registry](/wiki/National_Film_Registry) for being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".

## Other media

See also: [Shrek The Musical](/wiki/Shrek_The_Musical)

Several [video game](/wiki/Video_game) adaptations of the film have been published on various [game console](/wiki/Video_game_console) platforms, including *[Shrek](/wiki/Shrek_(video_game))* (2001), *[Shrek: Hassle at the Castle](/wiki/Shrek:_Hassle_at_the_Castle)* (2002), *[Shrek: Extra Large](/wiki/Shrek:_Extra_Large)* (2002), *[Shrek: Super Party](/wiki/Shrek:_Super_Party)* (2002) and *[Shrek SuperSlam](/wiki/Shrek_SuperSlam)* (2005). Shrek was also included as a bonus unlockable character in the video game *[Tony Hawk's Underground 2](/wiki/Tony_Hawk%27s_Underground_2)* (2004).

In 2003, [Dark Horse Comics](/wiki/Dark_Horse_Comics) released a three-issue mini-series [comic book](/wiki/Comic_book) adaptation of *Shrek* which was written by Mark Evanier, and the issues were later compiled into a [trade paperback](/wiki/Trade_paperback_(comics)).

A [musical](/wiki/Musical_theatre) version, based on the film, with music by [Jeanine Tesori](/wiki/Jeanine_Tesori) and a book and lyrics by [David Lindsay-Abaire](/wiki/David_Lindsay-Abaire), opened on [Broadway](/wiki/Broadway_theatre) on December 14, 2008, and closed January 3, 2010, running for a total of 441 performances. It starred [Brian d'Arcy James](/wiki/Brian_d%27Arcy_James) in the title role, [Sutton Foster](/wiki/Sutton_Foster) as Princess Fiona, [Christopher Sieber](/wiki/Christopher_Sieber) as Lord Farquaad, [Daniel Breaker](/wiki/Daniel_Breaker) as Donkey, and [John Tartaglia](/wiki/John_Tartaglia) as Pinocchio. The Broadway production was recorded and released on [DVD](/wiki/DVD), [Blu-ray](/wiki/Blu-ray) and digital media. A North American Tour opened July 25, 2010, in [Chicago](/wiki/Chicago_(band)). A [London](/wiki/London) production opened in the [West End](/wiki/West_End_theatre) on June 7, 2011. The musical received many [Tony Award](/wiki/Tony_Award) nominations and won the 2009 [Tony Award for Best Costume Design](/wiki/Tony_Award_for_Best_Costume_Design). It received five [Laurence Olivier Award](/wiki/Laurence_Olivier_Award) nominations including [Best New Musical](/wiki/Laurence_Olivier_Award_for_Best_New_Musical).

A [shot-for-shot](/wiki/Shot-for-shot) fan remake titled *[Shrek Retold](/wiki/Shrek_Retold)* was released through 3GI Industries on November 29, 2018. The project was a collaboration of 200 filmmakers and mixes live action, hand drawn animation, Flash animation, CGI and various other art forms to recreate the film. The film is available on [YouTube](/wiki/YouTube) for free.

## Future

Main article: [Shrek (franchise)](/wiki/Shrek_(franchise))

Three sequels were released over the years – the Oscar-nominated *[Shrek 2](/wiki/Shrek_2)* in 2004, *[Shrek the Third](/wiki/Shrek_the_Third)* in 2007, and *[Shrek Forever After](/wiki/Shrek_Forever_After)* in 2010. *Shrek 2* was the only one to receive similar acclaim from critics, though all three sequels were commercially successful. *[Shrek the Halls](/wiki/Shrek_the_Halls)* (2007) and *[Scared Shrekless](/wiki/Scared_Shrekless)* (2010) were released as holiday-themed short films. A spin-off prequel film entitled *[Puss in Boots](/wiki/Puss_in_Boots_(2011_film)),* centered on the titular character's life before his debut in *Shrek 2*, was released in 2011, while a sequel, titled *[Puss in Boots: The Last Wish](/wiki/Puss_in_Boots:_The_Last_Wish)* and set after the events of *Shrek Forever After*, was released in 2022. A fifth feature film was originally planned during the development of *Shrek Forever After*, but the idea was later abandoned by DreamWorks Animation CEO Jeffrey Katzenberg. Leading up to NBCUniversal's planned acquisition of DreamWorks Animation in 2016, it was announced that a fifth Shrek film would be released in 2019. On November 6, 2018, *[Variety](/wiki/Variety_(magazine))* reported that [Chris Meledandri](/wiki/Chris_Meledandri) had been tasked to reboot both Shrek and Puss in Boots, with the original cast potentially returning to reprise their roles. While cast members reported that a script was completed for a fifth Shrek film, development stalled and future plans have yet to be officially announced. After it was reported in April 2023 that a fifth *Shrek* film with the original cast and a spinoff featuring Donkey was planned, Murphy said in June 2024 that he had begun voice recording sessions for *Shrek 5* months ago and would begin working for the Donkey spinoff once it was finished. A fifth *Shrek* film is scheduled to be released in 2027.
