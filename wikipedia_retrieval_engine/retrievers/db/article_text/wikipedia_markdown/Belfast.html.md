This article is about the city in Northern Ireland. For other uses, see [Belfast (disambiguation)](/wiki/Belfast_(disambiguation)).

**Belfast** ([/ˈbɛlfæst/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [ⓘ](/wiki/File:LL-Q1860_(eng)-Vealhurl-Belfast.wav), [*BEL-fast*](/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key), [/-fɑːst/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English), [*-⁠fahst*](/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key); from [Irish](/wiki/Irish_language): *Béal Feirste* [[bʲeːlˠ ˈfʲɛɾˠ(ə)ʃtʲə]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Irish)[ⓘ](/wiki/File:Uladh_-_Aontroim_-_B%C3%A9al_Feirste.wav)) is the capital city and principal port of [Northern Ireland](/wiki/Northern_Ireland), standing on the banks of the [River Lagan](/wiki/River_Lagan) and connected to the open sea through [Belfast Lough](/wiki/Belfast_Lough) and the [North Channel](/wiki/North_Channel_(Great_Britain_and_Ireland)). It is the largest city in Northern Ireland and the second-largest city in [Ireland](/wiki/Ireland) (after [Dublin](/wiki/Dublin)), with an estimated population of 352,390 in 2024, and [its metropolitan area](/wiki/Belfast_metropolitan_area) has a population of 671,559.

While chartered as an English settlement in 1613, the town's early growth was driven by an influx of [Scottish](/wiki/Scottish_people) [Presbyterians](/wiki/Presbyterian_Church_in_Ireland). Their descendants' disaffection with [Ireland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland)'s [Anglican establishment](/wiki/Protestant_Ascendancy) contributed to the [rebellion of 1798](/wiki/Irish_Rebellion_of_1798), and to the [union](/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1800) with [Great Britain](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain) that followed in 1801—later regarded as a key to the town's industrial transformation. When granted [city status](/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom#Northern_Ireland) in 1888, Belfast was the world's largest centre of [linen](/wiki/Linen) manufacture, and by the 1900s its shipyards were building up to a quarter of total [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland) tonnage.

[Sectarian](/wiki/Sectarianism) tensions existed with the [Irish Catholic](/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Ireland) population that was drawn by mill and factory employment from western counties. Heightened by division over Ireland's future in the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland), these twice erupted in periods of sustained violence: [in 1920–22](/wiki/The_Troubles_in_Ulster_(1920%E2%80%931922)), as Belfast emerged as the capital of the [six northeast counties](/wiki/Partition_of_Ireland) retaining the British connection, and over [three decades from the late 1960s](/wiki/The_Troubles) during which the [British Army](/wiki/British_Army) was continually deployed on the streets. A legacy of conflict is the [barrier-reinforced separation](/wiki/Peace_lines) of [Protestant](/wiki/Protestantism_in_Ireland) and Catholic [working-class](/wiki/Working_class) districts.

Since the [Good Friday Agreement](/wiki/1998_Northern_Ireland_Good_Friday_Agreement_referendum), the electoral balance in the once [unionist](/wiki/Unionism_in_Ireland)-controlled city has shifted, albeit with no overall majority, in favour of [Irish nationalists](/wiki/Irish_nationalism). At the same time, new immigrants are adding to the growing number of residents unwilling to identify with either of the two communal traditions.

Belfast has seen significant [services sector](/wiki/Tertiary_sector_of_the_economy) growth, with important contributions from financial technology ([fintech](/wiki/Fintech)), tourism and, with facilities in the redeveloped [Harbour Estate](/wiki/Belfast_Harbour), film. It retains a port with commercial and industrial docks, including a reduced [Harland & Wolff](/wiki/Harland_%26_Wolff) shipyard and aerospace and defence contractors. Under the [Windsor Framework](/wiki/Windsor_Framework), Belfast and Northern Ireland remain within the United Kingdom’s internal market while continuing to apply aspects of the [EU single market](/wiki/European_single_market) rules for goods.

The city is served by two airports: [Belfast City Airport](/wiki/Belfast_City_Airport), located on the Lough shore, and [Belfast International Airport](/wiki/Belfast_International_Airport) (also known as Aldergrove), located 15 miles (24 kilometres) west of the city. Belfast supports two universities: [Ulster University](/wiki/Ulster_University) in the city centre, and in the Botanic area in the south of the city, [Queen's University Belfast](/wiki/Queen%27s_University_Belfast). Since 2021, Belfast has been a [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) designated [City of Music](/wiki/City_of_Music_(UNESCO)).

## History

Main article: [History of Belfast](/wiki/History_of_Belfast)

### Name

A 1685 plan of Belfast by the military engineer [Thomas Phillips](/wiki/Thomas_Phillips_(engineer)), showing the town's ramparts and [Lord Chichester's castle](/wiki/Belfast_Castle#Plantation_Castle), which was destroyed in a fire in 1708

The name Belfast derives from the Irish *Béal Feirste* (Irish pronunciation: [[bʲeːlˠ ˈfʲɛɾˠ(ə)ʃtʲə]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Irish)), "Mouth of the [Farset](/wiki/River_Farset)", a river whose name in the Irish, *Feirste,* refers to a sandbar or tidal ford. This was formed where the river ran—until culverted late in the 18th century, down High Street— into the Lagan. It was at this crossing, located under or close to the current Queen's Bridge, that the early settlement developed.

The compilers of [Ulster-Scots](/wiki/Ulster_Scots_dialects) use various transcriptions of local pronunciations of "Belfast" (with which they sometimes are also content) including *Bilfawst*, *Bilfaust* or *Baelfawst.*

As a legacy of emigration, Belfast has lent its name to more than a dozen settlements in the United States, of which the largest is [Belfast, Maine](/wiki/Belfast,_Maine), and to one each [in Australia](/wiki/Belfast,_Victoria), [Canada](/wiki/Belfast,_Prince_Edward_Island), [New Zealand](/wiki/Belfast,_New_Zealand) and [South Africa](/wiki/Belfast,_Mpumalanga).

### Early settlements

The site of Belfast has been occupied since the [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age). The [Giant's Ring](/wiki/Giants_Ring,_Belfast), a 5,000-year-old [henge](/wiki/Henge), is located near the city, and the remains of [Iron Age](/wiki/Iron_Age) [hill forts](/wiki/Hill_fort) can still be seen in the surrounding hills. At the beginning of the 14th century, [Papal](/wiki/Papacy) tax rolls record two churches: the "Chapel of Dundela" at Knock (Irish: *[cnoc](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/cnoc)*, meaning "hill") in the east, connected by some accounts to the 7th-century evangelist [St. Colmcille](/wiki/Columba),and, the "Chapel of the Ford", which may have been a successor to a much older parish church on the present [Shankill *(Seanchill*, "Old Church") Road](/wiki/Shankill_Road), dating back to the 9th, and possibly to [St. Patrick](/wiki/Saint_Patrick) in the mid 5th, century.

A [Norman](/wiki/Normans_in_Ireland) settlement at the ford, comprising the parish church (now [St. George's](/wiki/St_George%27s_Church,_Belfast)), a watermill, and a small fort, was an outpost of [Carrickfergus Castle](/wiki/Carrickfergus_Castle). Established in the late 12th century, 11 miles (18 km) out along the north shore of the Lough, Carrickfergus was to remain the principal English foothold in the north-east until the [scorched- earth](/wiki/Scorched_earth) [Nine Years' War](/wiki/Nine_Years%27_War_(Ireland)) at the end of the 16th century broke the remaining Irish power, the [O'Neills](/wiki/O%27Neill_dynasty).

### Developing port, radical politics

With a commission from [King James VI and I](/wiki/James_VI_and_I), in 1613 [Sir Arthur Chichester](/wiki/Arthur_Chichester,_1st_Baron_Chichester) undertook the [Plantation](/wiki/Plantations_of_Ireland) of Belfast and the surrounding area, attracting mainly English and [Manx](/wiki/Isle_of_Man) settlers. The subsequent arrival of [Scottish Presbyterians](/wiki/Ulster_Scots_people) embroiled Belfast in its only recorded siege: denounced from London by [John Milton](/wiki/John_Milton) as "ungrateful and treacherous guests", in 1649 the newcomers were temporarily expelled by an English [Parliamentarian](/wiki/Roundhead) army. In 1689, Catholic [Jacobite](/wiki/Jacobitism) forces, briefly in command of the town, abandoned it in advance of the landing at Carrickfergus of [William, Prince of Orange](/wiki/William_III_of_England), who proceeded through Belfast to his celebrated victory on 12 July 1690 at [the Boyne](/wiki/Battle_of_the_Boyne).

Together with French [Huguenots](/wiki/Huguenots), the Scots introduced the production of [linen](/wiki/Linen), a [flax](/wiki/Flax)-spinning industry that in the 18th century carried Belfast trade to the Americas. Fortunes were made carrying rough linen clothing and salted provisions to the [slave plantations](/wiki/Slave_plantation) of the [West Indies](/wiki/West_Indies); sugar and rum to [Baltimore](/wiki/Baltimore) and New York City; and for the return to Belfast [flaxseed](/wiki/Flax) and tobacco from [the colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies). From the 1760s, profits from the trade financed improvements in the town's commercial infrastructure, including the [Lagan Canal](/wiki/Lagan_Canal), new docks and quays, and the construction of the White Linen Hall which together attracted to Belfast the linen trade that had formerly gone through [Dublin](/wiki/Dublin). [Abolitionist](/wiki/Abolitionism) sentiment, however, defeated the proposal of the greatest of the merchant houses, [Cunningham and Greg](/wiki/Waddell_Cunningham), in 1786 to commission ships for the [Middle Passage](/wiki/Middle_Passage).

Volunteer Corps parade down High Street, [Bastille Day](/wiki/Bastille_Day), 1792

As "Dissenters" from the [established Anglican church](/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) (with its [episcopacy](/wiki/Episcopacy) and ritual), [Presbyterians](/wiki/Presbyterian_Church_in_Ireland) were conscious of sharing, if only in part, the [disabilities](/wiki/Penal_Laws_against_Irish_Catholics) of Ireland's dispossessed [Roman Catholic](/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Ireland) majority; and of being denied representation in the [Irish Parliament](/wiki/Parliament_of_Ireland). Belfast's two [MPs](/wiki/Member_of_parliament) remained nominees of the Chichesters ([Marquesses of Donegall](/wiki/Marquess_of_Donegall)). With their emigrant kinsmen in America, the region's Presbyterians were to share a growing disaffection from [the Crown.](/wiki/The_British_Crown)

When early in the [American War of Independence](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), [Belfast Lough was raided](/wiki/North_Channel_Naval_Duel) by the [privateer](/wiki/Privateer) [John Paul Jones](/wiki/John_Paul_Jones), the townspeople assembled their own [Volunteer militia](/wiki/Irish_Volunteers_(18th_century)). Formed ostensibly for defence of [the Kingdom](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland), Volunteer corps were soon pressing their own protest against "taxation without representation". Further emboldened by the [French Revolution](/wiki/French_Revolution), a more radical element in the town, the [Society of United Irishmen](/wiki/Society_of_United_Irishmen), called for [Catholic emancipation](/wiki/Catholic_emancipation) and a representative national government. In hopes of French assistance, in 1798 the Society organised a republican insurrection. The rebel tradesmen and tenant farmers were defeated north of the town at the [Battle of Antrim](/wiki/Battle_of_Antrim) and to the south at the [Battle of Ballynahinch](/wiki/Battle_of_Ballynahinch).

Britain seized on the rebellion to abolish the [Irish Parliament](/wiki/Parliament_of_Ireland), unlamented in Belfast, and to [incorporate Ireland](/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1800) in a [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland). In 1832, British [parliamentary reform](/wiki/Reform_Act_1832) permitted the town its first electoral contest – an occasion for an early and lethal [sectarian](/wiki/Sectarianism) riot.

### Industrial expansion, sectarian division

High Street, c. 1906

While other Irish towns experienced a loss of manufacturing, from the 1820s Belfast underwent rapid industrial expansion. After a cotton boom and bust, the town emerged as the global leader in the production of [linen](/wiki/Linen) goods (mill, and finishing, work largely employing women and children), winning the moniker "[Linenopolis](/wiki/Economy_of_Belfast)". Shipbuilding led the development of heavier industry. By the 1900s, her shipyards were building up to a quarter of the total United Kingdom tonnage, and on the eve of the [Great War](/wiki/World_War_I), in 1914, close one eighth of world production. This included from the yard of [Harland & Wolff](/wiki/Harland_%26_Wolff) the ill-fated RMS *[Titanic](/wiki/Titanic),* at the time of her launch in 1911 the largest ship afloat. Other major export industries included textile machinery, rope-making, tobacco and mineral waters.

Industry drew in a new Catholic population settling largely in the west of the town—refugees from a rural poverty intensified by Belfast's mechanisation of spinning and weaving and, in the 1840s, by [famine](/wiki/Great_Famine_(Ireland)). The plentiful supply of cheap labour helped attract English and Scottish capital to Belfast, but it was also a cause of insecurity. Protestant workers organised and dominated the apprenticed trades and gave a new lease of life to the once largely rural [Orange Order](/wiki/Orange_Order). Sectarian tensions, which frequently broke out in riots and workplace expulsions, were also driven by the "constitutional question": the prospect of a restored Irish parliament in which Protestants (and northern industry) feared being a minority interest.

On 28 September 1912, [unionists](/wiki/Unionism_in_Ireland) massed at [Belfast's City Hall](/wiki/Belfast_City_Hall) to sign the [Ulster Covenant](/wiki/Ulster_Covenant), pledging to use "all means which may be found necessary to defeat the present [conspiracy](/wiki/Conspiracy_(political)) to set up a [Home Rule](/wiki/Home_Rule) Parliament in Ireland". This was followed by the drilling and eventual arming of a 100,000-strong [Ulster Volunteer Force](/wiki/Ulster_Volunteers) (UVF). The immediate crisis was averted by the onset of the [Great War](/wiki/World_War_I). The UVF formed the [36th (Ulster) Division](/wiki/36th_(Ulster)_Division) whose sacrifices in the [Battle of the Somme](/wiki/Battle_of_the_Somme) continue to be commemorated in the city by unionist and [loyalist](/wiki/Ulster_loyalism) organisations.

In 1920–22, as Belfast emerged as the capital of the six counties remaining as [Northern Ireland](/wiki/Northern_Ireland) in the United Kingdom, there was [widespread violence](/wiki/The_Troubles_in_Ulster_(1920%E2%80%931922)). 8,000 "disloyal" workers were driven from their jobs in the shipyards: in addition to Catholics, "rotten Prods" – Protestants whose labour politics disregarded sectarian distinctions. Gun battles, grenade attacks and house burnings contributed to as many as 500 deaths. A curfew remained in force until 1924.

The lines drawn saw off the challenge to "unionist unity" posed by [labour](/wiki/Belfast_Labour_Party). Industry had been paralysed by [strikes in 1907](/wiki/1907_Belfast_Dock_strike) and again in 1919 (when the city was effectively policed by strikers). Until "troubles" returned at the end of the 1960s, it was not uncommon in Belfast for the [Ulster Unionist Party](/wiki/Ulster_Unionist_Party) to have its [council](/wiki/Category:Belfast_City_Council_elections) and [parliamentary](/wiki/Category:General_elections_to_the_Parliament_of_Northern_Ireland) candidates returned unopposed.

In 1932, the opening of the new buildings for [Northern Ireland's devolved Parliament](/wiki/Parliament_of_Northern_Ireland) at [Stormont](/wiki/Stormont_Buildings) was overshadowed by the protests of the unemployed and ten days of running street battles with the police. The government conceded increases in [Outdoor Relief](/wiki/Outdoor_relief), but labour unity was short lived. In 1935, celebrations of [King George V](/wiki/George_V)'s Jubilee and of the annual Twelfth were followed by deadly riots and expulsions, a sectarian logic that extended itself to the interpretation of darkening events in Europe. [Labour candidates](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Labour_Party) found support for the [anti-clerical](/wiki/Anti-clericalism) [Spanish Republic](/wiki/Second_Spanish_Republic) (marked today by a [*No Pasaran!*](/wiki/No_Pasaran) stained glass window in City Hall) characterised as another instance of [No-Popery](/wiki/Popery).

In 1938, nearly a third of industrial workers were unemployed, [malnutrition](/wiki/Malnutrition) was a major issue, and at 9.6% the city's [infant mortality](/wiki/Infant_mortality) rate (compared with 5.9% in [Sheffield](/wiki/Sheffield), England) was among the highest in United Kingdom.

### The Blitz and post-war development

Main article: [Belfast Blitz](/wiki/Belfast_Blitz)

Aftermath of the Blitz in May 1941

In the spring of 1941, the German *[Luftwaffe](/wiki/Luftwaffe)* appeared four times over Belfast. In addition to the shipyards and the [Short & Harland](/wiki/Short_and_Harland) aircraft factory, the [Belfast Blitz](/wiki/Belfast_Blitz) severely damaged or destroyed more than half the city's housing stock, and devastated the old town centre around High Street. In the greatest loss of life in any air raid outside of London, more than a thousand people were killed.

At the end of the [Second World War](/wiki/Second_World_War), the Unionist government undertook programmes of ["slum clearance](/wiki/Slum_clearance_in_the_United_Kingdom)" (the Blitz had exposed the "uninhabitable" condition of much of the city's housing) which involved decanting populations out of mill and factory built red-brick terraces and into new peripheral housing estates. At the same time, a British-funded [welfare state](/wiki/Welfare_state) "revolutionised access" to education and health care. The resulting rise in expectations; together with the uncertainty caused by the decline of the city's [Victorian-era](/wiki/Victorian_era) industries, contributed to growing protest, and counter protest, in the 1960s over the [Unionist government](/wiki/Government_of_Northern_Ireland_(1921%E2%80%931972))'s record on civil and political rights.

### The Troubles

Main article: [The Troubles](/wiki/The_Troubles)

For reasons that [nationalists](/wiki/Irish_nationalism) and [unionists](/wiki/Unionism_in_Ireland) dispute, the public protests of the late 1960s soon gave way to communal violence (in which as many as 60,000 people were intimidated from their homes) and to [loyalist](/wiki/Ulster_loyalism) and [republican](/wiki/Irish_republicanism) [paramilitarism](/wiki/Paramilitary). Introduced onto the streets in August 1969, the [British Army](/wiki/Operation_Banner) committed to the longest continuous deployment in its history, [Operation Banner](/wiki/Operation_Banner). Beginning in 1970 with the [Falls curfew](/wiki/Falls_Curfew), and followed in 1971 by [internment](/wiki/Operation_Demetrius), this included [counterinsurgency](/wiki/Counterinsurgency) measures directed chiefly at the [Provisional Irish Republican Army](/wiki/Provisional_Irish_Republican_Army). The PIRA characterised their operations, including the bombing of Belfast's commercial centre, as a struggle against British occupation.

Preceded by loyalist and republican ceasefires, the 1998 ["Good Friday" Belfast Agreement](/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement) returned a new [power-sharing](/wiki/Power_sharing) [legislative assembly](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly) and [executive](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Executive) to Stormont. In the intervening years in Belfast, some 20,000 people had been injured, and 1,500 killed.

Eighty-five percent of the conflict-related deaths had occurred within 1,000 metres of the communal [interfaces](/wiki/Interface_area), largely in the north and west of the city. The security barriers erected at these interfaces are an enduring physical legacy of the Troubles. The 14 neighbourhoods they separate are among the 20 most deprived [wards](/wiki/Ward_(electoral_subdivision)) in Northern Ireland. In May 2013, the [Northern Ireland Executive](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Executive) committed to the removal of all peace lines by mutual consent. The target date of 2023 was passed with only a small number dismantled.

The more affluent districts escaped the worst of the violence, but the city centre was a major target. This was especially so during the first phase of the PIRA campaign in the early 1970s, when the organisation hoped to secure quick political results through maximum destruction. Including [car bombs](/wiki/Car_bomb) and incendiaries, between 1969 and 1977 the city experienced 2,280 explosions. In addition to the death and injury caused, they accelerated the loss of the city's Victorian fabric.

### 21st century

Since the turn of the century, the loss of employment and population in the city centre has been reversed. This reflects the growth of the [service economy](/wiki/Service_economy), for which a new district has been developed on former dockland, the [Titanic Quarter](/wiki/Titanic_Quarter). The growing tourism sector paradoxically lists as attractions the [murals](/wiki/Murals_in_Northern_Ireland) and peace walls that echo the violence of the past. In recent years, "Troubles tourism" has presented visitors with new territorial markers: flags, murals and graffiti in which [loyalists](/wiki/Ulster_loyalism) and [republicans](/wiki/Irish_republicanism) take opposing sides in the [Israeli-Palestinian conflict](/wiki/Israeli%E2%80%93Palestinian_conflict).

The demographic balance of some areas has been changed by immigration (according to the 2021 census just under 10% of the city's population was born outside the British Isles), by local differences in births and deaths between Catholics and Protestants, and by a growing number of, particularly younger, people no longer willing to self-identify on traditional lines.

In 1997, unionists lost overall control of [Belfast City Council](/wiki/Belfast_City_Council) for the first time in its history. The election in 2011 saw Irish nationalist councillors outnumber unionist councillors, with [Sinn Féin](/wiki/Sinn_F%C3%A9in) becoming the largest party, and the cross-community [Alliance Party](/wiki/Alliance_Party_of_Northern_Ireland) holding the balance of power.

In the [2016 Brexit referendum](/wiki/2016_United_Kingdom_European_Union_membership_referendum), Belfast's four parliamentary constituencies returned a substantial majority (60 percent) for remaining within the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union), as did Northern Ireland as a whole (55.8), the only [UK region](/wiki/UK_regions) outside London and Scotland to do so. In February 2022, the [Democratic Unionist Party](/wiki/Democratic_Unionist_Party), which had actively campaigned for Brexit, withdrew from the power-sharing executive and collapsed the Stormont institutions to protest the 2020 UK-EU [Northern Ireland Protocol](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Protocol). With the promise of equal access to the British and European markets, this designates Belfast as a point of entry to the [European Single Market](/wiki/European_single_market) within whose regulatory framework local producers will continue to operate. After two years, the standoff was resolved with an agreement to eliminate routine checks on UK-destined goods.

## Cityscape

### Location and topography

Satellite image of Belfast with Lough

Belfast is at the mouth of the River Lagan at the head of Belfast Lough open through the [North Channel](/wiki/North_Channel_(Great_Britain_and_Ireland)) to the [Irish Sea](/wiki/Irish_Sea) and to the North Atlantic. In the course of the 19th century, the location's estuarine features were re-engineered. With dredging and reclamation, the lough was made to accommodate a deep sea port, and extensive shipyards. The Lagan was banked (in 1994 a [weir](/wiki/Lagan_Weir) raised its water level to cover what remained of the tidal mud flats) and its various tributaries were culverted On the model pioneered in 2008 by the Connswater Community Greenway some, including the course of the Farset, are now being considered for "daylighting".

It remains the case that much of the city centre is built on an estuarine bed of "sleech": silt, peat, mud and—a source the city's ubiquitous red brick— soft clay, that presents a challenge for high-rise construction. (In 2007 this unstable foundation persuaded [St Anne's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Anne%27s_Cathedral,_Belfast) to abandon plans for a bell tower and substitute a lightweight steel spire). The city centre is also subject to tidal flood risk. Rising sea levels could mean, that without significant investment, flooding in the coming decades will be persistent.

The city is overlooked on the [County Antrim](/wiki/County_Antrim) side (to the north and northwest) by a precipitous [basalt](/wiki/Basalt) [escarpment](/wiki/Escarpment)—the near continuous line of [Divis](/wiki/Divis) Mountain (478 m), [Black Mountain](/wiki/Black_Mountain_(Belfast)) (389 m) and [Cavehill](/wiki/Cavehill) (368 m)—whose "heathery slopes and hanging fields are visible from almost any part of the city". From [County Down](/wiki/County_Down) side (on the south and south east) it is flanked by the lower-lying [Castlereagh](/wiki/Castlereagh_(borough)) and [Hollywood](/wiki/Holywood,_County_Down) hills. The sand and gravel [Malone](/wiki/Malone_Road) Ridge extends up river to the south-west.

### North Belfast and Shankill

From 1820, Belfast began to spread rapidly beyond its 18th century limits. To the north, it stretched out along roads which drew into the town migrants from Scots-settled hinterland of [County Antrim](/wiki/County_Antrim). Largely Presbyterian, they enveloped a number of Catholic-occupied "[mill-row](/wiki/Tied_cottage)" clusters: [New Lodge](/wiki/New_Lodge,_Belfast), [Ardoyne](/wiki/Ardoyne) and "the Marrowbone". Together with areas of more substantial housing in the [Oldpark district](/wiki/Oldpark_(District_Electoral_Area)), these are wedged between Protestant working-class housing stretching from [Tiger's Bay](/wiki/Shore_Road,_Belfast) out the Shore Road on one side, and up the [Shankill](/wiki/Shankill_Road) (the original Antrim Road) on the other.

The Greater Shankill area, including [Crumlin](/wiki/Crumlin_Road) and [Woodvale](/wiki/Court_(District_Electoral_Area)), is over the line from the [Belfast North](/wiki/Belfast_North_(UK_Parliament_constituency)) parliamentary/assembly constituency, but is physically separated from the rest of [Belfast West](/wiki/Belfast_West_(UK_Parliament_constituency)) by an extensive series of separation barriers—[peace walls](/wiki/Peace_lines)—owned (together with five daytime gates into the [Falls area](/wiki/Falls_Road,_Belfast)) by the [Department of Justice](/wiki/Department_of_Justice_(Northern_Ireland)). These include Cupar Way where tourists are informed that, at 45 feet, the barrier is "three times higher than the [Berlin Wall](/wiki/Berlin_Wall) and has been in place for twice as long".

With other working-class districts, Shankill suffered from the "collapse of old industrial Belfast". But it was also greatly affected from the 1960s by the city's most ambitious programme of "slum clearance". Red-brick, "two up, two down" terraced streets, typical of 19th century working-class housing, were replaced with flats, maisonettes, and car parks but few facilities. In a period of twenty years, due largely to redevelopment, 50,000 residents left the area leaving an aging population of 26,000 and more than 100 acres of wasteland.

Meanwhile, [road schemes](/wiki/Belfast_Urban_Motorway), including the terminus of the [M1 motorway](/wiki/M1_motorway_(Northern_Ireland)) and the [Westlink](/wiki/Westlink_(road)), demolished a mixed dockland community, [Sailortown](/wiki/Sailortown_(Belfast)), and severed the streets linking the Shankill area and the rest of both north and west Belfast to the city centre.

New "green field" housing estates were built on the outer edges of the city. The onset of the Troubles overwhelmed attempts to promote these as "mixed" neighbourhoods so that the largest of these developments on the city's northern edge, [Rathcoole](/wiki/Rathcoole_(Newtownabbey)), rapidly solidified as a [loyalist](/wiki/Ulster_loyalism) community. In 2004, it was estimated that 98% of public housing in Belfast was divided along religious lines.

Among the principal landmarks of north Belfast are the [Crumlin Road Gaol](/wiki/HM_Prison_Belfast) (1845) now a major visitor attraction, [Belfast Royal Academy](/wiki/Belfast_Royal_Academy) (1785) - the oldest school in the city, [St Malachy's College](/wiki/St_Malachy%27s_College) (1833), [Holy Cross Church, Ardoyne](/wiki/Holy_Cross_Church,_Ardoyne) (1902), [Waterworks Park](https://www.belfastcity.gov.uk/things-to-do/parks-and-open-spaces/a-z-parks/waterworks) (1889), and [Belfast Zoo](/wiki/Belfast_Zoo) (1934).

### West Belfast

In the mid-19th century rural poverty and famine drove large numbers of Catholic tenant farmers, landless labourers and their families toward Belfast. Their route brought them down the [Falls Road](/wiki/Falls_Road,_Belfast) and into what are now remnants of an older Catholic enclave around [St Mary's Church](/wiki/St_Mary%27s_Church,_Belfast), the town's first Catholic chapel (opened in 1784 with Presbyterian subscriptions), and [Smithfield Market](/wiki/Smithfield_Market_and_Library_Quarter,_Belfast). Eventually, an entire west side of the city, stretching up the Falls Road, along the [Springfield Road](/wiki/Springfield_Road) (encompassing the new housing estates built 1950s and 60s: Highfield, New Barnsley, Ballymurphy, Whiterock and Turf Lodge) and out past [Andersonstown](/wiki/Andersonstown) on the Stewartstown Road toward [Poleglass](/wiki/Poleglass), became near-exclusively Catholic and, in political terms, nationalist.

Reflecting the nature of available employment as mill workers, domestics and shop assistants, the population, initially, was disproportionately female. Further opportunities for women on the Falls Road arose through developments in education and public health. In 1900, the [Dominican Order](/wiki/Dominican_Order) opened [St Mary's [Teacher] Training College](/wiki/St_Mary%27s_University_College,_Belfast), and in 1903 [King Edward VII](/wiki/Edward_VII) opened the [Royal Victoria Hospital](/wiki/Royal_Victoria_Hospital,_Belfast) at the junction with the Grosvenor Road. Extensively redeveloped and expanded, the hospital has a staff of more than 8,500.

Landmarks in the area include the [Gothic-revival](/wiki/Gothic_Revival_architecture) [St Peter's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Peter%27s_Cathedral,_Belfast) (1866, signature twin spires added in 1886); [Clonard Monastery](/wiki/Clonard_Monastery) (1911), the Conway Mill (1853/1901, re-developed as a community enterprise, arts and education centre in 1983); [Belfast City Cemetery](/wiki/Belfast_City_Cemetery) (1869) and, best known for its republican graves, [Milltown Cemetery](/wiki/Milltown_Cemetery) (1869).

The area's greatest visitor attractions are its wall and gable-end murals. In contrast to those in loyalist areas, where Israel is typically the only outside reference, these range more freely beyond the local conflict frequently expressing solidarity with [Palestinians](/wiki/Palestinians), with [Cuba](/wiki/Cuba), and with [Basque](/wiki/Basque_Country_(autonomous_community)) and [Catalan](/wiki/Catalonia) separatists.

### South Belfast

West Belfast is separated from South Belfast, and from the otherwise abutting loyalist districts of [Sandy Row](/wiki/Sandy_Row) and the [Donegall Road](/wiki/Donegall_Road), by rail lines, the [M1 Motorway](/wiki/M1_motorway_(Northern_Ireland)) (to Dublin and the west); industrial and retail parks, and the remnants of the [Blackstaff](/wiki/Blackstaff_River) (Owenvarra) bog meadows.

Belfast began stretching up-river in the 1840s and 50s: out the [Ormeau](/wiki/Ormeau_Road) and [Lisburn roads](/wiki/Lisburn_Road) and, between them, running along a ridge of higher ground, the [Malone Road](/wiki/Malone_Road). From "leafy" avenues of increasingly substantial (and in the course of time "mixed") housing, the Upper Malone broadened out into areas of parkland and villas.

Further out still, where they did not survive as public parks, from the 1960s the great-house demesnes of the city's former mill-owners and industrialists were developed for public housing: loyalist estates such as Seymour Hill and Belvoir. Meanwhile, in Malone and along the river embankments, new houses and apartment blocks have been squeezed in, increasing the general housing density.

Beyond the Queen's University area the area's principal landmarks are the 15-storey tower block of [Belfast City Hospital](/wiki/Belfast_City_Hospital) (1986) on the Lisburn Road, and the Lagan Valley Regional Park through which a [towpath](/wiki/Towpath) extends from the City-centre quayside to Lisburn.

Northern Ireland's [three permanent diplomatic missions](/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_in_Northern_Ireland) are situated on the Malone Road, the consulates of China, Poland and the United States.

### East Belfast

The first district on the right bank of the Lagan (the [County Down](/wiki/County_Down) side) to be incorporated in Belfast was [Ballymacarrett](/wiki/Ballymacarrett) in 1853. [Harland & Wolff](/wiki/Harland_%26_Wolff), whose gantry cranes, [Samson & Goliath](/wiki/Samson_and_Goliath_(cranes)), tower over the area, was long the mainstay of employment — although less securely so for the [townland](/wiki/Townland)'s Catholics (In 1970, when the yard still had a workforce of 10,000, only 400 Catholics were employed). Tolerated in periods of expansion as [navvies](/wiki/Navvy) and casual labourers, they concentrated in a small enclave, the [Short Strand](/wiki/Short_Strand), which has continued into this century to feature as a sectarian flashpoint. Home to around 2,500 people, it is the only distinctly nationalist area in the east of the river.

East Belfast developed from the [Queens Bridge](/wiki/Queen%27s_Bridge,_Belfast) (1843), through Ballymacarrett, east along the [Newtownards Road](/wiki/Newtownards_Road) and north (along the east shore of the Lough) up the [Holywood](/wiki/Holywood,_County_Down) Road; and from the [Albert Bridge](/wiki/Albert_Bridge,_Belfast) (1890) south east out the [Cregagh](/wiki/Cregagh) and Castlereagh roads. The further out, the more substantial, and less religiously segregated, the housing until again encountering the city's outer ring of public housing estates: loyalist Knocknagoney, [Lisnasharragh](/wiki/Lisnasharragh_(District_Electoral_Area)), and [Tullycarnet](/wiki/Tullycarnet).

This century, efforts have been made to add to East Belfast's two obvious visitor attractions: Samson & Goliath (the "banana yellow" Harland & Wolff cranes date only from the early 1970s) and the [Parliament Buildings at Stormont](/wiki/Parliament_Buildings_(Northern_Ireland)). What is marketed now as EastSide, features, at the intersection of the Connswater and Comber Greenways and next to the EastSide Visitor Centre, [CS Lewis](/wiki/C._S._Lewis) Square (2017), named and themed in honour of the local author of *[The Chronicles of Narnia](/wiki/The_Chronicles_of_Narnia).* Next to the former the Harland & Wolff Drawing Offices (now an hotel), stands the "cultural nucleus to Titanic Quarter", [*Titanic* Belfast](/wiki/Titanic_Belfast) (2012) whose interactive galleries tell the liner's ill-fated story.

In 2015, the [Orange Order](/wiki/Orange_Order) opened the Museum of Orange Heritage on the Cregagh Road with the aim of educating the wider public about "the origins, traditions and continued relevance" of the parading institution.

### City Centre

Inside St. Georges Market in 2017

[Belfast City Centre](/wiki/Belfast_City_Centre) is roughly bounded by the ring roads constructed since the 1970s: the [M3](/wiki/M3_motorway_(Northern_Ireland)) which sweeps across the dockland to the north; the Westlink that connects to the [M1](/wiki/M1_motorway_(Northern_Ireland)) for points south and west; and, with less certainty, the Bruce Street and Bankmore connectors that tie back toward the Lagan at the Gasworks Business Park and the beginning of the Ormeau Road. This embraces "the Markets", the one remaining inner-city area of housing. Of the various markets, including those for the sale and shipping of livestock, from which it derives its name, only one survives, the former produce market, [St George's Market](/wiki/St_George%27s_Market), now a food and craft market popular with visitors to the city.

#### Architectural heritage

Belfast Charitable Society, [Clifton House](/wiki/Clifton_House,_Belfast), 1774

Among surviving elements of the pre-Victorian town are the [Belfast Entries](/wiki/The_Belfast_Entries), 17th-century alleyways off High Street, including, in Winecellar Entry, White's Tavern (rebuilt 1790); the elliptical First [Presbyterian (Non-Subscribing)](/wiki/Non-subscribing_Presbyterian_Church_of_Ireland) Church (1781–83) in Rosemary Street (whose members led the abolitionist charge against Greg and Cunningham); the [Assembly Rooms](/wiki/Assembly_Rooms,_Belfast) (1769, 1776, 1845) on Bridge Street; [St George's Church of Ireland](/wiki/St_George%27s_Church,_Belfast) (1816) on the High Street site of the old Corporation Church; [St Mary's Church](/wiki/St_Mary%27s_Church,_Belfast) (1782) in Chapel Lane, which is the oldest Catholic church in the city. The oldest public building in Belfast, [Clifton House](/wiki/Clifton_House,_Belfast) (1771–74), the [Belfast Charitable Society](/wiki/Belfast_Charitable_Society) [poorhouse](/wiki/Poorhouse), is on North Queen Street. It is now partly cut off from the city centre by arterial roads. In addition there are small sets of city-centre [Georgian](/wiki/Georgian_architecture) terraces.

Of the much larger Victorian city a substantial legacy has survived the [Blitz](/wiki/Belfast_Blitz), The Troubles and planning and development. Among the more notable examples are [St Malachy's Roman Catholic Church](/wiki/St_Malachy%27s_Church,_Belfast) (1844) and the original college building of [Queen's University Belfast](/wiki/Queen%27s_University_Belfast) (1849), both in a [Tudor style](/wiki/Tudor_style_architecture); the Palm House in the [Botanic Gardens](/wiki/Botanic_Gardens_(Belfast)) (1852); the [Renaissance revival](/wiki/Renaissance_Revival_architecture) [Union Theological College](/wiki/Union_Theological_College) (1853) and Ulster Bank (now [Merchant Hotel](/wiki/Merchant_Hotel_(Belfast))) (1860); the [Italianate](/wiki/Italianate_architecture) [Ulster Hall](/wiki/Ulster_Hall) (1862), and the National Trust restored ornate [Crown Liquor Saloon](/wiki/Crown_Liquor_Saloon) (1885, 1898) (a setting for the classic film, *[Odd Man Out](/wiki/Odd_Man_Out)*, starring [James Mason](/wiki/James_Mason)); the oriental-themed [Grand Opera House](/wiki/Grand_Opera_House_(Belfast)) (1895) (bombed several times during the Troubles), and the Romanesque revival [St. Patrick's Catholic Church](/wiki/St_Patrick%27s_Church,_Belfast) in Donegall Street (1877).

Scottish Provident Institution, 1902

The [Baroque revival](/wiki/Baroque_Revival_architecture) [City Hall](/wiki/Belfast_City_Hall) was finished in 1906 on the site of the former White Linen Hall, and was built to reflect Belfast's city status, granted by [Queen Victoria](/wiki/Queen_Victoria) in 1888. Its [Edwardian](/wiki/Edwardian_Baroque_architecture) design influenced the [Victoria Memorial](/wiki/Victoria_Memorial_(India)) in [Calcutta](/wiki/Kolkata), India, and [Durban](/wiki/Durban) City Hall in South Africa. The dome is 173 ft (53 m) high and figures above the door state "[Hibernia](/wiki/Hibernia) encouraging and promoting the Commerce and Arts of the City".

Across from the City Hall, on Donegall Sq. West, is the Renaissance and Baroque revival Scottish Provident Institution (1902). On the east side, a branch of the [Ulster Bank](/wiki/Ulster_Bank) is built behind the classical portico of a former Methodist church dating from 1846. On the north side, facing the City Hall is the once palatial, copper-domed, "Royal Irish Linen Warehouse" of Robinson & Cleaver (1888), until its closure in 1984, the city's premier department store. Next door, its successor, [Marks and Spencers](/wiki/Marks_%26_Spencer), is housed behind the red sandstone, Florentine Gothic, facade (1869) of a rival linen business that was burned out in the [Blitz](/wiki/Belfast_Blitz).

Built in the [Romanesque-style](/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) on the site of an earlier [neo-classical](/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture) church, [St Anne's Church of Ireland Cathedral](/wiki/St_Anne%27s_Cathedral,_Belfast) was consecrated in 1904. The north transept, featuring on its exterior "the largest [Celtic cross](/wiki/Celtic_cross) in Ireland", was completed in 1981, and a final addition, a 40-metre stainless steel "Spire of Hope" was installed in 2007.

The neoclassical [Royal Courts of Justice](/wiki/Royal_Courts_of_Justice,_Belfast) were opened on Oxford Street in 1933.

#### Redevelopment

The opening [Victoria Square Shopping Centre](/wiki/Victoria_Square_Shopping_Centre) in 2008 was to symbolise the rebound of the city centre since its days as a restricted security zone during the Troubles. But retail footfall in the centre is limited by competition with out-of-town shopping centres and with internet retailing. As of November 2023, footfall had not recovered pre-[COVID pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) levels. There are compensating trends: the growth in tourism and hospitality which has included a sustained boom in hotel construction.

The City Council also talks of a "residential-led regeneration". New townhouse and apartments schemes are being developed for the city's quays, and for Titanic Quarter. The completion in 2023 of Ulster University's enhanced Belfast campus (in "one of the largest higher education capital builds in Europe") and the determination of Queen's University to compete with the private sector in the provision of student housing, has fostered the construction downtown of multiple new student residences.

#### Rough sleeping and homelessness

People can be found sleeping rough on the streets of the city centre. Numbers, while growing, may be comparatively small for a city of its size in the British Isles. In 2022, counts and estimates by the [Northern Ireland Housing Executive](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Housing_Executive) identified a total of 26 rough sleepers in Belfast. This is against a background (in 2023) of 2,317 people (0.67% of residents) presenting as homeless, many of whom are in temporary accommodation and shelters. Such figures, however, do not include all those living in severely overcrowded conditions, involuntarily sharing with other households on a long-term basis, or sleeping rough in hidden locations.

### The "Quarters"

St Anne's Cathedral

Since 2001, buoyed by increasing numbers of tourists, the city council has promoted a number of cultural [quarters](/wiki/Belfast_quarters).

The [Cathedral Quarter](/wiki/Cathedral_Quarter,_Belfast) comprises much of Belfast's old trade and warehousing district in the narrow streets and entries around [St Anne's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Anne%27s_Cathedral,_Belfast), with a concentration of bars, beer gardens, clubs and restaurants (including two establishments claiming descent from the early town, White's and The Duke of York) and performance spaces (most notably the Black Box and [Oh Yeah](/wiki/Oh_Yeah_(music_centre))). It hosts a yearly [visual and performing arts festival](/wiki/Cathedral_Quarter_Arts_Festival). The adjoining [Custom House](/wiki/Custom_House,_Belfast) Square is one of the city's main outdoor venues for free concerts and street entertainment.

Without defined geographical boundaries, the [Gaeltacht Quarter](/wiki/Gaeltacht_Quarter,_Belfast) encompasses [Irish-speaking](/wiki/Irish_language) Belfast. (According to the 2021 census, 15.5% of people in the city have [some knowledge of Irish](/wiki/Irish_language_in_Northern_Ireland), 4% speak it daily). It is generally understood as an area around the Falls Road in west Belfast served by the [Cultúrlann McAdam Ó Fiaich](/wiki/Cult%C3%BArlann_McAdam_%C3%93_Fiaich) cultural centre. It can be said to include, at the Skainos Centre in unionist east Belfast, [Turas](/wiki/Turas), a project that promotes Irish through night classes and cultural events in the belief that "the language belongs to all".

Aerial view of Belfast (2004)

The [Linen Quarter](/wiki/Linen_Quarter,_Belfast)', an area south of City Hall once dominated by linen warehouses, now includes, in addition to cafés, bars and restaurants, a dozen hotels (including the 23-storey [Grand Central Hotel](/wiki/Grand_Central_Hotel_Belfast)), and the city's two principal Victorian-era cultural venues, the [Grand Opera House](/wiki/Grand_Opera_House,_Belfast) and the [Ulster Hall](/wiki/Ulster_Hall).

Moving further south along the so-called ["Golden Mile"](/wiki/Golden_Mile_(Belfast)) of bars and clubs through [Shaftesbury Square](/wiki/Shaftesbury_Square), there is the [Queen's [University] Quarter](/wiki/Queen%27s_Quarter,_Belfast). In addition to the university (spread over 250 buildings, of which 120 are listed as being of architectural merit), it is home to [Botanic Gardens](/wiki/Belfast_Botanic_Gardens) and the [Ulster Museum](/wiki/Ulster_Museum).

Finally, the [Titanic Quarter](/wiki/Titanic_Quarter,_Belfast) covers 0.75 km2 (185 acres) of reclaimed land adjacent to [Belfast Harbour](/wiki/Port_of_Belfast), formerly known as *Queen's Island*. Named after [RMS *Titanic*](/wiki/RMS_Titanic), launched here in 1911, work began in 2003 to transform some former shipyard land into "one of the largest waterfront developments in Europe". The current area houses [*Titanic* Belfast](/wiki/Titanic_Belfast), the [Public Records Office of Northern Ireland](/wiki/Public_Record_Office_of_Northern_Ireland) (PRONI), two hotels, and multiple condo towers and shops, and the Titanic [film] Studios.

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Belfast](/wiki/Culture_of_Belfast)

### Arts venues and festivals

From [Georgian](/wiki/Georgian_era) Belfast, the city retains a civic legacy. In addition to [Clifton House](/wiki/Clifton_House,_Belfast) (Belfast Charitable Society, 1774), this includes the [Linen Hall Library](/wiki/Linen_Hall_Library) (Belfast Society for Promoting Knowledge, 1788), the [Ulster Museum](/wiki/Ulster_Museum) (founded in 1833 by the [Belfast Natural History Society](/wiki/Belfast_Natural_History_Society) as the Belfast Municipal Museum and Art Gallery), and the [Botanic Gardens](/wiki/Botanic_Gardens_(Belfast)) (established in 1828 by the Belfast Botanic and Horticultural Society). These remain important cultural venues: in the case of the Gardens, for outdoor festivities including the Belfast [Melā](/wiki/Mel%C4%81), the city's annual August celebration of global cultures.

Of the many stage venues built in the nineteenth century, and film theatres built in the twentieth, there remains the [Ulster Hall](/wiki/Ulster_Hall) (1862), which hosts concerts (including those of the [Ulster Orchestra](/wiki/Ulster_Orchestra)), [classical](/wiki/European_classical_music) [recitals](/wiki/Concert#Recital) and party-political meetings; the [Grand Opera House](/wiki/Grand_Opera_House,_Belfast) (1895) badly damaged in bomb blasts in the early 1990s, restored and enlarged 2020; the [Strand Cinema](/wiki/Strand_Cinema) (1935) now being developed as an arts centre; and the [Queens Film Theatre](/wiki/Queen%27s_Film_Theatre) (QFT) (1968) focussed on [art house](/wiki/Art_film) and [world cinema](/wiki/World_cinema). The two independent cinemas offer their screens for the [Belfast Film Festival](/wiki/Belfast_Film_Festival) and the [Belfast International Arts Festival](/wiki/Belfast_Festival_at_Queen%27s).

The principal stage for drama remains the [Lyric Theatre](/wiki/Lyric_Theatre,_Belfast) (1951, 2011), the largest employer of actors and other theatre professionals in the region. At Queens University, drama students stage their productions at the [Brian Friel](/wiki/Brian_Friel) Theatre, a 120-seat studio space (named after the renowned playwright).

In November 2011, Belfast became the smallest city to host the [MTV Europe Music Awards](/wiki/2011_MTV_Europe_Music_Awards). The event was made possible by the 11,000-seat Odyssey Arena (today the [SSE Arena](/wiki/SSE_Arena,_Belfast)) which opened in 2000 at the entrance to the [Titanic Quarter](/wiki/Titanic_Quarter) Among other annual events, the SSE Arena hosts the [Belfast International Tattoo](/wiki/Belfast_International_Tattoo) which aims to showcase [Ulster-Scots](/wiki/Ulster_Scots_people) heritage alongside international acts, including [pipe bands](/wiki/Pipe_band), [military](/wiki/Military_band) and civilian musicians, and dancers. A further large-scale venue is the [Waterfront Hall](/wiki/Waterfront_Hall), a multi-purpose conference and entertainment centre that first opened in 1997. The main circular Auditorium seats 2,241 and is modelled on the [Berlin Philharmonic Hall](/wiki/Berliner_Philharmonie). In 2012, the [Metropolitan Arts Centre](/wiki/Metropolitan_Arts_Centre), the "MAC", was opened in the [Cathedral Quarter](/wiki/Cathedral_Quarter,_Belfast), offering a performance mix of music, theatre, dance and visual art.

The [Belfast International Tattoo](/wiki/Belfast_International_Tattoo) is an annual music and dance event held at the [Odyssey Arena](/wiki/Odyssey,_Belfast), Belfast. Inspired by the tradition of [military tattoos](/wiki/Military_tattoo), it features performances from [pipe bands](/wiki/Pipe_band), [military](/wiki/Military_band) and civilian musicians, dancers, and cultural groups from Northern Ireland and around the world. Established in 2013, the event aims to showcase [Ulster-Scots](/wiki/Ulster_Scots_people) heritage alongside international acts, attracting large audiences from across the region and beyond.

The city has a number of community arts, and arts education, centres, among them the [Crescent Arts Centre](/wiki/Crescent_Arts_Centre) in south Belfast, the Irish-language [Cultúrlann McAdam Ó Fiaich](/wiki/Cult%C3%BArlann_McAdam_%C3%93_Fiaich) in west Belfast, The Duncairn in north Belfast and, in the east of the city, EastSide Arts.

[Féile an Phobail](/wiki/F%C3%A9ile_an_Phobail), a community arts organisation born out of the Internment Commemorations in the west of the city, stages one of the largest community festivals in Europe. It has grown from its original *August Féile* on the Falls Road, to a year-round programme with a broad range of arts events, talks and discussions.

### UNESCO City of Music

In November 2021, Belfast became the third city in the [British Isles](/wiki/British_Isles) to be designated by [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) as [City of Music](/wiki/City_of_Music_(UNESCO)) (after Glasgow in 2008 and Liverpool in 2016) and is one of 59 cities worldwide participating in the [UNESCO Creative Cities Network](/wiki/Creative_Cities_Network).

The greater part of Belfast's music scene is accommodated in the city's [pubs](/wiki/Pub) and clubs. [Irish traditional music](/wiki/Irish_traditional_music) ("trad") is a staple, and is supported, along with [Ulster-Scots](/wiki/Ulster_Scots_people) [snare drum](/wiki/Snare_drum) and [pipe](/wiki/Bagpipes) music, by the city's TradFest summer school.

Music offerings also draw on the legacy of the punk and the underground club scene that developed during [The Troubles](/wiki/The_Troubles) (associated with the groups [Stiff Little Fingers](/wiki/Stiff_Little_Fingers) and [The Undertones](/wiki/The_Undertones), and celebrated in the award-winning 2013 film, [Good Vibrations](/wiki/Good_Vibrations_(film))). [Snow Patrol](/wiki/Snow_Patrol)'s frontman [Gary Lightbody](/wiki/Gary_Lightbody) led a line up of private donors that together with public funders established the [Oh Yeah music centre](/wiki/Oh_Yeah_(music_centre)) in 2008. The Cathedral Quarter non-profit supports young musicians and these have engaged with a range of genres including [Alternative rock](/wiki/Alternative_rock), [Indie rock](/wiki/Indie_rock), [Electronica](/wiki/Electronica), [Post rock](/wiki/Post_rock), [Post punk](/wiki/Post_punk), [Crossover](/wiki/Crossover_(music)), and [Experimental rock](/wiki/Experimental_rock).

Queens University hosts the [Sonic Arts Research Centre](/wiki/Sonic_Arts_Research_Centre) (SARC), an institute for music-based practice and research. Its purpose designed building, Sonic Laboratory and multichannel studios were opened by [Karlheinz Stockhausen](/wiki/Karlheinz_Stockhausen), the German composer and "father of electronic music", in 2004.

### Media

[Broadcasting House, Belfast](/wiki/Broadcasting_House,_Belfast), headquarters of the BBC in Northern Ireland

Belfast is the home of the *[Belfast Telegraph](/wiki/Belfast_Telegraph)*, *[Irish News](/wiki/Irish_News)* and, first printed in 1737,*[The News Letter](/wiki/The_News_Letter)*, the oldest [English-language](/wiki/English_language) daily newspaper in the world still in publication.

The city is the headquarters of [BBC Northern Ireland](/wiki/BBC_Northern_Ireland), and [ITV](/wiki/ITV_(TV_network)) station [UTV](/wiki/Ulster_Television). The Irish public service broadcaster, [RTÉ](/wiki/Raidi%C3%B3_Teilif%C3%ADs_%C3%89ireann) has a studio in the city. The national radio station is [BBC Radio Ulster](/wiki/BBC_Radio_Ulster) with commercial radio stations such as [Q Radio](/wiki/Q_Radio_Belfast), [U105](/wiki/U105), [Blast 106](/wiki/Blast_106) and Irish-language station [Raidió Fáilte](/wiki/Raidi%C3%B3_F%C3%A1ilte). [Queen's Radio](/wiki/Queen%27s_Radio), a student-run radio station broadcasts from [Queen's University Students' Union](/wiki/Queen%27s_University_Belfast_Students%27_Union).

One of Northern Ireland's two community TV stations, [NvTv](/wiki/NvTv), is based in the [Cathedral Quarter](/wiki/Cathedral_Quarter,_Belfast) of the city. Broadcasting only over the Internet is Homely Planet, the Cultural Radio Station for Northern Ireland, supporting community relations.

### Parades

Since the lifting in 1872 of a twenty-year [party processions ban](/wiki/Party_Processions_Act_1850), [Orange](/wiki/Orange_Order) parades in celebration of "[the Twelfth](/wiki/The_Twelfth)" [of July] and the bonfires of the previous evening, the eleventh, have been a fixed fixture of the Belfast calendar. On what became a public holiday in 1926, Belfast and guest Orange lodges (from both across Ulster and Scotland) with their pipe, flute, drum and accordion bands muster at Carlisle Circus, and parade through the city centre past the City Hall and out the Lisburn Road to a gathering in "the field" at Barnett Demesne. While some local feeder and return marches have a history of sectarian disturbance, in recent years, events have generally passed off without serious incident. The tradition is documented and celebrated in the Museum of Orange Heritage on the Cregagh Road in East Belfast.

What is sometimes referred to as the Catholic equivalent of the Orangemen, the much smaller [Ancient Order of Hibernians](/wiki/Ancient_Order_of_Hibernians), confines its parades to nationalist areas in west and north Belfast, as do republicans commemorating the [Easter Rising](/wiki/Easter_Rising). In August 1993, in a break with a history of nationalist exclusion from the city centre, a parade marking the [introduction of internment](/wiki/Operation_Demetrius) in the 1971 proceeded up [Royal Avenue](/wiki/Royal_Avenue,_Belfast) toward the City Hall, where it was addressed by Sinn Féin president, [Gerry Adams](/wiki/Gerry_Adams), in front of the statue of [Queen Victoria](/wiki/Queen_Victoria).

Since 1998, the Belfast City Council has funded a city-centre [St. Patrick's Day](/wiki/Saint_Patrick%27s_Day) (17 March) celebration. It is organised by [Féile an Phobail](/wiki/F%C3%A9ile_an_Phobail) as a "carnival" complete with a parade featuring dancers, circus entertainers, floats, and giant puppets. Critical of what they perceive as an evolving nationalist festival, unionists on the City Council observe that "a lot of the Protestant Unionist Loyalist (PUL) community will stay away from the city centre on St Patrick's Day, the same as some stay away on the Twelfth of July".

In 1991, Belfast hosted its first [gay pride](/wiki/Gay_pride) event. Belfast Pride, culminating in a city-centre parade at the end of July, is now one of the biggest annual festivals in the city and, according to its organisers, the largest [LGBT](/wiki/LGBT)+ festival in Ireland.

The [Irish Congress of Trade Unions](/wiki/Irish_Congress_of_Trade_Unions) organises an annual city-centre May Day march and rally. The [International Workers Day](/wiki/International_Workers%27_Day) has been a public holiday since 1978.

In 2021, there were 345,418 residents within the expanded 2015 Belfast local government boundary and 634,600 in the [Belfast Metropolitan Area](/wiki/Belfast_Metropolitan_Area), approximately one third of Northern Ireland's 1.9 million population.

As with many cities, Belfast's inner city is currently characterised by the elderly, students and single young people, while families tend to live on the periphery. Socio-economic areas radiate out from the [Central Business District](/wiki/Belfast_City_Centre), with a pronounced wedge of affluence extending out the Malone Road and Upper Malone Road to the south. Deprivation levels are notable in the inner parts of the north and the west of the city. The areas around the [Falls Road](/wiki/Falls_Road,_Belfast), [Ardoyne](/wiki/Ardoyne) and [New Lodge](/wiki/New_Lodge,_Belfast) (Catholic nationalist) and the [Shankill Road](/wiki/Shankill_Road) (Protestant loyalist) experience some of the highest levels of social deprivation including higher levels of ill health and poor access to services. These areas remain firmly segregated, with 80 to 90 percent of residents being of the one religious designation.

The comparable figures in 2011 were 48.60% Catholic and 42.28% Protestant or other Christian-related denomination.

Insofar as the city's two indigenous minority languages (Irish and [Ulster Scots](/wiki/Ulster_Scots_dialect)) are concerned, figures are made available from the decennial UK census. On census day, 21 March 2021, 14.93% (43,798) in Belfast claimed to have some knowledge of the Irish language, whilst 5.21% (15,294) claimed to be able to speak, read, write and understand spoken Irish. 3.74% (10,963) of residents claimed to use Irish daily and 0.75% (2,192) claimed Irish is their main language. 7.17% (21,025) of people in the city claimed to have some knowledge of Ulster Scots, whilst 0.75% (2,207) claimed to be able to speak, read, write and understand spoken Ulster Scots. 0.83% (2,430) claimed to use Ulster Scots daily.

From the mid to late 19th century, there was a community [of central European Jews](/wiki/Belfast_Jewish_Community) (among its distinguished members, two-time Lord Mayor [Otto Jaffe](/wiki/Otto_Jaffe)) and of Italians in Belfast. In the 2021 census the percentage of the city's residents born outside the United Kingdom had risen to 9.8.

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Belfast](/wiki/Economy_of_Belfast)

[Samson and Goliath](/wiki/Samson_and_Goliath_(cranes)), Harland & Wolff's gantry cranes

### Employment profile

Services (including retail, health, professional & scientific) account for three quarters of jobs in the Belfast. Only 6% remain in manufacturing. The balance is in distribution and construction. In recent years, unemployment has been comparatively low (under 3% in the summer of 2023) for the UK. On the other hand, Belfast has a high rate of people economically inactive (close to 30%). It is a group, encompassing homemakers, full-time carers, students and retirees, that in Belfast has been swollen by the exceptionally large proportion of the population (27%) with [long-term health problems or disabilities](/wiki/Disability_in_Northern_Ireland) (and who, in Northern Ireland generally, are less likely to be employed than in other UK regions).

An early report on the post-Belfast Agreement prospects for the city economy underscored another distinctive feature of its working-age population. While it appeared well qualified, with 24 per cent educated to degree level, at "the other end of the educational spectrum", 26 per cent had no qualifications at all, a much higher share than in English cities.

### Shipbuilding and engineering

Of Belfast's Victorian-era industry, little remains. The last working linen factory—Copeland Linens Limited, based in the Shankill area—closed in 2013. In recent years [Harland & Wolff](/wiki/Harland_%26_Wolff), which at peak production in the Second World War had employed around 35,000 people, has had a workforce of no more than two or three hundred refurbishing oil rigs and fabricating off-shore wind turbines. A £1.6 billion [Royal Navy](/wiki/Royal_Navy) contract has offered the yard a new lease, returning it to shipbuilding in 2025, a prospect secured by the purchase of insolvent yard by Spain's state-owned shipbuilder, [Navantia](/wiki/Navantia).

In 1936, Short & Harland Ltd, a joint venture of [Short Brothers](/wiki/Short_Brothers) and Harland & Wolff, began the manufacture of aircraft in the docks area. In 1989, the British government, which had nationalised the company during the Second World War, sold it to the Canadian aerospace company [Bombardier](/wiki/Bombardier_Inc.). In 2020, it was sold on to the American aerostructure company [Spirit AeroSystems](/wiki/Spirit_AeroSystems). Producing aircraft components, it remains the largest manufacturing concern in Northern Ireland.

Originating in the [Short Brothers](/wiki/Short_Brothers)' missile division, since 2001 the [Thales Group](/wiki/Thales_Group) (owned by the French defense contractor [Thales Air Defence](/wiki/Thales_Air_Defence) Limited) has been producing [short range air defence](/wiki/Short_range_air_defence) and [anti-tank](/wiki/Anti-tank_guided_missile) [missiles](/wiki/Missile) including the [NLAW](/wiki/NLAW) shoulder-launched system and, from 2025, lightweight multirole missiles (LMMs), deployed in the [Russo-Ukrainian war](/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_(2022%E2%80%93present)) by [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine).

In 2025, the [Belfast Port and Harbour Estate](/wiki/Belfast_Harbour) announced capital projects amounting to than £313m over five years. These include a new deepwater quay to accommodate, in addition to larger cruise liners, an expansion the port’s capacity for offshore wind turbine assembly and installation.

### Fintech and cybersecurity

From the 1990s, Belfast established itself as a significant location for call centres and for other back-office services. Attracting U.S. operators such as [Citi](/wiki/Citigroup), [Allstate](/wiki/Allstate), [Liberty Mutual](/wiki/Liberty_Mutual), [Aflac](/wiki/Aflac) and [FD Technologies (Kx Systems)](/wiki/Kx_Systems) and most recently [Bank of America](/wiki/Bank_of_America), it has since been identified by the [UK Treasury](/wiki/HM_Treasury) as "key [fintech](/wiki/Fintech) [financial technology] hub". Fintech's key areas (its "ABCD") are [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence), [blockchain](/wiki/Blockchain), [cloud computing](/wiki/Cloud_computing), and [big data](/wiki/Big_data).

The sector's principal constraint, cyber security, has been addressed since 2004 by the Queens University Institute of Electronics, Communications and Information Technology (IECIT), and its [Centre for Secure Information Technologies](/wiki/Centre_for_Secure_Information_Technologies) (CSIT). The IECIT is the anchor tenant at [Catalyst (science park)](/wiki/Catalyst_(science_park)) in the Titanic Quarter, which hosts a cluster of cyber-security companies.

### Film

Between 2018 and 2023, film and television production based largely in Belfast, and occupying significant new studio capacity in the ports area, contributed £330m to Northern Ireland's economy. There are two 8-acre media complexes (serviced by the adjacent [City Airport](/wiki/Belfast_City_Airport)): the Titanic Studios on Queen's Island (the [Titanic Quarter](/wiki/Titanic_Quarter)) and across the Victoria Channel in [Giant's Park](/wiki/Giant%27s_Park) on the Lough's north foreshore, the Belfast Harbour Studios. Together they offer 226,000 ft2 of studio space, plus offices and workshops, and have attracted U.S. production companies such as [Amazon](/wiki/Amazon_Prime_Video), [HBO](/wiki/HBO) (including all eight series of its fantasy drama *[Game of Thrones](/wiki/Game_of_Thrones)*), [Paramount](/wiki/Paramount_Pictures), [Playtone](/wiki/Universal_Pictures), [Universal](/wiki/Universal_Pictures), and [Warner Bros](/wiki/Warner_Bros._Pictures).

At the beginning of 2024, [Ulster University](/wiki/Ulster_University), in partnership with [Belfast Harbour](/wiki/Belfast_Harbour) and supported by [Northern Ireland Screen](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Screen), announced an £72m investment to add to the complex a new virtual production, research and development, facility, Studio Ulster. Additional studio space is available at Loop Studios (formerly Britvic) on the Castlereagh Road in East Belfast.

In May 2025, Belfast was named "City of Film’"at the Global Production Awards ceremony held during the [Cannes Film Festival](/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival) in France.

### Tourism and hospitality

Northern Ireland's [peace dividend](/wiki/Peace_dividend) since the 1990s, which includes a marked increase in [inward investment](/wiki/Inward_investment), has contributed to a large-scale redevelopment of the city centre. Significant projects included [Victoria Square](/wiki/Victoria_Square,_Belfast), the [Cathedral Quarter](/wiki/Cathedral_Quarter,_Belfast), [Laganside](/wiki/Laganside) with the [Odyssey](/wiki/Odyssey_(Belfast)) complex and the landmark [Waterfront Hall](/wiki/Waterfront_Hall), the new [Titanic Quarter](/wiki/Titanic_Quarter,_Belfast) with its [*Titanic* Belfast](/wiki/Titanic_Belfast) visitor attraction, and the development of the original Short's harbour airfield as [Belfast City Airport](/wiki/Belfast_City_Airport).
These developments reflect a boom in tourism (32 million visitors between 2011 and 2018), and related hotel construction.

This has included an entirely new phenomenon for Belfast: in 1996, the port received its first cruise ship. In 2023, Belfast welcomed 153 calls, 8% up from the pre-[pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) record set in 2019. Ships from 32 different countries landed 320,000 passengers. By 2028, there is to be a new a £90 million deep-water quay capable of accommodating the world’s largest cruise liners.

Belfast has also seen growth of "conflict tourism". To the dismay of some, "tourists take photos of the division lines that are not consigned to history, but are a part of living Belfast: children play football against the walls that tourists flock to. The places and the people themselves have become a spectacle, an attraction." Tourist bosses and guides, however, are satisfied that the greater draw is city's other "must-see attractions", and its "convivial food and nightlife scene".

### EU/GB Trade

[Invest NI](/wiki/Invest_Northern_Ireland), Northern Ireland's economic development agency is pitching Belfast and its hinterland to foreign investors as "only region in the world able to trade goods freely with both [GB](/wiki/Great_Britain) and EU markets". This follows the 2020 [Northern Ireland Protocol](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Protocol) and the 2023 [Windsor Framework](/wiki/Windsor_Framework), agreements between the British government and [European Union](/wiki/European_Union), whereby, post-Brexit, Northern Ireland would effectively remain within the [European Single Market](/wiki/European_single_market) for goods while, in principle, retaining unfettered access to the British domestic market. Despite the DUP's derailment of devolved government in protest, local business leaders largely welcomed the new trade regime, hailing the promise of dual EU-GB access as a critical opportunity.

In February 2024, the DUP consented to a return of the devolved Assembly and Executive on the understanding that neither the EU nor the British government would defend the integrity of their respective internal markets by conducting *routine* checks on the bulk of goods passing through Belfast, or other Northern Ireland, ports.

## Education

See also: [List of primary schools in Belfast](/wiki/List_of_primary_schools_in_Belfast), [List of secondary schools in Belfast](/wiki/List_of_secondary_schools_in_Belfast), and [List of grammar schools in Belfast](/wiki/List_of_grammar_schools_in_Belfast)

### Primary and secondary education

Children from Catholic and Protestant homes in Belfast are taught, for the most part, separately on a pattern that, by the mid-nineteenth century, had been established throughout Ireland. Primary and secondary education is divided between (Catholic) Maintained Schools and (non-Catholic/ "Protestant") Controlled Schools. They are bound by the same curriculum, but their teaching staff are trained separately (in the university colleges of [St Mary's](/wiki/St_Mary%27s_University_College,_Belfast) and [Stranmillis](/wiki/Stranmillis_University_College)).

Since the 1980s, two smaller school sectors have emerged: grant-maintained [Integrated schools](/wiki/Integrated_education_in_Northern_Ireland), which by design bring together children and staff from both communities, and [Irish language medium schools](/wiki/Comhairle_na_Gaelscola%C3%ADochta)

The [Belfast [later *Royal* Belfast] Academical Institution](/wiki/Royal_Belfast_Academical_Institution), opened its doors in 1810 with the intention, in the words of its founder, former [United Irishman](/wiki/Society_of_United_Irishmen) [William Drennan](/wiki/William_Drennan), of being "perfectly unbiased by religious distinctions". The principle was not embraced by the town's middle-classes. In practice, "Inst" provided a [grammar education](/wiki/Grammar_school) to the town's Presbyterian families while Anglicans favoured the older [Royal Belfast Academy](/wiki/Belfast_Royal_Academy) (1785); Catholics, [St Malachy's](/wiki/St_Malachy%27s_College) [diocesan](/wiki/Diocese) college (1833) and [Wesleyans](/wiki/Wesleyans), [Methodist College Belfast](/wiki/Methodist_College_Belfast) (1865).

Denominational lines have since blurred, with Catholics in particular moving into the controlled grammars. But the presence of 18 [selective grammar schools in Belfast](/wiki/Category:Grammar_schools_in_Belfast) is a further feature of post-primary education in Belfast that distinguishes it from that of comparable cities in Great Britain where academic selection was abandoned in the 1960s and 70s. Partly prompted by the [COVID](/wiki/COVID-19) disruption of external testing in 2021/22, some of the city's grammars have begun to review and amend the practice. It is not clear that this will be on terms that reduce the degree of social segregation they have represented within the system.

In 2006, the [Belfast Education and Library Board](/wiki/Belfast_Education_and_Library_Board) became part of the consolidated [Education Authority](/wiki/Education_Authority) for Northern Ireland. In Belfast, the Authority has responsibility for 156 primary, and 48 [secondary](/wiki/List_of_secondary_schools_in_Belfast) schools (including the 18 grammars). The system is marked by stark inequalities in outcome. Around 30% of school leavers in the city do not attain 5 [GCSEs](/wiki/GCSE), A\* - C (including Maths and English). For those in receipt of free school meals, the figure rises to over 50%.

### Further and Higher education

[Belfast Metropolitan College](/wiki/Belfast_Metropolitan_College) ("Belfast Met") is a [further education](/wiki/Further_education) college with three main campuses around the city, including several smaller buildings. Formerly known as [Belfast Institute of Further and Higher Education](/wiki/Belfast_Institute_of_Further_and_Higher_Education), it specialises in [vocational education](/wiki/Vocational_education). The college has over 53,000 students enrolled on full-time and part-time courses, making it one of the largest further education colleges in the UK and the largest in the island of Ireland.

Belfast has two universities. [Queen's University Belfast](/wiki/Queen%27s_University_Belfast) was founded as a college in 1845. In 1908, the Catholic bishops lifted their ban on attendance and Queen's was granted university status. It is a member of the [Russell Group](/wiki/Russell_Group), an association of 24 leading research-intensive universities in the UK, and is one of the largest universities in the UK with over 25,000 students – among them over 4,000 international students.

[Ulster University](/wiki/Ulster_University), created in its current form in 1984, is a multi-centre university with a campus on the edge of the [Cathedral Quarter](/wiki/Cathedral_Quarter,_Belfast) of Belfast. Since 2021, this original "Arts College" campus has undergone a £1.4bn expansion to accommodate offerings across all departments. The project promises to bring 15,500 staff and students into the city, and to generate 5,000 new jobs.

## Governance

Belfast was granted [borough status](/wiki/Borough_status_in_the_United_Kingdom) by [James VI and I](/wiki/James_VI_and_I) in 1613 and official [city status](/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom) by [Queen Victoria](/wiki/Queen_Victoria) in 1888. Since 1973 it has been a [local government district](/wiki/Local_Government_(Boundaries)_Act_(Northern_Ireland)_1971) under local administration by [Belfast City Council](/wiki/Belfast_City_Council).

Belfast has been represented in the [British House of Commons](/wiki/British_House_of_Commons) since 1801, and in [Northern Ireland Assembly](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly), as presently constituted, since 1998.

### Local government

Further information: [Belfast City Council](/wiki/Belfast_City_Council)

Belfast City Council is responsible for a range of powers and services, including land-use and community planning, parks and recreation, building control, arts and cultural heritage. The city's principal offices are those of the [Lord Mayor of Belfast](/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_Belfast), Deputy Lord Mayor and [High Sheriff](/wiki/High_Sheriff). Like other elected positions within the Council such as Committee chairs, these are filled since 1998 using the [D'Hondt system](/wiki/D%27Hondt_system) so that in recent years the position has rotated between councillors from the three largest factions, [Sinn Féin](/wiki/Sinn_F%C3%A9in), the [Democratic Unionist Party](/wiki/Democratic_Unionist_Party) and the [Alliance Party](/wiki/Alliance_Party_of_Northern_Ireland).

The first Lord Mayor of Belfast in 1892, [Daniel Dixon](/wiki/Sir_Daniel_Dixon,_1st_Baronet), like [every mayor but one](/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Belfast) until 1997 (Alliance in 1979), was a [unionist](/wiki/Unionism_in_Ireland). The first [nationalist](/wiki/Irish_nationalism) Lord Mayor of Belfast was [Alban Maginness](/wiki/Alban_Maginness) of the [Social Democratic and Labour Party](/wiki/Social_Democratic_and_Labour_Party) (SDLP) in 1997. The current [Lord Mayor](/wiki/Lord_Mayor) is Councillor [Tracy Kelly](/wiki/Tracy_Kelly) of the DUP, who has been in the position of Lord Mayor since 2 June 2025. Her duties include presiding over meetings of the council, receiving distinguished visitors to the city, representing and promoting the city on the national and international stage.

In 1997, unionists lost overall control of Belfast City Council for the first time in its history, with the Alliance Party holding the balance of power. In 2023, unionists retained just 17 of 60 seats on the council, leaving nationalists (Sinn Féin and the SDLP) just 4 seats short of a majority. In addition to the 11 Alliance members there are four other councillors, 3 [Green](/wiki/Green_Party_Northern_Ireland) and 1 [People Before Profit (PBP)](/wiki/People_Before_Profit), who refuse a nationalist/unionist designation.

### Northern Ireland Assembly and Westminster elections

Further information: [Northern Ireland Assembly](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly) and [Parliament of the United Kingdom](/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom)

See also: [Belfast (Northern Ireland Parliament constituencies)](/wiki/Belfast_(Northern_Ireland_Parliament_constituencies)) and [Belfast (UK Parliament constituency)](/wiki/Belfast_(UK_Parliament_constituency))

As Northern Ireland's capital city, Belfast is host to the [Northern Ireland Assembly](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly) at [Stormont](/wiki/Parliament_Buildings_(Northern_Ireland)), the site of the [devolved](/wiki/Devolution#Northern_Ireland) legislature for Northern Ireland. Belfast is divided into four [Northern Ireland Assembly](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly) and [UK parliamentary](/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom) constituencies: [Belfast North](/wiki/Belfast_North_(UK_Parliament_constituency)), [Belfast West](/wiki/Belfast_West_(UK_Parliament_constituency)), [Belfast South and Mid Down](/wiki/Belfast_South_and_Mid_Down) and [Belfast East](/wiki/Belfast_East_(UK_Parliament_constituency)). All four extend beyond the city boundaries to include parts of [Antrim and Newtownabbey](/wiki/Antrim_and_Newtownabbey_Borough_Council) and [Lisburn and Castlereagh](/wiki/Lisburn_and_Castlereagh) districts. In [United Kingdom elections](/wiki/Elections_in_the_United_Kingdom), each constituency returns one [MP](/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_(United_Kingdom)), on a "[first past the post](/wiki/First-past-the-post_voting)" basis to Westminster. In NI Assembly elections each returns, on the basis of [proportional representation](/wiki/Proportional_representation), five [MLAs](/wiki/Member_of_the_Legislative_Assembly_(Northern_Ireland)) to [Stormont](/wiki/Parliament_Buildings_(Northern_Ireland)).

In the [Northern Ireland Assembly Elections in 2022](/wiki/2022_Northern_Ireland_Assembly_election), Belfast elected 7 [Sinn Féin](/wiki/Sinn_F%C3%A9in), 5 [DUP](/wiki/Democratic_Unionist_Party), 5 [Alliance Party](/wiki/Alliance_Party_of_Northern_Ireland), 1 [SDLP](/wiki/Social_Democratic_and_Labour_Party), 1 [UUP](/wiki/Ulster_Unionist_Party) and 1 [PBP](/wiki/People_Before_Profit_Alliance) MLAs. In the [2017 UK general election](/wiki/2017_United_Kingdom_general_election), the DUP won all but the Sinn Féin stronghold of Belfast West. In the [2019](/wiki/2019_UK_General_Election) and [2024 UK general elections](/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election), they retained only Belfast East, losing Belfast North to Sinn Féin and Belfast South to the SDLP.

## Infrastructure

### Hospitals

The [Belfast Health & Social Care Trust](/wiki/Belfast_Health_and_Social_Care_Trust) is one of five trusts that were created on 1 April 2007 by the [Department of Health](/wiki/Department_of_Health,_Social_Services_and_Public_Safety). Belfast contains most of Northern Ireland's regional specialist centres.

The Royal Hospitals site in west Belfast (junction of Grosvenor and Falls roads) contains two hospitals. The [Royal Victoria Hospital](/wiki/Royal_Victoria_Hospital,_Belfast) (its origins in a number of successive institutions, beginning in 1797 with The Belfast Fever Hospital) provides both local and regional services. Specialist services include cardiac surgery, critical care and the Regional Trauma Centre. The Children's Hospital ([Royal Belfast Hospital for Sick Children](/wiki/Royal_Belfast_Hospital_for_Sick_Children)) provides general hospital care for children in Belfast and provides most of the paediatric regional specialities.

The [Belfast City Hospital](/wiki/Belfast_City_Hospital) (evolved from the 1841 [Belfast Union Workhouse](/wiki/Belfast_Union_Workhouse) and infirmary) on the Lisburn Road is the regional specialist centre for haematology and is home to a major cancer centre. The Mary G McGeown Regional Nephrology Unit at the [City Hospital](/wiki/Belfast_City_Hospital) is the kidney transplant centre and provides regional renal services for Northern Ireland.
[Musgrave Park Hospital](/wiki/Musgrave_Park_Hospital) (1920) in south Belfast specialises in [orthopaedics](/wiki/Orthopedic_surgery), [rheumatology](/wiki/Rheumatology), [sports medicine](/wiki/Sports_medicine) and [rehabilitation](/wiki/Rehabilitation_psychology). It is home to Northern Ireland's first Acquired Brain Injury Unit.

The [Mater Hospital](/wiki/Mater_Infirmorum_Hospital) (founded in 1883 by the [Sisters of Mercy](/wiki/Sisters_of_Mercy)) on the Crumlin Road provides a wide range of services, including acute inpatient, emergency and maternity services, to north Belfast and the surrounding areas.

The [Ulster Hospital](/wiki/Ulster_Hospital), Upper Newtownards Road, [Dundonald](/wiki/Dundonald,_County_Down), on the eastern edge of the city, first founded as the Ulster Hospital for Women and Sick Children in 1872, is the major acute hospital for the [South Eastern Health and Social Care Trust](/wiki/South_Eastern_Health_and_Social_Care_Trust). It delivers a full range of outpatient, inpatient and daycare medical and surgical services.

### Transport

Main article: [Transport in Belfast](/wiki/Transport_in_Belfast)

Belfast is a relatively [car-dependent](/wiki/Car_dependency) city by European standards, with an extensive road network including the 22.5 miles (36 km) [M2](/wiki/M2_motorway_(Northern_Ireland)) and [M22 motorway](/wiki/M22_motorway_(Northern_Ireland)) route. Reflecting this high volume of traffic, a 2025 study ranked Belfast as the 15th noisiest city in the United Kingdom.

[Black taxis](/wiki/Hackney_carriage) are common in the city, operating on a [share](/wiki/Share_taxi) basis in some areas. These are outnumbered by [private hire taxis](/wiki/Taxicabs_of_the_United_Kingdom#Private_hire_('minicabs')). Bus and rail public transport in Northern Ireland is operated by subsidiaries of [Translink](/wiki/Translink_(Northern_Ireland)). Bus services in the city proper and the nearer suburbs are operated by [Translink Metro](/wiki/Metro_(Belfast)), with services focusing on linking residential districts with the city centre on 12 [quality bus corridors](/wiki/Quality_bus_corridor) running along main radial roads,

More distant suburbs are served by [Ulsterbus](/wiki/Ulsterbus). [Northern Ireland Railways](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Railways) provides [suburban services](/wiki/Belfast_Suburban_Rail) along three lines running through Belfast's northern suburbs to [Carrickfergus](/wiki/Carrickfergus), [Larne](/wiki/Larne) and [Larne Harbour](/wiki/Larne_Harbour_railway_station), eastwards towards [Bangor](/wiki/Bangor,_County_Down) and south-westwards towards [Lisburn](/wiki/Lisburn) and [Portadown](/wiki/Portadown_railway_station). This service is known as the [Belfast Suburban Rail](/wiki/Belfast_Suburban_Rail) system. Belfast is linked [directly](/wiki/Belfast-Derry_railway_line) to [Coleraine](/wiki/Coleraine_railway_station), [Portrush](/wiki/Portrush_railway_station) and [Derry](/wiki/Derry_~_Londonderry_railway_station). Belfast has a direct rail connection with [Dublin](/wiki/Dublin) called *[Enterprise](/wiki/Enterprise_(train))* operated jointly by NIR and the Irish rail company [Iarnród Éireann](/wiki/Iarnr%C3%B3d_%C3%89ireann).

In 2024, the city's Europa Bus Centre and Great Victoria Street rail station, was replaced by a new [Belfast Grand Central Station](/wiki/Belfast_Grand_Central_Station). It is "the largest integrated transport facility on the island of Ireland" with bus stands, railway platforms, and facilities for taxis and bicycles.

[Belfast City Airport](/wiki/Belfast_City_Airport)

The city has two airports: [Belfast City Airport](/wiki/Belfast_City_Airport), close to the city centre on the eastern shore of Belfast Lough and [Belfast International Airport](/wiki/Belfast_International_Airport) 30–40 minutes to the west on the shore of [Lough Neagh](/wiki/Lough_Neagh). Both operate UK domestic and European flights. The city is also served by [Dublin Airport](/wiki/Dublin_Airport), two hours to the south, with direct inter-continental connections.

In addition to its extensive freight business, the Belfast Port offers car-ferry sailings, operated by [Stena Line](/wiki/Stena_Line), to [Cairnryan](/wiki/Cairnryan) in Scotland (5 Sailings Daily. 2 hours 22 minutes) and to [Liverpool](/wiki/Liverpool)-[Birkenhead](/wiki/Birkenhead) (14 sailings weekly. 8 hours). The [Isle of Man Steam Packet](/wiki/Isle_of_Man_Steam_Packet) Company provides a seasonal connection to [Douglas, Isle of Man](/wiki/Douglas,_Isle_of_Man).

The [Glider](/wiki/Glider_(Belfast)) bus service is a new form of transport in Belfast. Introduced in 2018, it is a [bus rapid transit](/wiki/Bus_rapid_transit) system linking East Belfast, West Belfast and the Titanic Quarter from the City Centre. Using [articulated buses](/wiki/Articulated_buses), the £90 million service saw a 17% increase in its first month in Belfast, with 30,000 more people using the Gliders every week. The service is being recognised as helping to modernise the city's public transport.

[National Cycle Route 9](/wiki/National_Cycle_Route_9) to [Newry](/wiki/Newry), which will eventually connect with Dublin, starts in Belfast.

### Utilities

Silent Valley Reservoir, showing the brick-built overflow

Half of Belfast's water is supplied via the [Aquarius pipeline](/wiki/Mourne_Conduit) from the [Silent Valley Reservoir](/wiki/Silent_Valley_Reservoir) in County Down, created to collect water from the [Mourne Mountains](/wiki/Mourne_Mountains). The other half is now supplied from [Lough Neagh](/wiki/Lough_Neagh) via Dunore Water Treatment Works in County Antrim. The citizens of Belfast pay for their water in their [rates](/wiki/Rates_(tax)) bill. Plans to bring in additional water tariffs were deferred by [devolution](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly) in May 2007.

Power is provided from a number of [power stations](/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_Northern_Ireland) via [NIE Networks Limited](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Electricity) transmission lines. (Just under a half of electricity consumption in Northern Ireland is generated from [renewable sources](/wiki/Renewable_resource)). [Phoenix Natural Gas](/wiki/Phoenix_Natural_Gas) Ltd. started supplying customers in Larne and Greater Belfast with natural gas in 1996 via the newly constructed [Scotland-Northern Ireland pipeline](/wiki/Scotland-Northern_Ireland_pipeline). [Rates](/wiki/Rates_(tax)) in Belfast (and the rest of Northern Ireland) were reformed in April 2007. The discrete [capital](/wiki/Capital_(economics)) value system means rates bills are determined by the capital value of each domestic property as assessed by the Valuation and Lands Agency.

### Leisure centres

Belfast City Council owns and maintains 17 leisure centres across the city, run on its behalf by the non-profit [social enterprise](/wiki/Social_enterprise) [GLL](/wiki/Greenwich_Leisure_Limited) under the 'Better' brand. ### Parks and gardens

Main article: [List of parks and gardens in Belfast](/wiki/List_of_parks_and_gardens_in_Belfast)

Belfast has over [forty parks](/wiki/List_of_parks_and_gardens_in_Belfast). The oldest (1828) and one of the most popular parks [Botanic Gardens](/wiki/Belfast_Botanic_Gardens) in the [Queen's Quarter](/wiki/Queen%27s_Quarter,_Belfast). Built in the 1830s and designed by [Sir Charles Lanyon](/wiki/Charles_Lanyon), its Palm House is one of the earliest examples of a curvilinear and cast iron [glasshouse](/wiki/Greenhouse). Other attractions in the park include the recently restored Tropical Ravine, a humid jungle glen built in 1889, rose gardens and public events ranging from live opera broadcasts to pop concerts.

The largest municipal park in the city, and closest to the city centre, lies on the right bank of Lagan. The 100-acres of [Ormeau Park](/wiki/Ormeau_Park) were opened to the public in 1871 on what was the last demesne of the town's former proprietors, the Chichesters, [Marquesses of Donegall](/wiki/Marquess_of_Donegall).

In north Belfast, the [Waterworks](/wiki/Water_Works,_Belfast), two reservoirs to which the public have had access since 1897, are features of a park supporting angling and waterfowl. In 1906, a further water park, [Victoria](/wiki/Victoria_Park,_Belfast), opened behind industrial dockland on what had been the eastern shore of the Lough. It is now connected through east Belfast by the Connswater Community Greenway which offers 16 km of continuous cycle and walkway through east Belfast.

The largest green conservation area within the city's boundaries is a 2,116 hectares patchwork of "parks, demesnes, woodland and meadows" stretching upriver along the banks of the Lagan river and canal; Established in 1967, the Lagan Valley Regional Park envelopes in its course, Belvoir Park Forest, which contains ancient oaks and a 12th-century Norman [Motte](/wiki/Motte-and-bailey_castle), and [Sir Thomas and Lady Dixon Park](/wiki/Sir_Thomas_and_Lady_Dixon_Park), whose International Rose Garden attracts thousands of visitors each July.

Colin Glenn Forest Park, the [National Trust](/wiki/National_Trust) Divis and the Black Mountain Ridge Trail, and Cave Hill Country Park. offer panoramic views over Belfast and beyond from the west. Climbing the Castlereagh Hills, the National Trust Lisnabreeny Cregagh Glen does the same from the east.

Below Cave Hill, the council maintains one of the few local government-funded zoos in the British Isles. The [Belfast Zoo](/wiki/Belfast_Zoo) houses more than 1,200 animals of 140 species including [Asian elephants](/wiki/Asian_elephant), [Barbary lions](/wiki/Barbary_lion), [Malayan sun bears](/wiki/Malayan_sun_bear) (one of the few in the United Kingdom), two species of penguin, a family of [western lowland gorillas](/wiki/Western_lowland_gorilla), a troop of [common chimpanzees](/wiki/Common_chimpanzee), a pair of [red pandas](/wiki/Red_panda), a pair of [Goodfellow's tree-kangaroos](/wiki/Goodfellow%27s_tree-kangaroo) and [Francois' langurs](/wiki/Francois%27_langur). It carries out important conservation work and takes part in European and international breeding programmes which help to ensure the survival of many species under threat.

### Sports

Main article: [Sport in Belfast](/wiki/Sport_in_Belfast)

[Irish Football Association](/wiki/Irish_Football_Association) stadium Windsor Park

Belfast has several notable sports teams playing a diverse variety of sports such as [football](/wiki/Association_football), [Gaelic games](/wiki/Gaelic_games), [rugby](/wiki/Rugby_football), [cricket](/wiki/Cricket), and [ice hockey](/wiki/Ice_hockey). The [Belfast Marathon](/wiki/Belfast_Marathon) is run annually on May Day, The 41st Marathon in 2023, with related events (Wheelchair Race, Team Relay and 8 Mile Walk) attracted 15,000 participants.

The [Northern Ireland national football team](/wiki/Northern_Ireland_national_football_team) plays its home matches at [Windsor Park](/wiki/Windsor_Park). Football clubs with stadia and training grounds in the city include: [Linfield](/wiki/Linfield_F.C.), [Glentoran](/wiki/Glentoran_F.C.), [Crusaders](/wiki/Crusaders_F.C.), [Cliftonville](/wiki/Cliftonville_F.C.), [Donegal Celtic](/wiki/Donegal_Celtic), [Harland & Wolff Welders](/wiki/Harland_%26_Wolff_Welders_F.C.), [Dundela](/wiki/Dundela_F.C.), [Knockbreda](/wiki/Knockbreda_F.C.), [PSNI](/wiki/PSNI_F.C.), [Newington](/wiki/Newington_Youth_F.C.), [Sport & Leisure](/wiki/Sport_%26_Leisure_Swifts_F.C.) and [Brantwood](/wiki/Brantwood_F.C.).

Belfast is home to over twenty [Gaelic football](/wiki/Gaelic_football) and [hurling](/wiki/Hurling) clubs. [Casement Park](/wiki/Casement_Park) in west Belfast, home to the [Antrim](/wiki/Antrim_GAA) county teams, had a capacity of 31,500 making it the second largest [Gaelic Athletic Association](/wiki/Gaelic_Athletic_Association) ground in [Ulster](/wiki/Ulster). Listed as one of the venues for the UK and Ireland's successful [UEFA Euro 2028](/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2028) bid, with co-funding from the Irish government there are plans for a complete rebuild. In May 2020, the foundation of [East Belfast GAA](/wiki/East_Belfast_GAA) returned Gaelic Games to East Belfast after decades of its absence in the area. The current club president is Irish-language enthusiast [Linda Ervine](/wiki/Linda_Ervine) who comes from a unionist background in the area. The team currently plays in the Down Senior County League.

The 1999 [Heineken Cup](/wiki/Heineken_Cup) champions [Ulster Rugby](/wiki/Ulster_Rugby) play at [Ravenhill Stadium](/wiki/Ravenhill_Stadium) in the south of the city. Belfast has four teams in rugby's [All-Ireland League](/wiki/All-Ireland_League_(rugby_union)): [Belfast Harlequins](/wiki/Belfast_Harlequins) in Division 1B; and [Instonians](/wiki/Instonians), [Queen's University](/wiki/Queen%27s_University_RFC) and [Malone](/wiki/Malone_RFC) in Division 2A.

Belfast is home to the [Stormont cricket ground](/wiki/Stormont_(cricket_ground)) since 1949 and was the venue for the [Irish cricket team](/wiki/Irish_cricket_team)'s first ever [One Day International](/wiki/One_Day_International) against [England](/wiki/England_cricket_team) in 2006.

The 9,500 capacity [SSE Arena](/wiki/SSE_Arena,_Belfast) accommodates the [Belfast Giants](/wiki/Belfast_Giants), one of the biggest [ice hockey](/wiki/Ice_hockey) clubs in the UK. Featuring Canadian, ex-[NHL](/wiki/National_Hockey_League) players, the club competes the British [Elite Ice Hockey League](/wiki/Elite_Ice_Hockey_League).

Belfast was the home town of former [Manchester United](/wiki/Manchester_United_F.C.) player [George Best](/wiki/George_Best), the 1968 [European Footballer of the Year](/wiki/European_Footballer_of_the_Year), who died in November 2005. On the day he was buried in the city, 100,000 people lined the route from his home on the Cregagh Road to Roselawn cemetery. Since his death the [City Airport](/wiki/Belfast_City_Airport) was named after him and a trust has been set up to fund a memorial to him in the city centre. Other sportspeople celebrated in the city include double world snooker champion [Alex "Hurricane" Higgins](/wiki/Alex_Higgins) and world champion boxers [Wayne McCullough](/wiki/Wayne_McCullough), [Rinty Monaghan](/wiki/Rinty_Monaghan) and [Carl Frampton](/wiki/Carl_Frampton).

## Climate

Main article: [Climate of Belfast](/wiki/Climate_of_Belfast)

At [54°35′49″N 05°55′45″W / 54.59694°N 5.92917°W / 54.59694; -5.92917](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Belfast&params=54_35_49_N_05_55_45_W_), its northern latitude is characterised by short winter days and long summer evenings. During the [winter solstice](/wiki/Winter_solstice), the shortest day of the year, local sunset is before 16:00 while sunrise is around 08:45. At the [summer solstice](/wiki/Solstice) in June, the sun sets after 22:00 and rises before 05:00.

For this northern latitude, thanks to the influence of the [Gulf Stream](/wiki/Gulf_Stream) and [North Atlantic Drift](/wiki/North_Atlantic_Current), Belfast has a comparatively mild climate. In summer the temperatures rarely range above 25 °C (77 °F) or dip in winter below −5 °C (23 °F). The maritime influence, also ensures that the city gets significant precipitation. On 157 days in an average year, rainfall is greater than 1 mm. Average annual rainfall is 846 mm (33.3 in), less than areas of northern England or most of [Scotland](/wiki/Scotland), but higher than [Dublin](/wiki/Dublin) or the south-east coast of Ireland.

With its moderate temperatures and abundant rainfall, Belfast's climate is defined as a [temperate](/wiki/Temperate) [oceanic climate](/wiki/Oceanic_climate) (Cfb in the [Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) system), a classification it shares with most of northwest Europe.

## Twin towns – sister cities

Belfast City Council takes part in the [twinning scheme](/wiki/Twin_towns_and_sister_cities), and is twinned with the following sister cities:

- [Nashville](/wiki/Nashville), [Tennessee](/wiki/Tennessee), United States (since 1994)
- [Hefei](/wiki/Hefei), Anhui, China (since 2005)

- [Shenyang](/wiki/Shenyang), Liaoning, China (since 2016)
