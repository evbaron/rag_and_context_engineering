**Lonnie Rashid Lynn** (born March 13, 1972), known professionally as **Common** (formerly known as **Common Sense**), is an American rapper, actor and activist. The recipient of three [Grammy Awards](/wiki/Grammy_Award), an [Academy Award](/wiki/Academy_Award), a [Primetime Emmy Award](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award), and a [Golden Globe Award](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Award), he signed with the independent label [Relativity Records](/wiki/Relativity_Records) at the age of 20. The label released his first three studio albums: *[Can I Borrow a Dollar?](/wiki/Can_I_Borrow_a_Dollar%3F)* (1992), *[Resurrection](/wiki/Resurrection_(Common_album))* (1994) and *[One Day It'll All Make Sense](/wiki/One_Day_It%27ll_All_Make_Sense)* (1997). He maintained an [underground](/wiki/Underground_hip-hop) following into the late 1990s, and achieved mainstream success through his work with the [Black music](/wiki/Black_music) collective [Soulquarians](/wiki/Soulquarians).

After attaining a [major label](/wiki/Major_label) record deal, he released his fourth and fifth albums, *[Like Water for Chocolate](/wiki/Like_Water_for_Chocolate_(album))* (2000) and *[Electric Circus](/wiki/Electric_Circus_(album))* (2002), to continued acclaim and modest commercial response. He guest performed on fellow Soulquarian, [Erykah Badu](/wiki/Erykah_Badu)'s 2003 single, "[Love of My Life (An Ode to Hip-Hop)](/wiki/Love_of_My_Life_(An_Ode_to_Hip-Hop))", which became his highest entry — at number nine — on the [*Billboard* Hot 100](/wiki/Billboard_Hot_100) and won [Best R&B Song](/wiki/Grammy_Award_for_Best_R%26B_Song) at the [45th Annual Grammy Awards](/wiki/45th_Annual_Grammy_Awards). He signed with fellow Chicago rapper [Kanye West](/wiki/Kanye_West)'s record label [GOOD Music](/wiki/GOOD_Music), in a joint venture with [Geffen Records](/wiki/Geffen_Records) to release his sixth and seventh albums *[Be](/wiki/Be_(Common_album))* (2005) and *[Finding Forever](/wiki/Finding_Forever)* (2007); both were nominated for [Best Rap Album](/wiki/Best_Rap_Album) Grammys, while the latter became his first to debut atop the [*Billboard* 200](/wiki/Billboard_200) and contained the song "[Southside](/wiki/Southside_(Common_song))" (featuring Kanye West), the recipient of [Best Rap Performance by a Duo or Group](/wiki/Best_Rap_Performance_by_a_Duo_or_Group) at the [50th Annual Grammy Awards](/wiki/50th_Annual_Grammy_Awards). His eighth album, *[Universal Mind Control](/wiki/Universal_Mind_Control)* (2008), was met with a critical decline and served as his final release with GOOD.

Common's label imprint, Think Common Entertainment, was founded in 2011 and entered a joint venture with [Warner Bros. Records](/wiki/Warner_Records) to release his ninth album, *[The Dreamer/The Believer](/wiki/The_Dreamer/The_Believer)* (2011), and later [No I.D.](/wiki/No_I.D.)'s [ARTium Recordings](/wiki/ARTium_Recordings), an imprint of [Def Jam Recordings](/wiki/Def_Jam_Recordings) to release his tenth album, *[Nobody's Smiling](/wiki/Nobody%27s_Smiling)* (2014). Both received critical praise and further discussed social issues in [Black America](/wiki/African-American_middle_class); his eleventh album, *[Black America Again](/wiki/Black_America_Again)* (2016) saw widespread critical acclaim and served as his final release on a [major label](/wiki/Record_label#Major_labels). Signing a contract with [Loma Vista](/wiki/Loma_Vista_Recordings), he later released his twelfth album *[Let Love](/wiki/Let_Love)* (2019), thirteenth album *[A Beautiful Revolution Pt. 1](/wiki/A_Beautiful_Revolution_Pt._1)* (2020), and fourteenth album *[A Beautiful Revolution Pt. 2](/wiki/A_Beautiful_Revolution_Pt._2)* (2021), to positive reviews. For collaborative effort, he worked with [Karriem Riggins](/wiki/Karriem_Riggins) and [Robert Glasper](/wiki/Robert_Glasper) on the album [*August Greene*](/wiki/August_Greene_(album)) (2018) under the [supergroup of the same name](/wiki/August_Greene), and with Pete Rock on the album *[The Auditorium Vol. 1](/wiki/The_Auditorium_Vol._1)* (2024).

Lynn won the [Academy Award for Best Original Song](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Original_Song) for his song "[Glory](/wiki/Glory_(Common_and_John_Legend_song))" (with [John Legend](/wiki/John_Legend)), which he released for the film *[Selma](/wiki/Selma_(film))* (2014), wherein he co-starred as [civil rights](/wiki/Civil_rights_movement) leader [James Bevel](/wiki/James_Bevel). His other film roles include *[Smokin' Aces](/wiki/Smokin%27_Aces)* (2006), *[Street Kings](/wiki/Street_Kings)* (2008), *[American Gangster](/wiki/American_Gangster_(film))* (2007), *[Wanted](/wiki/Wanted_(2008_film))* (2008), *[Date Night](/wiki/Date_Night)* (2010), *[Just Wright](/wiki/Just_Wright)* (2010), *[Happy Feet Two](/wiki/Happy_Feet_Two)* (2011), *[Now You See Me](/wiki/Now_You_See_Me_(film))* (2013), *[Run All Night](/wiki/Run_All_Night_(film))* (2015), *[John Wick: Chapter 2](/wiki/John_Wick:_Chapter_2)* (2017), *[Smallfoot](/wiki/Smallfoot)* (2018), *[Alice](/wiki/Alice_(2022_film))* (2022), and *[Fool's Paradise](/wiki/Fool%27s_Paradise_(2023_film))* (2023). In television, he starred as Elam Ferguson in [AMC](/wiki/AMC_(TV_channel)) [western](/wiki/Western_(genre)) series *[Hell on Wheels](/wiki/Hell_on_Wheels_(TV_series))* from 2011 to 2014, and has played a supporting role in the [Apple TV+](/wiki/Apple_TV%2B) science fiction series [*Silo*](/wiki/Silo_(TV_series)) since 2023. His song "Letter to the Free" was released for the [Ava DuVernay](/wiki/Ava_DuVernay)-directed [Netflix](/wiki/Netflix) documentary *[13th](/wiki/13th_(film))* (2017), for which he won the [Primetime Emmy Award for Outstanding Original Music and Lyrics](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award_for_Outstanding_Original_Music_and_Lyrics). He made his [Broadway](/wiki/Broadway_(theatre)) acting debut on the play *[Between Riverside and Crazy](/wiki/Between_Riverside_and_Crazy)* (2023), which won a [Pulitzer Prize for Drama](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Drama).

## Early life and education

Lonnie Rashid Lynn was born on March 13, 1972, at the Chicago Osteopathic Hospital in Chicago, Illinois. He is the son of educator and former principal of [John Hope College Preparatory](/wiki/John_Hope_College_Prep) High School, Mahalia Ann Hines, and former [ABA](/wiki/American_Basketball_Association) basketball player turned youth counselor [Lonnie Lynn](/wiki/Lonnie_Lynn). Lynn shares the same first name as his father, grandfather, great-grandfather and great-great-grandfather. He is known by his middle name, Rashid, to his family and friends. Lynn's godfather is basketball player [Spencer Haywood](/wiki/Spencer_Haywood) who was a teammate of his father on the [Denver Rockets](/wiki/Denver_Rockets).

Lynn was raised in the [Calumet Heights](/wiki/Calumet_Heights,_Chicago) neighborhood. His father left the family because of his drug and alcohol addictions while Lynn was young. When he was aged two, Lynn's father attempted to take him and his mother to [Seattle](/wiki/Seattle), where he had arranged a tryout with the [Seattle SuperSonics](/wiki/Seattle_SuperSonics). His father was arrested when the family stopped at a motel in [Wisconsin](/wiki/Wisconsin) and charged with false imprisonment.

Lynn's parents divorced in the late 1970s, resulting in his father moving to [Denver](/wiki/Denver), Colorado. This left Lynn to be raised by his mother; however, his father remained active in his life, and was able to get him a job as a ballboy with the [Chicago Bulls](/wiki/Chicago_Bulls) when he was 11, also witnessing [Michael Jordan](/wiki/Michael_Jordan)'s first professional exhibition game. Lynn attended [Florida A&M University](/wiki/Florida_A%26M_University) (FAMU) for two years under a scholarship and majored in [business administration](/wiki/Business_administration) but did not graduate.

## Music career

Common's music has embraced several styles, including [Midwestern hip-hop](/wiki/Hip-hop), [neo soul](/wiki/Neo_soul) and [progressive rap](/wiki/Progressive_rap).

### 1987–1996: Career beginnings

Lynn began rapping in the late 1980s, while a student at [Luther High School South](/wiki/Luther_High_School_South) in Chicago, when he, along with two of his friends, formed C.D.R., a rap trio that opened for acts such as [N.W.A](/wiki/N.W.A) and [Big Daddy Kane](/wiki/Big_Daddy_Kane). When C.D.R dissolved by 1991, Lynn began a solo career under the stage name of Common Sense. After being featured in the [Unsigned Hype](/wiki/Unsigned_Hype) column of *[The Source](/wiki/The_Source_(magazine))* magazine, he debuted as a solo artist in 1992 with the single "[Take It EZ](/wiki/Take_It_EZ)", followed by the album *[Can I Borrow a Dollar?](/wiki/Can_I_Borrow_a_Dollar%3F)*.

With the 1994 release of *[Resurrection](/wiki/Resurrection_(Common_album))*, Common Sense achieved a much larger degree of critical acclaim which extended beyond the Chicago music scene. The album sold relatively well and received a strong positive reaction among [alternative](/wiki/Alternative_hip-hop) and [underground hip-hop](/wiki/Underground_hip-hop) fans at the time. *Resurrection* was Common Sense's last album produced almost entirely by his long-time production partner, [No I.D.](/wiki/No_I.D.), who would later become a mentor to a young [Kanye West](/wiki/Kanye_West).

In 1996, Common Sense appeared on the [Red Hot Organization](/wiki/Red_Hot_Organization)'s compilation CD, [America Is Dying Slowly](/wiki/America_Is_Dying_Slowly) (A.I.D.S.), alongside [Biz Markie](/wiki/Biz_Markie), [Wu-Tang Clan](/wiki/Wu-Tang_Clan), and [Fat Joe](/wiki/Fat_Joe), among many other prominent hip-hop artists. The CD, meant to raise awareness of the [HIV/AIDS](/wiki/HIV/AIDS) epidemic among [African American](/wiki/African_American) men, was heralded as "a masterpiece" by *[The Source](/wiki/The_Source_(magazine))* magazine. He would later also contribute to the [Red Hot Organization](/wiki/Red_Hot_Organization)'s [Fela Kuti](/wiki/Fela_Kuti) tribute album, *[Red Hot and Riot](/wiki/Red_Hot_and_Riot)* in 2002. He collaborated with [Djelimady Tounkara](/wiki/Djelimady_Tounkara) on a remake of Kuti's track, "Years of Tears and Sorrow".

Common performing with [Mos Def](/wiki/Mos_Def) in 1999

The song "[I Used to Love H.E.R.](/wiki/I_Used_to_Love_H.E.R.)" from *Resurrection* ignited a [feud](/wiki/Diss_track) with [West Coast rap group](/wiki/West_Coast_hip-hop) [Westside Connection](/wiki/Westside_Connection). The lyrics of the song criticized the path hip-hop music was taking, using a metaphor of a woman to convey hip-hop and were interpreted by some as directing blame towards the popularity of West Coast [gangsta rap](/wiki/Gangsta_rap). Westside Connection first responded with the 1995 song "Westside Slaughterhouse," with the lyrics "Used to love H.E.R., mad cause I f\*cked her". "Westside Slaughterhouse" also mentioned Common Sense by name, prompting the rapper to respond with the scathing [Pete Rock](/wiki/Pete_Rock)-produced attack song "[The Bitch in Yoo](/wiki/The_Bitch_in_Yoo)". Common Sense and Westside Connection continued to insult each other back and forth before finally meeting with [Louis Farrakhan](/wiki/Louis_Farrakhan) and setting aside their dispute. Following the popularity of *Resurrection*, Common Sense was sued by an [Orange County](/wiki/Orange_County,_California)-based [reggae](/wiki/Reggae) band with the [same name](/wiki/Common_Sense_(band)), and was forced to shorten his moniker to simply Common.

### 1996–1999: *One Day It'll All Make Sense*

Initially scheduled for an October 1996 release, Common released his third album, *[One Day It'll All Make Sense](/wiki/One_Day_It%27ll_All_Make_Sense)*, in September 1997. The album took a total of two years to complete and included collaborations with artists such as [Lauryn Hill](/wiki/Lauryn_Hill), [De La Soul](/wiki/De_La_Soul), [Q-Tip](/wiki/Q-Tip_(rapper)), [Canibus](/wiki/Canibus), [Black Thought](/wiki/Black_Thought), [Chantay Savage](/wiki/Chantay_Savage), and [Questlove](/wiki/Questlove) — a future fellow member of the [Soulquarians](/wiki/Soulquarians) outfit. The album, which made a point of eschewing any gangsterism (in response to questions about his musical integrity), was critically acclaimed and led to a [major label](/wiki/Major_label) contract with [MCA Records](/wiki/MCA_Records). In addition to releasing *One Day*, Common's first child, daughter Omoye Assata Lynn, was born shortly after the release of the album.

As documented by hip-hop journalist Raquel Cepeda, in the liner notes for the album, this event had a profound spiritual and mental effect on Common and enabled him to grow musically while becoming more responsible as an artist. She writes:

> Rashid found out that he was going to become a daddy in about 8 months. Stunned and confused, Rashid had life-altering decisions to make with his girlfriend, Kim Jones. The situation led to the composition of his favorite cut on *One Day...* that offers a male slant on abortion. "[Retrospect for Life](/wiki/Retrospect_for_Life)", produced by [James Poyser](/wiki/James_Poyser) and [No I.D.](/wiki/No_I.D.) featuring [Lauryn Hill](/wiki/Lauryn_Hill) (who was due on the same day as Rashid's girlfriend), is the song that is the driving force behind the project. Rashid listens to "[Retrospect for Life](/wiki/Retrospect_for_Life)" today at the mastering session geeked as if it were for the first time. He tells me as we listen to L-Boogie wail the chorus, "*when I listen to the song now, I think about how precious her (Omoye's) life is*".

Common addresses family ethics several times on *One Day...*, and the album sleeve is decorated with old family photos, illustrating the rapper's childhood, as well a quote from [1 Corinthians 13:11](/wiki/First_Epistle_to_the_Corinthians), which summarizes the path to manhood:

> When I was a child, I talked like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child. When I became a man, I put childish ways behind me.

### 1999–2003: Soulquarians era

Common in New York City, 2003

Following *One Day...*, Common signed a major label record deal with [MCA Records](/wiki/MCA_Records) and relocated from Chicago to New York City in 1999. He began recording almost exclusively with a loose collective of musicians and artists (dubbed the "[Soulquarians](/wiki/Soulquarians)" by central figure [Questlove](/wiki/Questlove)) throughout 1999, and made a few sporadic guest appearances on [The Roots](/wiki/The_Roots)' *[Things Fall Apart](/wiki/Things_Fall_Apart_(album))*, and the [Rawkus Records](/wiki/Rawkus_Records) compilation, *[Soundbombing 2](/wiki/Soundbombing_2)*.

In 2000, his fourth album, *[Like Water for Chocolate](/wiki/Like_Water_for_Chocolate_(album))*, was released to mass critical acclaim. [Executive produced](/wiki/Executive_producer) by Questlove and featuring significant contributions by [J Dilla](/wiki/J_Dilla), (who helmed every track except — "Cold Blooded", "Geto Heaven Part II", "A Song For Assata", "Pop's Rap Part 3...All My Children" & the [DJ Premier](/wiki/DJ_Premier)-produced track "[The 6th Sense](/wiki/The_6th_Sense)"), *Like Water for Chocolate* transpired to be a considerable commercial breakthrough for Common, earning the rapper his first [gold](/wiki/RIAA_Certification) record, and greatly expanding his fanbase among critics and listeners alike.

With both artists hailing from the [Great Lakes region](/wiki/Great_Lakes_region_(North_America)) of the United States (Chicago and Detroit, respectively), Common and [J Dilla](/wiki/J_Dilla) established their chemistry early on. Both became members of the [Soulquarians](/wiki/Soulquarians) collective, and collaborated on numerous projects together, even placing one song, "Thelonius", on both the [Slum Village](/wiki/Slum_Village) album *[Fantastic, Vol. 2](/wiki/Fantastic,_Vol._2)*, and Common's *[Like Water for Chocolate](/wiki/Like_Water_for_Chocolate_(album))*. As Dilla's health began to decline from the effects of [Lupus Nephritis](/wiki/Lupus_Nephritis), he relocated to Los Angeles, and asked Common to make the move with him as a roommate (Dilla would later lose his battle with the rare disease).

This album saw Common exploring themes (musically and lyrically), which were uncommon for a hip-hop record, as he does on the song "Time Travelin' (A Tribute To Fela)"; a homage to [Nigerian](/wiki/Nigerian) music legend, and political activist [Fela Kuti](/wiki/Fela_Kuti). The most popular single from the album "[The Light](/wiki/The_Light_(Common_song))" was nominated for a [Grammy Award](/wiki/Grammy_Award).

In 2002, Common released his fifth album, *[Electric Circus](/wiki/Electric_Circus_(album))*. The album was highly anticipated and praised by many critics for its ambitious vision. However, it was not as commercially successful as his previous album, *Like Water for Chocolate*, selling under 300,000 copies. An eclectic album, *Electric Circus* featured fusions of several genres such as hip-hop, pop, rock, electronic, and neo-soul. The album's style tended to divide critics; some praised its ambitious vision while others criticized it for the same reason. Most of the criticism tended to revolve around the album's experimental nature; some felt Common had strayed too far from his previous sound. This was Common's second and last album for MCA, and the label's final release prior to its absorption into Geffen Records.

Around this time, Common appeared as a guest performer on singer and fellow Soulquarian [Bilal](/wiki/Bilal_(American_singer))'s *[Love for Sale](/wiki/Love_for_Sale_(Bilal_album))* album, recording a remake of the 1977 [Fela Kuti](/wiki/Fela_Kuti) song "[Sorrow, Tears & Blood](/wiki/Sorrow_Tears_and_Blood)". Bilal also featured on *Electric Circus*, the first of many future collaborations with Common.

### 2004–2011: GOOD Music era

In early 2004, Common made an appearance on fellow Chicagoan Kanye West's multi-platinum debut album, *[The College Dropout](/wiki/The_College_Dropout)* (on the song "Get Em High"), and announced his signing to West's then-newfound label [GOOD Music](/wiki/GOOD_Music). West had been a longtime fan of Common and the two even participated in a friendly on-air MC battle, where West took jabs at his lyrical idol for "going soft" and wearing crochet pants (as he does for his appearance in the video for the [Mary J. Blige](/wiki/Mary_J._Blige) song "[Dance for Me](/wiki/Dance_for_Me_(Mary_J._Blige_song))"). The pair worked together on Common's next album, *[Be](/wiki/Be_(Common_album))*, almost entirely produced by Kanye West, with some help from Common's longtime collaborator [James Yancey](/wiki/James_Yancey) (J Dilla) — also a favorite of West. The album was released in May 2005, and performed very well, boosted by Kanye's involvement and the singles "[The Corner](/wiki/The_Corner_(song))", and "[Go](/wiki/Go_(Common_song))". *[Be](/wiki/Be_(Common_album))* earned Common the second gold record of his career, with sales topping out at around 800,000 copies. *[The Source](/wiki/The_Source_(magazine))* magazine gave it a near-perfect 4.5 mic rating, *[XXL](/wiki/XXL_(magazine))* magazine gave it their highest rating of "XXL", and AllHipHop gave the album 4 stars. The album was also nominated for four Grammy Awards in 2006.

Common in 2006

Following the release of *[Be](/wiki/Be_(Common_album))* in 2005, several mixed-race artists from the UK hip-hop scene took exception to Common's comments about interracial relationships on the song "Real People." [Yungun](/wiki/Yungun), [Doc Brown](/wiki/Doc_Brown_(rapper)) and Rising Son recorded a track over an instrumental version of "The Corner" named "Dear Common (The Corner Dub)." Common states that he has heard of the track but never actually taken the time to listen to it, and has not retaliated in song.

Common's seventh LP titled *[Finding Forever](/wiki/Finding_Forever)* was released on July 31, 2007. For this album, he continued his work with [Kanye West](/wiki/Kanye_West), as well as other producers such as [Will.i.am](/wiki/Will.i.am), [Devo Springsteen](/wiki/Devo_Springsteen), [Derrick Hodge](/wiki/Derrick_Hodge), and [Karriem Riggins](/wiki/Karriem_Riggins), as well as the only [J Dilla](/wiki/J_Dilla)-produced track, "So Far To Go". The album features guest spots from artists such as [Dwele](/wiki/Dwele), [Bilal](/wiki/Bilal_(American_singer)), [D'Angelo](/wiki/D%27Angelo), and UK pop starlet [Lily Allen](/wiki/Lily_Allen). The first single from the album was "[The People](/wiki/The_People_(Common_song))" b/w "[The Game](/wiki/The_Game_(Common_song))". West predicted that *Finding Forever* would win the 2008 Grammy Award for Best Rap Album. The album was nominated for Best Rap Album, but did not win, losing to West's *Graduation*; however, Common did win his second Grammy for "Southside," which won the 2008 Grammy for Best Rap Performance by Duo or Group.
On July 31, 2007, Common performed a free concert in Santa Monica, California, on the 3rd Street Promenade to promote the release of *Finding Forever*. Common explained to the audience that the title "Finding Forever" represented his quest to find an eternal place in hip-hop and also his wishes to be an artist for the rest of his life. The album debuted at No. 1 on the national [*Billboard* 200](/wiki/Billboard_200) charts.

In an August 2007 interview with *XXL*, rapper [Q-Tip](/wiki/Q-Tip_(rapper)) of the group [A Tribe Called Quest](/wiki/A_Tribe_Called_Quest) stated that he and Common were forming a group called 'The Standard', and were planning to record an album to be produced by Q-Tip; however, this never came to fruition.

Common was instrumental in bridging the trans-Atlantic gap by signing UK's Mr. Wong and J2K to Kanye West's Getting Out Our Dreams recording outfit. Common met the pair during his tour in the UK earlier on in the year. It is speculated that the deal is not only to bring the UK and US hip-hop genres together but that to rival Syco Music's cross-Atlantic success with Leona Lewis. He also has a deal with Zune mp3 players. In 2008 Common made an estimated 12 million dollars, making him equal in earnings to [Eminem](/wiki/Eminem) and [Akon](/wiki/Akon), tied for the 13th highest grossing Hip-Hop artist.

Common performing at Store Vega in Copenhagen, Denmark, in 2007

The eighth album from Chicago hip-hop artist Common was originally scheduled to be released on June 24, 2008, under the name *Invincible Summer*, but he announced at a [Temple University](/wiki/Temple_University) concert that he would change it to *[Universal Mind Control](/wiki/Universal_Mind_Control)*. The release date was pushed back to September 30, 2008, due to Common filming *[Wanted](/wiki/Wanted_(2008_film))*. The release date was set for November 11, 2008, however, it was once again pushed back to December 9, 2008.

The album's [eponymous](/wiki/Eponymous) [lead single](/wiki/Lead_single) "[Universal Mind Control](/wiki/Universal_Mind_Control_(song))", was officially released on July 1, 2008, via the US iTunes Store as part of *The Announcement EP* (sold as *Universal Mind Control EP* in the UK). The song features Pharrell, who also produced the track. *The Announcement EP* included an additional track titled "Announcement", also featuring Pharrell. The video for the lead single was filmed in September by director Hype Williams. In 2009, Common was prominently featured throughout his GOOD Music label-mate [Kid Cudi](/wiki/Kid_Cudi)'s debut album *[Man on the Moon: The End of Day](/wiki/Man_on_the_Moon:_The_End_of_Day)*, as a narrator and featured artist. In late 2009, it was revealed Common was nominated for two [Grammys](/wiki/Grammy_Awards) at the [52nd Annual Grammy Awards](/wiki/52nd_Annual_Grammy_Awards), including [Best Rap Performance by a Duo or Group](/wiki/Best_Rap_Performance_by_a_Duo_or_Group) for "[Make Her Say](/wiki/Make_Her_Say)", alongside Kid Cudi and Kanye West, as well as [Best Rap Album](/wiki/Best_Rap_Album) for *Universal Mind Control*.

### 2011–present: Think Common Ent.

#### *The Dreamer/The Believer* and feud with Drake

American producer [No I.D.](/wiki/No_I.D.), stated that he and [Kanye West](/wiki/Kanye_West) would be producing Common's album *The Dreamer/The Believer*. In July 2011, it was announced that No I.D. would be the album's sole producer. Common made an appearance on [The Jonas Brothers](/wiki/The_Jonas_Brothers)' most recent album, *[Lines, Vines and Trying Times](/wiki/Lines,_Vines_and_Trying_Times)* as a guest rapper for the group's 2009 song, "Don't Charge Me for the Crime."

On July 6, 2011, Common released his first single, titled "[Ghetto Dreams](/wiki/Ghetto_Dreams)", from his next album. A second single, "Blue Sky", was released on October 4, 2011. On December 20, 2011, Common released his ninth solo album, titled *[The Dreamer, The Believer](/wiki/The_Dreamer,_The_Believer)*. Although he left GOOD Music in 2011, Common was featured on the label's first compilation album, 2012's *[Cruel Summer](/wiki/Cruel_Summer_(GOOD_Music_album))*. Common released a song entitled "Sweet", from *The Dreamer/The Believer*, which included lyrics critical of rappers who sing, although this criticism was not aimed specifically at Canadian recording artist [Drake](/wiki/Drake_(rapper)). Drake took offense and responded by releasing "[Stay Schemin'](/wiki/Stay_Schemin%27)", a song with [Rick Ross](/wiki/Rick_Ross) and [French Montana](/wiki/French_Montana). Common fans only had to wait two-and-a-half days for him to respond to Drake's [diss track](/wiki/Diss_track). On February 13, 2012, Common commented on the feud by saying "It's over. But it was all in the art of hip hop. He said some things to me so I had to say some things back...I wouldn't say [he started it] but I know I heard something that I felt was directed to me so I addressed it. That's all. But you know, thank God we were able to move forward from it and all is good."

#### Artium Recordings and further albums

After a quiet 2012, Common announced he would release an [extended play](/wiki/Extended_play) (EP) in January 2013, and his first [mixtape](/wiki/Mixtape) in April. In February 2013, Common announced his tenth solo studio album would be released in September 2013 and will feature [Kanye West](/wiki/Kanye_West) and production from Kanye West and [No I.D.](/wiki/No_I.D.) Later on September 8, 2013, he gave an update to his projects saying the previously announced EP would be released soon, and would feature a song with new Def Jam signee [Vince Staples](/wiki/Vince_Staples). He also told HipHopDX, his tenth solo studio album would be released in early 2014.

On January 6, 2014, Common announced his tenth studio album to be titled *[Nobody's Smiling](/wiki/Nobody%27s_Smiling)* and would be produced entirely by longtime collaborator 'No I.D.'. The album, which Common revealed was originally going to be an EP, is set to feature [Vince Staples](/wiki/Vince_Staples), [James Fauntleroy](/wiki/James_Fauntleroy) and "some new artists from Chicago." The concept of the album was inspired by his troubled hometown of Chicago: "We came up with this concept 'nobody's smiling.' It was really a thought that came about because of all the violence in Chicago," he says. "It happens in Chicago, but it's happening around the world in many ways." He continues, "We was talking about the conditions of what's happening, when I say 'nobody's smiling.' But it's really a call to action." On June 4, 2014, it was announced Common signed a recording contract with [Def Jam Recordings](/wiki/Def_Jam_Recordings) and No I.D.'s Artium Records. It was also announced *Nobody's Smiling* would be released July 22, 2014. Lonnie "Pops" Lynn was to be featured on this album as well but the recording fell through as Lonnie's health declined. A recording was indeed made and is in process of being released on Dirty Laboratory Productions featuring production by [AwareNess](/wiki/Calm_(music_group)).
In 2016 Common released his eleventh studio album titled [Black America Again](/wiki/Black_America_Again) under [Def Jam Recordings](/wiki/Def_Jam_Recordings), the album featured guest appearances by [Stevie Wonder](/wiki/Stevie_Wonder), [Bilal](/wiki/Bilal_(American_singer)), [Marsha Ambrosius](/wiki/Marsha_Ambrosius), [BJ The Chicago Kid](/wiki/BJ_The_Chicago_Kid), [John Legend](/wiki/John_Legend), [PJ](/wiki/PJ_(singer)), Syd, Elena & [Tasha Cobbs](/wiki/Tasha_Cobbs). The album was primarily produced by [Karriem Riggins](/wiki/Karriem_Riggins) alongside many other contributors including [Robert Glasper](/wiki/Robert_Glasper).

In 2018, Common also announced he would be forming a [jazz](/wiki/Jazz) group called [August Greene](/wiki/August_Greene) with [Karriem Riggins](/wiki/Karriem_Riggins) & [Robert Glasper](/wiki/Robert_Glasper), shortly after this announcement an [August Greene](/wiki/August_Greene) album was released through a partnership with [Amazon Music](/wiki/Amazon_Music). The album featured guest vocals (credited and uncredited) by male singer [Samora Pinderhughes](/wiki/Samora_Pinderhughes) and featured award-winning singer [Brandy Norwood](/wiki/Brandy_Norwood) on two songs titled "Optimistic" and "Time" respectively. On August 30, 2019, Common released his twelfth solo studio album titled [Let Love](/wiki/Let_Love), the album was released by [Loma Vista Recordings](/wiki/Loma_Vista_Recordings) and [Concord Records](/wiki/Concord_Records). The album features guest appearances from [Samora Pinderhughes](/wiki/Samora_Pinderhughes), [Daniel Caesar](/wiki/Daniel_Caesar), [Swizz Beatz](/wiki/Swizz_Beatz), [Leikeli47](/wiki/Leikeli47), [BJ the Chicago Kid](/wiki/BJ_the_Chicago_Kid), [A-Trak](/wiki/A-Trak), [Jill Scott](/wiki/Jill_Scott_(singer)), [Leon Bridges](/wiki/Leon_Bridges) and [Jonathan McReynolds](/wiki/Jonathan_McReynolds) and production of the album was primarily handled by [Karriem Riggins](/wiki/Karriem_Riggins) and the song titled "HER Love" (feat. [Daniel Caesar](/wiki/Daniel_Caesar)) was produced by [J. Dilla](/wiki/J._Dilla).

In 2020, Common released a nine-track album titled *[A Beautiful Revolution Pt. 1](/wiki/A_Beautiful_Revolution_Pt._1)*. The album features female singer PJ on seven tracks and also features guest vocals from [Black Thought](/wiki/Black_Thought) and [Lenny Kravitz](/wiki/Lenny_Kravitz). In 2021, he released a sequel album, *[A Beautiful Revolution Pt. 2](/wiki/A_Beautiful_Revolution_Pt._2)*, which features guest vocals from [Jessica Care Moore](/wiki/Jessica_Care_Moore), PJ, Black Thought, [Seun Kuti](/wiki/Seun_Kuti), [Marcus King](/wiki/Marcus_King), [Isaiah Sharkey](/wiki/Isaiah_Sharkey), [Brittany Howard](/wiki/Brittany_Howard), and [Morgan Parker](/wiki/Morgan_Parker_(writer)). Production of both albums was handled primarily by long-time collaborator [Karriem Riggins](/wiki/Karriem_Riggins), with various other producers credited as session musicians.

On August 1, 2023, Common was featured in [Men's Health](/wiki/Men%27s_Health)'s Hip-Hop 50th Anniversary edition alongside [Method Man](/wiki/Method_Man), [Wiz Khalifa](/wiki/Wiz_Khalifa), [Ludacris](/wiki/Ludacris), [Busta Rhymes](/wiki/Busta_Rhymes), and [50 Cent](/wiki/50_Cent).

In 2024, it was announced that Common would be releasing a collaborative album with [Pete Rock](/wiki/Pete_Rock) titled *[The Auditorium Vol. 1](/wiki/The_Auditorium_Vol._1)*, and released the album's first single, "Wise Up". The album has since been nominated for rap album of the year at the Grammy's and has accumulated millions of streams on Spotify.

### Writing

Common was invited by First Lady [Michelle Obama](/wiki/Michelle_Obama) to appear at a poetry reading on May 11, 2011, at the [White House](/wiki/White_House). His poetry was found to be greatly influenced by [Maya Angelou](/wiki/Maya_Angelou)'s works. This invitation caused furor with the [New Jersey State Police](/wiki/New_Jersey_State_Police) and their union, who cited some of Common's previous lyrical content, most notably the song "A Song For Assata" about [Assata Shakur](/wiki/Assata_Shakur), a member of the [Black Liberation Army](/wiki/Black_Liberation_Army) (and step-aunt of deceased rapper [Tupac Shakur](/wiki/Tupac_Shakur)) who was convicted in 1977 of the first-degree murder of New Jersey state trooper Werner Foerster. At another poetry reading, Common had stated, "flyers say 'free Mumia' on my freezer", a reference to [Mumia Abu-Jamal](/wiki/Mumia_Abu-Jamal), who murdered Philadelphia Police Officer Daniel Faulkner in 1981.

[Jay Carney](/wiki/Jay_Carney), the [White House Press Secretary](/wiki/White_House_Press_Secretary) at the time, stated that President Obama opposed these particular lyrics, but supported what Common stood for more broadly. Common responded by saying: "I guess Sarah Palin and Fox News doesn't like me." On Facebook, he also stated, "The one thing that shouldn't be questioned is my support for the police officers and troops that protect us every day." Common later discussed the matter with [Jon Stewart](/wiki/Jon_Stewart) during a September 14, 2011, appearance on Comedy Central's *[The Daily Show](/wiki/The_Daily_Show)*.

Common and his mother, Dr. Mahalia Ann Hines, at a 2011 signing for his memoir at the [Barnes & Noble](/wiki/Barnes_%26_Noble) in [Tribeca](/wiki/Tribeca), Manhattan

In September 2011, Common published his memoir, *One Day It'll All Make Sense*, through Atria Books. As the book details how his close relationship with his mother influenced his life, it is partially narrated by her.

Common released his second memoir, *Let Love Have The Last Word*, in May 2019. The book highlights his relationship with his daughter Omoye, romantic relationships, his parents, and his struggle to cement his perspective in the concept of love.

## Activism

Common is a [vegan](/wiki/Veganism) and he is a supporter of [animal rights](/wiki/Animal_rights) and [PETA](/wiki/People_for_the_Ethical_Treatment_of_Animals). He appeared in a print advertisement for PETA titled "Think Before You Eat," and in a documentary titled *Holistic Wellness for the Hip-Hop Generation* where he promoted vegetarianism.

Common is also part of the "Knowing Is Beautiful" movement, which supports [HIV/AIDS](/wiki/HIV/AIDS) awareness. He is featured in the video for "[Yes We Can](/wiki/Yes_We_Can_(will.i.am_song))," a song in support of the candidacy of [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama), which made its debut on the internet on February 2, 2008. Common pledged to stop using [anti-gay](/wiki/Homophobia) lyrics in his music.

Common is the founder of the Common Ground Foundation, a non-profit that seeks to empower underprivileged youth to be contributing citizens and strong leaders in the world. The foundation includes programs dedicated to leadership development & empowerment, educational development, creative expression, as well as a book club. In 2014, Common Ground inaugurated the AAHH! Fest music festival in Chicago's [Union Park](/wiki/Union_Park_(Chicago)). After a one-year hiatus, AAHH! Fest returned in 2016, but was canceled one week before showtime in 2017. An official announcement stated the fest would return September 15, 2018.

In 2017, Common performed concerts in several California prisons, as well as on the grounds of the California State Capitol. This concert tour inspired him to found Imagine Justice, another non-profit organization.

In May 2020, in response to the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic), Common launched a social media campaign through Imagine Justice, dubbed #WeMatterToo, with dozens of advocacy and activist groups calling attention to the threat that the pandemic poses for incarcerated men and women and intended to create greater public awareness about conditions facing incarcerated populations. The campaign includes the production of short films that will include recordings of inmates expressing their concern about the coronavirus spreading inside prisons.

Along with other rappers and activists, Common appeared in the award-winning documentary short film [*#Bars4Justice*](/wiki/Bars4justice), which was shot in [Ferguson, Missouri](/wiki/Ferguson,_Missouri) and produced by *[Nation19](/wiki/Nation19)* Magazine. Common performed with [Andra Day](/wiki/Andra_Day) in the opening musical performance for the [March for Our Lives](/wiki/March_for_Our_Lives) anti-gun violence rally in Washington, D.C., on March 24, 2018. He also performed at the 50th anniversary of Martin Luther King's death in Memphis, at an event co-hosted by the labor union [UNITE HERE](/wiki/UNITE_HERE). Common was later accused of joining anti-union efforts by crossing a UNITE HERE picket line in Boston.

He supported the [Kamala Harris 2024 presidential campaign](/wiki/Kamala_Harris_2024_presidential_campaign) and performed at the [2024 Democratic National Convention](/wiki/2024_Democratic_National_Convention).

## Other activities and honours

On May 4, 2019, Common served as spring commencement speaker at the [Florida Agricultural and Mechanical University](/wiki/Florida_Agricultural_and_Mechanical_University) (FAMU) and after his address, was awarded an honorary [DFA](/wiki/Doctor_of_Fine_Arts) degree.

## Personal life

Common has a daughter, Omoye, from his relationship with his ex-fiancée Kim Jones. Omoye graduated from [Howard Law School](/wiki/Howard_Law_School) in 2022.

Common was in a relationship with comedian and actress [Tiffany Haddish](/wiki/Tiffany_Haddish) from mid-2020 to November 2021. In 2022, while working on the film [*Breathe*](/wiki/Breathe_(2024_film)), Common met and subsequently began dating singer and actress [Jennifer Hudson](/wiki/Jennifer_Hudson).

Common is a [vegan](/wiki/Vegan).

He is a lifelong fan of the [Chicago Bulls](/wiki/Chicago_Bulls) and [Chicago Bears](/wiki/Chicago_Bears). He is a Christian, attending [Trinity United Church of Christ](/wiki/Trinity_United_Church_of_Christ) in Chicago.
