# List of Chinese monarchs

Imagined depiction of [Qin Shi Huang](/wiki/Qin_Shi_Huang), the first [Emperor of a unified China](/wiki/Emperor_of_China), (made during the [Qing dynasty](/wiki/Qing_dynasty))

The [Chinese monarchs](/wiki/Chinese_sovereign) were the rulers of [China](/wiki/History_of_China) during [Ancient](/wiki/History_of_China#Ancient_China) and [Imperial](/wiki/History_of_China#Imperial_China) periods. The earliest rulers in traditional [Chinese historiography](/wiki/Chinese_historiography) are of [mythological origin](/wiki/Three_Sovereigns_and_Five_Emperors), and followed by the [Xia dynasty](/wiki/Xia_dynasty) of highly uncertain and contested historicity. During the subsequent [Shang](/wiki/Shang_dynasty) (c. 1600–1046 BCE) and [Zhou](/wiki/Zhou_dynasty) (1046–256 BCE) dynasties, rulers were referred to as *Wang* 王, meaning [king](/wiki/King). China was [fully united](/wiki/Qin%27s_wars_of_unification) for the first time by [Qin Shi Huang](/wiki/Qin_Shi_Huang) (r. 259–210 BCE), who established the [first Imperial dynasty](/wiki/Qin_dynasty), adopting the title *[Huangdi](/wiki/Emperor_of_China)* (皇帝), meaning Emperor, which remained in use until the Imperial system's [fall in 1912](/wiki/1911_Revolution).

At no point during Ancient or Imperial China was there a formalized means to confer legitimate succession between rulers. From the Zhou dynasty onwards, monarchs justified their reigns by claiming the [Mandate of Heaven](/wiki/Mandate_of_Heaven) (天命; *Tiānmìng*). The mandate held that a ruler and their successors had permission from the heavens to rule as long as they did so effectively. It also declared a ruler the [Son of Heaven](/wiki/Son_of_Heaven) (天子; *Tiānzǐ*), giving them the right to rule "[all under heaven](/wiki/Tianxia)" (天下; *Tiānxià*). Given the Mandate's subjective nature, rulers also utilized a variety of methods to retain support and justify their accession. This ranged from military enforcement, [political patronage](/wiki/Political_patronage), establishing peace and solidity, institutional reform, and [historical revisionism](/wiki/Historical_revisionism) to legitimize the dissolution of previous dynasties and their own succession. For most of Imperial China, the *[wuxing](/wiki/Wuxing_(Chinese_philosophy))* (五行; "Five Elements") philosophical scheme was also central to justify dynastic succession.

Most Chinese monarchs had many names. They were given a [personal name](/wiki/Personal_name) (名字; *Míngzi*) at birth, but later referred to by a [posthumous name](/wiki/Posthumous_name) (謚號; *Shìhào*)—which memorialized their accomplishments or character—due to a cultural [naming taboo](/wiki/Naming_taboo). Most emperors of the Imperial period also received a [temple name](/wiki/Temple_name) (廟號; *Miàohào*), used to venerate them in [ancestor worship](/wiki/Ancestor_veneration_in_China). From the rule of [Emperor Wu of Han](/wiki/Emperor_Wu_of_Han) (r. 141–87 BCE) onwards, emperors also adopted one or several [era names](/wiki/Chinese_era_name) (年號; *Niánhào*), or "reign mottos", to divide their rule by important events or accomplishments. [Ming](/wiki/Ming_dynasty) (1368–1644) and [Qing](/wiki/Qing_dynasty) (1644–1912) rulers are referred to solely by their era names, of which they only had one.

Apart from ethnic [Han](/wiki/Han_Chinese) rulers, China was also ruled by various non-Han monarchs, including [Jurchen](/wiki/Jurchen_people), [Khitan](/wiki/Khitan_people), [Manchu](/wiki/Manchu_people), [Mongol](/wiki/Mongols) and [Tangut](/wiki/Tangut_people) and many others. To justify their reign, non-Han rulers sometimes aligned themselves with the Confucian sages or the *[Chakravarti](/wiki/Chakravarti_(Sanskrit_term))* of [Chinese Buddhism](/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism). There are numerous lengthy periods where many competing kingdoms claimed the throne, many of whose legitimacy are still debated by scholars.

## Ancient China

### Mythological rulers

Further information: [Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors](/wiki/Three_Sovereigns_and_Five_Emperors)

In traditional [Chinese historiography](/wiki/Chinese_historiography), various models of mythological founding rulers exist. The relevancy of these figures to the earliest Chinese people is unknown, since most accounts of them were written from the [Warring States period](/wiki/Warring_States_period) (c. 475–221 BCE) onwards. The sinologist [Kwang-chih Chang](/wiki/Kwang-chih_Chang) has generalized the typical stages: "the first period was populated by [gods](/wiki/Deity), the second by [demigods](/wiki/Demigod)/[culture hero](/wiki/Culture_hero), and the third by the [legendary kings](/wiki/Mythological_king)." The primordial god [Pangu](/wiki/Pangu) is given by many texts as the earliest figure and is credited with [forming the world](/wiki/Creation_myth) by separating heaven and earth. Other gods include [Nüwa](/wiki/N%C3%BCwa), who repaired heaven; [Hou Yi](/wiki/Hou_Yi), a mythical archer; and [Gonggong](/wiki/Gonggong), a serpent-like [water deity](/wiki/List_of_water_deities).

Demigod and hero rulers from hero myths—the largest group [Chinese myths](/wiki/Chinese_mythology)—are attributed the invention of specific items, practices or traditions. Among the more important of them are [Fuxi](/wiki/Fuxi), the inventor of hunting; [Suiren](/wiki/Suiren), who invented fire; and [Shennong](/wiki/Shennong), who invented both agriculture and medicine. The subsequent legendary kings began with the [Yellow Emperor](/wiki/Yellow_Emperor) (黃帝), known as *Huangdi*, a major [culture hero](/wiki/Culture_hero) of Chinese civilization whose reign was considered exemplary. Succeeding rulers include some combination of [Shaohao](/wiki/Shaohao), [Zhuanxu](/wiki/Zhuanxu), [Emperor Ku](/wiki/Emperor_Ku), [Emperor Yao](/wiki/Emperor_Yao) and [Emperor Shun](/wiki/Emperor_Shun). Since the late Warring States onwards, early Chinese monarchs have traditionally been ground into the concept of the [Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors](/wiki/Three_Sovereigns_and_Five_Emperors); however, the chosen figures of this grouping varies considerably between sources. Generally, most accounts include at least Fuxi and Shennong among the Three Sovereigns as well as the Yellow Emperor, Yao and Shun among the Five Emperors.

### Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE)

Further information: [Shang dynasty](/wiki/Shang_dynasty)

Unlike the Xia, the [Shang dynasty](/wiki/Shang_dynasty)'s historicity is firmly established, due to [written records](/wiki/Oracle_bone_script) on divination objects known as [Oracle bones](/wiki/Oracle_bone). The oldest such oracle bones date to the [Late Shang](/wiki/Late_Shang) (c. 1250—1046 BCE), during the reign of [Wu Ding](/wiki/Wu_Ding) (1250–1192), putting the exact details of earlier rulers into doubt.
