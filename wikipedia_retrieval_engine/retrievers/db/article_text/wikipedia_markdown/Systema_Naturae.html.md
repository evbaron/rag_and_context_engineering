# Systema Naturae

## Infobox

- **Author:** Carl Linnaeus (Carl von Linné)
- **Subject:** Taxonomy
- **Genre:** Biological classification
- **Publication date:** 1735–1793
- **LC Class:** QH43.S21

****Systema Naturae**** (originally in [Latin](/wiki/Latin) written ****Systema Naturæ**** with the [ligature](/wiki/Ligature_(writing)) [æ](/wiki/%C3%86)) is one of the major works of [Swedish](/wiki/Sweden) botanist, zoologist, and physician [Carl Linnaeus](/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus) (1707–1778) and introduced the [Linnaean taxonomy](/wiki/Linnaean_taxonomy). Although the system, now known as [binomial nomenclature](/wiki/Binomial_nomenclature), was partially developed by the Bauhin brothers, [Gaspard](/wiki/Gaspard_Bauhin) and [Johann](/wiki/Johann_Bauhin), Linnaeus was the first to use it consistently throughout his book. The first edition was published in 1735. The full title of the 10th edition (1758), which was the most important one, was **Systema naturæ per regna tria naturæ, secundum classes, ordines, genera, species, cum characteribus, differentiis, synonymis, locis**, which appeared in English in 1806 with the title: "A General System of Nature, Through the Three Grand Kingdoms of Animals, Vegetables, and Minerals, Systematically Divided Into their Several Classes, Orders, Genera, Species, and Varieties, with their Habitations, Manners, Economy, Structure and Peculiarities".

The [tenth edition of this book](/wiki/10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae) (1758), published in [Stockholm](/wiki/Stockholm), is considered the starting point of [zoological nomenclature](/wiki/Zoological_nomenclature). In 1766–1768 Linnaeus published the much enhanced [12th edition](/wiki/12th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae), the last under his authorship. Another again enhanced work in the same style titled "**Systema Naturae**" was published by [Johann Friedrich Gmelin](/wiki/Johann_Friedrich_Gmelin) between 1788 and 1793. Since at least the early 20th century, zoologists have commonly recognized this as the last edition belonging to this series.

## Overview

Linnaeus (later known as "Carl von Linné", after his ennoblement in 1761) published the first edition of *Systema Naturae* in 1735, during his stay in the [Netherlands](/wiki/Netherlands). As was customary for the [scientific literature](/wiki/Scientific_literature) of its day, the book was published in [Latin](/wiki/Latin). In it, he outlined his ideas for the [hierarchical classification](/wiki/Hierarchical_classification) of the natural world, dividing it into the [animal kingdom](/wiki/Animal) (*regnum animale*), the [plant kingdom](/wiki/Plantae) (*regnum vegetabile*), and the "[mineral kingdom](/wiki/Mineral)" (*regnum lapideum*).

Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* lists only about 10,000 species of organisms, of which about 6,000 are plants and 4,236 are animals. According to the historian of botany [William T. Stearn](/wiki/William_T._Stearn), "Even in 1753, he believed that the number of species of plants in the whole world would hardly reach 10,000; in his whole career, he named about 7,700 species of flowering plants."

Linnaeus developed his classification of the plant kingdom in an attempt to describe and understand the natural world as a reflection of the logic of [God](/wiki/God)'s creation. His [sexual system](/wiki/Linnaean_taxonomy#For_plants), where species with the same number of [stamens](/wiki/Stamen) were treated in the same group, was convenient, but in his view artificial. Linnaeus believed in God's creation and that no deeper relationships were to be expressed. The classification of animals was more natural than for plants. For instance, [humans](/wiki/Human) were for the first time placed together with other [primates](/wiki/Primate), as [Anthropomorpha](/wiki/Anthropomorpha). They were also divided into four varieties, as distinguished by [skin color](/wiki/Human_skin_color) and corresponding with the four known [continents](/wiki/Continent) and [temperaments](/wiki/Four_temperaments). The tenth edition expanded on these varieties with [behavioral](/wiki/Behavior) and [cultural](/wiki/Culture) traits that the [Linnean Society](/wiki/The_Linnean_Society_of_London) acknowledges as having cemented [colonial](/wiki/Colonialism) [stereotypes](/wiki/Stereotype) and provided one of the foundations for [scientific racism](/wiki/Scientific_racism).

As a result of the popularity of the work, and the number of new specimens sent to him from around the world, Linnaeus kept publishing new and ever-expanding editions of his work. It grew from 11 very large pages in the first edition (1735) to 2,400 pages in the [12th edition](/wiki/12th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae) (1766–1768). Also, as the work progressed, he made changes; in the first edition, [whales](/wiki/Whale) were classified as [fishes](/wiki/Fish), following the work of Linnaeus' friend and "father of [ichthyology](/wiki/Ichthyology)" [Peter Artedi](/wiki/Peter_Artedi); in the 10th edition, published in 1758, whales were moved into the [mammal](/wiki/Mammal) class. In this same edition, he introduced two-part names (see [binomen](/wiki/Binomen)) for animal species, which he had done for plant species (see [binary name](/wiki/Binary_name)) in the 1753 publication of *[Species Plantarum](/wiki/Species_Plantarum)*. The system eventually developed into modern [Linnaean taxonomy](/wiki/Linnaean_taxonomy), a hierarchically organized [biological classification](/wiki/Biological_classification).

After Linnaeus' health declined in the early 1770s, publication of editions of *Systema Naturae* went in two directions. Another Swedish scientist, [Johan Andreas Murray](/wiki/Johan_Andreas_Murray), issued the *Regnum Vegetabile* section separately in 1774 as the *[Systema Vegetabilium](/wiki/Systema_Vegetabilium)*, confusingly labelled the 13th edition. Meanwhile, a 13th edition of the entire *Systema* appeared in parts between 1788 and 1793. It was as the *Systema Vegetabilium* that Linnaeus' work became widely known in England following translation from the Latin by the [Lichfield Botanical Society](/wiki/Lichfield_Botanical_Society), as *A System of Vegetables* (1783–1785).

## Taxonomy

In his **Imperium Naturæ**, Linnaeus established three kingdoms, namely **Regnum Animale**, **Regnum Vegetabile**, and **Regnum Lapideum**. This approach, the Animal, Vegetable, and Mineral Kingdoms, survives until today in the popular mind, notably in the form of parlour games: "Is it [animal, vegetable or mineral](/wiki/Animal,_vegetable_or_mineral)?" The classification was based on five levels: [kingdom](/wiki/Kingdom_(biology)), [class](/wiki/Class_(biology)), [order](/wiki/Order_(biology)), [genus](/wiki/Genus), and [species](/wiki/Species). While species and genus were seen as God-given (or "natural"), the three higher levels were seen by Linnaeus as constructs. The concept behind the set ranks being applied to all groups was to make a system that was easy to remember and navigate, a task in which most say he succeeded.

The 1735 classification of animals

Linnaeus's work had a huge impact on science; it was indispensable as a foundation for [biological nomenclature](/wiki/Biological_nomenclature), now regulated by the [Nomenclature Codes](/wiki/Nomenclature_Codes). Two of his works, the first edition of the **[Species Plantarum](/wiki/Species_Plantarum)** (1753) for plants and the [10th edition of the *Systema Naturæ*](/wiki/10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae) (1758), are accepted to be among the starting points of nomenclature. Most of his names for species and genera were published at very early dates and thus take [priority](/wiki/Priority_(biology)) over those of other, later authors. Zoology has one exception, which is a monograph on Swedish spiders, **[Svenska Spindlar](/wiki/Svenska_Spindlar)**, published by [Carl Clerck](/wiki/Carl_Alexander_Clerck) in 1757, so the names established there take priority over the Linnean names. His exceptional importance to science was less in the value of his taxonomy, but more in his deployment of skillful young students abroad to collect specimens. At the close of the 18th century, his system had effectively become the standard for biological classification.

### Animals

Only in the animal kingdom is the higher taxonomy of Linnaeus still more or less recognizable and some of these names are still in use, but usually not quite for the same groups as used by Linnaeus. He divided the Animal Kingdom into six classes; in the tenth edition (1758), these were:

1. **[Mammalia](/wiki/Mammalia_in_the_10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae)** comprised the [mammals](/wiki/Mammal). In the first edition, [whales](/wiki/Cetacea) and the [West Indian manatee](/wiki/West_Indian_manatee) were classified among the fishes.
2. **[Aves](/wiki/Aves_in_the_10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae)** comprised the [birds](/wiki/Bird). Linnaeus was the first to remove [bats](/wiki/Bat) from the birds and classify them under mammals.
3. **[Amphibia](/wiki/Amphibia_in_the_10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae)** comprised [amphibians](/wiki/Amphibian), [reptiles](/wiki/Reptile), and assorted fishes that are not of [Osteichthyes](/wiki/Osteichthyes).
4. **[Pisces](/wiki/Pisces_in_the_10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae)** comprised the [bony fishes](/wiki/Osteichthyes). These included the spiny-finned fishes ([Perciformes](/wiki/Perciformes)) as a separate order.
5. **[Insecta](/wiki/Insecta_in_the_10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae)** comprised all [arthropods](/wiki/Arthropod). [Crustaceans](/wiki/Crustacean), [arachnids](/wiki/Arachnid) and [myriapods](/wiki/Myriapod) were included as the order "Aptera".
6. **[Vermes](/wiki/Vermes_in_the_10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae)** comprised the remaining [invertebrates](/wiki/Invertebrate), roughly divided into "worms", [molluscs](/wiki/Mollusca), and hard-shelled organisms such as [echinoderms](/wiki/Echinoderm).

#### Humans

Linnaeus was one of the first scientists to classify humans as [primates](/wiki/Primates) (originally [Anthropomorpha](/wiki/Anthropomorpha) for "manlike"), eliciting some controversy for placing people among [animals](/wiki/Animals), thus not ruling over [nature](/wiki/Nature). He distinguished humans (*[Homo sapiens](/wiki/Homo_sapiens)*) from *[Homo troglodytes](/wiki/Human_taxonomy)*, a species of human-like creatures with exaggerated or [non-human](/wiki/Non-human) characteristics, despite finding limited evidence. He divided *Homo sapiens* into four varieties, corresponding with the four known [continents](/wiki/Continent) and [four temperaments](/wiki/Four_temperaments) (some editions also classify *Ferus* [wild children](/wiki/Feral_children) and *Monstrosus* [monstrous](/wiki/Human_taxonomy) to accommodate [adaptations](/wiki/Adaptations) to [extreme environments](/wiki/Extreme_environment)). The first edition included *Europæus albescens* (whitish Europeans), *Americanus rubescens* (reddish Americans), *Asiaticus fuscus* (tawny Asians), and *Africanus nigriculus* (blackish Africans). The 10th edition solidified these descriptions by removing the "ish" qualifiers (e.g. *albus* "white" instead of *albescens* "whitish") and revising the characterization of *Asiaticus* from *fuscus* (tawny) to *luridus* (pale yellow). It also incorporates [behavioral](/wiki/Behavior) and [cultural](/wiki/Culture) traits that the [Linnean Society](/wiki/The_Linnean_Society_of_London) recognizes as having cemented [colonial](/wiki/Colonialism) [stereotypes](/wiki/Stereotypes) and provided one of the foundations for [scientific racism](/wiki/Scientific_racism).

### Plants

Key to the Sexual System from the [10th (1758) edition](/wiki/10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae) of **Systema Naturæ**

The orders and classes of plants, according to his **Systema Sexuale**, were never intended to represent natural groups (as opposed to his **[ordines naturales](/wiki/Ordo_naturalis)** in his **Philosophia Botanica**), but only for use in identification. They were used in that sense well into the 19th century.

The Linnaean classes for plants, in the *Sexual System*, were:

- Classis 1. Monandria
- Classis 2. Diandria
- Classis 3. Triandria
- Classis 4. Tetrandria
- Classis 5. Pentandria
- Classis 6. Hexandria
- Classis 7. Heptandria
- Classis 8. Octandria
- Classis 9. Enneandria
- Classis 10. Decandria
- Classis 11. Dodecandria
- Classis 12. [Icosandria](/wiki/Icosandria)
- Classis 13. Polyandra
- Classis 14. Didynamia
- Classis 15. Tetradynamia
- Classis 16. Monadelphia
- Classis 17. Diadelphia
- Classis 18. Polyadelphia
- Classis 19. Syngenesia
- Classis 20. Gynandria
- Classis 21. Monoecia
- Classis 22. Dioecia
- Classis 23. Polygamia
- Classis 24. [Cryptogamia](/wiki/Cryptogam)

### Minerals

Linnaeus's taxonomy of [minerals](/wiki/Minerals) has long since fallen out of use. In the 10th edition, 1758, of the *Systema Naturæ*, the Linnaean classes were:

- Classis 1. Petræ ([rocks](/wiki/Rock_(geology)))
- Classis 2. Mineræ ([minerals](/wiki/Mineral) and [ores](/wiki/Ore))
- Classis 3. Fossilia ([fossils](/wiki/Fossil) and [aggregates](/wiki/Sediments))
