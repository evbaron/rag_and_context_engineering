The **Philippines**, officially the **Republic of the Philippines**, is an [archipelagic country](/wiki/Archipelagic_state) in [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia). Located in the western [Pacific Ocean](/wiki/Pacific_Ocean), it consists of about [7,641 islands](/wiki/List_of_islands_of_the_Philippines), with a total area of about 300,000 square kilometers, which are broadly categorized in [three main geographical divisions](/wiki/Island_groups_of_the_Philippines) from north to south: [Luzon](/wiki/Luzon), [Visayas](/wiki/Visayas), and [Mindanao](/wiki/Mindanao). With a population of over 114 million, it is the world's [twelfth-most-populous country](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population).

The Philippines is bounded by the [South China Sea](/wiki/South_China_Sea) to the west, the [Philippine Sea](/wiki/Philippine_Sea) to the east, and the [Celebes Sea](/wiki/Celebes_Sea) to the south. It shares [maritime borders](/wiki/Maritime_border) with [Taiwan](/wiki/Taiwan) to the north, [Japan](/wiki/Japan) to the northeast, [Palau](/wiki/Palau) to the east and southeast, [Indonesia](/wiki/Indonesia) to the south, [Malaysia](/wiki/Malaysia) to the southwest, [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam) to the west, and [China](/wiki/China) to the northwest. It has [diverse ethnicities](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_the_Philippines) and [a rich culture](/wiki/Culture_of_the_Philippines). [Manila](/wiki/Manila) is [the country's capital](/wiki/Capital_of_the_Philippines), and [its most populated city](/wiki/Cities_of_the_Philippines#Largest_cities) is [Quezon City](/wiki/Quezon_City). Both are within [Metro Manila](/wiki/Metro_Manila).

[Negritos](/wiki/Negrito), the archipelago's earliest inhabitants, were followed by [waves](/wiki/Models_of_migration_to_the_Philippines) of [Austronesian peoples](/wiki/Austronesian_peoples). The adoption of [animism](/wiki/Animism), [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism) with [Buddhist](/wiki/Buddhist) influence, and [Islam](/wiki/Islam) established [island-kingdoms](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(900%E2%80%931565)). Extensive overseas trade with neighbors such as the late [Tang](/wiki/Tang_dynasty) or [Song](/wiki/Southern_Song) empire brought [Chinese](/wiki/Sangley) people to the archipelago as well, which would also gradually settle in and [intermix](/wiki/Interethnic_marriage) over the centuries. The arrival of the explorer [Ferdinand Magellan](/wiki/Ferdinand_Magellan) marked the beginning of [Spanish colonization](/wiki/Spanish_Colonization_in_the_Philippines). In 1543, Spanish explorer [Ruy López de Villalobos](/wiki/Ruy_L%C3%B3pez_de_Villalobos) named the archipelago *las Islas Filipinas* in honor of [King Philip II](/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain). [Catholicism](/wiki/Catholic_Church) became the dominant religion, and Manila became the western hub of [trans-Pacific trade](/wiki/Spanish_treasure_fleet). [Hispanic](/wiki/Spaniard) immigrants from [Latin America](/wiki/Latin_American_Asian) and [Iberia](/wiki/Iberia) would also selectively colonize. The [Philippine Revolution](/wiki/Philippine_Revolution) began in 1896, and became entwined with the 1898 [Spanish–American War](/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War). Spain ceded the territory to the United States, and [Filipino revolutionaries](/wiki/Hong_Kong_Junta) declared the [First Philippine Republic](/wiki/First_Philippine_Republic). The ensuing [Philippine–American War](/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War) ended with the United States controlling the territory until the [Japanese invasion](/wiki/Philippines_campaign_(1941%E2%80%931942)) of the islands during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II). After [the United States retook the Philippines from the Japanese](/wiki/Philippines_campaign_(1944%E2%80%931945)), the Philippines became independent in 1946. Since then, the country notably experienced a [period of martial law](/wiki/Martial_law_under_Ferdinand_Marcos) from 1972 to 1981 under the dictatorship of [Ferdinand Marcos](/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos) and his subsequent overthrow by the [People Power Revolution](/wiki/People_Power_Revolution) in 1986, returning to democracy.

The Philippines is an [emerging market](/wiki/Emerging_market) and a [developing](/wiki/Developing_country) and [newly industrialized country](/wiki/Newly_industrialized_country), [whose economy](/wiki/Economy_of_the_Philippines) is transitioning from being agricultural to service- and manufacturing-centered. It has a variety of natural resources and a globally-significant [level of biodiversity](/wiki/Megadiverse_countries). The country is also part of [multiple international organizations and forums](/wiki/International_organization_membership_of_the_Philippines), mainly in [ASEAN](/wiki/ASEAN). Despite its fast economic growth, it continues to struggle with [inequality](/wiki/Income_inequality_in_the_Philippines), widespread [corruption](/wiki/Corruption_in_the_Philippines), and vulnerability to natural disasters due to its location within the Pacific [Ring of Fire](/wiki/Ring_of_Fire), and to the equator, making it prone to [earthquakes](/wiki/List_of_earthquakes_in_the_Philippines), [monsoon rains](/wiki/Monsoon), and [typhoons](/wiki/Typhoons_in_the_Philippines), to which the Philippines has built a [resilience](/wiki/Community_resilience).

## Etymology

Main article: [Names of the Philippines](/wiki/Names_of_the_Philippines)

During his 1542 expedition, Spanish explorer [Ruy López de Villalobos](/wiki/Ruy_L%C3%B3pez_de_Villalobos) named the islands of [Leyte](/wiki/Leyte) and [Samar](/wiki/Samar) "*Felipinas*" after the [Prince of Asturias](/wiki/Prince_of_Asturias), later [Philip II of Castile](/wiki/Philip_II_of_Castile). Eventually, the name "*Las Islas Filipinas*" would be used for the archipelago's Spanish possessions. Other names, such as "*Islas del Poniente*" (Western Islands), "*Islas del Oriente*" (Eastern Islands), Ferdinand Magellan's name, and "*San Lázaro*" (Islands of St. Lazarus), were used by the Spanish to refer to islands in the region before Spanish rule was established.

During the [Philippine Revolution](/wiki/Philippine_Revolution), the [Malolos Congress](/wiki/Malolos_Congress) proclaimed it the *República Filipina* ([Philippine Republic](/wiki/First_Philippine_Republic)). American colonial authorities referred to the country as the Philippine Islands (a translation of the Spanish name). The [United States](/wiki/United_States) began changing its nomenclature from "the Philippine Islands" to "the Philippines" in the Philippine Autonomy Act and the [Jones Law](/wiki/Jones_Law_(Philippines)). The official title "Republic of the Philippines" was included in the 1935 constitution as the name of the future independent state, and in all succeeding constitutional revisions.

## History

Main article: [History of the Philippines](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines)

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of Philippine history](/wiki/Timeline_of_Philippine_history).

### Prehistory (pre–900)

Main article: [Prehistory of the Philippines](/wiki/Prehistory_of_the_Philippines)

The [Manunggul burial jar](/wiki/Manunggul_Jar), one of the numerous [burial jars](/wiki/Burial_jar) found on the cave system

There is [evidence](/wiki/Archaeology_of_the_Philippines) of early [hominins](/wiki/Hominins) living in what is now the Philippines as early as 709,000 years ago. Bones from [Callao Cave](/wiki/Callao_Cave) potentially represent an otherwise unknown species, *[Homo luzonensis](/wiki/Homo_luzonensis)*, who lived 50,000 to 67,000 years ago. The oldest [modern human](/wiki/Modern_human) remains on the islands are from the [Tabon Caves](/wiki/Tabon_Caves) of [Palawan](/wiki/Palawan), [U/Th-dated](/wiki/U/Th-dated) to 47,000 ± 11–10,000 years ago. [Tabon Man](/wiki/Tabon_Man) is presumably a [Negrito](/wiki/Negrito), among the archipelago's earliest inhabitants descended from the first human migrations out of Africa via the coastal route along [southern Asia](/wiki/South_Asia) to the now-sunken landmasses of [Sundaland](/wiki/Sundaland) and [Sahul](/wiki/Sahul).

The first Austronesians reached the Philippines from Taiwan around 2200 BC, settling the [Batanes](/wiki/Batanes) Islands (where they built stone fortresses known as *[ijangs](/wiki/Ijang)*) and northern [Luzon](/wiki/Luzon). Jade artifacts have been dated to 2000 BC, with [lingling-o](/wiki/Lingling-o) jade items made in Luzon with raw materials from Taiwan. By 1000 BC, the inhabitants of the archipelago had developed into four societies: [hunter-gatherer](/wiki/Hunter-gatherer) tribes, warrior societies, highland [plutocracies](/wiki/Plutocracies), and port principalities. The Philippines prior to foreign colonization was believed to be egalitarian.

### Early states (900–1565)

Main article: [History of the Philippines (900–1565)](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(900%E2%80%931565))

The earliest known surviving written record in the Philippines is the 900 AD [Laguna Copperplate Inscription](/wiki/Laguna_Copperplate_Inscription), which was written in [Old Malay](/wiki/Old_Malay) using the early [Kawi](/wiki/Kawi_alphabet) script. By the 14th century, several large coastal settlements emerged as trading centers and became the focus of [societal changes](/wiki/Cultural_achievements_of_pre-colonial_Philippines). Some [polities](/wiki/Polities) had exchanges with other states throughout Asia. Trade with China began during the late [Tang dynasty](/wiki/Tang_dynasty), and expanded during the [Song dynasty](/wiki/Song_dynasty). Throughout the second millennium AD, some polities were part of the [tributary system of China](/wiki/Tributary_system_of_China). With extensive trade and diplomacy, this brought [Southern](/wiki/Northern_and_southern_China) [Chinese](/wiki/Han_Chinese) merchants and migrants from [Southern Fujian](/wiki/Southern_Fujian), who would gradually settle and intermix in the Philippines. Indian cultural traits such as linguistic terms and religious practices [began to spread](/wiki/Indian_influences_in_early_Philippine_polities) in the Philippines during the 14th century, via the Indianized Hindu [Majapahit Empire](/wiki/Majapahit). By the 15th century, Islam was established in the [Sulu Archipelago](/wiki/Sulu_Archipelago) and spread from there.

A couple portrayed in 1590's Early Spanish colonial period of the Philippines, draped in gold

Polities founded in the Philippines between the 10th and 16th centuries include [Maynila](/wiki/Maynila_(historical_polity)), [Tondo](/wiki/Tondo_(historical_polity)), [Namayan](/wiki/Namayan), [Pangasinan](/wiki/Pangasinan_(historical_polity)), [Caboloan](/wiki/Caboloan), [Cebu](/wiki/Cebu_(historical_state)), [Butuan](/wiki/Butuan_(historical_polity)), [Maguindanao](/wiki/Sultanate_of_Maguindanao), [Lanao](/wiki/Confederate_States_of_Lanao), [Sulu](/wiki/Sultanate_of_Sulu), and [Ma-i](/wiki/Ma-i). The early polities typically had a three-tier social structure: nobility, freemen, and dependent debtor-bondsmen. Among the nobility were leaders known as [datus](/wiki/Datu), who were responsible for ruling autonomous groups ([barangays](/wiki/Precolonial_barangay) or dulohan). When the barangays banded together to form a larger settlement or a geographically looser alliance, their more-esteemed members would be recognized as a "[paramount datu](/wiki/Paramount_rulers_in_early_Philippine_history)", [rajah](/wiki/Rajah) or [sultan](/wiki/Sultan), and would rule the community. Population density is thought to have been low during the 14th to 16th centuries due to the [frequency of typhoons](/wiki/Typhoon#Frequency) and the Philippines' location on the Pacific [Ring of Fire](/wiki/Ring_of_Fire). Portuguese explorer [Ferdinand Magellan](/wiki/Ferdinand_Magellan) arrived in 1521, claimed the islands for Spain, and was killed by [Lapulapu](/wiki/Lapulapu)'s men in the [Battle of Mactan](/wiki/Battle_of_Mactan).

### Spanish and American colonial rule (1565–1934)

Main articles: [History of the Philippines (1565–1898)](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1565%E2%80%931898)) and [History of the Philippines (1898–1946)](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1898%E2%80%931946))

[Manila](/wiki/Manila), 1847

Unification and colonization by the [Crown of Castile](/wiki/Crown_of_Castile) began when Spanish explorer [Miguel López de Legazpi](/wiki/Miguel_L%C3%B3pez_de_Legazpi) arrived from [New Spain](/wiki/New_Spain) in 1565. Many [Filipinos](/wiki/Filipinos) were brought to New Spain [as slaves](/wiki/History_of_Spanish_slavery_in_the_Philippines) and forced crew, whereas many [Latin Americans](/wiki/Latin_American_Asian) were brought to the Philippines as soldiers and colonists. The Philippines hosts the only Latin American established districts in Asia. [Spanish Manila](/wiki/Intramuros) became the capital of the [Captaincy General of the Philippines](/wiki/Captaincy_General_of_the_Philippines) and the [Spanish East Indies](/wiki/Spanish_East_Indies) in 1571, Spanish territories in Asia and the Pacific. The Spanish invaded local states using the principle of [divide and conquer](/wiki/Divide_and_rule), bringing most of what is the present-day Philippines under one unified administration.

Disparate barangays were deliberately [consolidated into towns](/wiki/Reductions), where [Catholic missionaries](/wiki/Friars_in_Spanish_Philippines) could more easily convert their inhabitants to [Christianity](/wiki/Christianity), which was initially [Syncretist](/wiki/Religious_Syncretism). [Christianization](/wiki/Christianization) by the [Spanish friars](/wiki/Friars_in_Spanish_Philippines) occurred mostly across the settled lowlands over the course of time. From 1565 to 1821, the Philippines was governed as a territory of the [Mexico City](/wiki/Mexico_City)-based Viceroyalty of New Spain; it was then administered from [Madrid](/wiki/Madrid) after the [Mexican War of Independence](/wiki/Mexican_War_of_Independence). Manila became the western hub of [trans-Pacific trade](/wiki/Spanish_treasure_fleet) by [Manila galleons](/wiki/Manila_galleon) built in [Bicol](/wiki/Bicol_Region) and [Cavite](/wiki/Cavite).

During its rule, Spain spent a significant sum of its treasury quelling [indigenous revolts](/wiki/Philippine_revolts_against_Spain) and defending against external military attacks, including [Moro piracy](/wiki/Piracy_in_the_Sulu_and_Celebes_Seas), a 17th-century [war against the Dutch](/wiki/Battles_of_La_Naval_de_Manila), 18th-century [British occupation of Manila](/wiki/British_occupation_of_Manila), and conflict with Muslims in the south.

Administration of the Philippines was considered a drain on the economy of New Spain, and abandoning it or trading it for other territory was debated. This course of action was opposed because of the islands' economic potential, security, and the desire to continue religious conversion in the region. The colony survived on an annual subsidy from the Spanish crown averaging 250,000 pesos, usually paid as 75 tons of silver bullion from the Americas. [British forces occupied Manila](/wiki/British_occupation_of_Manila) from 1762 to 1764 during the [Seven Years' War](/wiki/Seven_Years%27_War). Spanish rule was restored with the [1763 Treaty of Paris](/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1763)). The Spanish considered their war with the Muslims in Southeast Asia an extension of the *[Reconquista](/wiki/Reconquista)*. The [Spanish–Moro conflict](/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93Moro_conflict) lasted for several hundred years. Spain conquered portions of [Mindanao](/wiki/Mindanao) and [Jolo](/wiki/Jolo) during the last quarter of the 19th century, and the Muslim [Moro](/wiki/Moro_people) in the [Sultanate of Sulu](/wiki/Sultanate_of_Sulu) acknowledged Spanish sovereignty.

*[Ilustrados](/wiki/Ilustrado)* in [Madrid](/wiki/Madrid) around 1890

Philippine ports opened to world trade during the 19th century, and Filipino society began to change. Social identity changed, with the term *Filipino* encompassing all residents of the archipelago instead of solely referring to [Spaniards born in the Philippines](/wiki/Spanish_Filipino).

Revolutionary sentiment grew in 1872 after 200 locally recruited [colonial troops](/wiki/Colonial_troops) and laborers alongside [three activist Catholic priests](/wiki/Gomburza) were executed on [questionable grounds](/wiki/1872_Cavite_mutiny). This inspired the [Propaganda Movement](/wiki/Propaganda_Movement), organized by [Marcelo H. del Pilar](/wiki/Marcelo_H._del_Pilar), [José Rizal](/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Rizal), [Graciano López Jaena](/wiki/Graciano_L%C3%B3pez_Jaena), and [Mariano Ponce](/wiki/Mariano_Ponce), which advocated political reform in the Philippines. Rizal was executed on December 30, 1896, for rebellion, and his death radicalized many who had been loyal to Spain. Attempts at reform met with resistance; [Andrés Bonifacio](/wiki/Andr%C3%A9s_Bonifacio) founded the [Katipunan](/wiki/Katipunan) secret society, which sought independence from Spain through armed revolt, in 1892.

The Katipunan [Cry of Pugad Lawin](/wiki/Cry_of_Pugad_Lawin) began the [Philippine Revolution](/wiki/Philippine_Revolution) in 1896. Internal disputes led to the [Tejeros Convention](/wiki/Tejeros_Convention), at which Bonifacio lost his position and [Emilio Aguinaldo](/wiki/Emilio_Aguinaldo) was elected the new leader of the revolution. The 1897 [Pact of Biak-na-Bato](/wiki/Pact_of_Biak-na-Bato) resulted in the [Hong Kong Junta](/wiki/Hong_Kong_Junta) government in exile. The [Spanish–American War](/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War) began the following year, and reached the Philippines; Aguinaldo returned, resumed the revolution, and [declared independence](/wiki/Philippine_Declaration_of_Independence) from Spain on June 12, 1898. In December 1898, the islands were [ceded by Spain](/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1898)) to the United States with [Puerto Rico](/wiki/Puerto_Rico) and [Guam](/wiki/Guam) after the [Spanish–American War](/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War). [Spain](/wiki/Spain) ruled the Philippines for 333 years.

The [First Philippine Republic](/wiki/First_Philippine_Republic) was promulgated on January 21, 1899. Lack of recognition by the United States led to an [outbreak of hostilities](/wiki/Battle_of_Manila_(1899)) that, after refusal by the U.S. on-scene military commander of a cease-fire proposal and a declaration of war by the nascent Republic, escalated into the [Philippine–American War](/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War).

Filipino General [Gregorio del Pilar](/wiki/Gregorio_del_Pilar) and his troops in Pampanga around 1898, during the [Philippine-American War](/wiki/Philippine-American_War)

The war resulted in the deaths of 250,000 to 1 million civilians, primarily due to famine and disease. Many Filipinos were transported by the Americans to [concentration camps](/wiki/List_of_concentration_and_internment_camps#Philippines), where thousands died. After the fall of the First Philippine Republic in 1902, an [American civilian government](/wiki/Insular_Government_of_the_Philippine_Islands) was established with the [Philippine Organic Act](/wiki/Philippine_Organic_Act_(1902)). American forces continued to secure and extend their control of the islands, suppressing an attempted [extension of the Philippine Republic](/wiki/Tagalog_Republic#Sakay), [securing the Sultanate of Sulu](/wiki/Kiram%E2%80%93Bates_Treaty), establishing control of interior mountainous areas which had resisted Spanish conquest, and encouraging large-scale resettlement of Christians in once-predominantly-Muslim Mindanao.

### Commonwealth and World War II (1935–1946)

Further information: [Commonwealth of the Philippines](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_the_Philippines) and [Military history of the Philippines during World War II](/wiki/Military_history_of_the_Philippines_during_World_War_II)

Cultural developments in the Philippines strengthened a national identity, and [Tagalog](/wiki/Tagalog_language) began to take precedence over other local languages. Governmental functions were gradually given to Filipinos by the [Taft Commission](/wiki/Taft_Commission); the 1934 [Tydings–McDuffie Act](/wiki/Tydings%E2%80%93McDuffie_Act) granted a ten-year transition to independence through the creation of the Commonwealth of the Philippines the following year, with [Manuel Quezon](/wiki/Manuel_L._Quezon) president and [Sergio Osmeña](/wiki/Sergio_Osme%C3%B1a) vice president. Quezon's priorities were defence, social justice, inequality, economic diversification, and national character. [Filipino](/wiki/Filipino_language) (a standardized variety of Tagalog) became the national language, [women's suffrage was introduced](/wiki/1937_Philippine_women%27s_suffrage_plebiscite), and [land reform](/wiki/Land_reform_in_the_Philippines) was considered. The introduction of women's suffrage during the Commonwealth period (1935–1946) signifies a progressive step towards [gender equality](/wiki/Gender_equality) in the Philippines. This gave women the right to vote and participate in the country's political processes.

[General Douglas MacArthur](/wiki/General_Douglas_MacArthur) and [Sergio Osmeña](/wiki/Sergio_Osme%C3%B1a) *(left)* coming ashore during the [Battle of Leyte](/wiki/Battle_of_Leyte) on October 20, 1944

The [Empire of Japan](/wiki/Empire_of_Japan) invaded the Philippines in December 1941 [during World War II](/wiki/Military_history_of_the_Philippines_during_World_War_II), and the [Second Philippine Republic](/wiki/Second_Philippine_Republic) was established as a [puppet state](/wiki/Puppet_state) governed by [Jose P. Laurel](/wiki/Jose_P._Laurel). Beginning in 1942, the [Japanese occupation of the Philippines](/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_the_Philippines) was [opposed](/wiki/Philippine_resistance_against_Japan) by large-scale [underground guerrilla activity](/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_the_Philippines#Resistance). [Atrocities and war crimes](/wiki/Japanese_war_crimes) were committed during the war, including the [Bataan Death March](/wiki/Bataan_Death_March) and the [Manila massacre](/wiki/Manila_massacre). The Philippine resistance and Allied troops [defeated the Japanese](/wiki/Philippines_campaign_(1944%E2%80%931945)) in 1944 and 1945. Over one million Filipinos were estimated to have died by the end of the war. On October 11, 1945, the Philippines became a [founding member](/wiki/Member_states_of_the_United_Nations) of the [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations). On July 4, 1946, during the presidency of [Manuel Roxas](/wiki/Manuel_Roxas), the country's independence was recognized by the United States with the [Treaty of Manila](/wiki/Treaty_of_Manila_(1946)).

### Independence (1946–present)

Main articles: [History of the Philippines (1946–1965)](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1946%E2%80%931965)), [History of the Philippines (1965–1986)](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1965%E2%80%931986)), and [History of the Philippines (1986–present)](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1986%E2%80%93present))

Efforts at post-war reconstruction and ending the [Hukbalahap Rebellion](/wiki/Hukbalahap_Rebellion) succeeded during [Ramon Magsaysay](/wiki/Ramon_Magsaysay)'s presidency, but sporadic communist insurgency continued to flare up long afterward. Under Magsaysay's successor, [Carlos P. Garcia](/wiki/Carlos_P._Garcia), the government initiated a [Filipino First policy](/wiki/Filipino_First_policy) which promoted Filipino-owned businesses. Succeeding Garcia, [Diosdado Macapagal](/wiki/Diosdado_Macapagal) moved Independence Day from July 4 to June 12—the date of Emilio Aguinaldo's declaration— and pursued [a claim](/wiki/North_Borneo_dispute) on eastern [North Borneo](/wiki/North_Borneo).

The attending leaders of the [Manila Summit Conference](/wiki/Manila_Summit_Conference) in front of the Congress Building in Manila, hosted by [Ferdinand Marcos](/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos) on October 24, 1966

In 1965, Macapagal [lost](/wiki/1965_Philippine_presidential_election) the presidential election to [Ferdinand Marcos](/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos). [Early in his presidency](/wiki/First_term_of_the_presidency_of_Ferdinand_Marcos), Marcos began infrastructure projects funded mostly by foreign loans; this improved the economy, and contributed to his [reelection in 1969](/wiki/1969_Philippine_presidential_election). Near the end of his last constitutionally-permitted term, Marcos [declared martial law](/wiki/Proclamation_No._1081) on September 21, 1972 using the specter of communism and began to [rule by decree](/wiki/Rule_by_decree); the period was characterized by [political repression](/wiki/Political_detainees_under_the_Marcos_dictatorship), [censorship](/wiki/Journalism_during_the_Marcos_dictatorship), and [human rights violations](/wiki/Human_rights_abuses_of_the_Marcos_dictatorship). [Monopolies](/wiki/Monopolies_in_the_Philippines_(1965%E2%80%931986)) controlled by [Marcos's cronies](/wiki/Cronies_of_Ferdinand_Marcos) were established in key industries, including [logging](/wiki/Deforestation_in_the_Philippines#Deforestation_during_the_martial_law_era) and broadcasting; a sugar monopoly led to [a famine on the island of Negros](/wiki/Negros_famine). With his wife, [Imelda](/wiki/Imelda_Marcos), Marcos was accused of corruption and [embezzling billions of dollars](/wiki/Unexplained_wealth_of_the_Marcos_family) of public funds. Marcos's heavy borrowing [early in his presidency](/wiki/1969_Philippine_balance_of_payments_crisis) resulted in [economic crashes](/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_Philippines_(1965%E2%80%931986)), exacerbated by an [early 1980s recession](/wiki/Early_1980s_recession) where the economy contracted by 7.3 percent annually in 1984 and 1985.

On August 21, 1983, opposition leader [Benigno Aquino Jr.](/wiki/Ninoy_Aquino) (Marcos's chief rival) was [assassinated on the tarmac](/wiki/Assassination_of_Ninoy_Aquino) at [Manila International Airport](/wiki/Ninoy_Aquino_International_Airport). Marcos called a snap [presidential election in 1986](/wiki/1986_Philippine_presidential_election) which proclaimed him the winner, but the results were widely regarded as fraudulent. The resulting protests led to the [People Power Revolution](/wiki/People_Power_Revolution), which forced Marcos and his allies to flee to [Hawaii](/wiki/Hawaii). Aquino's widow, [Corazon](/wiki/Corazon_Aquino), was installed as president and a new constitution was promulgated.

The return of democracy and government reforms which began in 1986 were hampered by [national debt](/wiki/National_debt_of_the_Philippines), government corruption, and [coup attempts](/wiki/Coup_attempts_against_Corazon_Aquino). A [communist insurgency](/wiki/Communist_rebellion_in_the_Philippines) and military conflict with [Moro separatists](/wiki/Moro_conflict) persisted; the administration also faced a series of disasters, including the eruption of [Mount Pinatubo](/wiki/Mount_Pinatubo) in June 1991. Aquino was succeeded by [Fidel V. Ramos](/wiki/Fidel_V._Ramos), who [liberalized](/wiki/Economic_liberalization) the national economy with [privatization](/wiki/Privatization) and [deregulation](/wiki/Deregulation). Ramos's economic gains were overshadowed by the onset of the [1997 Asian financial crisis](/wiki/1997_Asian_financial_crisis). His successor, [Joseph Estrada](/wiki/Joseph_Estrada), prioritized public housing but faced corruption allegations which led to his overthrow by the [2001 EDSA Revolution](/wiki/Second_EDSA_Revolution) and the succession of Vice President [Gloria Macapagal Arroyo](/wiki/Gloria_Macapagal_Arroyo) on January 20, 2001.

Arroyo's [nine-year administration](/wiki/Presidency_of_Gloria_Macapagal_Arroyo) was marked by economic growth, but was tainted by corruption and political scandals, including [electoral fraud allegations](/wiki/Hello_Garci_scandal) during the [2004 presidential election](/wiki/2004_Philippine_presidential_election). Economic growth continued during [Benigno Aquino III](/wiki/Benigno_Aquino_III)'s administration, which advocated good governance and transparency. Aquino III signed [a peace agreement](/wiki/Comprehensive_Agreement_on_the_Bangsamoro) with the [Moro Islamic Liberation Front](/wiki/Moro_Islamic_Liberation_Front) (MILF) resulting in the [Bangsamoro Organic Law](/wiki/Bangsamoro_Organic_Law) establishing an autonomous [Bangsamoro](/wiki/Bangsamoro) region, but a [shootout with MILF rebels in Mamasapano](/wiki/Mamasapano_clash) delayed passage of the law.

Growing public frustration with post-[EDSA](/wiki/People_Power_Revolution) governance led to the [2016 election](/wiki/2016_Philippine_presidential_election) of populist [Rodrigo Duterte](/wiki/Rodrigo_Duterte), whose [presidency](/wiki/Presidency_of_Rodrigo_Duterte) saw the decline of [liberalism in the country](/wiki/Liberalism_in_the_Philippines) albeit largely retaining liberal economic policies. Among Duterte's priorities was [aggressively increasing infrastructure spending](/wiki/Build!_Build!_Build!) to spur economic growth; the enactment of the Bangsamoro Organic Law; an intensified crackdown on crime and communist insurgencies; and [an anti-drug campaign](/wiki/Philippine_drug_war) that reduced drug proliferation but that has also led to [extrajudicial killings](/wiki/Extrajudicial_killing). In early 2020, the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_Philippines) reached the Philippines, necessitating [nationwide lockdowns](/wiki/COVID-19_community_quarantines_in_the_Philippines) that caused a brief but severe [economic recession](/wiki/COVID-19_recession). Under a promise of continuing Duterte's policies, Marcos's son, [Bongbong Marcos](/wiki/Bongbong_Marcos), ran with Duterte's daughter, [Sara](/wiki/Sara_Duterte), and won the [2022 election](/wiki/2022_Philippine_presidential_election). Marcos's renewal of a pro-US foreign policy, however, has been viewed as a reversal of Duterte's cordiality with China, and [territorial disputes in the South China Sea](/wiki/Territorial_disputes_in_the_South_China_Sea) have since escalated. The Marcos administration also [arrested](/wiki/Arrest_of_Rodrigo_Duterte) his predecessor Duterte, who was sent to the [International Criminal Court](/wiki/International_Criminal_Court) in [The Netherlands](/wiki/The_Netherlands) for trial of [crimes against humanity](/wiki/Crimes_against_humanity), which led to the collapse of the Marcos-Duterte alliance. Later on, a series of [corruption, mismanagement and irregularities in government-funded flood management projects](/wiki/Flood_control_projects_controversy_in_the_Philippines) occurred under his administration, resulting in [numerous protests nationwide](/wiki/September_2025_Philippine_protests). The country suffered an [energy crisis](/wiki/2026_energy_crisis_in_the_Philippines) as a result of the [conflict in Middle East](/wiki/2026_Iran_war).

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of the Philippines](/wiki/Geography_of_the_Philippines)

Further information: [List of islands of the Philippines](/wiki/List_of_islands_of_the_Philippines)

The Philippines is generally mountainous. Uplands make up 65 percent of the Philippines's land area.

The Philippines is an archipelago of approximately [7,641 islands](/wiki/List_of_islands_of_the_Philippines), covering a total area (including inland bodies of water) of about 300,000 square kilometers (115,831 sq mi). Stretching 1,850 kilometers (1,150 mi) north to south, from the [South China Sea](/wiki/South_China_Sea) to the [Celebes Sea](/wiki/Celebes_Sea), the Philippines [is bordered](/wiki/Borders_of_the_Philippines) by the [Philippine Sea](/wiki/Philippine_Sea) to the east, and the [Sulu Sea](/wiki/Sulu_Sea) to the southwest. The [Luzon Strait](/wiki/Luzon_Strait) is the most economical (quickest) traffic node for shipping between the [Americas](/wiki/Americas) and [Asia](/wiki/Asia).
It is also a critical [junction](/wiki/Electrical_junction) of Asia-Pacific-Americas [undersea communication cables](/wiki/Undersea_communication_cable). The [country's 11 largest islands](/wiki/List_of_islands_of_the_Philippines) are [Luzon](/wiki/Luzon), [Mindanao](/wiki/Mindanao), [Samar](/wiki/Samar), [Negros](/wiki/Negros), [Palawan](/wiki/Palawan_(island)), [Panay](/wiki/Panay), [Mindoro](/wiki/Mindoro), [Leyte](/wiki/Leyte), [Cebu](/wiki/Cebu_(island)), [Bohol](/wiki/Bohol) and [Masbate](/wiki/Masbate_(island)), about 95 percent of its total land area. The Philippines' coastline measures 36,289 kilometers (22,549 mi), the world's [fifth-longest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_length_of_coastline), and the [country's exclusive economic zone](/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone_of_the_Philippines) covers 2,263,816 km2 (874,064 sq mi).

Its [highest mountain](/wiki/List_of_mountains_in_the_Philippines) is [Mount Apo](/wiki/Mount_Apo) on Mindanao, with an altitude of 2,954 meters (9,692 ft) above sea level. The Philippines' [longest river](/wiki/List_of_rivers_of_the_Philippines) is the [Cagayan River](/wiki/Cagayan_River) in northern Luzon, which flows for about 520 kilometers (320 mi). [Manila Bay](/wiki/Manila_Bay), on which is the capital city of [Manila](/wiki/Manila), is connected to [Laguna de Bay](/wiki/Laguna_de_Bay) (the country's [largest lake](/wiki/List_of_lakes_of_the_Philippines)) by the [Pasig River](/wiki/Pasig_River).

On the western fringes of the Pacific [Ring of Fire](/wiki/Ring_of_Fire), the Philippines has frequent seismic and volcanic activity. The region is [seismically](/wiki/Seismology) active, and has been [constructed by plates](/wiki/Subduction_tectonics_of_the_Philippines) converging towards each other from multiple directions. About five earthquakes are recorded daily, although most are too weak to be felt. The [last major earthquakes](/wiki/List_of_earthquakes_in_the_Philippines) were in 1976 in the [Moro Gulf](/wiki/1976_Moro_Gulf_earthquake) and in 1990 on [Luzon](/wiki/1990_Luzon_earthquake). The Philippines has [23 active volcanoes](/wiki/List_of_active_volcanoes_in_the_Philippines); of them, [Mayon](/wiki/Mayon), [Taal](/wiki/Taal_Volcano), [Canlaon](/wiki/Mount_Canlaon), and [Bulusan](/wiki/Mount_Bulusan) have the largest number of recorded eruptions.

The country has valuable mineral deposits as a result of its complex geologic structure and high level of seismic activity. It is thought to have the world's second-largest gold deposits (after South Africa), large copper deposits, and the world's largest deposits of [palladium](/wiki/Palladium). The country's gold production in 2015 is 21 metric tonnes. Other minerals include [chromium](/wiki/Chromium), [nickel](/wiki/Nickel), [molybdenum](/wiki/Molybdenum), [platinum](/wiki/Platinum), and [zinc](/wiki/Zinc). However, poor management and law enforcement, opposition from indigenous communities, and past environmental damage have left these resources largely untapped.

### Biodiversity

Main article: [Wildlife of the Philippines](/wiki/Wildlife_of_the_Philippines)

See also: [List of threatened species of the Philippines](/wiki/List_of_threatened_species_of_the_Philippines) and [Ecoregions in the Philippines](/wiki/Ecoregions_in_the_Philippines)

The [carabao](/wiki/Carabao) is the national animal of the Philippines. It symbolizes strength, power, efficiency, perseverance and hard work.

The Philippines is a [megadiverse country](/wiki/Megadiverse_countries), with some of the world's highest rates of discovery and [endemism](/wiki/List_of_ecoregions_with_high_endemism) (67 percent). With an [estimated 13,500 plant species](/wiki/Flora_of_the_Philippines) in the country (3,500 of which are endemic), Philippine rain forests have an array of flora: about 3,500 species of trees, 8,000 [flowering plant](/wiki/Flowering_plant) species, 1,100 [ferns](/wiki/Fern), and 998 [orchid](/wiki/List_of_the_orchids_of_the_Philippines) species have been identified. The Philippines has 167 terrestrial [mammals](/wiki/Mammal) (102 endemic species), 235 [reptiles](/wiki/Reptile) (160 endemic species), 99 [amphibians](/wiki/Amphibian) (74 endemic species), 686 [birds](/wiki/List_of_birds_of_the_Philippines) (224 endemic species), and over 20,000 [insect](/wiki/Insect) species.

As an important part of the [Coral Triangle](/wiki/Coral_Triangle) ecoregion, Philippine waters have unique, diverse marine life and the world's greatest diversity of shore-fish species. The country has over 3,200 fish species, 121 endemic. Philippine waters sustain [the cultivation](/wiki/Aquaculture_in_the_Philippines) of fish, crustaceans, oysters, and seaweeds.

Eight major types of forests are distributed throughout the Philippines: [dipterocarp](/wiki/Dipterocarpaceae), [beach](/wiki/Beach) forest, [pine](/wiki/Pine) forest, [molave](/wiki/Vitex_parviflora) forest, [lower montane forest](/wiki/Montane_ecosystems), upper montane (or [mossy forest](/wiki/Cloud_forest)), [mangroves](/wiki/Mangrove), and [ultrabasic](/wiki/Ultramafic_rock) forest. According to official estimates, the Philippines had 7,000,000 hectares (27,000 sq mi) of forest cover in 2023. Logging had been systemized during the American colonial period and [deforestation](/wiki/Deforestation_in_the_Philippines) continued after independence, accelerating during the [Marcos presidency](/wiki/Martial_law_under_Ferdinand_Marcos) due to unregulated logging concessions. Forest cover declined from 70 percent of the Philippines' total land area in 1900 to about 18.3 percent in 1999. Rehabilitation efforts have had marginal success.

The Philippines is a [priority hotspot for biodiversity conservation](/wiki/Biodiversity_hotspot); it has [more than 200 protected areas](/wiki/List_of_protected_areas_of_the_Philippines), which was expanded to 7,790,000 hectares (30,100 sq mi) as of 2023. [Three sites](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_the_Philippines) in the Philippines have been included on the UNESCO World Heritage List: the [Tubbataha Reef](/wiki/Tubbataha_Reef) in the Sulu Sea, the [Puerto Princesa Subterranean River](/wiki/Puerto_Princesa_Subterranean_River_National_Park), and the [Mount Hamiguitan](/wiki/Mount_Hamiguitan) Wildlife Sanctuary.

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of the Philippines](/wiki/Climate_of_the_Philippines)

Evening thunderstorms bringing rain over the Philippines are common from June to November.

The Philippines has a tropical maritime climate which is usually hot and humid. There are three seasons: a hot [dry season](/wiki/Dry_season) from March to May, a [rainy season](/wiki/Wet_season) from June to November, and a cool dry season from December to February. The southwest [monsoon](/wiki/Monsoon) (known as the *habagat*) lasts from May to October. The northeast monsoon (*[amihan](/wiki/Amihan)*) lasts from November to April.

The coolest month is January, and the warmest is May. Temperatures at sea level across the Philippines tend to be in the same range, regardless of latitude. The average annual temperature is around 26.6 °C (79.9 °F). It is 18.3 °C (64.9 °F) in [Baguio](/wiki/Baguio), 1,500 meters (4,900 ft) above sea level. The country's average [humidity](/wiki/Humidity) is 82 percent. Annual rainfall is as high as 5,000 millimeters (200 in) on the mountainous east coast, but less than 1,000 millimeters (39 in) in some sheltered valleys.

The [Philippine Area of Responsibility](/wiki/Philippine_Area_of_Responsibility) has 19 [typhoons](/wiki/Typhoons_in_the_Philippines) in a typical year, usually from July to October. Eight or nine of them make [landfall](/wiki/Landfall). The wettest recorded typhoon to hit the Philippines dropped 2,210 millimeters (87 in) in Baguio from July 14 to 18, 1911. The country [is among](/wiki/Climate_change_in_the_Philippines) the world's ten most [vulnerable to climate change](/wiki/Climate_change_vulnerability).

## Government and politics

Main articles: [Government of the Philippines](/wiki/Government_of_the_Philippines) and [Politics of the Philippines](/wiki/Politics_of_the_Philippines)

See also: [Political history of the Philippines](/wiki/Political_history_of_the_Philippines)

[Malacañang Palace](/wiki/Malaca%C3%B1ang_Palace) is the president's official residence.

[Bongbong Marcos](/wiki/Bongbong_Marcos)
[President](/wiki/President_of_the_Philippines)

[Sara Duterte](/wiki/Sara_Duterte)
[Vice President](/wiki/Vice_President_of_the_Philippines)

The Philippines has a [democratic](/wiki/Democracy) government, a constitutional [republic](/wiki/Republic) with a [presidential system](/wiki/Presidential_system). The [president](/wiki/President_of_the_Philippines) is [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state) and [head of government](/wiki/Head_of_government), and is the [commander-in-chief](/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the [armed forces](/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_the_Philippines). The president [is elected](/wiki/Presidential_elections_in_the_Philippines) through [direct election](/wiki/Direct_election) by the [citizens of the Philippines](/wiki/Philippine_nationality_law) for a six-year term. The [vice president](/wiki/Vice_President_of_the_Philippines), limited to two consecutive six-year terms, is elected separately from the president. This means the president and vice president may be from different political parties. The president appoints and presides over the [cabinet](/wiki/Cabinet_of_the_Philippines) and officials of various national government agencies and institutions. The [bicameral](/wiki/Bicameralism) [Congress](/wiki/Congress_of_the_Philippines) is composed of the [Senate](/wiki/Senate_of_the_Philippines) (the [upper house](/wiki/Upper_house), with members [elected to a six-year term](/wiki/Philippine_Senate_elections)) and the [House of Representatives](/wiki/House_of_Representatives_of_the_Philippines) (the [lower house](/wiki/Lower_house), with members elected to a three-year term).

Senators are elected [at-large](/wiki/At-large), and representatives are elected from [legislative districts](/wiki/Legislative_districts_of_the_Philippines) and [party lists](/wiki/Party-list_representation_in_the_House_of_Representatives_of_the_Philippines). Judicial authority is vested in the [Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_Philippines), composed of a [chief justice](/wiki/Chief_Justice_of_the_Philippines) and fourteen [associate justices](/wiki/Associate_Justice_of_the_Supreme_Court_of_the_Philippines), who are appointed by the president from nominations submitted by the [Judicial and Bar Council](/wiki/Judicial_and_Bar_Council).

[Attempts to change](/wiki/Constitutional_reform_in_the_Philippines) the government to a [federal](/wiki/Federation), [unicameral](/wiki/Unicameralism), or [parliamentary government](/wiki/Parliamentary_system) have been made since the [Ramos administration](/wiki/Presidency_of_Fidel_V._Ramos). Philippine politics tends to be dominated by [well-known families](/wiki/List_of_political_families_in_the_Philippines), such as [political dynasties](/wiki/Political_dynasties_in_the_Philippines) or [celebrities](/wiki/Celebrity_influence_in_politics), and [party switching](/wiki/Party_switching) is widely practiced. Corruption is significant, attributed by some historians to the Spanish colonial period's [padrino system](/wiki/Padrino_system). The Roman Catholic church exerts considerable but waning influence in political affairs, although a constitutional provision for the [separation of Church and State](/wiki/Separation_of_Church_and_State) exists.

As with many other governments, it is also engaged in [for profit](/wiki/For_profit) activities through various [State-owned Enterprises](/wiki/State-owned_enterprise) it terms as [Government-owned and controlled corporation](/wiki/Government-owned_and_controlled_corporation) (GOCC). Except for the [Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas](/wiki/Bangko_Sentral_ng_Pilipinas), these entities (the newest of which is the controversial [Maharlika Investment Corporation](/wiki/Maharlika_Investment_Corporation)) are supervised by the [Governance Commission for GOCCs](/wiki/Governance_Commission_for_GOCCs) (GCG).

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of the Philippines](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_the_Philippines)

[Philippine diplomatic missions worldwide](/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_of_the_Philippines)

A [founding and active member](/wiki/Philippines_and_the_United_Nations) of the United Nations, the Philippines has been a non-permanent member of the [Security Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council). The country participates in [peacekeeping](/wiki/Peacekeeping) missions, particularly in [East Timor](/wiki/United_Nations_Transitional_Administration_in_East_Timor). The Philippines is a [founding](/wiki/ASEAN_Declaration) and active member of [ASEAN](/wiki/ASEAN) (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) and a member of the [East Asia Summit](/wiki/East_Asia_Summit), the [Group of 24](/wiki/Group_of_24), and the [Non-Aligned Movement](/wiki/Non-Aligned_Movement). The country has sought to obtain observer status in the [Organisation of Islamic Cooperation](/wiki/Organisation_of_Islamic_Cooperation) since 2003, and was a member of [SEATO](/wiki/SEATO). Over 10 million Filipinos [live](/wiki/Overseas_Filipinos) and [work in 200 countries](/wiki/Overseas_Filipino_Worker), giving the Philippines [soft power](/wiki/Soft_power).

During the 1990s, the Philippines began to seek [economic liberalization](/wiki/Economic_liberalization) and [free trade](/wiki/Free_trade) to help spur [foreign direct investment](/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment). It is a member of the [World Trade Organization](/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) and the [Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation](/wiki/Asia-Pacific_Economic_Cooperation). The Philippines entered into the [ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement](/wiki/ASEAN_Free_Trade_Area) in 2010 and the [Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership](/wiki/Regional_Comprehensive_Economic_Partnership) [free trade agreement](/wiki/Free_trade_agreement) (FTA) in 2023. Through ASEAN, the Philippines has signed FTAs with [China](/wiki/ASEAN%E2%80%93China_Free_Trade_Area), [India](/wiki/ASEAN%E2%80%93India_Free_Trade_Area), Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand. The country has bilateral FTAs with [Japan](/wiki/Japan%E2%80%93Philippines_Economic_Partnership_Agreement), South Korea, and [four European states](/wiki/European_Free_Trade_Association): Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland.

Filipino soldiers painting a U.S. and Philippine flag

The Philippines has a long [relationship with the United States](/wiki/Philippines%E2%80%93United_States_relations), involving economics, [security](/wiki/CIA_activities_in_the_Philippines), and interpersonal relations. The Philippines' location [serves](/wiki/United_States_bases_in_the_Philippines) an [important role](/wiki/First_island_chain) in the United States' [island chain strategy](/wiki/Island_chain_strategy) in the West Pacific; a [Mutual Defense Treaty](/wiki/Mutual_Defense_Treaty_(United_States%E2%80%93Philippines)) between the two countries was signed in 1951, and was supplemented with the [1999 Visiting Forces Agreement](/wiki/Philippines%E2%80%93United_States_Visiting_Forces_Agreement) and the 2016 [Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement](/wiki/Enhanced_Defense_Cooperation_Agreement). The country supported American policies during the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) and participated in the [Korean](/wiki/Korean_War) and [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam_War) wars. In 2003, the Philippines was designated a major non-NATO ally. [Under President Duterte](/wiki/Presidency_of_Rodrigo_Duterte), ties with the United States weakened in favor of improved relations with China and [Russia](/wiki/Philippines%E2%80%93Russia_relations). The Philippines relies heavily on the United States for its external defense; the U.S. has made regular assurances to defend the Philippines, including the [South China Sea](/wiki/South_China_Sea).

Since 1975, the Philippines has valued its [relations with China](/wiki/China%E2%80%93Philippines_relations)—its top trading partner, and cooperates significantly with the country. Japan is the biggest bilateral contributor of [official development assistance](/wiki/Official_development_assistance) to the Philippines; although some tension exists because of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), much animosity has faded. Historical and cultural ties continue to affect [relations with Spain](/wiki/Philippines%E2%80%93Spain_relations). Relations with Middle Eastern countries are shaped by the high number of Filipinos working in those countries, and by issues related to the Muslim minority in the Philippines; concerns have been raised about domestic abuse and war affecting the approximately 2.5 million overseas Filipino workers in the region.

The Philippines [has claims](/wiki/Spratly_Islands_dispute) in the [Spratly Islands](/wiki/Spratly_Islands) which overlap with claims by China, Malaysia, Taiwan, and Vietnam. The largest of [its controlled islands](/wiki/Philippines_and_the_Spratly_Islands) is [Thitu Island](/wiki/Thitu_Island), which contains the [Philippines' smallest town](/wiki/Kalayaan,_Palawan). The 2012 [Scarborough Shoal standoff](/wiki/Scarborough_Shoal_standoff), after China seized the shoal from the Philippines, led to an [international arbitration case](/wiki/Philippines_v._China) which the Philippines eventually won; China rejected the result, and other new Philippine maritime laws, and made the shoal a prominent symbol of the broader dispute.

### Military

Main article: [Armed Forces of the Philippines](/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_the_Philippines)

[BRP Miguel Malvar (FFG6)](/wiki/BRP_Miguel_Malvar_(FFG-6)) sailing during the group sail phase of Exercise Balikatan 2026.

The [volunteer](/wiki/Volunteer_military) Armed Forces of the Philippines (AFP) consist of three branches: the [Philippine Air Force](/wiki/Philippine_Air_Force), the [Philippine Army](/wiki/Philippine_Army), and the [Philippine Navy](/wiki/Philippine_Navy). Civilian security is handled by the [Philippine National Police](/wiki/Philippine_National_Police) under the [Department of the Interior and Local Government](/wiki/Department_of_the_Interior_and_Local_Government). The AFP had a total manpower of around 280,000 as of 2022, of which 130,000 were active military personnel, 100,000 were reserves, and 50,000 were [paramilitaries](/wiki/Citizen_Armed_Force_Geographical_Unit).

In 2023, US$477 million, 1.4 percent of GDP, was spent on the Philippine military. Most of the country's defense spending is on the Philippine Army, which leads operations against internal threats such as [communist](/wiki/Communist_armed_conflicts_in_the_Philippines) and [Muslim](/wiki/Moro_conflict) separatist insurgencies; its preoccupation with internal security contributed to the decline of [Philippine naval capability](/wiki/List_of_equipment_of_the_Philippine_Navy) which began during the 1970s. [A military modernization program](/wiki/AFP_Modernization_Act) began in 1995 and expanded in 2012 to build a more capable defense system.

The Philippines has long struggled against [local insurgencies](/wiki/Civil_conflict_in_the_Philippines), [separatism](/wiki/Separatism_in_the_Philippines), and [terrorism](/wiki/Terrorism_in_the_Philippines). [Bangsamoro](/wiki/Bangsamoro)'s largest separatist organizations, the [Moro National Liberation Front](/wiki/Moro_National_Liberation_Front) and the [Moro Islamic Liberation Front](/wiki/Moro_Islamic_Liberation_Front), signed final peace agreements with the government in [1996](/wiki/1996_Final_Peace_Agreement) and [2014](/wiki/Comprehensive_Agreement_on_the_Bangsamoro) respectively. Other, more-militant groups such as [Abu Sayyaf](/wiki/Abu_Sayyaf) and [Bangsamoro Islamic Freedom Fighters](/wiki/Bangsamoro_Islamic_Freedom_Fighters) have kidnapped foreigners for ransom, particularly in the Sulu Archipelago and [Maguindanao](/wiki/Maguindanao), but their presence has been reduced.

The [Communist Party of the Philippines](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Philippines) (CPP) and its military wing, the [New People's Army](/wiki/New_People%27s_Army) (NPA), have been waging [guerrilla warfare](/wiki/New_People%27s_Army_rebellion) against the government since the 1970s and have [engaged in ambushes, bombings, and assassinations](/wiki/Timeline_of_the_communist_rebellion_in_the_Philippines) of government officials and security forces; although shrinking militarily and politically after the return of democracy in 1986, the CPP-NPA, through the [National Democratic Front of the Philippines](/wiki/National_Democratic_Front_of_the_Philippines), continues to gather public support in urban areas by setting up [communist fronts](/wiki/Communist_front), infiltrating sectoral organizations, and rallying public discontent and increased militancy against the government. The Philippines ranked 104th out of 163 countries in the 2024 [Global Peace Index](/wiki/Global_Peace_Index).

### Administrative divisions

Main article: [Administrative divisions of the Philippines](/wiki/Administrative_divisions_of_the_Philippines)

The Philippines' regions and provinces

The Philippines is divided into 18 [regions](/wiki/Regions_of_the_Philippines), 82 [provinces](/wiki/Provinces_of_the_Philippines), 149 [cities](/wiki/Cities_of_the_Philippines), 1,493 [municipalities](/wiki/Municipalities_of_the_Philippines), and 42,011 [barangays](/wiki/Barangay). Regions other than [Bangsamoro](/wiki/Bangsamoro) are divided for administrative convenience. [Calabarzon](/wiki/Calabarzon) was the region with the greatest population as of 2020. The [National Capital Region](/wiki/Metro_Manila) (NCR) was the most densely populated.

The Philippines is a [unitary state](/wiki/Unitary_state), with the exception of the [Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao](/wiki/Bangsamoro_Autonomous_Region_in_Muslim_Mindanao) (BARMM). There have been [steps towards decentralization](/wiki/Federalism_in_the_Philippines). A 1991 law [devolved](/wiki/Devolution) some powers to [local governments](/wiki/Local_government_in_the_Philippines).

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of the Philippines](/wiki/Economy_of_the_Philippines)

According to the [International Monetary Fund](/wiki/International_Monetary_Fund) (IMF) in its October 2025 update, the Philippines is the world's 33rd largest economy with a nominal [gross domestic product](/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) (GDP) of $494.16 billion. As a [newly industrialized country](/wiki/Newly_industrialized_country), the Philippine economy has been transitioning from an agricultural base to one with more emphasis on services and manufacturing. The country's labor force is about 52.09 million with an [unemployment rate](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_by_unemployment_rate) of 5.1 percent as of February 2026. Gross international reserves totalled US$106.64 billion as of March 2026. [Debt-to-GDP ratio](/wiki/Debt-to-GDP_ratio) decreased to 60.2 percent at the end of 2023 from a 17-year high 63.7 percent at the end of the third quarter of that year, and indicated resiliency during the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_Philippines). The country's unit of [currency](/wiki/Currency) is the [Philippine peso](/wiki/Philippine_peso) (₱ or PHP).

The Philippines is a net importer, and a [debtor nation](/wiki/Net_international_investment_position). As of 2020, the country's main export markets were China, the United States, Japan, Hong Kong, and Singapore; primary exports included [integrated circuits](/wiki/Integrated_circuit), office machinery and parts, electrical transformers, insulated wiring, and [semiconductors](/wiki/Semiconductor). Its primary import markets that year were China, Japan, South Korea, the United States, and Indonesia. Major export crops include [coconuts](/wiki/Coconut_production_in_the_Philippines), [bananas](/wiki/Banana_industry#Production_and_export), and [pineapples](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_pineapple_production); it is the world's largest producer of [abaca](/wiki/Abaca), and was the world's second biggest exporter of [nickel ore](/wiki/Nickel) in 2022, as well as the biggest exporter of gold-clad metals and the biggest importer of [copra](/wiki/Copra) in 2020.

Headquarters of the [International Rice Research Institute](/wiki/International_Rice_Research_Institute) in [Los Baños, Laguna](/wiki/Los_Ba%C3%B1os,_Laguna)

With an average annual growth rate of six to seven percent since around 2010, the Philippines has emerged as one of the world's fastest-growing economies, driven primarily by its increasing reliance on the service sector. Regional development is uneven, however, with Manila (in particular) gaining most of the new economic growth. [Remittances](/wiki/Remittance) from [overseas Filipinos](/wiki/Overseas_Filipinos), mainly [seamen](/wiki/Sailor) and [nurses](/wiki/Nurses), which the country is the world's largest suppliers of, contribute significantly to the country's economy; they reached a record US$37.20 billion in 2023, accounting for 8.5 percent of GDP. The Philippines is the world's primary [business process outsourcing](/wiki/Business_process_outsourcing_in_the_Philippines) (BPO) center. About 1.3 million Filipinos work in the BPO sector, primarily in [customer service](/wiki/Customer_service). The country is a pioneer in digital finance, as the first country to put its entire national budget under the [blockchain](/wiki/Blockchain) system.

### Science and technology

Main article: [Science and technology in the Philippines](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_the_Philippines)

Further information: [Philippine space program](/wiki/Philippine_space_program)

The Philippines has one of the largest agricultural-research systems in Asia, despite relatively low spending on agricultural research and development. The country has developed new varieties of crops, including [rice](/wiki/Rice_production_in_the_Philippines), coconuts, and bananas. Research organizations include the [Philippine Rice Research Institute](/wiki/Philippine_Rice_Research_Institute) and the [International Rice Research Institute](/wiki/International_Rice_Research_Institute).

The [Philippine Space Agency](/wiki/Philippine_Space_Agency) maintains the [country's space program](/wiki/Philippine_space_program), and the country bought its first [satellite](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_satellites) in 1996. [Diwata-1](/wiki/Diwata-1), its first [micro-satellite](/wiki/Small_satellite#Microsatellites), was launched on the United States' [Cygnus](/wiki/Cygnus_(spacecraft)) spacecraft in 2016.

The Philippines has a [high concentration](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_smartphone_penetration) of [cellular-phone](/wiki/Mobile_phone) users, and a high level of [mobile commerce](/wiki/Mobile_commerce). [Text messaging](/wiki/Text_messaging) is a popular form of communication, and the nation sent an average of one billion [SMS](/wiki/SMS) messages per day in 2007. The [Philippine telecommunications industry](/wiki/Telecommunications_in_the_Philippines) had been dominated by the [PLDT](/wiki/PLDT)-[Globe Telecom](/wiki/Globe_Telecom) duopoly for more than two decades, and the 2021 entry of [Dito Telecommunity](/wiki/Dito_Telecommunity) improved the country's telecommunications service. The Philippines was ranked 50th in the [Global Innovation Index](/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2025.

### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in the Philippines](/wiki/Tourism_in_the_Philippines)

Tourists at [Chocolate Hills](/wiki/Chocolate_Hills), conical [karst](/wiki/Karst) hills in [Bohol](/wiki/Bohol)

The Philippines is a popular retirement destination for foreigners because of its climate and low cost of living. The country's main tourist attractions are [its numerous beaches](/wiki/List_of_beaches_in_the_Philippines); the Philippines is also a top destination for diving enthusiasts. [Tourist spots](/wiki/List_of_tourist_attractions_in_the_Philippines) include [Boracay](/wiki/Boracay), called the best island in the world by *[Travel + Leisure](/wiki/Travel_%2B_Leisure)* in 2012; [Coron](/wiki/Coron,_Palawan) and [El Nido](/wiki/El_Nido,_Palawan) in Palawan; [Cebu](/wiki/Cebu); [Siargao](/wiki/Siargao), and [Bohol](/wiki/Bohol).

Tourism contributed 5.2 percent to the Philippine GDP in 2021 (lower than 12.7 percent in 2019, before the COVID-19 pandemic), and provided 5.7 million jobs in 2019. The Philippines attracted 5.45 million international visitors in 2023, 30 percent lower than the 8.26 million record in pre-pandemic 2019; most tourists came from South Korea (26.4 percent), United States (16.5 percent), Japan (5.6 percent), Australia (4.89 percent), and China (4.84 percent).

## Infrastructure

### Transportation

Main article: [Transportation in the Philippines](/wiki/Transportation_in_the_Philippines)

Traditional *(left)* and modern [jeepneys](/wiki/Jeepney) in [Quezon City](/wiki/Quezon_City). Public utility vehicles older than 15 years are [gradually being phased out](/wiki/Public_Utility_Vehicle_Modernization_Program) in favor of eco-friendly [Euro 4](/wiki/European_emission_standards)-compliant vehicles.

Transportation in the Philippines is by road, air, rail and water. Roads are the dominant form of transport, carrying 98 percent of people and 58 percent of cargo. In December 2018, there were 210,528 kilometers (130,816 mi) of roads in the country. The backbone of land-based transportation in the country is the [Pan-Philippine Highway](/wiki/Pan-Philippine_Highway), which connects the islands of Luzon, Samar, Leyte, and Mindanao. Inter-island transport is by the 919-kilometer (571 mi) [Strong Republic Nautical Highway](/wiki/Strong_Republic_Nautical_Highway), an integrated set of highways and [ferry](/wiki/Ferry) routes linking 17 cities. [Jeepneys](/wiki/Jeepney) are a popular, iconic public utility vehicle; other public land transport includes [buses](/wiki/List_of_bus_companies_of_the_Philippines), [UV Express](/wiki/UV_Express), TNVS, Filcab, [taxis](/wiki/Taxis_of_the_Philippines), and [tricycles](/wiki/Motorized_tricycle_(Philippines)). Traffic is a significant issue [in Manila](/wiki/Traffic_in_Metro_Manila) and on arterial roads to the capital.

Despite wider historical use, [rail transportation in the Philippines](/wiki/Rail_transportation_in_the_Philippines) is limited to [transporting passengers within Metro Manila](/wiki/Rail_transportation_in_the_Greater_Manila_Area) and the provinces of [Laguna](/wiki/Laguna_(province)) and [Quezon](/wiki/Quezon), with a short track in the [Bicol Region](/wiki/Bicol_Region). The country had a railway footprint of only 79 kilometers (49 mi) as of 2019, which it planned to expand to 244 kilometers (152 mi). A revival of [freight rail](/wiki/Freight_rail) is planned to reduce road congestion.

The Philippines had [90 national government-owned airports](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_the_Philippines) as of 2022, of which eight are [international](/wiki/International_airport). [Ninoy Aquino International Airport](/wiki/Ninoy_Aquino_International_Airport), formerly known as Manila International Airport, has the [greatest number of passengers](/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_the_Philippines). The 2017 [air domestic market](/wiki/List_of_airlines_of_the_Philippines) was dominated by [Philippine Airlines](/wiki/Philippine_Airlines), the country's [flag carrier](/wiki/Flag_carrier) and Asia's oldest commercial airline, and [Cebu Pacific](/wiki/Cebu_Pacific) (the country's leading [low-cost carrier](/wiki/Low-cost_carrier)).

A variety of boats are used throughout the Philippines; most are [double-outrigger](/wiki/Outrigger_boat) vessels known as *banca* or *[bangka](/wiki/Bangka_(boat))*. Modern ships use plywood instead of logs, and motor engines instead of sails; they are used for fishing and inter-island travel. The Philippines has [over 1,800 seaports](/wiki/List_of_ports_in_the_Philippines); of these, the principal seaports of [Manila](/wiki/Port_of_Manila) (the country's chief, and busiest, port), [Batangas](/wiki/Batangas_International_Port), [Subic Bay](/wiki/Port_of_Subic_Bay), [Cebu](/wiki/Port_of_Cebu), [Iloilo](/wiki/Port_of_Iloilo), [Davao](/wiki/Port_of_Davao), [Cagayan de Oro](/wiki/Port_of_Cagayan_de_Oro), [General Santos](/wiki/Port_of_General_Santos), and [Zamboanga](/wiki/Port_of_Zamboanga) are part of the ASEAN Transport Network.

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in the Philippines](/wiki/Energy_in_the_Philippines)

The [Ambuklao Dam](/wiki/Ambuklao_Dam) on the [Agno River](/wiki/Agno_River) in [Bokod, Benguet](/wiki/Bokod,_Benguet)

The Philippines had a total installed power capacity of 26,882 [MW](/wiki/Watt) in 2021; 43 percent was generated from [coal](/wiki/Coal_mining_in_the_Philippines), 14 percent from [oil](/wiki/Oil), 14 percent [hydropower](/wiki/Hydropower), 12 percent from [natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas), and seven percent from [geothermal](/wiki/Geothermal_power) sources. It is the world's third-biggest [geothermal-energy producer](/wiki/Geothermal_power_in_the_Philippines), behind the United States and Indonesia. The country's largest dam is the 1.2-kilometer-long (0.75 mi) [San Roque Dam](/wiki/San_Roque_Dam_(Philippines)) on the [Agno River](/wiki/Agno_River) in [Pangasinan](/wiki/Pangasinan). The [Malampaya gas field](/wiki/Malampaya_gas_field), discovered in the early 1990s off the coast of Palawan, reduced the Philippines' reliance on imported oil; it provides about 40 percent of Luzon's energy requirements, and 30 percent of the country's energy needs.

The Philippines has three [electrical grids](/wiki/Electrical_grid), one each for Luzon, Visayas, and Mindanao. The [National Grid Corporation of the Philippines](/wiki/National_Grid_Corporation_of_the_Philippines) manages [the country's power grid](/wiki/Electricity_sector_in_the_Philippines) since 2009 and provides [overhead transmission lines](/wiki/Overhead_power_line) across the country's islands. Electric distribution to consumers [is provided](/wiki/List_of_electric_distribution_utilities_in_the_Philippines) by privately owned distribution utilities and government-owned [electric cooperatives](/wiki/Electric_cooperative). As of end-2021, the Philippines' household electrification level was about 95.41%.

Plans to harness [nuclear energy](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_the_Philippines) began during the early 1970s during the [presidency of Ferdinand Marcos](/wiki/Presidency_of_Ferdinand_Marcos) in response to the [1973 oil crisis](/wiki/1973_oil_crisis). The Philippines completed [Southeast Asia's first nuclear power plant](/wiki/Bataan_Nuclear_Power_Plant) in [Bataan](/wiki/Bataan) in 1984. Political issues following Marcos' ouster and safety concerns after the 1986 [Chernobyl disaster](/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) prevented the plant from being commissioned, and plans to operate it remain controversial.

### Water supply and sanitation

Main article: [Water supply and sanitation in the Philippines](/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_the_Philippines)

A water-district office in [Banate, Iloilo](/wiki/Banate,_Iloilo)

Water supply and sanitation outside Metro Manila is provided by the government through local [water districts](/wiki/Water_district) in cities or towns. Metro Manila is served by [Manila Water](/wiki/Manila_Water) and [Maynilad Water Services](/wiki/Maynilad_Water_Services). Except for shallow wells for domestic use, groundwater users are required to obtain a permit from the [National Water Resources Board](/wiki/National_Water_Resources_Board). In 2022, the total water withdrawals increased to 91 billion cubic meters (3.2×10~~^~~12 cu ft) from 89 billion cubic meters (3.1×10~~^~~12 cu ft) in 2021 and the total expenditures on water were amounted to [₱](/wiki/Philippine_peso)144.81 billion.

Most sewage in the Philippines flows into septic tanks. In 2015, the [Joint Monitoring Programme for Water Supply and Sanitation](/wiki/Joint_Monitoring_Programme_for_Water_Supply_and_Sanitation) noted that 74 percent of the Philippine population had access to [improved sanitation](/wiki/Improved_sanitation) and "good progress" had been made between 1990 and 2015. 96 percent of Filipino households had an improved source of drinking water and 92 percent of households had sanitary toilet facilities as of 2016; connections of toilet facilities to appropriate sewerage systems remain largely insufficient, however, especially in rural and urban poor communities.

## Demographics

Main article: [Demographics of the Philippines](/wiki/Demographics_of_the_Philippines)

See also: [List of cities in the Philippines](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_the_Philippines)

In July 2024, the Philippines had a population of 112,729,484. More than 60 percent of the country's population live in the [coastal zone](/wiki/Coastal_zone). In 2020, 54 percent lived in [urban areas](/wiki/Urban_area). [Manila](/wiki/Manila), its capital, and [Quezon City](/wiki/Quezon_City), the country's most populous city, are in [Metro Manila](/wiki/Metro_Manila). About 13.48 million people, 12 percent of the Philippines' population, live in Metro Manila, the [country's most populous metropolitan area](/wiki/List_of_metropolitan_areas_in_the_Philippines) and the world's [fifth most populous](/wiki/List_of_largest_cities). Between 1948 and 2010, the population of the Philippines increased almost fivefold, from 19 million to 92 million.

The [country's median age](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_median_age) is 25.3, and 63.9 percent of its population is between 15 and 64 years old. The Philippines' average annual population growth rate is decreasing, although government attempts to further reduce population growth [have been contentious](/wiki/Reproductive_Health_Bill_(Philippines)). The country [reduced its poverty rate](/wiki/Poverty_in_the_Philippines) from 49.2 percent in 1985, to 18.1 percent in 2021. [The Philippines' income inequality](/wiki/Income_inequality_in_the_Philippines) began to decline in 2012.

### Ethnicity

Main article: [Ethnic groups in the Philippines](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_the_Philippines)

See also: [Filipinos](/wiki/Filipinos), [Pinoy](/wiki/Pinoy), and [Genetic and anthropology studies on Filipinos](/wiki/Genetic_and_anthropology_studies_on_Filipinos)

Dominant ethnic groups by province

The Philippines has substantial ethnic diversity, due to foreign influence and the archipelago's division by water and topography. In the 2020 census, the Philippines' largest ethnic groups were [Tagalog](/wiki/Tagalog_people) (26.0 percent), [Visayans](/wiki/Visayans) [excluding the [Cebuano](/wiki/Cebuano_people), [Hiligaynon](/wiki/Hiligaynon_people), and [Waray](/wiki/Waray_people)] (14.3 percent), [Ilocano](/wiki/Ilocano_people) and Cebuano (both eight percent), Hiligaynon (7.9 percent), [Bicolano](/wiki/Bicolano_people) (6.5 percent), and Waray (3.8 percent). The [country's indigenous peoples](/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Philippines) consisted of 110 enthnolinguistic groups, with a combined population of 15.56 million, in 2020. They include the [Igorot](/wiki/Igorot_people), [Lumad](/wiki/Lumad), [Mangyan](/wiki/Mangyan), and the [indigenous peoples of Palawan](/wiki/Peoples_of_Palawan).

[Negritos](/wiki/Negrito) are thought to be among the islands' earliest inhabitants. These minority aboriginal settlers are an [Australoid](/wiki/Australo-Melanesian) group, a remnant of the [first human migration from Africa to Australia](/wiki/Southern_Dispersal) who were probably displaced by later waves of migration. Some Philippine Negritos have a [Denisovan](/wiki/Denisovan) admixture in their [genome](/wiki/Genome). Ethnic Filipinos generally belong to several Southeast Asian ethnic groups, classified linguistically as [Austronesians](/wiki/Austronesian_peoples) speaking [Malayo-Polynesian languages](/wiki/Malayo-Polynesian_languages).

The Austronesian population's origin is uncertain, but relatives of [Taiwanese aborigines](/wiki/Taiwanese_indigenous_peoples) probably brought their language and mixed with the region's existing population. The Lumad and [Sama-Bajau](/wiki/Sama-Bajau) ethnic groups have an ancestral affinity with the [Austroasiatic](/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages) and [Mlabri-speaking](/wiki/Mlabri_language) [Htin](/wiki/Lua_people) peoples of mainland Southeast Asia. Westward expansion of [Papuan ancestry](/wiki/Papuan_languages) from [Papua New Guinea](/wiki/Papua_New_Guinea) to eastern Indonesia and [Mindanao](/wiki/Mindanao) has been detected in the [Blaan people](/wiki/Blaan_people) and the [Sangir language](/wiki/Sangir_language), while ancient immigration added some [Indian](/wiki/India) ancestry to the precolonial [Indianized kingdoms](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(900%E2%80%931565)) of the islands.

Immigrants arrived in the Philippines from elsewhere in the Spanish Empire, especially [from the Spanish Americas](/wiki/Latin_American_Asian). A 2016 [National Geographic](/wiki/Geno_2.0_Next_Generation) project [concluded](/wiki/Genetic_studies_on_Filipinos) that people living in the Philippine archipelago carried [genetic markers](/wiki/Genetic_marker) in the following percentages: 53 percent [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia) and [Oceania](/wiki/Oceania), 36 percent [East Asia](/wiki/East_Asia), 5 percent [Southern Europe](/wiki/Southern_Europe), 3 percent [Southern Asia](/wiki/South_Asia), and 2 percent Native American (from [Latin America](/wiki/Latin_America)).

Descendants of mixed-race couples are known as [Mestizos](/wiki/Filipino_Mestizos) or *tisoy*, which during the [Spanish colonial times](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1565%E2%80%931898)), were mostly composed of [Chinese mestizos](/wiki/Sangley) (*Mestizos de Sangley*), [Spanish mestizos](/wiki/Spanish_Filipino) (*Mestizos de Español*) and the mix thereof (*[tornatrás](/wiki/Torna_atr%C3%A1s)*). The modern [Chinese Filipinos](/wiki/Chinese_Filipino) are well-integrated into Filipino society. Primarily the descendants of immigrants from [Fujian](/wiki/Fujian), the pure ethnic Chinese Filipinos during the [American colonial era](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1898%E2%80%931946)) (early 1900s) purportedly numbered about 1.35 million; while an estimated 22.8 million (around 20 percent) of Filipinos have half or partial Chinese ancestry from precolonial, colonial, and 20th century [Chinese migrants](/wiki/Overseas_Chinese). During the Hispanic era (late 1700s), the tribute-census showed that [mixed Spanish Filipinos](/wiki/Spanish_Filipino) made up a moderate ratio (around 5 percent) of all citizens. Meanwhile, [a smaller proportion (2.33 percent) of the population were Mexican Filipinos](/wiki/Mexican_settlement_in_the_Philippines). Almost 300,000 [American citizens](/wiki/Americans_in_the_Philippines) live in the country as of 2023, this increased to 750,000 by year 2025, (forming 0.75% of the demographics) and up to 250,000 [Amerasians](/wiki/Amerasian), (0.25% of the country), are in the cities of [Clark](/wiki/Clark_Global_City), [Angeles](/wiki/Angeles_City), Manila, and [Olongapo](/wiki/Olongapo) (When combined, [American](/wiki/Americans) and [Amerasian](/wiki/Amerasian) descent, form 1% of the population). Other significant non-indigenous minorities include [Indians](/wiki/Indian_Filipino) and [Arabs](/wiki/Arabs_in_the_Philippines). [Japanese Filipinos](/wiki/Japanese_in_the_Philippines) include escaped Christians ([Kirishitan](/wiki/Kirishitan)) who [fled persecutions by Shogun Tokugawa Ieyasu](/wiki/Martyrs_of_Japan).

### Languages

Main article: [Languages of the Philippines](/wiki/Languages_of_the_Philippines)

*[Ethnologue](/wiki/Ethnologue)* lists 186 languages for the Philippines, 182 of which are [living languages](/wiki/Living_language). The other four no longer have any known speakers. Most native languages are part of the [Philippine branch](/wiki/Philippine_languages) of the [Malayo-Polynesian languages](/wiki/Malayo-Polynesian_languages), which is a branch of the [Austronesian language family](/wiki/Austronesian_languages). Spanish-based [creole](/wiki/Creole_language) varieties, collectively known as [Chavacano](/wiki/Chavacano), are also spoken. Many [Philippine Negrito languages](/wiki/Philippine_Negrito_languages#Unique_vocabulary) have unique vocabularies which survived Austronesian acculturation.

[Filipino](/wiki/Filipino_language) and [English](/wiki/Philippine_English) are the country's official languages. Filipino, a [standardized version](/wiki/Standard_language) of [Tagalog](/wiki/Tagalog_language), is spoken primarily in Metro Manila. Filipino and English are used in government, education, print, broadcast media, and business, often with a third local language. [Code-switching](/wiki/Code-switching) between English and other local languages, notably [Tagalog](/wiki/Taglish), is common. The Philippine constitution provides for [Spanish](/wiki/Philippine_Spanish) and Arabic on a voluntary, optional basis. Spanish, a widely used [lingua franca](/wiki/Lingua_franca) during the late nineteenth century, [has declined greatly in use](/wiki/Spanish_language_in_the_Philippines), although Spanish [loanwords](/wiki/Loanword) are still present in Philippine languages. Arabic is primarily taught in [Mindanao](/wiki/Mindanao) Islamic schools.

In 2020, the top languages generally spoken at home were Tagalog, [Binisaya](/wiki/Binisaya), [Hiligaynon](/wiki/Hiligaynon_language), [Ilocano](/wiki/Ilocano_language), [Cebuano](/wiki/Cebuano_language), and [Bikol](/wiki/Bikol_languages). Nineteen [regional languages](/wiki/List_of_regional_languages_of_the_Philippines) are auxiliary official languages as media of instruction:

- [Aklanon](/wiki/Aklanon_language)
- Bikol
- Cebuano
- [Chavacano](/wiki/Chavacano)
- Hiligaynon
- [Ibanag](/wiki/Ibanag_language)
- Ilocano
- [Ivatan](/wiki/Ivatan_language)
- [Kapampangan](/wiki/Kapampangan_language)
- [Kinaray-a](/wiki/Karay-a_language)
- [Maguindanao](/wiki/Maguindanao_language)
- [Maranao](/wiki/Maranao_language)
- [Pangasinan](/wiki/Pangasinan_language)
- [Sambal](/wiki/Sambal_language)
- [Surigaonon](/wiki/Surigaonon_language)
- Tagalog
- [Tausug](/wiki/Tausug_language)
- [Waray](/wiki/Waray_language)
- [Yakan](/wiki/Yakan_language)

Other indigenous languages, including [Cuyonon](/wiki/Cuyonon_language), [Ifugao](/wiki/Ifugao_language), [Itbayat](/wiki/Itbayat_language), [Kalinga](/wiki/Kalinga_language), [Kamayo](/wiki/Kamayo_language), [Kankanaey](/wiki/Kankanaey_language), [Masbateño](/wiki/Masbate%C3%B1o_language), [Romblomanon](/wiki/Romblomanon_language), [Manobo](/wiki/Manobo_languages), and several [Visayan languages](/wiki/Visayan_languages), are used in their respective provinces. [Filipino Sign Language](/wiki/Filipino_Sign_Language) is the national sign language, and the language of [deaf education](/wiki/Deafness_in_the_Philippines).

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in the Philippines](/wiki/Religion_in_the_Philippines)

Catholics attend Mass at [Basilica del Santo Niño](/wiki/Basilica_del_Santo_Ni%C3%B1o) during the annual [Sinulog](/wiki/Sinulog) festival in [Cebu](/wiki/Cebu).

Although the Philippines is a [secular state](/wiki/Secularism_in_the_Philippines) with [freedom of religion](/wiki/Freedom_of_religion_in_the_Philippines), an overwhelming majority of Filipinos [consider religion very important](/wiki/Importance_of_religion_by_country) and [irreligion](/wiki/Irreligion_in_the_Philippines) is very low. [Christianity](/wiki/Christianity_in_the_Philippines) is the dominant religion followed by about 89 percent of the population. The country had the [world's third-largest Roman Catholic population](/wiki/Catholic_Church_by_country) as of 2013, and was Asia's [largest Christian nation](/wiki/Christianity_in_Asia).

Census data from [2020](/wiki/2020_Philippine_census) found that 78.8 percent of the population professed [Roman Catholicism](/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_the_Philippines). The Catholic church in the Philippines is unique for being by granted by the Roman Pontiff, the Cerulean Indult, the privilege to wear the liturgical color of [Cerulean](/wiki/Cerulean) or [Celeste](/wiki/Light_blue), in honor of the [Immaculate Conception](/wiki/Immaculate_Conception) of Mary, a privilege, not shared by most other Latin-Rite Catholics, except those under the ambit of the Spanish Empire where it also doubly serves as the color of [Native American Royalty](/wiki/Turquoise#Cultural_associations) even though the indult had initially fallen out of use due to wars of independence against Spain, the primary supporter of the Immaculate Conception, but was restored to all former Spanish-territories following the First Provincial Council of Manila as the Philippines earned it through reverting to, or staying loyal towards Catholicism, even though the church was divided on the issue of Philippine independence, amidst the schisms produced by the [Philippine Independent Church](/wiki/Philippine_Independent_Church) as well as other break away Protestant and [Masonic influenced churches](/wiki/Freemasonry_in_the_Philippines), after Spanish Colonialism was replaced by the American Imperialism. Other [Christian denominations](/wiki/List_of_Christian_denominations_in_the_Philippines) include [Iglesia ni Cristo](/wiki/Iglesia_ni_Cristo), the [Philippine Independent Church](/wiki/Philippine_Independent_Church), and [Seventh-day Adventism](/wiki/Seventh-day_Adventist_Church). [Protestants](/wiki/Protestantism_in_the_Philippines) made up about 5% to 7% of the population in 2010. The Philippines sends many [Christian missionaries](/wiki/Christian_mission) around the world, and is a training center for foreign priests and nuns.

[Islam](/wiki/Islam_in_the_Philippines) is the country's second-largest religion, with 6.4 percent of the population in the 2020 census. Most Muslims live in Mindanao and nearby islands, and most adhere to the [Shafi'i school](/wiki/Shafi%27i_school) of [Sunni Islam](/wiki/Sunni_Islam).

About 0.2 percent of the population [follow indigenous religions](/wiki/Indigenous_Philippine_folk_religions), whose practices and folk beliefs are often [syncretized](/wiki/Religious_syncretism) with Christianity and Islam. [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism_in_the_Philippines) is practiced by about 0.04% of the population, primarily by Filipinos of Chinese descent.

### Health

Main article: [Health in the Philippines](/wiki/Health_in_the_Philippines)

Life expectancy in the Philippines, 1938–2021

[Health care in the Philippines](/wiki/Health_care_in_the_Philippines) is provided by the national and local governments, although private payments account for most healthcare spending. Per-capita health expenditure in 2024 was [₱](/wiki/Philippine_peso)12,751 and health expenditures were 5.9 percent of the country's GDP. The 2023 budget allocation for healthcare was ₱334.9 billion. The 2019 enactment of the [Universal Health Care Act](/wiki/Universal_health_care) by President Duterte facilitated the automatic enrollment of all Filipinos in the [national health insurance program](/wiki/Philippine_Health_Insurance_Corporation). Since 2018, [Malasakit Centers](/wiki/Malasakit_Center) (one-stop shops) have been set up in several government-operated hospitals to provide medical and financial assistance to indigent patients.

Average life expectancy in the Philippines as of 2023 is 70.48 years (66.97 years for males, and 74.15 years for females). Access to medicine has improved due to increasing Filipino acceptance of [generic drugs](/wiki/Generic_drug). The country's leading causes of death in 2021 were [ischaemic heart diseases](/wiki/Ischaemic_heart_diseases), [cerebrovascular diseases](/wiki/Cerebrovascular_disease), [COVID-19](/wiki/COVID-19), [neoplasms](/wiki/Neoplasm), and [diabetes](/wiki/Diabetes). [Communicable diseases](/wiki/Communicable_disease) are correlated with natural disasters, primarily floods. One million Filipinos have active [tuberculosis](/wiki/Tuberculosis), the fourth highest global prevalence rate.

The Philippines has 1,387 [hospitals](/wiki/List_of_hospitals_in_the_Philippines), 33 percent of which are government-run; 23,281 barangay health stations, 2,592 rural health units, 2,411 [birthing homes](/wiki/Birthing_center), and 659 infirmaries provide primary care throughout the country. Since 1967, the Philippines had become the largest global supplier of nurses; seventy percent of [nursing graduates](/wiki/Nursing_in_the_Philippines) go overseas to work, causing problems in retaining skilled practitioners.

### Education

Main article: [Education in the Philippines](/wiki/Education_in_the_Philippines)

Further information: [Higher education in the Philippines](/wiki/Higher_education_in_the_Philippines)

Founded in 1611, the [University of Santo Tomas](/wiki/University_of_Santo_Tomas) is Asia's oldest extant university.

Primary and secondary schooling in the Philippines consists of six years of elementary period, four years of junior high school, and two years of senior high school. Public education, provided by the government, is free at the elementary and secondary levels and at [most public higher-education institutions](/wiki/Universal_Access_to_Quality_Tertiary_Education_Act). [Science high schools](/wiki/Philippine_Science_High_School_System) for talented students were established in 1963. The government provides technical-vocational training and development through the [Technical Education and Skills Development Authority](/wiki/Technical_Education_and_Skills_Development_Authority). In 2004, the government began offering [alternative education](/wiki/Alternative_Learning_System_(Philippines)) to out-of-school children, youth, and adults to improve literacy; [madaris](/wiki/Madrasa) were mainstreamed in 16 regions that year, primarily in Mindanao Muslim areas under the [Department of Education](/wiki/Department_of_Education_(Philippines)). [Catholic schools](/wiki/List_of_Catholic_universities_and_colleges_in_the_Philippines), which number more than 1,500, and higher education institutions are an integral part of the educational system.

The Philippines has [1,975 higher education institutions](/wiki/List_of_colleges_and_universities_in_the_Philippines) as of 2019, of which 246 are public and 1,729 are private. Public universities are non-sectarian, and are primarily classified as [state-administered](/wiki/List_of_state_schools,_colleges_and_universities_in_the_Philippines) or [local government-funded](/wiki/Local_colleges_and_universities_(Philippines)). The [national university](/wiki/National_university) is the eight-school [University of the Philippines](/wiki/University_of_the_Philippines) (UP) system. The country's top-ranked universities are the [University of the Philippines Diliman](/wiki/University_of_the_Philippines_Diliman), [Ateneo de Manila University](/wiki/Ateneo_de_Manila_University), [De La Salle University](/wiki/De_La_Salle_University), and [University of Santo Tomas](/wiki/University_of_Santo_Tomas).

In 2019, the Philippines had a basic [literacy](/wiki/Literacy) rate of 93.8 percent of those five years old or older, and a [functional literacy](/wiki/Functional_illiteracy) rate of 91.6 percent of those aged 10 to 64. Education, a significant proportion of the national budget, was allocated ₱900.9 billion from the ₱5.268 trillion 2023 budget. As of 2023, the country has 1,640 [public libraries](/wiki/Public_libraries) affiliated with the [National Library of the Philippines](/wiki/National_Library_of_the_Philippines).

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of the Philippines](/wiki/Culture_of_the_Philippines)

The [Banaue Rice Terraces](/wiki/Banaue_Rice_Terraces), carved by ancestors of the [Ifugao people](/wiki/Ifugao_people)

The Philippines has significant cultural diversity, reinforced by the country's fragmented geography. Spanish and American cultures profoundly influenced Filipino culture as a result of long colonization. The cultures of [Lumads](/wiki/Lumad) of Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago developed distinctly, since they had limited Spanish influence and more influence from nearby Islamic regions. Indigenous groups such as the [Igorots](/wiki/Igorot) have preserved their precolonial customs and traditions by [resisting the Spanish](/wiki/Igorot_resistance_to_Spanish_colonization). A [national identity](/wiki/Filipino_nationalism) [emerged](/wiki/National_revival) during the 19th century, however, with [shared national symbols](/wiki/National_symbols_of_the_Philippines) and cultural and historical [touchstones](/wiki/Touchstone_(metaphor)).

[Hispanic legacies](/wiki/Spanish_influence_on_Filipino_culture) includes the dominance of Catholicism, a [creole language](/wiki/Chavacano), numerous [loanwords](/wiki/List_of_loanwords_in_the_Tagalog_language#Spanish), the endangered [Philippine Spanish](/wiki/Philippine_Spanish), arts, architecture, [literature](/wiki/Philippine_literature_in_Spanish), drama, food, music, dance, fashion and the prevalence of [Spanish names and surnames](/wiki/Cat%C3%A1logo_alfab%C3%A9tico_de_apellidos), which resulted from an 1849 edict ordering the systematic distribution of family names and the implementation of [Spanish naming customs](/wiki/Spanish_naming_customs); the [names of many locations](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_place_names_of_Spanish_origin) also have Spanish origins. American influence on modern Filipino culture is evident in the use of English, and Filipino consumption of [fast food](/wiki/Fast_food) and American films and music.

[Public holidays in the Philippines](/wiki/Public_holidays_in_the_Philippines) are classified as regular or special. [Festivals](/wiki/List_of_festivals_in_the_Philippines) are primarily religious, and most towns and villages have such a festival (usually to honor a [patron saint](/wiki/Patron_saint)). Better-known festivals include [Ati-Atihan](/wiki/Ati-Atihan_festival), [Dinagyang](/wiki/Dinagyang), [Moriones](/wiki/Moriones_Festival), [Sinulog](/wiki/Sinulog), and [Flores de Mayo](/wiki/Flores_de_Mayo)—a month-long devotion to the [Virgin Mary](/wiki/Virgin_Mary) held in May. The country's [Christmas season](/wiki/Christmas_in_the_Philippines) begins as early as September 1, and [Holy Week](/wiki/Holy_Week_in_the_Philippines) is a solemn religious observance for its Christian population.

### Values

Main article: [Filipino values](/wiki/Filipino_values)

Further information: [Filipino psychology](/wiki/Filipino_psychology)

Statue in [Iriga](/wiki/Iriga) commemorating *[mano po](/wiki/Mano_(gesture))*

Filipino [values](/wiki/Value_(ethics_and_social_sciences)) are rooted primarily in personal alliances based in [kinship](/wiki/Philippine_kinship), obligation, friendship, religion (particularly Christianity), and commerce. They center around social harmony through *[pakikisama](/wiki/Pakikisama)*, motivated primarily by the desire for acceptance by a group. Reciprocity through *[utang na loob](/wiki/Utang_na_loob)* (a debt of gratitude) is a significant Filipino cultural trait, and an internalized debt can never be fully repaid. The main sanction for divergence from these values are the concepts of *hiya* (shame) and loss of *amor propio* ([self-esteem](/wiki/Self-esteem)).

The family is central to Philippine society; norms such as loyalty, maintaining close relationships and care for elderly parents are ingrained in Philippine society. Respect for authority and the elderly is valued, and is shown with gestures such as *mano* and the [honorifics](/wiki/Filipino_styles_and_honorifics) *po* and *opo* and *kuya* (older brother) or *ate* (older sister). Other Filipino values are optimism about the future, pessimism about the present, concern about other people, friendship and friendliness, [hospitality](/wiki/Hospitality), religiosity, respect for oneself and others (particularly [women](/wiki/Women_in_the_Philippines)), and integrity.

### Art and architecture

Main articles: [Arts in the Philippines](/wiki/Arts_in_the_Philippines) and [Architecture of the Philippines](/wiki/Architecture_of_the_Philippines)

Philippine art combines indigenous folk art, east-Asian and [Classical traditions](/wiki/Classical_art). During the Spanish colonial period, art was used to spread Catholicism mainly through paintings and sculptures. The first recorded sculptor in the Philippines is Juan de los Santos (1590 –1660) known for making retablos. In 1821, [Damian Domingo](/wiki/Dami%C3%A1n_Domingo), the father of Filipino paintings, opened the [*Academia de Dibujo*](/wiki/Academia_de_Dibujo_y_Pintura) art school in Binondo. Other artists during Spanish colonial rule included painters such as Josef Luciano Dans, [Jose Honorato Lozano](/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Honorato_Lozano), Mariano Asuncion and sculptors such as [Isabelo Tampinco](/wiki/Isabelo_Tampinco) and Crispulo Hocson. Artist whose works drew attention to the Philippines are [Juan Luna](/wiki/Juan_Luna) and [Félix Resurrección Hidalgo](/wiki/F%C3%A9lix_Resurrecci%C3%B3n_Hidalgo). [Fernando Amorsolo](/wiki/Fernando_Amorsolo) dominated Philippine paintings during the American colonial period, popular for his [pastoral](/wiki/Pastoral) scenes of Philippine countryside. [Victorio Edades](/wiki/Modernism) known as the father of Modern Philippine Art, popularized [Modernism](/wiki/Modernism) in the Philippines in 1920s and 1930s.

Traditional Philippine architecture has two main models: the indigenous *[bahay kubo](/wiki/Bahay_kubo)* and the *[bahay na bato](/wiki/Bahay_na_bato)*, which developed under Spanish rule. Some regions, such as [Batanes](/wiki/Batanes), differ slightly due to climate; limestone was used as a building material, and houses were built to withstand typhoons.

[Zamboanga City](/wiki/Zamboanga_City) [Hall](/wiki/Zamboanga_City_Hall)

[Spanish architecture](/wiki/Spanish_architecture) left an imprint in town designs around a [central square](/wiki/Poblacion) or *plaza mayor*, but many of its buildings were damaged or destroyed during World War II. [Several](/wiki/List_of_Baroque_churches_in_the_Philippines) Philippine [churches](/wiki/List_of_Catholic_churches_in_the_Philippines) adapted [baroque architecture](/wiki/Baroque_architecture) to withstand earthquakes, leading to the development of [Earthquake Baroque](/wiki/Earthquake_Baroque); [four baroque churches](/wiki/Baroque_Churches_of_the_Philippines) have been listed as a collective UNESCO [World Heritage Site](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site). [Spanish colonial fortifications](/wiki/Spanish_colonial_fortifications_in_the_Philippines) (*fuerzas*) in several parts of the Philippines were primarily designed by missionary architects and built by Filipino stonemasons. [Vigan](/wiki/Vigan), in [Ilocos Sur](/wiki/Ilocos_Sur), is known for its Hispanic-style houses and buildings.

American rule introduced new architectural styles in the construction of government buildings and [Art Deco](/wiki/Art_Deco) theaters. During the American period, construction of [Gabaldon school buildings](/wiki/Gabaldon_school_buildings) began, and some city planning using architectural designs and master plans by [Daniel Burnham](/wiki/Daniel_Burnham) was done in portions of [Manila](/wiki/Burnham_Plan_of_Manila) and [Baguio](/wiki/Baguio). Part of the Burnham plan was the construction of government buildings reminiscent of [Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture) or [Neoclassical architecture](/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture). Buildings from the Spanish and American periods can be seen in [Iloilo](/wiki/Iloilo), especially in [Calle Real](/wiki/Calle_Real,_Iloilo).

### Music and dance

Main articles: [Music of the Philippines](/wiki/Music_of_the_Philippines) and [Dance in the Philippines](/wiki/Dance_in_the_Philippines)

[Tinikling](/wiki/Tinikling), a dance depicting the swift leg movements of a *tikling* bird eluding a farmer's traps

There are two types of Philippine [folk dance](/wiki/Folk_dance), stemming from traditional indigenous influences and Spanish influence. Although native dances had become less popular, folk dancing began to revive during the 1920s. The [Cariñosa](/wiki/Cari%C3%B1osa), a Hispanic Filipino dance, is unofficially considered the country's national dance. Popular indigenous dances include the [Tinikling](/wiki/Tinikling) and [Singkil](/wiki/Singkil_(Maranao_dance)), which include the rhythmic clapping of bamboo poles. Present-day dances vary from delicate [ballet](/wiki/Ballet) to street-oriented [breakdancing](/wiki/Breakdancing).

[Rondalya](/wiki/Rondalla) music, with traditional [mandolin](/wiki/Mandolin)-type [instruments](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_musical_instruments), was popular during the Spanish era. Spanish-influenced musicians are primarily [bandurria](/wiki/Bandurria)-based bands with 14-string guitars. [Kundiman](/wiki/Kundiman) developed during the 1920s and 1930s. The American colonial period exposed many Filipinos to [U.S. culture](/wiki/Culture_of_the_United_States) and [popular music](/wiki/Music_of_the_United_States). [Rock music](/wiki/Rock_music) was introduced to Filipinos during the 1960s and developed into Filipino rock (or [Pinoy rock](/wiki/Pinoy_rock)), a term encompassing [pop rock](/wiki/Pop_rock), [alternative rock](/wiki/Alternative_rock), [heavy metal](/wiki/Heavy_metal_music), [punk](/wiki/Punk_rock), [new wave](/wiki/New_wave_music), [ska](/wiki/Ska), and [reggae](/wiki/Reggae). Martial law in the 1970s produced [Filipino folk rock](/wiki/Philippine_folk_music) bands and artists who [were at the forefront](/wiki/Protest_music_against_the_Marcos_dictatorship) of political demonstrations. The decade also saw the birth of the [Manila sound](/wiki/Manila_sound) and [Original Pilipino Music](/wiki/Original_Pilipino_Music) (OPM). [Filipino hip-hop](/wiki/Filipino_hip-hop), which originated in 1979, entered the mainstream in 1990. [Karaoke](/wiki/Karaoke) is also popular. From 2010 to 2020, [Pinoy pop](/wiki/Pinoy_pop) (P-pop) was influenced by [K-pop](/wiki/K-pop) and [J-pop](/wiki/J-pop).

Locally produced theatrical drama became established during the late 1870s. Spanish influence around that time introduced *[zarzuela](/wiki/Zarzuela)* plays (with music) and *[comedia](/wiki/Comedia_(play))*s, with dance. The plays became popular throughout the country, and were written in a number of local languages. American influence introduced [vaudeville](/wiki/Vaudeville) and ballet. [Realistic](/wiki/Realism_(theatre)) theater became dominant during the 20th century, with plays focusing on contemporary political and social issues.

### Literature

Main article: [Philippine literature](/wiki/Philippine_literature)

José Rizal's writings inspired the [Philippine Revolution](/wiki/Philippine_Revolution).

Philippine literature consists of [works](/wiki/Filipiniana) usually written in Filipino, [Spanish](/wiki/Philippine_literature_in_Spanish), or [English](/wiki/Philippine_literature_in_English). Some of the earliest well-known works were created from the 17th to the 19th centuries. They include *[Ibong Adarna](/wiki/Ibong_Adarna)*, an epic about an eponymous magical bird, and *[Florante at Laura](/wiki/Florante_at_Laura)* by Tagalog author [Francisco Balagtas](/wiki/Francisco_Balagtas). [José Rizal](/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Rizal) wrote the novels *[Noli Me Tángere](/wiki/Noli_Me_T%C3%A1ngere_(novel))* and *[El filibusterismo](/wiki/El_filibusterismo)*, both of which depict the injustices of Spanish colonial rule.

[Folk literature](/wiki/Philippine_folk_literature) was relatively unaffected by colonial influence until the 19th century due to Spanish indifference. Most printed literary works during Spanish colonial rule were religious in nature, although [Filipino elites](/wiki/Ilustrado) who later learned Spanish wrote [nationalistic](/wiki/Filipino_nationalism) literature. The American arrival [began](/wiki/Education_in_the_Philippines_during_American_rule) Filipino literary use of English and influenced the development of the [Philippine comics](/wiki/Philippine_comics) industry that flourished from the 1920s through the 1970s. In the late 1960s, during the presidency of Ferdinand Marcos, Philippine literature was [influenced by political activism](/wiki/Student_activism_in_the_Philippines_(1965%E2%80%931972)); many poets began using Tagalog, in keeping with the country's oral traditions.

[Philippine mythology](/wiki/Philippine_mythology) has been handed down primarily through oral tradition; popular [figures](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_mythological_figures) are [Maria Makiling](/wiki/Maria_Makiling), [Lam-ang](/wiki/Biag_ni_Lam-Ang), and the [Sarimanok](/wiki/Sarimanok). The country has a number of [folk epics](/wiki/Philippine_epic_poetry). Wealthy families could preserve transcriptions of the epics as family heirlooms, particularly in Mindanao; the [Maranao-language](/wiki/Maranao_language) *[Darangen](/wiki/Darangen)* is an example.

### Media

Main article: [Mass media in the Philippines](/wiki/Mass_media_in_the_Philippines)

Further information: [Cinema of the Philippines](/wiki/Cinema_of_the_Philippines)

People's Television Network logo

Philippine media primarily uses Filipino and English, although [broadcasting](/wiki/Broadcasting) has shifted to Filipino. [Television shows](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_television_shows), commercials, and [films](/wiki/Lists_of_Philippine_films) are regulated by the [Movie and Television Review and Classification Board](/wiki/Movie_and_Television_Review_and_Classification_Board). Most Filipinos obtain news and information from television, the Internet, and [social media](/wiki/Social_media_use_in_the_Philippines). The country's flagship state-owned broadcast-television network is the [People's Television Network](/wiki/People%27s_Television_Network) (PTV). [ABS-CBN](/wiki/ABS-CBN) and [GMA](/wiki/GMA_Network), both [free-to-air](/wiki/Free-to-air), were the dominant TV networks; before the May 2020 [Philippine government denial of ABS-CBN's franchise renewal](/wiki/ABS-CBN_franchise_renewal_controversy), it was the country's largest network. [Philippine television dramas](/wiki/Philippine_television_drama), known as *teleserye*s and mainly produced by [ABS-CBN](/wiki/List_of_ABS-CBN_original_drama_series) and [GMA](/wiki/List_of_GMA_Network_original_drama_series), are also seen in several other countries.

Local film-making [began in 1919](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_films_before_1940) with the release of the first Filipino-produced [feature film](/wiki/Feature_film): *[Dalagang Bukid](/wiki/Dalagang_Bukid)* (*A Girl from the Country*), directed by [Jose Nepomuceno](/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Nepomuceno). [Production companies](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_film_studios) remained small during the [silent film](/wiki/Silent_film) era, but sound films and larger productions emerged in 1933. The postwar 1940s to the early 1960s are considered a high point for Philippine cinema. The 1962–1971 decade saw a decline in quality films, although the commercial film industry expanded until the 1980s. Critically acclaimed Philippine films include *[Himala](/wiki/Himala)* (*Miracle*) and *[Oro, Plata, Mata](/wiki/Oro,_Plata,_Mata)* (*Gold, Silver, Death*), both released in 1982. Since the turn of the 21st century, the country's film industry has struggled to compete with larger-budget foreign films (particularly [Hollywood films](/wiki/Cinema_of_the_United_States)). [Art films](/wiki/Art_film) have thrived, however, and several [indie films](/wiki/Philippine_New_Wave) have been successful domestically and abroad.

The Philippines has a large number of [radio stations](/wiki/List_of_radio_stations_in_the_Philippines) and [newspapers](/wiki/List_of_newspapers_in_the_Philippines). English [broadsheets](/wiki/Broadsheet) are popular among executives, professionals and students. Less-expensive Tagalog [tabloids](/wiki/Tabloid_(newspaper_format)), which grew during the 1990s, are popular (particularly in Manila); however, overall newspaper readership is declining in favor of [online news](/wiki/Social_media_as_a_news_source). The top three newspapers, by nationwide readership and credibility, are the *[Philippine Daily Inquirer](/wiki/Philippine_Daily_Inquirer)*, *[Manila Bulletin](/wiki/Manila_Bulletin)*, and *[The Philippine Star](/wiki/The_Philippine_Star)*. Although [freedom of the press](/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press) is protected by the constitution, the country was listed as the seventh-most-dangerous country for journalists in 2022 by the [Committee to Protect Journalists](/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists) due to 13 unsolved murders of journalists.

The Philippine population are the world's top Internet users. In early 2021, 67 percent of Filipinos (73.91 million) had Internet access; the overwhelming majority used [smartphones](/wiki/Smartphone).

### Cuisine

Main article: [Filipino cuisine](/wiki/Filipino_cuisine)

A bowl of fish *[sinigang](/wiki/Sinigang)*

From its Malayo-Polynesian origins, traditional Philippine cuisine has evolved since the 16th century. It was primarily influenced by Hispanic, [Chinese](/wiki/Filipino_Chinese_cuisine), and [American](/wiki/American_cuisine) cuisines, which were adapted to the Filipino palate. Filipinos tend to prefer robust flavors, centered on sweet, salty, and sour combinations. Regional variations exist throughout the country; rice is the general staple [starch](/wiki/Starch#Food) but [cassava](/wiki/Cassava-based_dishes) is more common in parts of Mindanao. [Adobo](/wiki/Philippine_adobo) is the unofficial national dish. Other [popular dishes](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_dishes) include *[lechón](/wiki/Lech%C3%B3n)*, *[kare-kare](/wiki/Kare-kare)*, *[sinigang](/wiki/Sinigang)*, *[pancit](/wiki/Pancit)*, *[lumpia](/wiki/Lumpia)*, and *[arroz caldo](/wiki/Arroz_caldo)*. [Traditional desserts](/wiki/List_of_Philippine_desserts) are *kakanin* ([rice cakes](/wiki/Rice_cake)), which include *[puto](/wiki/Puto_(food))*, *[suman](/wiki/Suman_(food))*, and *[bibingka](/wiki/Bibingka)*. Ingredients such as [calamansi](/wiki/Calamansi), *[ube](/wiki/Ube_halaya)*, and *[pili](/wiki/Canarium_ovatum)* are used in Filipino desserts. The generous use of [condiments](/wiki/Philippine_condiments) such as *[patis](/wiki/Patis_(sauce))*, *[bagoong](/wiki/Bagoong)*, and *[toyo](/wiki/Soy_sauce#Filipino)* impart a distinctive Philippine flavor.There are vegan filipino food and they include laing, lumpiang gulay, ensaladang talong, turon, ginataang bilo-bilo, biko, espasol, banana cue, camote cue, taho, suman, carioca, and lumpiang sariwa.

Unlike other East or Southeast Asian countries, most Filipinos do not eat with [chopsticks](/wiki/Chopsticks); they use spoons and forks. Traditional eating with the hands or fingers (known as *[kamayan](/wiki/Kamayan)*) had been used in less urbanized areas, but has been popularized with the introduction of Filipino food to foreigners and city residents.

### Sports and recreation

Main articles: [Sports in the Philippines](/wiki/Sports_in_the_Philippines) and [Traditional games in the Philippines](/wiki/Traditional_games_in_the_Philippines)

The [Philippines men's national basketball team](/wiki/Philippines_men%27s_national_basketball_team) celebrating their [2015 Southeast Asian Games](/wiki/Basketball_at_the_2015_Southeast_Asian_Games) championship

[Basketball](/wiki/Basketball_in_the_Philippines), played at the amateur and professional levels, is considered the country's most popular sport. Other popular sports include [boxing](/wiki/History_of_boxing_in_the_Philippines) and billiards, boosted by the achievements of [Manny Pacquiao](/wiki/Manny_Pacquiao) and [Efren Reyes](/wiki/Efren_Reyes). The national [martial art](/wiki/Filipino_martial_arts) is [Arnis](/wiki/Arnis). *Sabong* ([cockfighting](/wiki/Cockfight)) is popular entertainment, especially among Filipino men, and was documented by the [Magellan expedition](/wiki/Magellan_expedition). [Video gaming](/wiki/Video_games_in_the_Philippines) and [esports](/wiki/Esports_in_the_Philippines) are emerging pastimes, with the popularity of [indigenous games](/wiki/Traditional_games_in_the_Philippines) such as *[patintero](/wiki/Patintero)*, *[tumbang preso](/wiki/Tumbang_preso)*, *[luksong tinik](/wiki/Luksong_tinik)*, and *[piko](/wiki/Piko_(Filipino_game))* declining among young people.

The [men's national football team](/wiki/Philippines_national_football_team) has participated in one [Asian Cup](/wiki/2019_AFC_Asian_Cup). The [women's national football team](/wiki/Philippines_women%27s_national_football_team) qualified for the [2023 FIFA Women's World Cup](/wiki/2023_FIFA_Women%27s_World_Cup), their first [World Cup](/wiki/FIFA_Women%27s_World_Cup), in January 2022. The Philippines has participated in every [Summer Olympic Games](/wiki/Summer_Olympic_Games) since [1924](/wiki/Philippines_at_the_1924_Summer_Olympics), except when they supported the [American-led boycott of the 1980 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1980_Summer_Olympics_boycott). It was the first [tropical nation](/wiki/Tropical_nations_at_the_Winter_Olympics) to compete at the [Winter Olympic Games](/wiki/Winter_Olympic_Games), [debuting in 1972](/wiki/Philippines_at_the_1972_Winter_Olympics). In 2021, the Philippines received its first-ever Olympic gold medal with [weightlifter](/wiki/Olympic_weightlifting) [Hidilyn Diaz](/wiki/Hidilyn_Diaz)'s victory in [Tokyo](/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics).
