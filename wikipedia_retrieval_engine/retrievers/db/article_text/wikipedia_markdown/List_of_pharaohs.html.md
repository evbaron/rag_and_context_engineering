The [pharaohs](/wiki/Pharaoh) were the monarchs of [ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) from the unification of [Upper](/wiki/Upper_Egypt) and [Lower Egypt](/wiki/Lower_Egypt) c. 3100 BC, with several times of fragmentation and foreign rule. The specific title of "pharaoh" ([*pr-ꜥꜣ*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/pr-%EA%9C%A5%EA%9C%A3)) was not used until the [New Kingdom](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt), c. 1400 BC, but it is retroactively applied to all Egyptian kings; the generic term for monarchs was "nesut" ([*nswt*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/nswt)). In addition to these titles, pharaohs had a [complex royal titulary](/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_royal_titulary) that remained relatively constant during its 3000-year history, having up to five royal names.

[Egypt](/wiki/Egypt) was continually governed, at least in part, by [native](/wiki/Egyptians) pharaohs for approximately 2500 years, until it was conquered by the [Libyan](/wiki/Meshwesh) [22nd dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-second_Dynasty_of_Egypt), and subsequently, the [Kingdom of Kush](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush) in the late 8th century BC, whose rulers adopted the pharaonic titulature and became the [25th Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). Following 100 years of Kushite rule, Egypt experienced another century of independent native rule before being conquered by the [Persian](/wiki/Persians) [Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire). The last native pharaoh was [Nectanebo II](/wiki/Nectanebo_II) of the short-lived [30th Dynasty](/wiki/Thirtieth_Dynasty_of_Egypt), which ended when the Persians conquered Egypt for a second time in 342 BC. The Persians were in turn conquered by the [Macedonian Greeks](/wiki/Ancient_Macedonians) of [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) in 332 BC, after which Egypt was ruled by the [Hellenic](/wiki/Ancient_Greece) pharaohs of the [Ptolemaic dynasty](/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty). Their rule came to an end with the [Roman](/wiki/Ancient_Rome) conquest of Egypt in 30 BC, and pharaonic Egypt ceased to be an independent monarchy. However, [Roman emperors](/wiki/Roman_emperor) continued to be [accorded pharaonic titles](/wiki/Roman_pharaoh) by the Egyptians until the reign of [Maximinus Daza](/wiki/Maximinus_Daza) in 313 AD.

The dates provided for most of Egypt's early history are only approximate and may vary depending on the author, sometimes by centuries. The names and order of kings is mostly based on the Digital Egypt for Universities database developed by the [Petrie Museum of Egyptian Archaeology](/wiki/Petrie_Museum_of_Egyptian_Archaeology). For royal titles and hieroglyphs, see the handbook of [Jürgen von Beckerath](/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_von_Beckerath), as well as the website *Pharaohs of Ancient Egypt*, which itself contains extensive bibliography.

[Regnal numbers](/wiki/Regnal_number) did not exist in ancient Egypt and is a modern way to distinguish pharaohs known by the same name.

## Sources and chronology

Modern lists of pharaohs are based on historical records, including ancient Egyptian king lists and later histories, as well as archaeological evidence. Concerning ancient sources, Egyptologists and historians alike call for caution regarding their credibility, exactitude, and completeness, many of which were written long after the reigns they report. An additional problem is that ancient king lists are often damaged, inconsistent with one another, and/or selective.

Given the lack of sources, it's impossible to fix exact dates for the early dynasties. However, most authors give dates around 3000 BC. According to computations derived from the [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List) (c. 1250 BC), the [First Dynasty](/wiki/First_Dynasty_of_Egypt) most likely began c. 3100 BC, ± 150 years. This appears to be supported by [radiocarbon dating](/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating) studies, which often show earlier dates.

Astronomical events, mainly the [Sothic cycle](/wiki/Sothic_cycle), have also been used to try to fix "absolute dates", but these have their own set of problems. [Richard Parker](/wiki/Richard_Anthony_Parker) dated the accession of the 12th-dynasty pharaoh [Senusret III](/wiki/Senusret_III) to 1878 BC (with his 7th year falling in 1872 BC). However, other authors such as [Rolf Krauss](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Krauss) have refuted Parker's computations; Krauss instead dates Senusret's accession to 1837/36 BC (7th year in 1831/30 BC). Similarly, the accession [Amenhotep I](/wiki/Amenhotep_I) was dated c. 1550–1515 BC (9th year c. 1541–1506 BC). Scholars often use the Egyptian "high" chronology, which assumes these astronomical observations were made in a single place (at [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt)). While there is debate on its accuracy, the high chronology used by scholars such as [Ian Shaw](/wiki/Ian_Shaw_(Egyptologist)) appears to be closer to the dates implied by [radiocarbon dating](/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating).

Most pharaohs' reigns are not fully documented, leading to many discrepancies in reign lengths. A formal regnal dating system was not adopted until the [Middle Kingdom](/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt), and the day following a coronation was automatically recorded as "Year 1", meaning that regnal years alone can't be used to calculate exact [Julian years](/wiki/Julian_calendar). Thus, it is impossible to determine exact dates even when some events can be securely dated. Dates with some certainty can only be given for the [New Kingdom](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt) onwards, and only dates from the [Late period](/wiki/Late_Period_of_Egypt) can be securely dated and synchronized with other ancient chronologies.

Compare the chronological lists in [Beckerath 1997](#CITEREFBeckerath1997), pp. 187–192, [Beckerath 1999](#CITEREFBeckerath1999), pp. 283–289, [University College London 2000](#CITEREFUniversity_College_London2000), [Bunson 2002](#CITEREFBunson2002), pp. 105–108, [Shaw 2003](#CITEREFShaw2003), pp. 481–488, [Hornung, Krauss & Warburton 2006](#CITEREFHornungKraussWarburton2006), pp. 490–495, [Mladjov 2021a](#CITEREFMladjov2021a) and [Mladjov 2021b](#CITEREFMladjov2021b).

### Ancient Egyptian king lists

Royal lists after the [Fifth Dynasty](/wiki/Fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) give only the [throne name](/wiki/Prenomen_(Ancient_Egypt)) of each pharaoh, which has often led to confusion in identifying particular kings. The most detailed king lists, the [Abydos](/wiki/Abydos_King_List), [Saqqara](/wiki/Saqqara_Tablet) and [Turin](/wiki/Turin_King_List) canons, date to the [New Kingdom](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt), also known as the Ramesside period. Unfortunately, most of these Ramesside lists are of little value for the early dynasties (from which they are separated by over 1000 years), as they feature corrupted names and often disagree with contemporary sources. Complete king lists were certainly made after the 20th dynasty, but they have been lost.

The following king lists are known:

- [Den seal impressions](/wiki/Den_seal_impressions) (1st Dynasty, c. 3000 BC); found on a cylinder seal in [Den](/wiki/Den_(pharaoh))'s tomb. It lists all 1st Dynasty kings from [Narmer](/wiki/Narmer) to Den by their [Horus names](/wiki/Horus_name).
- [Qa'a seal impressions](/wiki/Narmer) (1st Dynasty, c. 2900 BC); found in [Qa'a](/wiki/Qa%27a)'s tomb. It lists all eight kings of the 1st Dynasty by their [Horus names](/wiki/Horus_name).
- [Tomb of Sekhemkare](/wiki/Sekhemkare_(vizier)) (5th Dynasty, c. 2500 BC); records five kings from [Khafre](/wiki/Khafre) to [Sahure](/wiki/Sahure); the contemporary tomb of Netjerpunesut also includes [Djedefre](/wiki/Djedefre).
- [Royal Annals of the Old Kingdom](/wiki/Royal_Annals_of_the_Old_Kingdom) (5th Dynasty, c. 2400 BC); carved on an [stele](/wiki/Stele). Recorded the individual years of about 30 kings from [Menes](/wiki/Menes) to at least [Kakai](/wiki/Neferirkare_Kakai). Broken into small pieces, mostly known for the [Palermo Stone](/wiki/Palermo_Stone).
- [Tomb of Seshemnefer III](/wiki/Seshemnefer_III) (5th Dynasty, c. 2400 BC); carved onto the walls of Seshemnefer's mastaba tomb. Not an actual king list; it mentions at least 7 kings from [Sneferu](/wiki/Sneferu) to [Nyuserre Ini](/wiki/Nyuserre_Ini).
- [Giza writing board](/wiki/Giza_writing_board) (6th Dynasty, c. 2400 BC); painted on [gypsum](/wiki/Gypsum) and cedar wood. Records 6 kings of various dynasties.
- [South Saqqara Stone](/wiki/South_Saqqara_Stone) (6th Dynasty, c. 2200 BC); carved on a black basalt slab. Recorded the reigns of the first 5 kings of the [Sixth Dynasty](/wiki/Sixth_Dynasty); it was later reused as a [sarcophagus](/wiki/Sarcophagus) lid and thus almost all of the original text is lost.
- [Wadi Hammamat king list](/wiki/Baufra) (12th dynasty, c. 2000–1800 BC); records five kings of the [Fourth Dynasty](/wiki/Fourth_Dynasty).
- [Karnak King List](/wiki/Karnak_King_List) (18th Dynasty, c. 1450 BC); carved on [limestone](/wiki/Limestone) at the [Festival Hall](/wiki/Festival_Hall_of_Thutmose_III) of [Thutmose III](/wiki/Thutmose_III). Lists 61 kings from [Sneferu](/wiki/Sneferu), but with no apparent order and omitting several names. 39 names have been lost.
- [Tomb of Amenmose](/wiki/TT19) (19th Dynasty, c. 1280 BC); lists [Mentuhotep II](/wiki/Mentuhotep_II) and 11 kings from [Ahmose I](/wiki/Ahmose_I) to [Seti I](/wiki/Seti_I).
- **[Abydos King List](/wiki/Abydos_King_List)** (19th Dynasty, c. 1280 BC); carved at the [Temple of Seti I](/wiki/Temple_of_Seti_I_(Abydos)). Very detailed, but omitting some kings from the [First Intermediate Period](/wiki/First_Intermediate_Period) and all from the [Second Intermediate Period](/wiki/Second_Intermediate_Period). Records a total of 76 kings from [Menes](/wiki/Menes) to Seti I. A [similar but damaged list](/wiki/Abydos_King_List_(Ramesses_II)) is found in the Abydos temple of [Ramesses II](/wiki/Ramesses_II), Seti's son and successor.
- **[Saqqara Tablet](/wiki/Saqqara_Tablet)** (19th Dynasty, c. 1250 BC), carved on limestone in a private tomb. Very detailed, lists 68 kings from [Anedjib](/wiki/Anedjib) until Ramesses II.
- **[Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List)** (19th Dynasty, c. 1250 BC); written on a [papyrus](/wiki/Papyrus) dating to the reign of [Ramesses II](/wiki/Ramesses_II). Listed every known king with their exact reign length, and divided some of them into groups similar to [Manetho](/wiki/Manetho)'s dynasties. The document itself is a sloppy copy of a much more detailed original, which is in turn based on much older sources. The document is today damaged and incomplete, with most of the last sections missing. The papyrus lists 223 kings, but only about 1⁄3 of the names have survived. The original likely included further kings until Ramesses II.
- Table of Qenhirkhopshef (19th Dynasty, c. 1250 BC); found in the [Karnak](/wiki/Karnak) complex. Lists 17 kings from [Senakhtenre Ahmose](/wiki/Senakhtenre_Ahmose) to Ramesses II.
- [Tomb of Khabekhnet](/wiki/TT2) (19th Dynasty, c. 1250 BC); lists [Mentuhotep II](/wiki/Mentuhotep_II) and five kings from [Senakhtenre Ahmose](/wiki/Senakhtenre_Ahmose) to [Amenhotep I](/wiki/Amenhotep_I) alongside other family members.
- [Ramesseum king list](/wiki/Ramesseum_king_list) (19th Dynasty, c. 1250 BC); carved in the mortuary temple of Ramesses II, lists [Menes](/wiki/Menes), [Mentuhotep II](/wiki/Mentuhotep_II) and most of the New Kingdom pharaohs.
- [Medinet Habu king list](/wiki/Medinet_Habu_king_list) (20th Dynasty, c. 1150 BC); carved in the mortuary temple of [Ramesses III](/wiki/Ramesses_III), very similar to the Ramesseum king list.
- [Tomb of Inherkhau](/wiki/Tomb_of_Inherkhau) (20th Dynasty, c. 1150 BC); records seven kings of the New Kingdom until [Ramesses IV](/wiki/Ramesses_IV) alongside other family members.
- [Genealogy of Ankhefensekhmet](/wiki/Genealogy_of_Ankhefensekhmet) (22nd Dynasty, c. 750 BC); carved on a limestone dating to the reign of [Shoshenq V](/wiki/Shoshenq_V), today damaged. Not an actual king list; it mentions at least 18 kings from [Mentuhotep II](/wiki/Mentuhotep_II) to [Psusennes I](/wiki/Psusennes_I).

### Manetho

Before the [decipherment of Egyptian scripts](/wiki/Decipherment_of_ancient_Egyptian_scripts) in the early 19th century, [Manetho](/wiki/Manetho)'s *[Aegyptiaca](/wiki/Aegyptiaca)*, written in Greek in the early 3rd century BC, was the sole source for all ancient Egyptian history. Manetho, himself an Egyptian priest, recorded the entirety of his country's history from mythological times until the conquest of [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) in 332 BC. He also created the dynastic framework still used by modern scholars, as well as coining the term "[dynasty](/wiki/Dynasty)" itself. The original work is now lost and survives only through later [epitomes](/wiki/Epitome) and quotations, chiefly in the writings of [Josephus](/wiki/Josephus) (*[Contra Apionem](/wiki/Contra_Apionem)*, c. 100 AD), [Julius Africanus](/wiki/Sextus_Julius_Africanus) (c. 230 AD), and [Eusebius](/wiki/Eusebius) (c. 330 AD). These summaries transmit primarily chronological data in the form of regnal lists, though Josephus' quotations show that Manetho originally included detailed narratives, regnal lengths in years and months (rounded as years alone by Africanus and Eusebius), and even alternate royal names.

Africanus' epitome, which is preserved by [George Syncellus](/wiki/George_Syncellus) (c. 810 AD), is generally regarded as the most reliable, while that of Eusebius is considered more problematic, having been derived from an incomplete and already corrupted source. All surviving transmissions suffer from errors, inconsistencies in regnal totals, variant name spellings, and a failure to account for contemporaneous dynasties—whether due to Manetho himself or to later copyists. Josephus, Africanus, and Eusebius all used independent, and sometimes contradictory, versions of the same work, each copy adding a new layer of typos and corruptions.

The content of the *Aegyptiaca* must be treated with caution. Manetho likely blended historical tradition with mythology, and later Christian authors are known to have altered Manetho's figures, especially for the [Second Intermediate period](/wiki/Second_Intermediate_Period), to accommodate events into the [Biblical narrative](/wiki/The_Exodus). Additional distortion arose from the transmission of Egyptian royal names into Greek and from repeated copying over centuries ([Greek](/wiki/Greek_language), like [Latin](/wiki/Latin), was written using *[scriptio continua](/wiki/Scriptio_continua)*, with no spaces or punctuation between words). Despite these limitations, Manetho remains a foundational source for Egyptian chronology, provided his data are critically evaluated and corroborated with archaeological and contemporary evidence.

Some fragments of Egyptian history are also covered by some Greek historians such a [Herodotus](/wiki/Herodotus) (c. 450 BC) and [Diodorus Siculus](/wiki/Diodorus_Siculus) (c. 50 BC).

### Number of kings

The [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List) records 207 kings up to the end of [Dynasty XVI](/wiki/Dynasty_XVI). In addition, it preserves 16 damaged names that [Kim Ryholt](/wiki/Kim_Ryholt) associates with the so-called [Abydos Dynasty](/wiki/Abydos_Dynasty), totaling 223 kings in the preserved papyrus. The original document, however, likely extended until the reign of [Ramesses II](/wiki/Ramesses_II), just as the [Saqqara](/wiki/Saqqara_Tablet) and [Ramesseum](/wiki/Ramesseum_king_list) king lists. On this basis, the Turin King List probably listed at least 27 additional rulers: nine of [Dynasty XVII](/wiki/Dynasty_XVII), fifteen of [Dynasty XVIII](/wiki/Dynasty_XVIII), and the first three kings of [Dynasty XIX](/wiki/Dynasty_XIX), yielding a minimum total of 250 kings.

The papyrus also contains several [*lacunae*](/wiki/Lacuna_(manuscripts)), explicit gaps marked by the Ramesside scribes when names in their sources were illegible. Ryholt estimates that these *lacunae* represent at least 12 missing kings: six for [Dynasty XIII](/wiki/Dynasty_XIII), and at least six for [Dynasty XIV](/wiki/Dynasty_XIV). In addition, there is a *lacuna* in Column 5.15 that very likely corresponds to Manetho's [Dynasty VII](/wiki/Dynasty_VII), consisting of 10 additional kings recorded only in the [Abydos King List](/wiki/Abydos_King_List). There are also two instances of fictitious kings, both in [Dynasty IV](/wiki/Dynasty_IV) (likely Manetho's *[Bikheris](/wiki/Bikheris)* and *[Thamphthis](/wiki/Thamphthis)*). Taking this into account, the total rises to at least 227 kings up to the end of [Dynasty XVI](/wiki/Dynasty_XVI), 243 up to the end of the [Abydos Dynasty](/wiki/Abydos_Dynasty), and 270 until Ramesses II. Even these figures likely remain conservative, since a small number of ephemeral or disputed rulers —such as [Sneferka](/wiki/Sneferka) or [Ba](/wiki/Horus_Bird_(pharaoh)) at the end of [Dynasty I](/wiki/Dynasty_I)— were probably omitted altogether.

Including the subsequent periods of native and foreign rule, the total of kings reaches more than 300 before the first [Persian conquest](/wiki/First_Achaemenid_conquest_of_Egypt), which closely aligns with [Herodotus](/wiki/Herodotus)' statement that, following [Menes](/wiki/Menes), Egypt was ruled by "three hundred and thirty kings, whose names the priests recited from a papyrus roll".:100 Manetho's own total of kings is roughly 360 kings in 5470 years, but the sum of individual reigns amounts to more than 500 kings in 5370 years. [Diodorus Siculus](/wiki/Diodorus_Siculus) writes that mortal kings have ruled Egypt for "a little less than five thousand years".

### Royal names and titulature

Main article: [Ancient Egyptian royal titulary](/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_royal_titulary)

The first Egyptian kings were known by their **[Horus name](/wiki/Horus_name)** ([serekh](/wiki/Serekh)), with additional [Nebty](/wiki/Nebty_name) and [Golden Horus](/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_royal_titulary#Horus_of_Gold) honorifics. From the late [First Dynasty](/wiki/First_Dynasty_of_Egypt) onwards, kings began to use a **throne name** known in English as the [**prenomen**](/wiki/Prenomen_(Ancient_Egypt)) (*nswt-bjtj* or *nisut-bity*, "Sedge and Bee"), which was the main name used during the [Fourth Dynasty](/wiki/Fourth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) as well as the royal name found in most king-lists. The next dynasty introduced the [**nomen**](/wiki/Nomen_(ancient_Egypt)) (*Sa-Ra*, "son of [Ra](/wiki/Ra)"), which is often understood as a **personal name**. This is the name used by scholars after the [Fifth Dynasty](/wiki/Fifth_Dynasty), as well as the name used by [Manetho](/wiki/Manetho) for most pharaohs. Given the confusing nature of royal names, only kings who are recorded with their two [cartouche](/wiki/Cartouche) names (*prenomen* and *nomen*) can be securely identified. Throne names assigned to pre-Fifth Dynasty pharaohs are sometimes referred to as "personal names" by scholars, as there was probably no distinction between throne and personal names at the time. For a listing and description of all royal names, from Predynastic to [Ptolemaic](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom) times, see [Leprohon 2013](#CITEREFLeprohon2013).

## Protodynastic period

Main article: [Naqada III](/wiki/Naqada_III)

The [Protodynastic period of Egypt](/wiki/Protodynastic_Period_of_Egypt) spans from the formation of the first Egyptian states about 3300 BC to about 3000 BC, when Egypt was first unified as a single kingdom by the rulers of [Upper Egypt](/wiki/Upper_Egypt). Before this, both Lower and Upper Egypt were divided into several small kingdoms. The terms "Lower Egypt" and "Upper Egypt" are based on the direction of the [Nile](/wiki/Nile)'s flow, which is north. Consequently, "Lower Egypt" is located north (downstream), while "Upper Egypt" is located south (upstream).

### Lower Egypt

[Lower Egypt](/wiki/Lower_Egypt) geographically consists of the northern [Nile](/wiki/Nile) and the [Nile delta](/wiki/River_delta).

The [Palermo Stone](/wiki/Palermo_Stone) mentions 14 predynastic pharaohs by their Horus name from [Lower Egypt](/wiki/Lower_Egypt). These may be mythical kings or semi-gods preserved through oral tradition, or posthumous names of earlier pharaohs. The original document likely contained rulers for Upper Egypt as well.

cemetery at Abydos), [Naqada](/wiki/Naqada) and [Hierakonpolis](/wiki/Hierakonpolis).

While the early dynasties were aware of their pre-dynastic history, this appears to have been all but forgotten by the Ramessid period. Later sources refer to the [Menes](/wiki/Menes)/[Narmer](/wiki/Narmer) as the first "human king", succeeding the gods that ruled Egypt for times immemorial. Later tradition also identified him as the founder of [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt), when in fact the city had existed since before the reign of [Iry-Hor](/wiki/Iry-Hor).

## Early Dynastic period (c. 3100–2700 BC)

See also: [Early Dynastic Period of Egypt](/wiki/Early_Dynastic_Period_of_Egypt)

The sole "contemporary" source for early dynastic chronology is the [Palermo Stone](/wiki/Palermo_Stone) and its associated fragments. The [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List), is not considered reliable for this period, as it often contradicts contemporary sources. The annals are badly damaged, and only a few names are explicitly recorded, leading to several and divergent reconstructions over the decades. Unless otherwise noted, the reconstruction follows [Hornung, Krauss & Warburton 2006](#CITEREFHornungKraussWarburton2006). Lastly, it's worth noting that virtually all [transliterations](/wiki/Transliteration) are just approximations ([Egyptian](/wiki/Egyptian_language) is an [extinct language](/wiki/Extinct_language)); certain spellings may change depending on the author.

### First Dynasty

Main article: [First Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/First_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

Ancient sources, including [Manetho](/wiki/Manetho), assign eight rulers to the First Dynasty, a fact confirmed by the records in the necropolis of [Qa'a](/wiki/Qa%27a), the last pharaoh. The tombs of all pharaohs, including queen [Merneith](/wiki/Merneith), are located in the necropolis of [Umm El Qa'ab](/wiki/Umm_El_Qa%27ab), [Abydos](/wiki/Abydos,_Egypt). The first two dynasties originated from the [lost city](/wiki/Lost_city) of [Thinis](/wiki/Thinis), and ruled from [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt) according to later traditions.

Manetho correctly assigns eight kings to this dynasty, but his order and naming are troublesome. [Ramesside](/wiki/Nineteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) king lists, made 1000 years later, identify all eight kings with throne names, but none of them match contemporary records. Throne names were introduced by the fifth king, so the first names must be later additions, possibly posthumous epithets. As noted before, the dynasty must have begun c. **3100 BC**, ± 150 years.

## Old Kingdom (c. 2700–2200 BC)

Main article: [Old Kingdom of Egypt](/wiki/Old_Kingdom_of_Egypt)

The history of ancient Egypt is divided into three "golden ages" ([Old](/wiki/Old_Kingdom_of_Egypt), [Middle](/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt), and [New Kingdom](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt)), interspersed between three periods of fragmentation known as "intermediate periods". The capital during the period of the Old Kingdom was also [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt). According to [radiocarbon dating](/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating), the Old Kingdom began sometime around the mid-27th century BC, possibly between 2676 and 2643 BC, but with more certainty between 2691 and 2625 BC.

### Third Dynasty

Main article: [Third Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Third_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

Ancient sources assign five rulers to the Third Dynasty, but there is uncertainty regarding their exact order and chronology. Both the Turin and Abydos king lists claim that the Third Dynasty was founded by [Nebka](/wiki/Nebka), hence his position in some modern lists. However, archeological evidence has shown that the founder was in fact Djoser, who is correctly identified as its first king in the [Saqqara Tablet](/wiki/Saqqara_Tablet). The dynasty ruled 74 years according to the Turin King List, but its numbers do not match with the earlier [Palermo Stone](/wiki/Palermo_Stone); the actual duration may be as low as 50 years.

**Proposed dates**: c. 2682–2614 BC (68 years), c. 2649–2575 BC (74 years), c. 2686–2600 BC (86 years), c. 2686–2613 BC (73 years), c. 2592–2544 BC (48 years)

### Fourth Dynasty

Main article: [Fourth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Fourth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

The Fourth Dynasty is renowned for its colossal monuments and pyramids, most notably the [Great Pyramid of Giza](/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza), one of the [Seven Wonders of the Ancient World](/wiki/Seven_Wonders_of_the_Ancient_World). While the first four kings are well attested, there is some uncertainty regarding the successors of Khafre in later sources. However, the tomb of [Sekhemkare](/wiki/Sekhemkare_(vizier)), vizier and son of [Khafre](/wiki/Khafre), records the correct succession from Khafre [Sahure](/wiki/Sahure). Sekhemkare supposedly lived under five pharaohs, which implies that the latter kings did not reign long. Some tales of this period were recorded by the Greek historian [Herodotus](/wiki/Herodotus), although he notably places it *after* the New Kingdom period.

The exact chronology of this dynasty is disputed, as scholars disagree whether [cattle counts](/wiki/Cattle_count) were made annually or biennially at this time. The following numbers assume most were annual.

### Fifth Dynasty

Main article: [Fifth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

Starting with the reign of [Neferirkare Kakai](/wiki/Neferirkare_Kakai), pharaohs became mainly known by two names, a regnal ([prenomen](/wiki/Prenomen_(Ancient_Egypt))) and personal ([nomen](/wiki/Nomen_(ancient_Egypt))) name. The pharaohs of the Sixth Dynasty onwards are generally known by their nomen alone.

All variants of Manetho refer to the Fifth Dynasty as "kings from [Elephantine](/wiki/Elephantine)". This is probably a misreading of "Sakhebu", a city near [Heliopolis](/wiki/Heliopolis_(ancient_Egypt)) that was likely the hometown of [Userkaf](/wiki/Userkaf).

### Sixth Dynasty

Main article: [Sixth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Sixth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

By the Fifth Dynasty, the religious institution had established itself as the dominant force in society; a trend of growth in the bureaucracy and the priesthood, and a decline in the pharaoh's power had been established during [Neferirkare Kakai](/wiki/Neferirkare_Kakai)'s reign. During [Djedkare Isesi](/wiki/Djedkare_Isesi)'s rule, officials were endowed with greater authority—evidenced by the opulent private tombs they constructed—eventually leading to the creation of a feudal system in effect. These established trends—decentralization of authority, coupled with growth in bureaucracy—intensified during the three decades of [Unas](/wiki/Unas)'s rule, which also witnessed economic decline.

**Proposed dates**: c. 2322–2191 BC (131 years), c. 2323–2150 BC (173 years), c. 2300–2181 BC (150 years), c. 2345–2181 BC (164 years), c. 2305–2118 BC (187 years)

According to Africanus, the total sum for the kings of the First through Sixth dynasties amounts to 1497 years, but the actual sum of items gives 1478. Eusebius closely follows Africanus and records 1498 total years, but the actual sum of his numbers is 1504. This may imply that the summation figures are more accurate than the individual reigns.

The [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List) records a total of 52 kings up to [Neferirkare](/wiki/Neferirkare), that is, 45 kings from [Menes](/wiki/Menes) to [Merenre Nemtyemsaf II](/wiki/Merenre_Nemtyemsaf_II), although the list includes two fictitious kings in the [Fourth Dynasty](/wiki/Fourth_Dynasty_of_Egypt), thus totaling 43 kings. Africanus records a total of 48 kings, while Eusebius records about 70. This discrepancy is the result of a misunderstanding of the text, as later both Eusebius and Africanus record 192 kings before the Twelfth Dynasty.

## First Intermediate period (c. 2200–2050 BC)

Main article: [First Intermediate Period of Egypt](/wiki/First_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt)

The [Old Kingdom](/wiki/Old_Kingdom) rapidly collapsed after the death of [Pepi II Neferkare](/wiki/Pepi_II_Neferkare), who supposedly reigned up to 94 years, longer than any monarch in history. The latter years of his reign were probably marked by inefficiency because of his advanced age. This was further exacerbated by the [4.2-kiloyear event](/wiki/4.2-kiloyear_event), a climate shift around 2200 BC which caused widespread desertification and [famine](/wiki/Famine).

The kings of the 7th and 8th Dynasties, who represented the successors of the 6th Dynasty, tried to hold onto some power in Memphis but owed much of it to powerful [nomarchs](/wiki/Nomarch). After a couple of decades, they were overthrown by a new line of pharaohs based in [Heracleopolis Magna](/wiki/Heracleopolis_Magna). Some time after these events, a rival line based at [Thebes](/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt) revolted against their Northern overlords and united [Upper Egypt](/wiki/Upper_Egypt). The Theban pharaoh [Mentuhotep II](/wiki/Mentuhotep_II), the son and successor of [Intef III](/wiki/Intef_III), defeated the Herakleopolitan pharaohs and reunited the Two Lands, thereby starting the Middle Kingdom.

### Seventh and Eighth Dynasties

Main articles: [Seventh Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Seventh_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and [Eighth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Eighth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

[Africanus](/wiki/Sextus_Julius_Africanus) and [Eusebius](/wiki/Eusebius) record 70 kings in 70 days, which is clearly an embellishment. Some historians dismiss the existence of a Seventh Dynasty, while others argue it could correspond to the "[lacuna](/wiki/Lacuna_(manuscripts)) of 6 years" recorded in the [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List). The following tables are based on [Beckerath 1999](#CITEREFBeckerath1999), pp. 66–69 & 284, which is itself based on the [Abydos King List](/wiki/Abydos_King_List). The Turin King List only records [Netjerkare Siptah](/wiki/Netjerkare_Siptah) and the last 6 names. This line ruled from [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt), and it is thought that it lasted only about one generation, or about 30 years. Manetho reports 27 kings for the Eighth Dynasty; the number may be a corruption of "17 kings", the total of kings for this line.

### Ninth and Tenth Dynasties

Main articles: [Ninth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Ninth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and [Tenth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Tenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

Africanus records 19 kings for 409 years, but only lists one *Achthoes*, a tyrant who was killed by a crocodile. Meanwhile, Eusebius records 4 kings in 100 years. For the next dynasty, they both record 19 kings for 185 years, but list none. It is possible that the 9th and 10th dynasties were in fact a single dynasty that was duplicated by Manetho (or one of his scribes), resulting in two [Heracleopolean](/wiki/Heracleopolis_Magna) dynasties of 19 kings. The [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List) records an unbroken line of 18 kings, but almost all names, as well as the sum of regnal years, are damaged. A break between dynasties, while still possible, can't be pinpointed with any certainty.

## Middle Kingdom (c. 2050–1800 BC)

Main article: [Middle Kingdom of Egypt](/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt)

The kings of the 11th Dynasty ruled from [Thebes](/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt) and the kings of the 12th Dynasty ruled from [Itjtawy](/wiki/Itjtawy), near [Lisht](/wiki/Lisht). The periodization of the Middle Kingdom is somewhat disputed; some authors include only the [Eleventh](/wiki/Eleventh_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and [Twelfth Dynasties](/wiki/Twelfth_Dynasty_of_Egypt), while others include the [Eleventh](/wiki/Eleventh_Dynasty_of_Egypt), [Thirteenth](/wiki/Thirteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and [Fourteenth Dynasties](/wiki/Fourteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt).

Egyptian chronology becomes more reliable from this point onward, although dates still differ depending on the author. The reign of [Senusret III](/wiki/Senusret_III) can securely be dated to the middle of the 19th century BC (c. 1850, ± 25 years), thus dating the beginning of the 12th dynasty to the mid 20th century BC (c. 1950), and the 11th dynasty to the mid 21st century BC (c. 2050 BC). This is supported by [radiocarbon dating](/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating), which shows that the Middle Kingdom likely began in the range of 2057–2040 BC, with more certainty between 2064 and 2019 BC. The accession of [Senusret III](/wiki/Senusret_III) is dated to 1884–1860 BC, with more certainty between 1889 and 1836 BC.

### Twelfth Dynasty

Main article: [Twelfth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Twelfth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

Starting from the Twelfth Dynasty, pharaohs often appointed their heirs as co-rulers before their own death. This is one of the main obstacles when reconstructing the chronology of ancient Egypt. According to [radiocarbon dating](/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating), the dynasty certainly began between the years 2000 and 1950 BC, perhaps around 1980 BC. The Turin King List gives the sum of years as 213, but does not take co-regencies into account.

## Second Intermediate period (c. 1800–1550 BC)

Main article: [Second Intermediate Period of Egypt](/wiki/Second_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt)

Reconstructing the chronology of the [Second Intermediate period](/wiki/Second_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt) is even more difficult than the [First](/wiki/First_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt), as there are very few and contradictory records. The [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List) is the only one to include rulers of this period, but it is badly damaged after the Twelfth Dynasty. This period is best known for the rule of the [Fifteenth Dynasty](/wiki/Fifteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) established by the [Hyksos](/wiki/Hyksos) people of West Asia. It was a time of political rather than social upheavals.

At some point during the weak [Thirteenth Dynasty](/wiki/Thirteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt), the provincial ruling family in [Xois](/wiki/Xois), located in the [Nile Delta](/wiki/Nile_Delta), broke away to form [Fourteenth Dynasty](/wiki/Fourteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). Around the same time, the Asiatic people known as the [Hyksos](/wiki/Hyksos) established themselves around the Delta, and soon after took control of [Avaris](/wiki/Avaris) (modern [Tell el-Dab'a](/wiki/Tell_el-Dab%27a)), beginning [Fifteenth Dynasty](/wiki/Fifteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). The Hyksos took over most of Lower Egypt and ended the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Dynasties. They were also known as the "Great Hyksos" because of their dominance. The power vacuum in Upper Egypt enabled the [Sixteenth Dynasty](/wiki/Sixteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) to declare independence in [Thebes](/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt), only to be overrun by the Hyksos shortly thereafter. Subsequently, as the Hyksos withdrew from Upper Egypt, a new native Egyptian house in Thebes set itself up as [Seventeenth](/wiki/Seventeenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). This dynasty eventually drove the Hyksos back into Asia, starting the New Kingdom.

### Thirteenth Dynasty

Main article: [Thirteenth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Thirteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

As opposed to the previous dynasty, there are little to no historical records regarding this dynasty, with only a few kings leaving archeological records. The dynasty possibly originates from [Canaan](/wiki/Canaan), as some rulers ([Sekhemkare](/wiki/Sekhemkare), [Hotepibre](/wiki/Hotepibre)) are called "Asiatics". [Merneferre Ay](/wiki/Merneferre_Ay), while not the final pharaoh, was the last king attested in Lower and Upper Egypt, later pharaohs are only attested in the south. The earlier part of the dynasty until Ay is sometimes considered part of the Middle kingdom, but more often the whole dynasty is considered part of the Second Intermediate period.

Manetho records "60 kings of Diospolis for 453 years". Notably, the number of kings appears to be somewhat correct, as the [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List) records a minimum of **51 kings**, which Ryholt rises to **57**. However, only the first half of the names survives, and the reign lengths are also damaged. Manetho's total of 453 years is sometimes emended as 153, but there is no definitive proof of this. According to radiocarbon dating, the Thirteenth Dynasty began between the years 1800 and 1740 BC. According to Hornung, [Ay](/wiki/Merneferre_Ay)'s reign ended around 1650 BC, at least 100 years after the beginning of the dynasty.

The succession is based on the reconstruction of [Kim Ryholt](/wiki/Kim_Ryholt). Almost all kings reigned for a very short time, for about 1 to 3 years each. Ryholt also suggests that the division by Manetho is based not on a familiar break, but because of the rise of the [Dynasty XIV](/wiki/Fourteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). The exact succession of rulers is highly disputed.

**Proposed dates**: c. 1794/3–1648/5 (146/8 years), c. 1793/73–after 1650/40+ (120+ years), c. 1760–1630 BC (130 years), c. 1803–1649 BC (154 years)

### Fourteenth Dynasty (Xois)

Main article: [Fourteenth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Fourteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

Drawing of an ancient Egyptian stele depicting [Merdjefare](/wiki/Merdjefare), the only king of the 14th dynasty to leave a surviving portrait.

[Manetho](/wiki/Manetho) records "76 kings of [Xois](/wiki/Xois) for 184 years". Ryholt argues that these kings ruled from [Avaris](/wiki/Avaris), but there is no strong evidence for this. Almost nothing is known about the dynasty, and all its rulers appear to have had very short reigns. The following is the royal succession according to the [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List). The document records a minimum of **51 kings** (Ryholt raises the number to **56**), many of whom ruled alongside Dynasties XIII and XV. They were also of Near Eastern origin.

Only four rulers are contemporarily attested: [Nehesy](/wiki/Nehesy), [Nebsenre](/wiki/Nebsenre), [Merdjefare](/wiki/Merdjefare), and [Sekheperenre](/wiki/Sekheperenre). Beckerath, who mostly agrees with Ryholt, records 16 additional names of disputed identity. This dynasty notably includes some of the shortest recorded reigns; the shortest being that of [Sekheperenre](/wiki/Sekheperenre) (61–65 days), which is also the shortest reign of a native pharaoh.

**Proposed dates**: unknown–c. 1645, c. 1773–1650 (123 years), c. 1805–1649 (156 years)

### Fifteenth Dynasty (Hyksos)

Main articles: [Fifteenth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Fifteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and [Hyksos](/wiki/Hyksos)

The "[Hyksos](/wiki/Hyksos)", literally meaning "rulers of foreign lands", were people who emerged from the [Fertile Crescent](/wiki/Fertile_Crescent) and conquered much of [Lower Egypt](/wiki/Lower_Egypt), as well as parts of [Middle Egypt](/wiki/Middle_Egypt). They were excluded from contemporary Egyptian sources because they were perceived as foreign usurpers. The [Turin King List](/wiki/Turin_King_List) records **6 rulers of foreign lands** ruling for roughly **150 years**, but the list is very damaged and much of the information is now lost. [Manetho](/wiki/Manetho), as quoted by [Josephus](/wiki/Josephus), reported six "shepherd kings from [Phoenicia](/wiki/Phoenicia)" ruling for 254 years (the term "shepherd kings" appears to be the result of pronunciation changes). The following is the tentative reconstruction of [Kim Ryholt](/wiki/Kim_Ryholt), based on attested names bearing the title of "Hyksos".

The versions of Manetho differ significantly for this period. Eusebius notably places the rulers of Africanus' [Dynasty XV](/wiki/Dynasty_XV) to [Dynasty XVII](/wiki/Dynasty_XVII), likely to accommodate his Biblical chronology. According to Josephus, the Hyksos domination, that is, the period between [Dynasties XV](/wiki/Fifteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) until the end of [Dynasty XVII](/wiki/Dynasty_XVII), lasted 511 years, while Africanus attributes 518 years to [Dynasty XVI](/wiki/Dynasty_XVI) alone. This is likely the summation of all three dynasties, because Josephus' sum of reigns for the Hyksos dynasty is actually 253 years and 10 months. This figure must be a century off, as the Turin King List only notes about 150 years.

### Seventeenth Dynasty (Thebes)

Main article: [Seventeenth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Seventeenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

The succession of the last three kings of the Seventeenth Dynasty is attested in the [Tomb of Khabekhnet](/wiki/TT2) and Table of Qenhirkhopshef, but the placement of the previous ruler is problematic. The [Karnak King List](/wiki/Karnak_King_List) includes the throne names of [Sekhemre Wahkhau Rahotep](/wiki/Sekhemre_Wahkhau_Rahotep), [Sobekemsaf I](/wiki/Sobekemsaf_I), and [Nubkheperre Intef](/wiki/Nubkheperre_Intef), but they are not in order. These Theban kings ruled an area from [Elephantine](/wiki/Elephantine) to [Abydos](/wiki/Abydos,_Egypt). They likely began as minor vassals of the Hyksos, as their origins are very obscure. The last kings fought and defeated the Hyksos, and established the [New Kingdom of Egypt](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt).

Kyholt identified 9 kings of this line, although the exact order is uncertain. Beckerath included 6 additional names belonging to kings that Ryholt placed on the previous dynasties ([Sekhemre Sementawy Djehuty](/wiki/Sekhemre_Sementawy_Djehuty), [Seankhenre Mentuhotepi](/wiki/Seankhenre_Mentuhotepi), [Nebiryraw I](/wiki/Nebiryraw_I), [Nebiryraw II](/wiki/Nebiryraw_II), [Semenre](/wiki/Semenre), and [Bebiankh](/wiki/Bebiankh)). Hornung postulates that the rulers between Dynasties XIII and XVIII ruled for roughly a century.

**Proposed dates**: c. 1645–1550 BC (95 years), c. 1640–1550 BC (90 years), c. 1580–1550 BC (30 years)

## New Kingdom (c. 1550–1075 BC)

Main article: [New Kingdom of Egypt](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt)

The New Kingdom is considered the greatest period in Egyptian history. It began with the expulsion of the [Hyksos](/wiki/Hyksos), and it saw Egypt's greatest territorial extent. The Egyptians expanded far into [Nubia](/wiki/Nubia) in the south, and held wide territories in the [Near East](/wiki/Near_East). Three of the best-known pharaohs originate from this period. These are [Akhenaten](/wiki/Akhenaten), whose exclusive worship of the [Aten](/wiki/Aten) is often interpreted as the first instance of [monotheism](/wiki/Monotheism), [Tutankhamun](/wiki/Tutankhamun), known for the discovery of his nearly intact tomb, and [Ramesses II the Great](/wiki/Ramesses_II), who attempted to recover the territories in the [Levant](/wiki/Levant) that had been held in the Eighteenth Dynasty. His reconquest led to the [Battle of Kadesh](/wiki/Battle_of_Kadesh), where he led the Egyptian armies against the Hittite king [Muwatalli II](/wiki/Muwatalli_II). The ensuing [Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty](/wiki/Egyptian%E2%80%93Hittite_peace_treaty) is the oldest of its kind.

The accession of [Ahmose I](/wiki/Ahmose_I) and the beginning of the New Kingdom can be securely dated to c. 1550 BC (± 10 years), which is also the date adopted by most scholars. [Radiocarbon studies](/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating) have yielded two intervals for Ahmose's date of accession: 1570–1544 BC and 1564–1528 BC. While most scholars use Shaw's figure of c. 1550 BC, Hornung argues in favor of an slightly later date, c. 1540 BC. Many of the reign lengths given by [Manetho](/wiki/Manetho), as preserved by [Josephus](/wiki/Josephus), appear to be confirmed by contemporary sources. The years are mostly derived only from the highest known year of each pharaoh, meaning that there is still some uncertainty on their exactitude.

### Eighteenth Dynasty

Main article: [Eighteenth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Eighteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

The Eighteenth dynasty lasted roughly 250 years and was the longest-reigning native [Egyptian dynasty](/wiki/Dynasties_of_ancient_Egypt). It started as a continuation of the Seventeen dynasty, with [Ahmose I](/wiki/Ahmose_I) being the brother of [Kamose](/wiki/Kamose). However, the death of [Amenhotep I](/wiki/Amenhotep_I) without known children ended the male line, bringing the common-born [Thutmose I](/wiki/Thutmose_I) to the throne. Thutmose probably married into the royal family by marrying two presumed daughters of Ahmose I, [Ahmose](/wiki/Ahmose_(queen)) and [Mutnofret](/wiki/Mutnofret). If the identification of Mutnofret as a child of Ahmose is correct, that would mean that–via her issue–the family composed of members of the Seventeenth and Eighteenth dynasties ruled Egypt for nearly three centuries, until [Tutankhamun](/wiki/Tutankhamun)'s death.

**Proposed dates**: 1550–1307 BC (243 years), 1550–1295 BC (255 years), 1550–1292 BC (258 years), 1539–1292 BC (247 years). Regnal years may still be off by one or two years at most.

Despite being the best-documented period in Egypt's history, almost all of Manetho's names and reign lengths were mixed. His sources were likely already corrupted due to the *[damnatio memoriae](/wiki/Damnatio_memoriae)* imposed over the [Amarna rulers](/wiki/Amarna_Period), and co-regency may have caused additional confusion. The period c. 1540–1292 BC spans 248 years; the sum of estimates reigns amounts to a minimum of 245 years, which closely matches Josephus' sum of 246 years.

## Third Intermediate period (c. 1075–664 BC)

Main article: [Third Intermediate Period of Egypt](/wiki/Third_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt)

Following the [Late Bronze Age collapse](/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse), the Egyptian Empire collapsed and fragmented once more. The rulers of the [Twenty-first Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-first_Dynasty_of_Egypt) claimed to rule over all of Egypt, but in practice their influence was limited to Lower Egypt, with the [High Priests of Amun](/wiki/High_Priest_of_Amun) ruling over Upper Egypt. The [Twenty-second Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-second_Dynasty_of_Egypt) maintained control over Egypt for a few decades, but their power soon deteriorated due to infighting. This led to the rise of several competing royal lines in [Thebes](/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt), [Heracleopolis](/wiki/Heracleopolis_Magna), [Hermopolis](/wiki/Hermopolis), [Leontopolis](/wiki/Leontopolis), and [Tanis](/wiki/Tanis), which are collectively known as the [Twenty-third Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-third_Dynasty_of_Egypt). Another short-lived line, from [Sais](/wiki/Sais,_Egypt), is referred to as the [Twenty-fourth Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-fourth_Dynasty).

The fragmentation of Egypt came to an end with the arrival of the [Nubian](/wiki/Nubians) [kings of Kush](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush), who conquered Egypt c. 745 BC . This was the first time all of Egypt was ruled by foreigners (the [Hyksos](/wiki/Hyksos) mostly ruled in Lower Upper), although the Nubians were highly Egyptianized and considered themselves legitimate pharaohs, even [reviving the tradition of pyramid building](/wiki/Nubian_pyramids) (the [last pyramid](/wiki/Pyramid_of_Ahmose) was built by [Ahmose I](/wiki/Ahmose_I) nearly 800 years before).

While not as chaotic as the previous two intermediate periods, the lack of official king lists makes it difficult to reconstruct the exact succession of rulers. The only surviving king list is that of [Manetho](/wiki/Manetho), which is likely based on a biased source dated to the Kushite period. Note that the sum of items does not match the stated total of years.

#### High Priests of Amun

The [High Priests of Amun](/wiki/High_Priests_of_Amun) at [Thebes](/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt) were the *de facto* rulers of [Upper Egypt](/wiki/Upper_Egypt) during the [Twenty-first dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-first_Dynasty_of_Egypt), writing their names in [cartouches](/wiki/Cartouche), being buried in royal tombs, and passing their office to their sons. They likely did not consider themselves actual pharaohs, but their use of royal titles implied an equivalent level of authority. Dates are taken from Payraudeau (2020), which may not perfectly align with Hornung's. There is insufficient evidence to reconstruct a reliable chronology of these rulers.

### Twenty-second Dynasty (Libyan)

Main article: [Twenty-second Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Twenty-second_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

The succession of kings of this dynasty is highly disputed; the following reconstruction is mostly based on Hornung, but dates follow [Frédéric Payraudeau](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric_Payraudeau) (2020). Other scholars date the dynasty c. 945–715 BC, after Shaw. Despite the political turmoil of this period, this dynasty managed to last for roughly 200 years, being exceeded only by the [Eighteenth](/wiki/Eighteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and the [Ptolemaic](/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty) dynasties.

### Twenty-third Dynasty (Libyan)

Main article: [Twenty-third Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Twenty-third_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

The designation "[Twenty-Third Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-third_Dynasty_of_Egypt)" is applied variously by scholars to branches of the Twenty-Second Dynasty ruling in mostly Upper Egypt (at least intermittently at [Thebes](/wiki/Thebes,_Egypt)), to various local rulers based at [Heracleopolis Magna](/wiki/Heracleopolis_Magna), [Hermopolis](/wiki/Hermopolis), [Leontopolis](/wiki/Leontopolis), and to a line of kings who succeeded the Twenty-Second Dynasty at [Tanis](/wiki/Tanis), all of [Libyan](/wiki/Ancient_Libya) origin. The following is the most recent reconstruction by Frédéric Payraudeau (2020). There are roughly 20 kings attested around this period, but this may vary significantly depending on the author (compare the lists given above). See also the discussion of David Aston and [Karl Jansen-Winkeln](/wiki/Karl_Jansen-Winkeln).

### Twenty-fourth Dynasty (1st Saite)

Main article: [Twenty-fourth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Twenty-fourth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

### Twenty-fifth Dynasty (Nubian)

Main article: [Twenty-fifth Dynasty of Egypt](/wiki/Twenty-fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)

Following the collapse of the New Kingdom, the [Nubians](/wiki/Nubia), a people located around Southern Egypt, founded the [Kingdom of Kush](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush). One of its rulers, [Piye](/wiki/Piye), invaded Lower Egypt and took the title of pharaoh, although the Nubians already controlled Upper Egypt in the early years of his reign. [Kashta](/wiki/Kashta), Piye's father, is sometimes also listed as part of the dynasty. The capital during this dynasty was [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt).

Manetho claims that the 25th Dynasty ruled for 40 years, but this is about half of its actual duration. This dynasty is also mentioned by [Herodotus](/wiki/Herodotus), who records "eighteen Ethiopian kings", and by [Diodorus Siculus](/wiki/Diodorus_Siculus), who writes that "four Ethiopians held the throne, not consecutively but with intervals between, for a little less than thirty-six years in all.":100:44 The accession of [Taharqa](/wiki/Taharqa) can be securely dated to **690 BC**, marking the first certain year in Egyptian history according to Hornung. Dates follow Payraudeau.

**Proposed dates**: c. 753–655 BC (98 years), c. 747–656 BC (91 years), c. 746–655 BC (91 years);

## Late period (664–332 BC)

Main article: [Late Period of Egypt](/wiki/Late_Period_of_Egypt)

The [Twenty-fifth Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) ended after the [Assyrian conquest of Egypt](/wiki/Assyrian_conquest_of_Egypt). In 671 BC, [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt), the capital of the first dynasties, was plundered by [Esarhaddon](/wiki/Esarhaddon), and the [sack of Thebes](/wiki/Sack_of_Thebes) followed a few years later. Despite this, a new dynasty, the [Twenty-sixth](/wiki/Twenty-sixth_Dynasty_of_Egypt), managed to restore Egypt's influence in the region and repelled the [Assyrians](/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire) and [Babylonians](/wiki/Babylonia). Still, they all eventually fell to the [Achaemenid Persians](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire), who became the [Twenty-seventh Dynasty](/wiki/Twenty-seventh_Dynasty) in 525 BC. Persian rule was interrupted by three short-lived dynasties (the [28th](/wiki/Twenty-eighth_Dynasty_of_Egypt), [29th](/wiki/Twenty-ninth_Dynasty_of_Egypt), and [30th](/wiki/Thirtieth_Dynasty_of_Egypt)) ruling for six decades, between 404 and 342 BC. The first one was founded by [Amyrtaeus](/wiki/Amyrtaeus), and the last one ended with [Nectanebo II](/wiki/Nectanebo_II), the last native ruler of Egypt until the 20th century.

The chronology starting from [Taharqa](/wiki/Taharqa), the penultimate Nubian king, can be fixed thanks to the [Canon of Kings](/wiki/Canon_of_Kings) of [Ptolemy](/wiki/Ptolemy), which is itself based on ancient [Babylonian](/wiki/Babylon) sources and fits perfectly with Manetho's reign lengths. The only period with disputed chronology is the restored native Egyptian rule between the 27th and 31st Dynasties; all other dates are fixed.

## Hellenistic period (332–30 BC)

Main article: [Hellenistic period](/wiki/Hellenistic_period)

The [Macedonian Greeks](/wiki/Ancient_Macedonians) under [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) ushered in the [Hellenistic period](/wiki/Hellenistic_period) with his conquest of Persia and Egypt in 332 BC, creating one of the [largest empires in history](/wiki/List_of_largest_empires). However, this empire quickly fragmented after his sudden death in 323 BC, which led to the chaotic [Wars of the Diadochi](/wiki/Wars_of_the_Diadochi). [Ptolemy](/wiki/Ptolemy_I_Soter), one of Alexander's generals, claimed the pharaonic titles and established the [Ptolemaic Kingdom](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom), the last and longest-reigning dynasty of Egypt. The former Persian territories were absorbed into the Hellenic [Seleucid Empire](/wiki/Seleucid_Empire), which bordered Egypt to the East. Both states would eventually fall to the [Roman Republic](/wiki/Roman_Republic) over the course of the 1st century BC.

### Thirty-third Dynasty (Ptolemaic)

Main articles: [Ptolemaic dynasty](/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty) and [Ptolemaic Kingdom](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom)

The second [Hellenistic](/wiki/Hellenistic_period) dynasty, the [Ptolemaic](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom), ruled Egypt for nearly 275 years, from 305/4 BC to 30 BC. It was the longest-reigning dynasty of Egypt, followed closely by the [Eighteenth](/wiki/Eighteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt). The most famous member of this dynasty was Cleopatra VII, better known simply as [Cleopatra](/wiki/Cleopatra), who was successively the lover of [Julius Caesar](/wiki/Julius_Caesar) and, after his death, of [Mark Antony](/wiki/Mark_Antony), having children with both of them. She strove to create a dynastic and political union with Rome, but the assassination of Caesar and the defeat of Antony doomed their plans. Egypt was ultimately [conquered by the Romans](/wiki/War_of_Actium) under Octavian, who would become the [Roman emperor](/wiki/Roman_emperor) [Augustus](/wiki/Augustus).
All members of the Ptolemaic dynasty shared the same names, but adopted one or several Greek [epithets](/wiki/Epithet) on accession. Regnal numbers are only a modern convention.

## Roman period

Main articles: [Roman pharaoh](/wiki/Roman_pharaoh) and [Roman Egypt](/wiki/Roman_Egypt)

Egyptian relief depicting the Roman emperor [Trajan](/wiki/Trajan) (right, r.  98–117 AD) in full pharaonic style.

Egypt became a client kingdom of [Rome](/wiki/Ancient_Rome) in the reign of [Ptolemy X Alexander I](/wiki/Ptolemy_X_Alexander_I) (r.  107–88 BC) but it was not until the [death of Cleopatra](/wiki/Death_of_Cleopatra) (and defeat of [Mark Antony](/wiki/Mark_Antony)) in 30 BC, that Egypt became a [Roman province](/wiki/Roman_province). The [Roman emperors](/wiki/Roman_emperor) were accorded the title of pharaoh by the Egyptians, but the title was not acknowledged outside the province. The last Roman emperor known from a pharaonic titulary is [Maximinus Daza](/wiki/Maximinus_Daza) (r.  310–313 AD). Royal cartouches are attested as late as 340 AD, but the pagan Egyptians chose to posthumously use cartouches of [Diocletian](/wiki/Diocletian) (r.  284–305 AD) rather than acknowledging the later Christian emperors. This was around the same time that the knowledge and understanding of [hieroglyphs](/wiki/Egyptian_hieroglyphs) began to be forgotten; the last known inscription of its kind, known as the [Graffito of Esmet-Akhom](/wiki/Graffito_of_Esmet-Akhom), dates to the year 394.

Roman rule in Egypt continued almost uninterrupted until the 7th century, when the province fell to the [Arab conquest](/wiki/Arab_conquest_of_Egypt), whereafter Egypt was ruled by the successive [caliphates](/wiki/Caliphate).
