The **68th Academy Awards** ceremony, organized by the [Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences](/wiki/Academy_of_Motion_Picture_Arts_and_Sciences) (AMPAS), honored the best [films of 1995](/wiki/1995_in_film) in the United States and took place on March 25, 1996, at the [Dorothy Chandler Pavilion](/wiki/Dorothy_Chandler_Pavilion) in Los Angeles beginning at 6:00 p.m. [PST](/wiki/Pacific_Time_Zone) / 9:00 p.m. [EST](/wiki/Eastern_Time_Zone). During the ceremony, AMPAS presented [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Awards) (commonly referred to as *Oscars*) in 24 categories. The ceremony, televised in the United States by [ABC](/wiki/American_Broadcasting_Company), was produced by [David Salzman](/wiki/David_Salzman) and [Quincy Jones](/wiki/Quincy_Jones) and directed by [Jeff Margolis](/wiki/Jeff_Margolis). Actress [Whoopi Goldberg](/wiki/Whoopi_Goldberg) hosted the show for the second time, having previously presided over the [66th ceremony](/wiki/66th_Academy_Awards) in 1994. Three weeks earlier, in a ceremony held at the [Regent Beverly Wilshire Hotel](/wiki/Beverly_Wilshire_Hotel) in [Beverly Hills, California](/wiki/Beverly_Hills,_California), on March 2, the [Academy Awards for Technical Achievement](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Technical_Achievement) were presented by host [Richard Dreyfuss](/wiki/Richard_Dreyfuss).

*[Braveheart](/wiki/Braveheart)* won five awards, including [Best Picture](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Picture). Other winners included *[Apollo 13](/wiki/Apollo_13_(film))*, *[Pocahontas](/wiki/Pocahontas_(1995_film))*, *[Restoration](/wiki/Restoration_(1995_film))*, and *[The Usual Suspects](/wiki/The_Usual_Suspects)* with two awards and *[Anne Frank Remembered](/wiki/Anne_Frank_Remembered)*, *[Antonia's Line](/wiki/Antonia%27s_Line)*, *[Babe](/wiki/Babe_(film))*, *[A Close Shave](/wiki/A_Close_Shave)*, *[Dead Man Walking](/wiki/Dead_Man_Walking_(film))*, *[Leaving Las Vegas](/wiki/Leaving_Las_Vegas)*, *[Lieberman in Love](/wiki/Lieberman_in_Love)*, *[Mighty Aphrodite](/wiki/Mighty_Aphrodite)*, *[One Survivor Remembers](/wiki/One_Survivor_Remembers)*, *[Il Postino: The Postman](/wiki/Il_Postino:_The_Postman)*, and *[Sense and Sensibility](/wiki/Sense_and_Sensibility_(1995_film))* with one. The telecast garnered almost 45 million viewers in the United States.

## Winners and nominees

The nominees for the 68th Academy Awards were announced on February 13, 1996, at 5:38 a.m. [PST](/wiki/Pacific_Time_Zone) (13:38 [UTC](/wiki/Coordinated_Universal_Time)) at the [Samuel Goldwyn Theater](/wiki/Samuel_Goldwyn_Theater) by president of the academy, and the music producer [Quincy Jones](/wiki/Quincy_Jones). *[Braveheart](/wiki/Braveheart)* led all nominees with ten nominations; *[Apollo 13](/wiki/Apollo_13_(film))* came in second with nine.

The winners were announced during the awards ceremony on March 25, 1996. *Braveheart* was the ninth film to win Best Picture with no acting nominations. With her Best Supporting Actress win for *[Mighty Aphrodite](/wiki/Mighty_Aphrodite)*, Mira Sorvino became the second consecutive actress to win the aforementioned category for a performance in a film directed by [Woody Allen](/wiki/Woody_Allen). Best Adapted Screenplay winner [Emma Thompson](/wiki/Emma_Thompson) was the first person to win Oscars for both acting and screenwriting. She had previously won Best Actress for her performance in the 1992 film *[Howards End](/wiki/Howards_End_(film))*. This was the first year since the [42nd Academy Awards](/wiki/42nd_Academy_Awards)—and last to date—that none of the acting winners appeared in Best Picture nominees.

## Ceremony information

[Whoopi Goldberg](/wiki/Whoopi_Goldberg) hosted the 68th Academy Awards.

As a result of the negative reception of [David Letterman](/wiki/David_Letterman)'s stint as host from the [preceding year's ceremony](/wiki/67th_Academy_Awards), veteran film and television director [Gil Cates](/wiki/Gil_Cates) declined to helm the upcoming festivities. In November 1995, AMPAS recruited music producer and [Jean Hersholt Humanitarian Award](/wiki/Jean_Hersholt_Humanitarian_Award) recipient [Quincy Jones](/wiki/Quincy_Jones) as producer of the 1996 ceremony. Jones immediately selected actress and comedian [Whoopi Goldberg](/wiki/Whoopi_Goldberg) to host the ceremony. In an interview with *[Los Angeles Times](/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)* writer Susan King, Jones explained the decision to hire Goldberg saying, "She has all the qualifications to move on a dime, to carry the elegance and the dignity of the show and is very funny. She understands the street. She has everything."

One segment that was staged during the ceremony was an elaborate fashion show showcasing the nominees for [Best Costume Design](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Costume_Design). Produced by fashion photographer [Matthew Rolston](/wiki/Matthew_Rolston), the production featured models such as [Cameron Alborzian](/wiki/Cameron_Alborzian), [Tyson Beckford](/wiki/Tyson_Beckford), [Tyra Banks](/wiki/Tyra_Banks), [Marcus Schenkenberg](/wiki/Marcus_Schenkenberg) and [Joel West](/wiki/Joel_West) sporting various costumes from the five films nominated in the category. Initially, actor [Jack Nicholson](/wiki/Jack_Nicholson) was approached to introduce the segment along with models [Naomi Campbell](/wiki/Naomi_Campbell) and [Claudia Schiffer](/wiki/Claudia_Schiffer). However, actor [Pierce Brosnan](/wiki/Pierce_Brosnan) accepted the role of presenter of the segment and award after Nicholson declined those respective duties.

Several other people and elements were also involved with the production of the ceremony. Jeff Margolis served as director for the program. Actress and talk show host [Oprah Winfrey](/wiki/Oprah_Winfrey) interviewed several nominees and other attendees during a seven-minute red carpet arrival segment shown at the beginning of the telecast. Musician and saxophonist [Tom Scott](/wiki/Tom_Scott_(saxophonist)) served as musical director for the ceremony. Choreographer [Jamie King](/wiki/Jamie_King) supervised the performances of the Best Song nominees and two dance numbers. Babe, the pig from the [eponymous film](/wiki/Babe_(film)), and [Miss Piggy](/wiki/Miss_Piggy) participated in a comedy sketch during the proceedings. Actor [Christopher Reeve](/wiki/Christopher_Reeve), who was paralyzed in a horse riding accident nearly a year earlier, made a surprise appearance on the telecast urging filmmakers to make movies that face the world's most important issues head-on.

### Rainbow Coalition protest

Several days before the ceremony, activist group [Rainbow Coalition](/wiki/Rainbow/PUSH), led by Reverend [Jesse Jackson](/wiki/Jesse_Jackson), planned a protest regarding [African Americans](/wiki/African_Americans) and other [racial minorities](/wiki/Racial_minorities) in the film industry. The group was voicing its objections to unflattering portrayals of minorities in film and television and the fact that minorities were underemployed in the entertainment industry. Jackson further pointed out the disparity in racial minorities in Hollywood by noting that Best Live Action Short Film nominee [Dianne Houston](/wiki/Dianne_Houston) was the only African American nominated that year. Although the group initially planned to demonstrate outside the [Dorothy Chandler Pavilion](/wiki/Dorothy_Chandler_Pavilion), an agreement between Jackson and producer Jones caused the protest to be moved adjacent to the broadcast facilities of the local [ABC](/wiki/American_Broadcasting_Company) affiliate [KABC-TV](/wiki/KABC-TV). Nevertheless, Jones remarked that the Academy Awards were not the appropriate venue for such protest declaring "Why should the movie business be different from anything else in America? It's a problem that permeates everything in the country. Every facet of America discriminates."

### Division of Best Original Score category

Beginning with this ceremony, the AMPAS music branch divided the category of [Best Original Score](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Original_Score) into two categories: Best Dramatic Score and Best Musical or Comedy Score. This was seen as a response to the dominance of [Walt Disney Feature Animation](/wiki/Walt_Disney_Feature_Animation) films in the Original Score and Original Song categories in recent years. Four years later, the two scoring categories were merged back into one category.

### Christopher Reeve appearance

At the ceremony, [Christopher Reeve](/wiki/Christopher_Reeve) presented a montage of films which tackled social issues. His appearance was a surprise to the majority of those present and occurred less than a year after the horse-riding accident in which he was paralyzed. Reeve's appearance was kept secret in part so that if any physical issue came about, he could drop out quietly. He attended an early morning closed-door rehearsal, during which he vetoed the idea of using [John Williams](/wiki/John_Williams_(composer))' 1978 [Superman Theme](/wiki/Music_of_Superman) as entrance music. Reeve, along with Jones, had chosen the film clips used in the montage. Reeve received a two-minute standing ovation during his appearance.

### Box office performance of nominees

At the time of the nominations announcement on February 13, the combined gross of the five Best Picture nominees at the US box office was $333 million, with an average of $66.5 million per film. *Apollo 13* was the highest earner among the Best Picture nominees with $172 million in domestic box office receipts. The film was followed by *Braveheart* ($67 million), *Babe* ($58.2 million), *Sense and Sensibility* ($24.6 million) and finally *Il Postino: The Postman* ($10.7 million).

Of the top 50 grossing movies of the year, 47 nominations went to 14 films on the list. Only *Toy Story* (2nd), *Apollo 13* (3rd), *Braveheart* (23rd), *Babe* (29th), *12 Monkeys* (31st), *Casino* (38th) and *Mr. Holland's Opus* (39th) were nominated for directing, acting, screenwriting, or Best Picture. The other box office hits that earned nominations were *Batman Forever* (1st), *Pocahontas* (4th), *Seven* (9th), *Crimson Tide* (10th), *Waterworld* (12th), *The Bridges of Madison County* (21st), *The American President* (27th) and *Sabrina* (34th).

### Critical reviews

The show received a positive reception from most media publications. *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* film critic [Janet Maslin](/wiki/Janet_Maslin) raved, "Mr. Jones pointedly turned this year's ceremony into a showcase for Hollywood's new guard." She also praised host Goldberg's opening monologue, remarking that it "established the sharpness of this year's gag writing." *[People](/wiki/People_(magazine))* columnist Janice Min wrote that "the most egregious crime at the 68th Academy Awards on March 25 was–egad!–the relentless elegance and good taste that deprived viewers of genuine, Grade A snicker fodder. Television critic [Howard Rosenberg](/wiki/Howard_Rosenberg) of the *[Los Angeles Times](/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)* applauded Goldberg's performance, noting that her "confident performance [...] was symbolic of her whopping improvement as host over her showing on the [1994 Oscars](/wiki/66th_Academy_Awards)."

Some media outlets were more critical of the show. *[Chicago Tribune](/wiki/Chicago_Tribune)* television critic Steve Johnson lamented that Goldberg "settled into bland script reading that made one long for David Letterman's cranky unpredictability in the role last year." He also stated that the "Best Costume Design fashion show" was the silliest opening Oscar production number since [Rob Lowe](/wiki/Rob_Lowe) and [Snow White](/wiki/Snow_White_(Disney_character)) [sang "Proud Mary" in 1989](/wiki/61st_Academy_Awards#Opening_number). [Ken Tucker](/wiki/Ken_Tucker) of *[Entertainment Weekly](/wiki/Entertainment_Weekly)* bemoaned that the dominance of Best Picture winner *Braveheart* and the lack of fashion glamour "had the makings of a tiresome evening."

### Ratings and reception

The American telecast on ABC drew in an average of 44.81 million people over its length, which was a 9% decrease from the [previous year's ceremony](/wiki/67th_Academy_Awards). The show also garnered lower [Nielsen ratings](/wiki/Nielsen_ratings) compared to the previous ceremony, with 30.48% of households watching over a 48.88 share. It also earned a lower 18–49 demographic rating with an 18.76 rating over a 35.27 share among viewers in that demographic.

In July 1996, the ceremony presentation received seven nominations at the [48th Primetime Emmys](/wiki/48th_Primetime_Emmy_Awards). Two months later, the ceremony won one of those nominations for Greg Brunton's lighting design and direction during the telecast.
