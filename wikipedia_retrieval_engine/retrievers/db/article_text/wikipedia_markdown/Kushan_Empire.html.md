The **Kushan Empire** (c. 30–c. 375 CE) was a [syncretic](/wiki/Syncretism) empire formed by the [Yuezhi](/wiki/Yuezhi) in the [Bactrian](/wiki/Bactria) territories in the early 1st century. It spread to encompass much of what is now [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan), [India](/wiki/India), [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan), [Nepal](/wiki/Nepal), [Tajikistan](/wiki/Tajikistan) and [Uzbekistan](/wiki/Uzbekistan). Kushan territory in India went at least as far as [Saketa](/wiki/Saketa) and [Sarnath](/wiki/Sarnath), now near [Varanasi](/wiki/Varanasi_district) in [Uttar Pradesh](/wiki/Uttar_Pradesh), where inscriptions have been found dating to the era of the Kushan emperor [Kanishka the Great](/wiki/Kanishka_the_Great).

The Kushans were most probably one of five branches of the [Yuezhi](/wiki/Yuezhi) confederation, an [Indo-European](/wiki/Proto-Indo-Europeans) nomadic people of possible [Tocharian](/wiki/Tocharians) origin, who migrated from northwestern China (Xinjiang and Gansu) and settled in ancient Bactria. The founder of the dynasty, [Kujula Kadphises](/wiki/Kujula_Kadphises), followed Iranian and Greek cultural ideas and iconography after the [Greco-Bactrian](/wiki/Greco-Bactrian) tradition and was a follower of the [Shaivite](/wiki/Shaivism) sect of [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism). Many of the later Kushan kings after Kujula, were also patrons of Hinduism, including (but not limited to) [Vima Kadphises](/wiki/Vima_Kadphises) and [Vasudeva II](/wiki/Vasudeva_II). The Kushans in general were also great patrons of [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism), and, starting with Emperor Kanishka, they employed elements of [Zoroastrianism](/wiki/Zoroastrianism) in their pantheon. They played an important role in the spread of Buddhism to Central Asia and China, ushering in a period of relative peace for 200 years, sometimes described as "[Pax Kushana](/wiki/Pax_Kushana)".

The Kushans possibly used the [Greek language](/wiki/Greek_language) initially for administrative purposes but soon began to use the Eastern Iranian [Bactrian language](/wiki/Bactrian_language). Kanishka sent his armies north of the [Karakoram](/wiki/Karakoram) mountains. A direct road from [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara) to China remained under Kushan control for more than a century, encouraged travel across the Karakoram, and facilitated the [spread of Mahayana Buddhism](/wiki/Silk_Road_transmission_of_Buddhism) to China. The Kushan dynasty had diplomatic contacts with the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire), [Sasanian Persia](/wiki/Sasanian_Empire), the [Aksumite Empire](/wiki/Aksumite_Empire), and the [Han dynasty](/wiki/Han_dynasty) of China. The Kushan Empire was at the center of trade relations between the Roman Empire and China: according to [Alain Daniélou](/wiki/Alain_Dani%C3%A9lou), "for a time, the Kushana Empire was the centerpoint of the major civilizations". While much philosophy, art, and science was created within its borders, the only textual record of the empire's history today comes from inscriptions and accounts in other languages, particularly Chinese.

The Kushan Empire fragmented into semi-independent kingdoms in the 3rd century AD, which fell to the Sasanians invading from the west and establishing the [Kushano-Sasanian Kingdom](/wiki/Kushano-Sasanian_Kingdom) in the areas of [Sogdiana](/wiki/Sogdiana), Bactria, and Gandhara. In the 4th century, the [Guptas](/wiki/Gupta_Empire), another Indian dynasty, also pressed from the east. The last of the Kushan and Kushano-Sasanian kingdoms were eventually overwhelmed by invaders from the north, known as the [Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites), and later the [Hephthalites](/wiki/Hephthalites).

## Origins

[Yuezhi](/wiki/Yuezhi) nobleman and priest over a fire altar. [Noin-Ula](/wiki/Noin-Ula).

Chinese sources describe the *Guìshuāng* ([貴霜](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E8%B2%B4%E9%9C%9C), [Old Chinese](/wiki/Old_Chinese): *\*kuj-s [s]raŋ*), *i.e.* the Kushans, as one of the five aristocratic tribes of the [Yuezhi](/wiki/Yuezhi). Many scholars believe that the Yuezhi were a people of [Indo-European](/wiki/Proto-Indo-Europeans) origin. A specifically [Tocharian](/wiki/Tocharians) origin of the Yuezhi is often suggested. An [Iranian](/wiki/Iranian_peoples), specifically [Saka](/wiki/Saka) origin, has also been suggested by some scholars.

The Yuezhi were described in the *[Records of the Great Historian](/wiki/Records_of_the_Great_Historian)* and the *[Book of Han](/wiki/Book_of_Han)* as living in the grasslands of eastern Xinjiang and northwestern part of [Gansu](/wiki/Gansu), in the northwest of modern-day China, until their King was beheaded by the [Xiongnu](/wiki/Xiongnu) (匈奴) who were also at war with China, which eventually forced them to migrate west in 176–160 BC. The five tribes constituting the Yuezhi are known in Chinese history as *Xiūmì* (休密), *Guìshuāng* (貴霜), *Shuāngmǐ* (雙靡), *Xìdùn* (肸頓), and *Dūmì* (都密).

The [ethnonym](/wiki/Ethnonym) "KO[Ϸ](/wiki/Sho_(letter))[Ϸ](/wiki/Sho_(letter))ANO" (*Koshshano*, "Kushan") in [Greek alphabet](/wiki/Greek_alphabet) (with the addition of the letter [Ϸ](/wiki/Sho_(letter)), "*Sh*") on a coin of the first known Kushan ruler [Heraios](/wiki/Heraios) (1st century AD).

The Yuezhi reached the Hellenic kingdom of [Greco-Bactria](/wiki/Greco-Bactria) (in northern Afghanistan and Uzbekistan) around 135 BC. The displaced Greek dynasties resettled to the southeast in areas of the [Hindu Kush](/wiki/Hindu_Kush) (in present-day Afghanistan and Pakistan) and the [Indus](/wiki/Indus_river) basin (in present-day Pakistan and India), occupying the western part of the [Indo-Greek Kingdom](/wiki/Indo-Greeks).

In [South Asia](/wiki/South_Asia), Kushan emperors regularly used the dynastic name *ΚΟϷΑΝΟ* ("Koshano") on their coinage. Several inscriptions in [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit) in the [Brahmi script](/wiki/Brahmi_script), such as the [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura) inscription of the statue of [Vima Kadphises](/wiki/Vima_Kadphises), refer to the Kushan Emperor as   , *Ku-ṣā-ṇa* ("Kushana"). Some later Indian literary sources referred to the Kushans as *Turushka*, a name which in later Sanskrit sources was confused with [Turk](/wiki/Turkic_peoples), "probably due to the fact that [Tukharistan](/wiki/Tokharistan) passed into the hands of the [western Turks](/wiki/Western_Turkic_Khaganate) in the seventh century". According to [John M. Rosenfield](/wiki/John_M._Rosenfield), *Turushka*, *Tukhāra* or *Tukhāra* are variations of the word *Tokhari* in Indian writings. Yet, according to Wink, "nowadays no historian considers them to be Turkish-Mongoloid or "Hun", although there is no doubt about their Central-Asian origin."

Earlier theories regarding the link between the Tocharians and the Yuezhi were often based on linguistic evidence, oral traditions or historical sources but usually without exploring the archaeological evidence for these two ancient groups. However, genetic studies of the ancient remains of [Xinjiang](/wiki/Xinjiang) indicates that the Tocharians were not the Yuezhi. The Tocharians' paternal lineages were from East Eurasians, which differed from the Yuezhi. This suggests that the Tocharians and the Yuezhi people did not share the same origin. The [Yuezhis](/wiki/Yuezhis) were a unique [Central Asian](/wiki/Central_Asian) group with West Eurasian origin, dissimilar to the origins of the [Tocharians](/wiki/Tocharians). With the subsequent expansion of the Kushan Empire, the [Yuezhis](/wiki/Yuezhis) were integrated and assimilated, leading to their disappearance as a separately identifiable entity in historical sources. Probably due to the complicated history of [Bactria](/wiki/Bactria), may be the reasons to the misunderstandings of the relationship between the [Tocharians](/wiki/Tocharians) and the [Yuezhis](/wiki/Yuezhis). More evidence is in need to resolve the [Tocharian](/wiki/Tocharians)-[Yuezhi](/wiki/Yuezhi) relationship.

Some traces remain of the presence of the Kushans in the area of Bactria and [Sogdiana](/wiki/Sogdiana) in the 2nd-1st century BC, where they had displaced the [Sakas](/wiki/Sakas), who moved further south. Archaeological structures are known in [Takht-i Sangin](/wiki/Takht-i_Sangin), [Surkh Kotal](/wiki/Surkh_Kotal) (a monumental temple), and in the palace of [Khalchayan](/wiki/Khalchayan). On the ruins of ancient Hellenistic cities such as [Ai-Khanoum](/wiki/Ai-Khanoum), the Kushans are known to have built fortresses. Various sculptures and friezes from this period are known, representing horse-riding archers, and, significantly, men such as the [Kushan prince of Khalchayan](/wiki/File:Kalchayan_Prince_profile.jpg) with [artificially deformed skulls](/wiki/Artificial_cranial_deformation), a practice well attested in nomadic Central Asia. Some of the Khalchayan sculptural scenes are also thought to depict the Kushans fighting against the [Sakas](/wiki/Saka). In these portrayals, the Yuezhis are shown with a majestic demeanour, whereas the Sakas are typically represented with side-whiskers, and more or less grotesque facial expressions.

The Chinese first referred to these people as the Yuezhi and said they established the Kushan Empire, although the relationship between the Yuezhi and the Kushans is still unclear. [Ban Gu](/wiki/Ban_Gu)'s *[Book of Han](/wiki/Book_of_Han)* tells us the Kushans (Kuei-shuang) divided up Bactria in 128 BC. [Fan Ye](/wiki/Fan_Ye_(historian))'s *[Book of Later Han](/wiki/Book_of_Later_Han)* "relates how the chief of the Kushans, Ch'iu-shiu-ch'ueh (the Kujula Kadphises of coins), founded by means of the submission of the other Yueh-chih clans the Kushan Empire."

The earliest documented ruler, and the first one to proclaim himself as a Kushan ruler, was [Heraios](/wiki/Heraios). He calls himself a "[tyrant](/wiki/Tyrant)" in [Greek](/wiki/Greco-Bactria) on his coins, and also exhibits skull deformation. He may have been an ally of the Greeks, and he shared the same style of coinage. Heraios may have been the father of the first Kushan emperor Kujula Kadphises.

The Chinese *[Book of Later Han](/wiki/Book_of_Later_Han)* chronicles then gives an account of the formation of the Kushan empire based on a report made by the Chinese general [Ban Yong](/wiki/Ban_Yong) to the Chinese Emperor c. AD 125:

> More than a hundred years later [than the conquest of Bactria by the Yuezhi], the prince [*xihou*] of Guishuang ([Badakhshan](/wiki/Badakhshan)) established himself as king, and his dynasty was called that of the Guishuang (Kushan) King. He invaded Anxi (Indo-Parthia), and took the Gaofu ([Kabul](/wiki/Kabul)) region. He also defeated the whole of the kingdoms of Puda ([Paktiya](/wiki/Kapisi_(city))) and Jibin ([Kapisha](/wiki/Kapisi_(city)) and [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara)). Qiujiuque (Kujula Kadphises) was more than eighty years old when he died. His son, Yangaozhen [probably [Vema Tahk](/wiki/Vima_Takto) (tu) or, possibly, his brother [Sadaṣkaṇa](/wiki/Sada%E1%B9%A3ka%E1%B9%87a) ], became king in his place. He defeated [Tianzhu](/wiki/Tianzhu_(India)) [North-western India] and installed Generals to supervise and lead it. The Yuezhi then became extremely rich. All the kingdoms call [their king] the Guishuang [Kushan] king, but the Han call them by their original name, Da Yuezhi.

— *Book of Later Han*.

## Diverse cultural influences

In the 1st century BC, the *Guishuang* (Ch: 貴霜) gained prominence over the other Yuezhi tribes, and welded them into a tight confederation under commander Kujula Kadphises. The name *Guishuang* was adopted in the West and modified into *Kushan* to designate the confederation, although the Chinese continued to call them *Yuezhi*.

Gradually wresting control of the area from the [Scythian](/wiki/Indo-Scythians) tribes, the Kushans expanded south into the region traditionally known as [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara) (an area primarily in [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan)'s [Pothowar](/wiki/Pothohar_Plateau) and [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](/wiki/Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) region) and established twin capitals in [Kapisa](/wiki/Kapisi) (near modern Bagram) and [Pushkalavati](/wiki/Pushkalavati) (later [Charsadda](/wiki/Charsadda)).

[Greek alphabet](/wiki/Greek_alphabet) (narrow columns) with Kushan script (wide columns)

The Kushans adopted elements of the [Hellenistic](/wiki/Hellenistic_civilization) culture of Bactria. They adopted the [Greek alphabet](/wiki/Greek_alphabet) to suit their own language (with the additional development of the letter Þ "sh", as in "Kushan") and soon began minting coinage on the Greek model. On their coins they used Greek language legends combined with Pali legends (in the [Kharoshthi](/wiki/Kharoshthi) script), until the first few years of the reign of Kanishka. After the middle of Kanishka's reign, they used Kushan language legends (in an adapted Greek script), combined with legends in Greek (Greek script) and legends in Prakrit (Kharoshthi script).

Interestingly there is evidence for the collaboration between Greek populations and the Kushans in the 2nd century AD. Apparently the main architect of the Kushan temple at [Surkh Kotal](/wiki/Surkh_Kotal) was a Greek named Palamedes. A Greek inscription has been found which could be read as: ΔΙΑ ΠΑΛΑΜΕΔΟΥΣ, i.e. *dia Palamedous*, meaning "through or by Palamedes". This proves that Hellenistic populations still remained in Bactria up into the Kushan era, and also explains how the Greek alphabet could have been applied to the Bactrian language.

The Kushans "adopted many local beliefs and customs, including [Zoroastrianism](/wiki/Zoroastrianism) and the two rising religions in the region, the Greek cults and [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism)". From the time of [Vima Takto](/wiki/Vima_Takto), many Kushans started adopting aspects of [Buddhist](/wiki/Buddhism) culture, and like the Egyptians, they absorbed the strong remnants of the Greek culture of the Hellenistic Kingdoms, becoming at least partly [Hellenised](/wiki/Hellenise). The great Kushan emperor [Vima Kadphises](/wiki/Vima_Kadphises), father of [Kanishka](/wiki/Kanishka), embraced [Shaivism](/wiki/Shaivism), a sect of [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism), as surmised by coins minted during the period. The following Kushan emperors represented a wide variety of faiths including [Buddhism](/wiki/Mahayana), Zoroastrianism and [Hindu Shaivism](/wiki/Shaivism).

The rule of the Kushans linked the seagoing trade of the [Indian Ocean](/wiki/Indian_Ocean) with the commerce of the [Silk Road](/wiki/Silk_Road) through the long-civilized [Indus Valley](/wiki/Indus_Valley). At the height of the dynasty, the Kushans loosely ruled a territory that extended to the [Aral Sea](/wiki/Aral_Sea) through present-day [Uzbekistan](/wiki/Uzbekistan), [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan), [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan) and [northern India](/wiki/Northern_India).

The loose unity and comparative peace of such a vast expanse encouraged long-distance trade, brought Chinese silks to [Rome](/wiki/Rome), and created strings of flourishing urban centers.

The extent of Kushan control is notably documented in the [Rabatak inscription](/wiki/Rabatak_inscription). The northern expansion into the [Tarim Basin](/wiki/Tarim_Basin) is mainly suggested by coin finds and Chinese chronicles.

Rosenfield notes that archaeological evidence of a Kushan rule of long duration is present in an area stretching from Surkh Kotal, [Kapisa](/wiki/Kapisi), the summer capital of the Kushans, [Purushapura](/wiki/Purushapura) (modern Peshawar), the capital under Kanishka I, [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila), and [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura,_Uttar_Pradesh), the winter capital of the Kushans. The Kushans introduced for the first time a form of governance which consisted of *Kshatrapas* ([Brahmi](/wiki/Brahmi):, *Kṣatrapa*, "[Satraps](/wiki/Satrap)") and *Mahakshatrapa* ([Brahmi](/wiki/Brahmi):  , *Mahakṣatrapa*, "Great [Satraps](/wiki/Satrap)").

Other areas of probable rule include [Khwarezm](/wiki/Khwarezm) and its capital city of [Toprak-Kala](/wiki/Toprak-Kala), [Kausambi](/wiki/Kausambi) (excavations of [Allahabad University](/wiki/Allahabad_University)), [Sanchi](/wiki/Sanchi) and [Sarnath](/wiki/Sarnath) (inscriptions with names and dates of Kushan kings), [Malwa](/wiki/Malwa) and [Maharashtra](/wiki/Maharashtra), and [Odisha](/wiki/Odisha) (imitation of Kushan coins, and large Kushan hoards).

The [Rabatak inscription](/wiki/Rabatak_inscription), discovered in 1993, confirms the account of the Hou Hanshu, [Weilüe](/wiki/Weil%C3%BCe), and inscriptions dated early in the Kanishka era (incept probably AD 127), that large Kushan dominions expanded into the heartland of northern India in the early 2nd century AD. Lines 4 to 7 of the inscription describe the cities which were under the rule of Kanishka, among which six names are identifiable: [Ujjain](/wiki/Ujjain), [Kundina](/wiki/Kundina), [Saketa](/wiki/Saketa), [Kausambi](/wiki/Kausambi), [Pataliputra](/wiki/Pataliputra), and [Champa](/wiki/Champa,_Chhattisgarh) (although the text is not clear whether Champa was a possession of Kanishka or just beyond it). The Buddhist text *Śrīdharmapiṭakanidānasūtra*—known via a Chinese translation made in AD 472—refers to the conquest of Pataliputra by Kanishka. A 2nd century stone inscription by a Great Satrap named [Rupiamma](/wiki/Rupiamma) was discovered in [Pauni](/wiki/Pauni), south of the [Narmada river](/wiki/Narmada_river), suggesting that Kushan control extended this far south, although this could alternatively have been controlled by the [Western Satraps](/wiki/Western_Satraps).

Eastern reach as far as [Bengal](/wiki/Bengal): [Samatata](/wiki/Samatata) coinage of king Vira Jadamarah, in imitation of the [Kushan coinage](/wiki/Kushan_coinage) of [Kanishka I](/wiki/Kanishka_I). The text of the legend is a meaningless imitation. [Bengal](/wiki/Bengal), circa 2nd-3rd century AD.

In the East, as late as the 3rd century AD, decorated coins of Huvishka were dedicated at [Bodh Gaya](/wiki/Bodh_Gaya) together with other gold offerings under the "Enlightenment Throne" of the Buddha, suggesting direct Kushan influence in the area during that period. Coins of the Kushans are found in abundance as far as [Bengal](/wiki/Bengal), and the ancient Bengali state of [Samatata](/wiki/Samatata) issued coins copied from the coinage of Kanishka I, although probably only as a result of commercial influence. Coins in imitation of Kushan coinage have also been found abundantly in the eastern state of [Orissa](/wiki/Orissa).

In the West, the Kushan state covered the [Pārata state](/wiki/Paratarajas) of [Balochistan](/wiki/Balochistan_(Pakistan)), western [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan), [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan), [Kyrgyzstan](/wiki/Kyrgyzstan), [Tajikistan](/wiki/Tajikistan), [Uzbekistan](/wiki/Uzbekistan), and [Turkmenistan](/wiki/Turkmenistan). [Turkmenistan](/wiki/Turkmenistan) was known for the Kushan Buddhist city of [Merv](/wiki/Merv).

Northward, in the 1st century AD, the [Kujula Kadphises](/wiki/Kujula_Kadphises) sent an army to the [Tarim Basin](/wiki/Tarim_Basin) to support the city-state of [Kucha](/wiki/Kucha), which had been resisting the Chinese invasion of the region, but they retreated after minor encounters. In the 2nd century AD, the Kushans under Kanishka made various forays into the [Tarim Basin](/wiki/Tarim_Basin), where they had various contacts with the Chinese. Kanishka held areas of the [Tarim Basin](/wiki/Tarim_Basin) apparently corresponding to the ancient regions held by the [Yüeh-zhi](/wiki/Yuezhi), the possible ancestors of the Kushan. There was Kushan influence on coinage in [Kashgar](/wiki/Kashgar), [Yarkand](/wiki/Yarkent_County), and [Khotan](/wiki/Hotan). According to Chinese chronicles, the Kushans (referred to as [Da Yuezhi](/wiki/Da_Yuezhi) in Chinese sources) requested, but were denied, a [Han](/wiki/Han_Chinese) princess, even though they had sent presents to the Chinese court. In retaliation, they marched on Ban Chao in AD 90 with a force of 70,000 but were defeated by the smaller Chinese force. Chinese chronicles relate battles between the Kushans and the Chinese general [Ban Chao](/wiki/Ban_Chao). The Yuezhi retreated and paid tribute to the Chinese Empire. The regions of the Tarim Basin were all ultimately conquered by [Ban Chao](/wiki/Ban_Chao). Later, during the [Yuánchū](/wiki/Emperor_An_of_Han) period (AD 114–120), the Kushans sent a military force to install Chenpan, who had been a hostage among them, as king of [Kashgar](/wiki/Kashgar).

## History

Kushan rulers are recorded for a period of about three centuries, from circa 30 CE to circa 375 CE, until the invasions of the [Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites). They ruled around the same time as the [Western Satraps](/wiki/Western_Satraps), the [Satavahanas](/wiki/Satavahanas), and the first [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire) rulers.

### Kujula Kadphises (c. 25 – c. 85)

Main article: [Kujula Kadphises](/wiki/Kujula_Kadphises)

> ...the prince [elavoor] of Guishuang, named thilac [Kujula Kadphises], attacked and exterminated the four other xihou. He established himself as king, and his dynasty was called that of the Guishuang [Kushan] King. He invaded Anxi [Indo-Parthia] and took the Gaofu [Kabul] region. He also defeated the whole of the kingdoms of Puda [Paktiya] and Jibin [Kapisha and Gandhara]. Qiujiuque [Kujula Kadphises] was more than eighty years old when he died."

— [Hou Hanshu](/wiki/Book_of_the_Later_Han)

These conquests by [Kujula Kadphises](/wiki/Kujula_Kadphises) probably took place sometime between AD 45 and 60 and laid the basis for the Kushan Empire which was rapidly expanded by his descendants.

Kujula issued an extensive series of coins and fathered at least two sons, [Sadaṣkaṇa](/wiki/Sadashkana) (who is known from only two inscriptions, especially the Rabatak inscription, and apparently never ruled), and seemingly Vima Takto.

Kujula Kadphises was the great-grandfather of Kanishka.

### Vima Taktu or Sadashkana (c. 80 – c. 95)

Main article: [Vima Takto](/wiki/Vima_Takto)

[Vima Takto](/wiki/Vima_Takto) (Ancient Chinese: 閻膏珍 *Yangaozhen*) is mentioned in the Rabatak inscription (another son, Sadashkana, is mentioned in an inscription of Senavarman, the King of Odi). He was the predecessor of Vima Kadphises, and Kanishka I. He expanded the Kushan Empire into the northwest of South Asia. The Hou Hanshu says:

> "His son, Yangaozhen [probably Vema Tahk (tu) or, possibly, his brother Sadaṣkaṇa], became king in his place. He defeated Tianzhu [North-western India] and installed Generals to supervise and lead it. The Yuezhi then became extremely rich. All the kingdoms call [their king] the Guishuang [Kushan] king, but the Han call them by their original name, Da Yuezhi."

— Hou Hanshu

### Vima Kadphises (c. 95 – c. 127)

Main article: [Vima Kadphises](/wiki/Vima_Kadphises)

[Vima Kadphises](/wiki/Vima_Kadphises) (Kushan language: Οοημο Καδφισης) was a Kushan emperor from around AD 95–127, the son of Sadashkana and the grandson of Kujula Kadphises, and the father of Kanishka I, as detailed by the Rabatak inscription.

Vima Kadphises added to the Kushan territory by his conquests in Bactria. He issued an extensive series of coins and inscriptions. He issued gold coins in addition to the existing copper and silver coinage.

### Kanishka I (c. 127 – c. 150)

Main article: [Kanishka](/wiki/Kanishka)

Mathura statue of Kanishka

Statue of [Kanishka](/wiki/Kanishka) in long coat and boots, holding a [mace](/wiki/Mace_(bludgeon)) and a [sword](/wiki/Sword), in the [Mathura Museum](/wiki/Mathura_Museum). An inscription runs along the bottom of the coat.

The inscription is in middle [Brahmi script](/wiki/Brahmi_script):

*Mahārāja Rājadhirāja Devaputra Kāṇiṣka*
"The Great King, King of Kings, Son of God, Kanishka".
[Mathura art](/wiki/Mathura_art), [Mathura Museum](/wiki/Mathura_Museum)

The rule of [Kanishka the Great](/wiki/Kanishka_I), fourth Kushan king, lasted for about 23 years from c. AD 127. Upon his accession, Kanishka ruled a huge territory (virtually all of northern India), south to Ujjain and Kundina and east beyond Pataliputra, according to the Rabatak inscription:

> In the year one, it has been proclaimed unto India, unto the whole realm of the governing class, including Koonadeano (Kaundiny, [Kundina](/wiki/Kundina)) and the city of Ozeno (Ozene, [Ujjain](/wiki/Ujjain)) and the city of Zageda ([Saketa](/wiki/Saketa)) and the city of Kozambo ([Kausambi](/wiki/Kausambi)) and the city of Palabotro ([Pataliputra](/wiki/Pataliputra)) and as far as the city of Ziri-tambo ([Sri-Champa](/wiki/Sri-Champa)), whatever rulers and other important persons (they might have) he had submitted to (his) will, and he had submitted all India to (his) will.

— Rabatak inscription, Lines 4–8

His territory was administered from two capitals: [Purushapura](/wiki/Purushapura) (now [Peshawar](/wiki/Peshawar) in northwestern [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan)) and [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura), in northern India. He is also credited (along with [Raja Dab](/wiki/Raja_Dab)) for building the massive, ancient [Fort at Bathinda](/wiki/Fort_at_Bathinda) ([Qila Mubarak](/wiki/Qila_Mubarak,_Bathinda)), in the modern city of [Bathinda](/wiki/Bathinda), Indian [Punjab](/wiki/Punjab_(India)).

The Kushans also had a summer capital in [Kapisa](/wiki/Kapisi) (near modern Bagram), where the "[Begram Treasure](/wiki/Begram_Treasure)", comprising works of art from Greece to China, has been found. According to the Rabatak inscription, Kanishka was the son of Vima Kadphises, the grandson of Sadashkana, and the great-grandson of Kujula Kadphises. Kanishka's era is now generally accepted to have begun in 127 on the basis of Harry Falk's ground-breaking research. Kanishka's era was used as a calendar reference by the Kushans for about a century, until the decline of the Kushan realm.

### Huvishka (c. 150 – c. 190)

Main article: [Huvishka](/wiki/Huvishka)

[Huvishka](/wiki/Huvishka) (Kushan: Οοηϸκι, "Ooishki") was a Kushan emperor from the death of Kanishka (assumed on the best evidence available to be in 150) until the succession of [Vasudeva I](/wiki/Vasudeva_I) about thirty years later. His rule was a period of retrenchment and consolidation for the Empire. In particular he devoted time and effort early in his reign to the exertion of greater control over the city of Mathura.

### Vasudeva I (c. 190 – c. 230)

Main article: [Vasudeva I](/wiki/Vasudeva_I)

[Vasudeva I](/wiki/Vasudeva_I) (Kushan: Βαζοδηο "Bazodeo", Chinese: 波調 "Bodiao") was the last of the "Great Kushans". Named inscriptions dating from year 64 to 98 of Kanishka's era suggest his reign extended from at least AD 191 to 225. He was the last great Kushan emperor, and the end of his rule coincides with the invasion of the [Sasanians](/wiki/Sasanian_Empire) as far as northwestern India, and the establishment of the [Indo-Sasanians](/wiki/Indo-Sasanians) or *Kushanshahs* in what is nowadays Afghanistan, Pakistan and northwestern India from around AD 240.

### Kanishka II (c. 232 – c. 247)

Main article: [Kanishka II](/wiki/Kanishka_II)

### Vāsishka (c. 247 – c. 267)

Main article: [Vāsishka](/wiki/V%C4%81sishka)

[Vāsishka](/wiki/V%C4%81sishka) was a Kushan emperor who seems to have had a 20-year reign following [Kanishka II](/wiki/Kanishka_II). His rule is recorded at [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura), in [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara) and as far south as Sanchi (near [Vidisa](/wiki/Vidisa)), where several inscriptions in his name have been found, dated to the year 22 (the Sanchi inscription of "Vaksushana" – i.e., Vasishka Kushana) and year 28 (the Sanchi inscription of Vasaska – i.e., Vasishka) of a possible second Kanishka era.

### Little Kushans (AD 270 – 350)

Following territorial losses in the west ([Bactria](/wiki/Bactria) lost to the [Kushano-Sasanians](/wiki/Kushano-Sasanians)), and in the east (loss of [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura) to the [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire)), several "Little Kushans" are known, who ruled locally in the area of [Punjab](/wiki/Punjab) with their capital at [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila): [Vasudeva II](/wiki/Vasudeva_II) (270 – 300), [Mahi](/wiki/Mahi_(Kushan)) (300 – 305), [Shaka](/wiki/Shaka_(Kushan)) (305 – 335) and [Kipunada](/wiki/Kipunada) (335 – 350). They probably were vassals of the [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire), until the invasion of the [Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites) destroyed the last remains of Kushan rule.

## Kushan deities

Kumara/[Kartikeya](/wiki/Kartikeya) with a Kushan devotee, 2nd century AD

Kushan prince, said to be [Huvishka](/wiki/Huvishka), making a donation to a [Boddhisattva](/wiki/Boddhisattva).

[Shiva](/wiki/Shiva) [Linga](/wiki/Linga) worshipped by Kushan devotees, circa 2nd century AD

The Kushan religious [pantheon](/wiki/Pantheon_(gods)) is extremely varied, as revealed by their coins that were made in gold, silver, and copper. These coins contained more than thirty different gods, belonging mainly to their own Iranian, as well as Greek and Indian worlds as well. Kushan coins had images of Kushan Kings, Buddha, and figures from the Indo-Aryan and Iranian pantheons. Greek deities, with Greek names are represented on early coins. During Kanishka's reign, the language of the coinage changes to [Bactrian](/wiki/Bactrian_language) (though it remained in Greek script for all kings). After Huvishka, only two divinities appear on the coins: *Ardoxsho* and *[Oesho](/wiki/Oesho)* (see details below).

## Kushans and Buddhism

The [Ahin Posh](/wiki/Ahin_Posh) stupa was dedicated in the 2nd century AD under the Kushans, and contained coins of Kushan and Roman Emperors.

Early Mahayana Buddhist triad. From left to right, a Kushan devotee, [Maitreya](/wiki/Maitreya), the [Buddha](/wiki/Gautama_Buddha), [Avalokitesvara](/wiki/Avalokitesvara), and a Buddhist monk. 2nd–3rd century, [Shotorak](/wiki/Shotorak_monastery).

The Kushans inherited the [Greco-Buddhist](/wiki/Greco-Buddhist) traditions of the [Indo-Greek Kingdom](/wiki/Indo-Greek_Kingdom) they replaced, and their patronage of Buddhist institutions allowed them to grow as a commercial power. Between the mid-1st century and the mid-3rd century, Buddhism, patronised by the Kushans, extended to China and other Asian countries through the [Silk Road](/wiki/Silk_Road).

Kanishka is renowned in Buddhist tradition for having convened a [great Buddhist council](/wiki/Buddhist_Councils#The_fourth_Buddhist_council) in [Kashmir](/wiki/Kashmir). Along with his predecessors in the region, the Indo-Greek king [Menander I](/wiki/Menander_I) (Milinda) and the Indian emperors [Ashoka](/wiki/Ashoka) and [Harsha Vardhana](/wiki/Harsha), Kanishka is considered by Buddhism as one of its greatest benefactors.

During the 1st century AD, Buddhist books were being produced and carried by monks, and their trader patrons. Also, monasteries were being established along these land routes that went from China and other parts of Asia. With the development of Buddhist books, it caused a new written language called Gandhara. Gandhara consists of eastern Afghanistan and northern Pakistan. Scholars are said to have found many Buddhist scrolls that contained the Gandhari language.

The reign of Huvishka corresponds to the first known epigraphic evidence of the Buddha [Amitabha](/wiki/Amitabha), on the bottom part of a 2nd-century statue which has been found in Govindo-Nagar, and now at the [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura,_Uttar_Pradesh) Museum. The statue is dated to "the 28th year of the reign of Huvishka", and dedicated to "Amitabha Buddha" by a family of merchants. There is also some evidence that Huvishka himself was a follower of [Mahayana](/wiki/Mahayana) Buddhism. A [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit) manuscript fragment in the [Schøyen Collection](/wiki/Schoyen_Collection) describes Huvishka as one who has "set forth in the Mahāyāna."

The 12th century historical chronicle *[Rajatarangini](/wiki/Rajatarangini)* mentions in detail the rule of the Kushan kings and their benevolence towards Buddhism:

> Then there ruled in this very land the founders of cities called after their own appellations the three kings named [Huska](/wiki/Huvishka), [Juska](/wiki/Vashishka) and [Kaniska](/wiki/Kanishka) (...) These kings albeit belonging to the Turkish race found refuge in acts of piety; they constructed in Suskaletra and other places [monasteries](/wiki/Vihara), [Caityas](/wiki/Chaitya) and similar edificies. During the glorious period of their regime the kingdom of [Kashmir](/wiki/Kashmir) was for the most part an appanage of the Buddhists who had acquired lustre by renunciation. At this time since the Nirvana of the blessed [Sakya Simha](/wiki/Sakyamuni) in this terrestrial world one hundred fifty years, it is said, had elapsed. And a Bodhisattva was in this country the sole supreme ruler of the land; he was the illustrious [Nagarjuna](/wiki/Nagarjuna) who dwelt in Sadarhadvana.

— *[Rajatarangini](/wiki/Rajatarangini)* (I168-I173)

## Kushan art

Main articles: [Kushan art](/wiki/Kushan_art), [Greco-Buddhist art](/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_art), and [Art of Mathura](/wiki/Art_of_Mathura)

Portrait of a Kushan prince from [Khalchayan](/wiki/Khalchayan) (left), and head of a Gandhara [Bodhisattava](/wiki/Bodhisattava) (right), said to have similar characteristics ([Philadelphia Museum of Art](/wiki/Philadelphia_Museum_of_Art)).

The art and culture of Gandhara, at the crossroads of the Kushan hegemony, developed the traditions of [Greco-Buddhist art](/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_art) and are the best known expressions of Kushan influences to Westerners. Several direct depictions of Kushans are known from Gandhara, where they are represented with a tunic, belt and trousers and play the role of devotees to the Buddha, as well as the [Bodhisattva](/wiki/Bodhisattva) and future Buddha Maitreya.

According to Benjamin Rowland, the first expression of Kushan art appears at [Khalchayan](/wiki/Khalchayan) at the end of the 2nd century BC. It is derived from [Hellenistic art](/wiki/Hellenistic_art), and possibly from the art of the cities of [Ai-Khanoum](/wiki/Ai-Khanoum) and [Nysa](/wiki/Nisa,_Turkmenistan), and clearly has similarities with the later [Art of Gandhara](/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_art), and may even have been at the origin of its development. Rowland particularly draws attention to the similarity of the ethnic types represented at Khalchayan and in the art of Gandhara, and also in the style of portraiture itself. For example, Rowland find a great proximity between [the famous head of a Yuezhi prince](/wiki/File:KushanHead.jpg) from Khalchayan, and the head of Gandharan [Bodhisattvas](/wiki/Bodhisattva), giving the example of the Gandharan head of a Bodhisattva in the [Philadelphia Museum of Art](/wiki/Philadelphia_Museum_of_Art). The similarity of the Gandhara Bodhisattva with the portrait of the Kushan ruler [Heraios](/wiki/Heraios) is also striking. According to Rowland the Bactrian art of Khalchayan thus survived for several centuries through its influence in the art of Gandhara, thanks to the patronage of the [Kushans](/wiki/Kushans).

During the Kushan Empire, many images of [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara) share a strong resemblance to the features of Greek, Syrian, Persian and Indian figures. These Western-looking stylistic signatures often include heavy drapery and curly hair, representing a composite (the Greeks, for example, often possessed curly hair).

As the Kushans took control of the area of [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura) as well, the [Art of Mathura](/wiki/Art_of_Mathura) developed considerably, and free-standing statues of the [Buddha](/wiki/Buddha) came to be mass-produced around this time, possibly encouraged by doctrinal changes in Buddhism allowing to depart from the [aniconism](/wiki/Aniconism) that had prevailed in the Buddhist sculptures at Mathura, [Bharhut](/wiki/Bharhut) or [Sanchi](/wiki/Sanchi) from the end of the 2nd century BC. The artistic cultural influence of kushans declined slowly due to Hellenistic Greek and Indian influences.

### Kushan monetary system

Main article: [Kushan coinage](/wiki/Kushan_coinage)

Kushan gold ingots, from the [Dalverzin Tepe](/wiki/Dalverzin_Tepe) treasure, 1st century CE

The Kushans used gold ingots as part of their monetary system, as shown by the gold treasure discovered in 1972 in [Dalverzin Tepe](/wiki/Dalverzin_Tepe). The main objects from the treasure were circular and parallelepipedic ingots, followed by various decorative objects and jewellery items. The circular ingots used to be progressively cut up as needed, depending on the amount required for a transaction. On the contrary, the parallelepipedic ingots were used to stock wealth in a not-divisible form; these ingots bear inscriptions in [Kharoshthi](/wiki/Kharoshthi) mentioning their weight and the god [Mitra](/wiki/Mitra) (protector of contractual relations) These ingots are all attributed to the monetary system of the Kushan Empire.

The coinage of the Kushans was abundant and an important tool of propaganda in promoting each Kushan ruler. One of the names for Kushan coins was *[Dinara](/wiki/Dinar)*, which ultimately came from the Roman name *[Denarius](/wiki/Denarius) [aureus](/wiki/Aureus)*. The coinage of the Kushans was copied as far as the [Kushano-Sasanians](/wiki/Kushano-Sasanians) in the west, and the kingdom of [Samatata](/wiki/Samatata) in [Bengal](/wiki/Bengal) to the east. The coinage of the [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire) was also initially derived from the coinage of the Kushan Empire, adopting its weight standard, techniques and designs, following the conquests of [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta) in the northwest. The imagery on Gupta coins then became more Indian in both style and subject matter compared to earlier dynasties, where Greco-Roman and Persian styles were mostly followed.

It has long been suggested that the gold contained in Kushan coins was ultimately of Roman origin, and that Roman coins were imported as a consequence of trade and melted in India to mint Kushan coins. However, a recent [archaeometallurgical study](/wiki/Archaeometallurgy) of trace elements through proton activation analysis has shown that Kushan gold contains high concentrations of [platinum](/wiki/Platinum) and [palladium](/wiki/Palladium), which rules out the hypothesis of a Roman provenance. To this day, the origin of Kushan gold remains unknown.

## Contacts with Rome

Main article: [Indo-Roman trade relations](/wiki/Indo-Roman_trade_relations)

Several Roman sources describe the visit of ambassadors from the Kings of Bactria and India during the 2nd century, probably referring to the Kushans.

[Historia Augusta](/wiki/Historia_Augusta), speaking of [Emperor Hadrian](/wiki/Hadrian) (117–138) tells:

Greco-Roman [gladiator](/wiki/Gladiator) on a glass vessel, Kapisa, 2nd century

> *Reges Bactrianorum legatos ad eum, amicitiae petendae causa, supplices miserunt*
> "The kings of the Bactrians sent supplicant ambassadors to him, to seek his friendship."

Also in 138, according to [Aurelius Victor](/wiki/Aurelius_Victor) (*Epitome*‚ XV, 4), and [Appian](/wiki/Appian) (*Praef.*, 7), [Antoninus Pius](/wiki/Antoninus_Pius), successor to Hadrian, received some Indian, Bactrian, and [Hyrcanian](/wiki/Hyrcanian) ambassadors.

Some Kushan coins have an effigy of "Roma", suggesting a strong level of awareness and some level of diplomatic relations.

The summer capital of the Kushan Empire in Kapisa has yielded a considerable amount of goods imported from the Roman Empire—in particular, various types of glassware. The Chinese described the presence of Roman goods in the Kushan realm:

> "Precious things from *Da Qin* [the Roman Empire] can be found there [in Tianzhu or Northwestern India], as well as fine [cotton](/wiki/Cotton) cloths, fine wool carpets, perfumes of all sorts, [sugar candy](/wiki/Sugar_candy), [pepper](/wiki/Black_pepper), [ginger](/wiki/Ginger), and black salt."

— Hou Hanshu

[Parthamaspates of Parthia](/wiki/Parthamaspates_of_Parthia), a client of Rome and ruler of the kingdom of [Osroene](/wiki/Osroene), is known to have traded with the Kushan Empire, goods being sent by sea and through the [Indus River](/wiki/Indus_River).

## Contacts with China

During the 1st and 2nd century AD, the Kushan Empire expanded militarily to the north, putting them at the center of the profitable Central Asian commerce. They are related to have collaborated militarily with the Chinese against nomadic incursion, particularly when they allied with the [Han dynasty](/wiki/Han_dynasty) general [Ban Chao](/wiki/Ban_Chao) against the Sogdians in 84, when the latter were trying to support a revolt by the king of Kashgar. Around 85, they also assisted the Chinese general in an attack on [Turpan](/wiki/Turpan), east of the Tarim Basin.

Kushan coinage in China

A bronze coin of Kanishka the Great found in [Khotan](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Khotan), [Tarim Basin](/wiki/Tarim_Basin).

[Eastern Han](/wiki/Eastern_Han) inscriptions on lead ingot, using barbarous Greek alphabet in the style of the [Kushans](/wiki/Kushans), excavated in [Shaanxi](/wiki/Shaanxi), 1st–2nd century AD. [Gansu Provincial Museum](/wiki/Gansu_Provincial_Museum).

In recognition for their support to the Chinese, the Kushans requested a Han princess, but were denied, even after they had sent presents to the Chinese court. In retaliation, they marched on Ban Chao in 86 with a force of 70,000, but were defeated by a smaller Chinese force. The Yuezhi retreated and paid tribute to the Chinese Empire during the reign of emperor [He of Han](/wiki/Emperor_He_of_Han_China) (89–106).

The Kushans are again recorded to have sent presents to the Chinese court in 158–159 during the reign of [Emperor Huan of Han](/wiki/Emperor_Huan_of_Han).

Following these interactions, cultural exchanges further increased, and Kushan Buddhist missionaries, such as [Lokaksema](/wiki/Lokaksema_(Buddhist_monk)), became active in the Chinese capital cities of [Luoyang](/wiki/Luoyang) and sometimes [Nanjing](/wiki/Nanjing), where they particularly distinguished themselves by their translation work. They were the first recorded promoters of Hinayana and Mahayana scriptures in China, greatly contributing to the [Silk Road transmission of Buddhism](/wiki/Silk_Road_transmission_of_Buddhism).

## Decline

### Kushano-Sassanians

Main article: [Kushano-Sasanians](/wiki/Kushano-Sasanians)

Sasanian control of the Western Kushans

[Hormizd I Kushanshah](/wiki/Hormizd_I_Kushanshah) (AD 277–286), king of the [Indo-Sasanians](/wiki/Indo-Sasanians), maintained [Sasanian](/wiki/Sasanian) rule in former Kushan territories of the northwest. [Naqsh-e Rustam](/wiki/Naqsh-e_Rustam) [Bahram II](/wiki/Bahram_II) panel.

The Kushano-Sasanians imitated the Kushans in some of their Bactrian coinage. Coin of Sasanian ruler [Peroz I Kushanshah](/wiki/Peroz_I_Kushanshah), with [Bactrian](/wiki/Bactrian_script) legend around "Peroz the Great Kushan King"

After the death of [Vasudeva I](/wiki/Vasudeva_I) in 225, the Kushan empire split into western and eastern halves. The Western Kushans (in Afghanistan) were soon subjugated by the Persian [Sasanian Empire](/wiki/Sasanian_Empire) and lost [Sogdiana](/wiki/Sogdiana), [Bactria](/wiki/Bactria), and [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara) to them. The Sassanian king [Shapur I](/wiki/Shapur_I) (240–270) claims in his [Naqsh-e Rostam](/wiki/Naqsh-e_Rostam) inscription possession of the territory of the Kushans (Kūšān šahr) as far as [Purushapura](/wiki/Purushapura) (modern Peshawar), suggesting he controlled [Bactria](/wiki/Bactria) and areas as far as the [Hindu-Kush](/wiki/Hindu-Kush) or even south of it:

> I, the Mazda-worshipping lord, Shapur, king of kings of Iran and An-Iran... (I) am the Master of the Domain of Iran (Ērānšahr) and possess the territory of Persis, Parthian... Hindestan, the Domain of the Kushan up to the limits of Paškabur and up to Kash, Sughd, and Chachestan.

— [Shapur I's inscription at the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht](/wiki/Shapur_I%27s_inscription_at_the_Ka%27ba-ye_Zartosht), [Naqsh-e Rostam](/wiki/Naqsh-e_Rostam)

This is also confirmed by the [Rag-i-Bibi](/wiki/Rag-i-Bibi) inscription in modern [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan).

The Sasanians deposed the Western dynasty and replaced them with Persian vassals known as the [Kushanshas](/wiki/Kushansha) (in [Bactrian](/wiki/Bactrian_language) on their coinage: KΟÞANΟ ÞAΟ *Koshano Shao*) also called [Indo-Sasanians](/wiki/Indo-Sasanians) or [Kushano-Sasanians](/wiki/Kushano-Sasanians). The Kushano-Sasanians ultimately became very powerful under [Hormizd I Kushanshah](/wiki/Hormizd_I_Kushanshah) (277–286) and rebelled against the Sasanian Empire, while continuing many aspects of the Kushan culture, visible in particular in their titulature and their coinage.

### "Little Kushans" and Gupta suzerainty

Gupta control over the Eastern Kushans

The expression *Devaputra Shāhi Shāhānu Shāhi* in Middle [Brahmi](/wiki/Brahmi) in the [Allahabad pillar](/wiki/Allahabad_pillar) (Line 23), claimed by [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta) to be under his dominion.

Coin minted in the [Punjab](/wiki/Punjab) area with the name "Samudra" (   *Sa-mu-dra*), thought to be the Gupta ruler [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta). These coins imitate those of the last Kushan ruler [Kipunada](/wiki/Kipunada), and precede the coinage of the first [Kidarite Huns](/wiki/Kidarites) in northwestern India. Circa 350-375.

The Eastern Kushan kingdom, also known as the "Little Kushans", was based in the Punjab. Around 270 their territories on the Gangetic plain became independent under local dynasties such as the [Yaudheyas](/wiki/Yaudheyas). Then in the mid-4th century they were subjugated by the Gupta Empire under [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta). In his inscription on the [Allahabad pillar](/wiki/Allahabad_pillar) Samudragupta proclaims that the *Dēvaputra-Shāhi-Shāhānushāhi* (referring to the last Kushan rulers, being a deformation of the Kushan regnal titles *Devaputra*, *Shao* and *Shaonanoshao*: "Son of God, King, King of Kings") are now under his dominion, and that they were forced to "self-surrender, offering (their own) daughters in marriage and a request for the administration of their own districts and provinces". This suggests that by the time of the Allahabad inscription the Kushans still ruled in [Punjab](/wiki/Punjab), but under the suzerainty of the Gupta Emperor.

Numismatics indicate that the coinage of the Eastern Kushans was much weakened: silver coinage was abandoned altogether, and gold coinage was debased. This suggests that the Eastern Kushans had lost their central trading role on the trade routes that supplied luxury goods and [gold](/wiki/Gold). Still, the [Buddhist art of Gandhara](/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_art) continued to flourish, and cities such as [Sirsukh](/wiki/Sirsukh) near [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila) were established.

### Sasanian, Kidarite and Alchon invasions

Main articles: [Sasanian Empire](/wiki/Sasanian_Empire), [Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites), and [Alchon Huns](/wiki/Alchon_Huns)

In the east around 350, Shapur II regained the upper hand against the [Kushano-Sasanian Kingdom](/wiki/Kushano-Sasanian_Kingdom) and took control of large territories in areas now known as [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan) and [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan), possibly as a consequence of the destruction of the Kushano-Sasanians by the [Chionites](/wiki/Chionites). The Kushano-Sasanian still ruled in the north. Important finds of Sasanian coinage beyond the [Indus River](/wiki/Indus_River) in the city of [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila) only start with the reigns of Shapur II (r.309-379) and [Shapur III](/wiki/Shapur_III) (r.383-388), suggesting that the expansion of Sasanian control beyond the Indus was the result of the wars of Shapur II "with the [Chionites](/wiki/Kidarites) and Kushans" in 350-358 as described by [Ammianus Marcellinus](/wiki/Ammianus_Marcellinus). They probably maintained control until the rise of the [Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites) under their ruler [Kidara](/wiki/Kidara).

In 360 a [Kidarite Hun](/wiki/Kidarites) named [Kidara](/wiki/Kidara) overthrew the [Kushano-Sasanians](/wiki/Kushano-Sasanians) and remnants of the old Kushan dynasty, and established the [Kidarite Kingdom](/wiki/Kidarite_Kingdom). The Kushan style of Kidarite coins indicates they claimed Kushan heritage. The Kidarite seem to have been rather prosperous, although on a smaller scale than their Kushan predecessors. East of the [Punjab](/wiki/Punjab), the former eastern territories of the Kushans were controlled by the mighty [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire).

The remnants of Kushan culture under the Kidarites in the northwest were ultimately wiped out in the end of the 5th century by the invasions of the [Alchon Huns](/wiki/Alchon_Huns) (sometimes considered as a branch of the [Hephthalites](/wiki/Hephthalites)), and later the [Nezak Huns](/wiki/Nezak_Huns).
