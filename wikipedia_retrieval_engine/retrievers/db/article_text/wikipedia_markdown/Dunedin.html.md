**Dunedin** ([/dʌˈniːdɪn/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [ⓘ](/wiki/File:En-nz-Dunedin.ogg) [*dun-EE-din*](/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key); [Māori](/wiki/M%C4%81ori_language): *Ōtepoti*) is the second-most populous city in the [South Island](/wiki/South_Island) of [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand) (after [Christchurch](/wiki/Christchurch)), and the principal city of the [Otago](/wiki/Otago) region. Its name comes from *Dùn Èideann* ("fort of Edin"), the [Scottish Gaelic](/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic) name for [Edinburgh](/wiki/Edinburgh), the capital of [Scotland](/wiki/Scotland).

With an estimated population of 132,800 as of June 2025, Dunedin is New Zealand's seventh-most populous metropolitan and urban area. For cultural, geographical, and historical reasons, the city has long been considered one of New Zealand's four main centres. The urban area of Dunedin lies on the central-eastern coast of Otago, surrounding the head of [Otago Harbour](/wiki/Otago_Harbour). The harbour and hills around Dunedin are the remnants of an extinct volcano. The city suburbs extend out into the surrounding valleys and hills, onto the isthmus of the [Otago Peninsula](/wiki/Otago_Peninsula), and along the shores of the Otago Harbour and the Pacific Ocean.

Archaeological evidence points to the beginning of a lengthy occupation of the Dunedin area by Māori between 1250 and 1300 AD, with many pā constructed during the Classical period. Migrations by Kāi Tahu in the mid-17th century were followed by the arrival of Europeans by the 1830s. A Scottish settlement was established in 1848 by the Lay Association of the [Free Church of Scotland](/wiki/Free_Church_of_Scotland_(1843%E2%80%931900)), and between 1855 and 1900 many thousands of [Scots](/wiki/Scottish_New_Zealanders) emigrated to the incorporated city. Dunedin's population and wealth boomed during the 1860s' [Otago gold rush](/wiki/Otago_gold_rush). For a brief period of time it became New Zealand's largest urban area. The [Union Company](/wiki/Union_Company), which became the biggest [shipping line](/wiki/Shipping_line) in the [Southern Hemisphere](/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere), was founded in Dunedin in 1875. The city saw substantial migration from mainland China at the same time, predominantly from [Guangdong](/wiki/Guangdong) and [Guangxi](/wiki/Guangxi). Dunedin is home to New Zealand's oldest [Chinese community](/wiki/Chinese_New_Zealanders).

Today, Dunedin has a diverse economy which includes manufacturing, publishing, arts, tourism and technology-based industries. The mainstay of the city's economy remains centred on [tertiary education](/wiki/Tertiary_education_in_New_Zealand), with students from the [University of Otago](/wiki/University_of_Otago), New Zealand's [oldest university](/wiki/List_of_universities_in_New_Zealand), and the [Otago Polytechnic](/wiki/Otago_Polytechnic), accounting for a large proportion of the population; 21.6 per cent of the city's population was aged between 15 and 24 at the 2006 census, compared to the New Zealand average of 14.2 per cent. Dunedin is also noted for its vibrant music scene, as the 1980s birthplace of the [Dunedin sound](/wiki/Dunedin_sound) (which heavily influenced [grunge](/wiki/Grunge), [indie](/wiki/Indie_rock) and modern [alternative rock](/wiki/Alternative_rock)). In 2014, the city was designated as a UNESCO [City of Literature](/wiki/City_of_Literature).

## History

Main article: [History of the Dunedin urban area](/wiki/History_of_the_Dunedin_urban_area)

### Māori settlements

Dunedin Museum Māori artefact

Archaeological evidence shows the first human (Māori) occupation of New Zealand occurred between 1250 and 1300 AD, with the population concentrated along the southeast coast. A camp site at Kaikai Beach, near [Long Beach](/wiki/Long_Beach,_New_Zealand) to the north of the present-day city of Dunedin, has been dated from about that time. There are numerous archaic ([moa](/wiki/Moa)-hunter) sites in what is now Dunedin, several of them large and permanently occupied, particularly in the 14th century. The population contracted but expanded again with the evolution of the Classical [Māori culture](/wiki/M%C4%81ori_culture) which saw the building of several [pā](/wiki/P%C4%81_(M%C4%81ori)), fortified settlements, notably Pukekura (at [Taiaroa Head](/wiki/Taiaroa_Head)), about 1650. There was a settlement in what is now central Dunedin (Ōtepoti), occupied as late as about 1785 but abandoned by 1826. There were also Māori settlements at [Whareakeake](/wiki/Whareakeake) (Murdering Beach), [Pūrākaunui](/wiki/P%C5%ABr%C4%81kaunui), Mapoutahi (Goat Island Peninsula) and Huriawa ([Karitane](/wiki/Karitane) Peninsula) to the north, and at [Taieri Mouth](/wiki/Taieri_Mouth) and Otokia ([Henley](/wiki/Henley,_New_Zealand)) to the south, all inside the present boundaries of Dunedin.

[Māori tradition](/wiki/M%C4%81ori_mythology) tells first of a people called Kahui Tipua living in the area, then Te Rapuwai, semi-legendary but considered to be historical. The next arrivals were [Waitaha](/wiki/Waitaha_(South_Island_iwi)), followed by [Kāti Māmoe](/wiki/K%C4%81ti_M%C4%81moe) late in the 16th century and then [Kāi Tahu](/wiki/K%C4%81i_Tahu) (*Ngāi Tahu* in modern standard [Māori](/wiki/M%C4%81ori_language)) who arrived in the mid-17th century. European accounts have often represented these successive influxes as "invasions", but modern scholarship has cast doubt on that view. They were probably migrations – like those of the Europeans – which incidentally resulted in bloodshed.
The sealer [John Boultbee](/wiki/John_Boultbee_(explorer)) recorded in the late 1820s that the 'Kaika Otargo' (settlements around and near [Otago Harbour](/wiki/Otago_Harbour)) were the oldest and largest in the south.

### Early arrivals from Europe

Dunedin, New Zealand, c. 1856

Lieutenant [James Cook](/wiki/James_Cook) stood off what is now the coast of Dunedin between 25 February 1770 and 5 March 1770, naming [Cape Saunders](/wiki/Cape_Saunders) (on the [Otago Peninsula](/wiki/Otago_Peninsula)) and Saddle Hill. He reported penguins and seals in the vicinity, which led Australian, American and British [sealers](/wiki/Seal_hunting) to visit from the beginning of the 19th century. The early years of sealing saw a feud between sealers and local Māori from 1810 to 1823, the "[Sealers' War](/wiki/Sealers%27_War)" sparked by an incident on Otago Harbour. [William Tucker](/wiki/William_Tucker_(settler)) became the first European to settle in the area – in 1815.

Permanent European occupation dates from 1831, when the [Weller brothers](/wiki/Weller_brothers) of [New South Wales](/wiki/Colony_of_New_South_Wales) founded their [whaling station](/wiki/Whaling_station) at Otago (present-day [Otakou](/wiki/Otakou)) on the Otago Harbour. Epidemics severely reduced the Māori population. By the late 1830s, the Harbour had become an international whaling port. Wright & Richards started a whaling station at [Karitane](/wiki/Karitane) in 1837 and Sydney-born [Johnny Jones](/wiki/Johnny_Jones_(pioneer)) established a farming settlement and a [mission station](/wiki/Mission_station) (the South Island's first) at [Waikouaiti](/wiki/Waikouaiti) in 1840. The settlements at Karitane and Waikouaiti have endured, making modern Dunedin one of the longest-standing European-settled territories in New Zealand.

Early in 1844, the *Deborah*, captained by Thomas Wing and carrying (among others) his wife Lucy and a representative of the [New Zealand Company](/wiki/New_Zealand_Company), [Frederick Tuckett](/wiki/Frederick_Tuckett), sailed south from [Nelson](/wiki/Nelson,_New_Zealand) to determine the location of a planned [Free Church](/wiki/Free_Church_of_Scotland_(1843%E2%80%931900)) settlement. After inspecting several areas around the eastern coast of the South Island, Tuckett selected the site which would become known as Dunedin. (Tuckett rejected the site of what would become [Christchurch](/wiki/Christchurch), as he felt the ground around the [Avon River / Ōtākaro](/wiki/Avon_River_/_%C5%8Ct%C4%81karo) was swampy.)

The Lay Association of the [Free Church of Scotland](/wiki/Free_Church_of_Scotland_(1843%E2%80%931900)), through a company called the [Otago Association](/wiki/Otago_Association), founded Dunedin at the head of Otago Harbour in 1848 as the principal town
of its special settlement.

The name "Dunedin" comes from *Dùn Èideann*, the [Scottish Gaelic](/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic) name for [Edinburgh](/wiki/Edinburgh), the capital of [Scotland](/wiki/Scotland). [Charles Kettle](/wiki/Charles_Kettle) the city's surveyor, instructed to emulate the characteristics of Edinburgh, produced a striking, "Romantic" town-planning design. There resulted both grand and quirky streets, as the builders struggled and sometimes failed to construct his bold vision across the challenging landscape. Captain [William Cargill](/wiki/William_Cargill_(New_Zealand_politician)) (1784–1860), a veteran of the [Napoleonic Wars](/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars), served as the secular leader of the new colony. The Reverend [Thomas Burns](/wiki/Thomas_Burns_(minister,_born_1796)) (1796–1871), a nephew of the poet [Robert Burns](/wiki/Robert_Burns), provided spiritual guidance. By the end of the 1850s, around 12,000 Scots had emigrated to Dunedin, many from the industrial [lowlands](/wiki/Scottish_Lowlands).

### Gold rush era

In 1852, Dunedin became the capital of the [Otago Province](/wiki/Otago_Province), the whole of New Zealand from the [Waitaki](/wiki/Waitaki_River) south. In 1861, the discovery of gold at [Gabriel's Gully](/wiki/Gabriel%27s_Gully), to the south-west, led to a rapid influx of people and saw Dunedin become New Zealand's first city by growth of population in 1865. The new arrivals included many Irish, but also Italians, Lebanese, French, Germans, Jews and Chinese. The [Dunedin Southern Cemetery](/wiki/Dunedin_Southern_Cemetery) was established in 1858, the [Dunedin Northern Cemetery](/wiki/Dunedin_Northern_Cemetery) in 1872. In the 1860s, [Ross Creek Reservoir](/wiki/Ross_Creek_Reservoir) was created so as to serve Dunedin's need for water.

The London-owned [Bank of Otago](/wiki/Bank_of_Otago) opened its doors in Dunedin in 1863, opened 12 branches throughout its region, then in 1873 merged with the new [National Bank of New Zealand](/wiki/National_Bank_of_New_Zealand) also based in London and also operated from Dunedin but, true to its name, it rapidly expanded throughout New Zealand. Dunedin remained the principal local source of the nation's development capital until the [Second World War](/wiki/Second_World_War).

Dunedin and the region industrialised and consolidated, and the [Main South Line](/wiki/Main_South_Line) connected the city with [Christchurch](/wiki/Christchurch) in 1878 and Invercargill in 1879. Otago Boys' High School was founded in 1863. The [Otago Museum](/wiki/Otago_Museum) opened in 1868. The [University of Otago](/wiki/University_of_Otago), the oldest university in New Zealand, in 1869. [Otago Girls' High School](/wiki/Otago_Girls%27_High_School) was established in 1871.

By 1874, Dunedin and its suburbs had become New Zealand's largest city with a population of 29,832 displacing Auckland's 27,840 residents to second place. A major innovation for the city's rapidly expanding economy was the foundation of the [Union Company](/wiki/Union_Company), a shipping line founded by [James Mills](/wiki/James_Mills_(ship_owner)) in Dunedin in 1875, with the backing of Scottish shipbuilder [Peter Denny](/wiki/Peter_Denny). The line became the largest in the Southern Hemisphere, and by the 1890s dominated shipping along its [San Francisco](/wiki/San_Francisco)-[Auckland](/wiki/Auckland)-[Sydney](/wiki/Sydney) route. Competition from the American line [Matson, Inc.](/wiki/Matson,_Inc.) after the [annexation of Hawaii](/wiki/Newlands_Resolution) threatened Union Company's dominance and forced them to switch the Auckland stop for [Wellington](/wiki/Wellington)-[Rarotonga](/wiki/Rarotonga)-[Papeete](/wiki/Papeete).

Between 1881 and 1957, Dunedin was home to [cable trams](/wiki/Dunedin_cable_tramway_system), being both one of the first and last such systems in the world. Early in the 1880s the inauguration of the frozen meat industry, with the first shipment leaving from [Port Chalmers](/wiki/Port_Chalmers) in 1882, saw the beginning of a later great national industry. The first successful commercial shipment of frozen meat from New Zealand to the United Kingdom was on [the Dunedin](/wiki/Dunedin_(1874_ship)) in 1881.

After ten years of gold rushes the economy slowed but [Julius Vogel](/wiki/Julius_Vogel)'s immigration and development scheme brought thousands more, especially to Dunedin and Otago, before recession set in again in the 1880s. In these first and second times of prosperity, many institutions and businesses were established, New Zealand's first daily newspaper, [art school](/wiki/Art_school), [medical school](/wiki/Medical_school) and [public art](/wiki/Public_art) gallery. The [Dunedin Public Art Gallery](/wiki/Dunedin_Public_Art_Gallery) was among these new foundations. It had been actively promulgated by artist [William Mathew Hodgkins](/wiki/W._M._Hodgkins)**.** There was also a remarkable architectural flowering producing many substantial and ornamental buildings. [R. A. Lawson](/wiki/Robert_Lawson_(architect))'s [First Church of Otago](/wiki/First_Church,_Dunedin) and [Knox Church](/wiki/Knox_Church,_Dunedin) are notable examples, as are buildings by [Maxwell Bury](/wiki/Maxwell_Bury) and [F. W. Petre](/wiki/Francis_Petre). The other visual arts also flourished under the leadership of [W. M. Hodgkins](/wiki/W._M._Hodgkins). The city's landscape and burgeoning townscape were vividly portrayed by [George O'Brien](/wiki/George_O%27Brien_(painter)) (1821–1888). From the mid-1890s, the economy revived. Institutions such as the Otago Settlers Museum (now renamed as [Toitū Otago Settlers Museum](/wiki/Toit%C5%AB_Otago_Settlers_Museum)) and the [Hocken Collections](/wiki/Hocken_Collections)—the first of their kind in New Zealand—were founded. More notable buildings such as the [Railway Station](/wiki/Dunedin_Railway_Station) and [Olveston](/wiki/Olveston_(house)) were erected. New energy in the visual arts represented by [G. P. Nerli](/wiki/G._P._Nerli) culminated in the career of [Frances Hodgkins](/wiki/Frances_Hodgkins).

[Dunedin railway station](/wiki/Dunedin_railway_station), built in 1906, is famed for its "gingerbread" architecture.

### Early modern era

By 1900, Dunedin was no longer the country's biggest city. Influence and activity moved north to the other centres ("the drift north"), a trend which continued for much of the following century. Despite this, the university continued to expand, and a student quarter became established. At the same time, people started to notice Dunedin's mellowing, the ageing of its grand old buildings, with writers like E. H. McCormick pointing out its atmospheric charm. In 1901 the British royals, the Duke and Duchess of Cornwall and York toured Dunedin. Dunedin grew during the early 20th-century via annexation of surrounding municipalities — between 1904 and 1916 Dunedin annexed the boroughs of [Caversham](/wiki/Caversham,_New_Zealand), [South Dunedin](/wiki/South_Dunedin), [North East Valley](/wiki/North_East_Valley), [Maori Hill](/wiki/Maori_Hill), [Roslyn](/wiki/Roslyn,_Dunedin), and [Mornington](/wiki/Mornington,_Dunedin) as well as the Bay Town District. In 1963 [West Harbour](/wiki/West_Harbour,_Otago) Borough was annexed by Dunedin and in 1968 it annexed the entire Peninsula County.

In the 1930s and early 1940s a new generation of artists such as [M. T. (Toss) Woollaston](/wiki/Toss_Woollaston), [Doris Lusk](/wiki/Doris_Lusk), Anne Hamblett, [Colin McCahon](/wiki/Colin_McCahon) and [Patrick Hayman](/wiki/Patrick_Hayman) once again represented the best of the country's talent. The Second World War saw the dispersal of these painters, but not before McCahon had met a very youthful poet, [James K. Baxter](/wiki/James_K._Baxter), in a central city studio.

Numerous large companies had been established in Dunedin, many of which became national leaders. Late among them was [Fletcher Construction](/wiki/Fletcher_Construction), founded by Sir [James Fletcher](/wiki/James_Fletcher_(industrialist)) in the early 20th century. [Kempthorne Prosser](/wiki/Kempthorne_Prosser), established in 1879 in Stafford Street, was the largest fertiliser and drug manufacturer in the country for over 100 years. G. Methven, a metalworking and tap manufacturer based in [South Dunedin](/wiki/South_Dunedin), was also a leading firm, as was [H. E. Shacklock](/wiki/Henry_Ely_Shacklock), an iron founder and appliance manufacturer later taken over by the Auckland concern [Fisher and Paykel](/wiki/Fisher_and_Paykel). The Mosgiel Woollens was another Victorian Dunedin foundation. [Hallensteins](/wiki/Hallensteins) was the colloquial name of a menswear manufacturer and national retail chain, while the DIC and [Arthur Barnett](/wiki/Arthur_Barnett_(department_store)) were department stores, the former a nationwide concern. Coulls, Somerville Wilkie—later part of the [Whitcoulls](/wiki/Whitcoulls) group—had its origins in Dunedin in the 19th century. There were also the [National Mortgage and Agency Company of New Zealand](/wiki/National_Mortgage_and_Agency_Company_of_New_Zealand), [Wright Stephensons Limited](/wiki/Wright_Stephenson), the Union Steamship Company and the National Insurance Company and the Standard Insurance Company among many others, which survived into the 20th century.

### Post-war developments

Dunedin Botanic Gardens in winter

After the [Second World War](/wiki/World_War_II) prosperity and population growth revived, although Dunedin trailed as the fourth 'main centre'. A generation reacting against [Victorianism](/wiki/Victorian_architecture) started demolishing its buildings and many were lost, notably [William Mason](/wiki/William_Mason_(architect))'s [Stock exchange](/wiki/Stock_exchange) in 1969. ([Dunedin Stock Exchange building](/wiki/Princes_Street,_Dunedin)) Although the university continued to expand, the city's population contracted, notably from 1976 to 1981. This was a culturally vibrant time with the university's new privately endowed arts fellowships bringing writers including [James K Baxter](/wiki/James_K._Baxter), [Ralph Hotere](/wiki/Ralph_Hotere), [Janet Frame](/wiki/Janet_Frame) and [Hone Tuwhare](/wiki/Hone_Tuwhare) to the city.

During the 1980s Dunedin's [popular music](/wiki/Popular_music) scene blossomed, with many acts, such as [The Chills](/wiki/The_Chills), [The Clean](/wiki/The_Clean), [The Verlaines](/wiki/The_Verlaines) and [Straitjacket Fits](/wiki/Straitjacket_Fits), gaining national and international recognition. The term "The [Dunedin sound](/wiki/Dunedin_sound)" was coined to describe the 1960s-influenced, guitar-led music which flourished at the time. Bands and musicians are still playing and recording in many styles.

By 1990, [population decline](/wiki/Population_decline) had steadied and slow growth occurred thereafter with Dunedin re-inventing itself as a 'heritage city' with its main streets refurbished in the [Victorian style](/wiki/Victorian_architecture). [R. A. Lawson](/wiki/Robert_Lawson_(architect))'s Municipal Chambers ([Dunedin Town Hall](/wiki/Dunedin_Town_Hall)) in [the Octagon](/wiki/The_Octagon,_Dunedin) were handsomely restored. The city was also recognised as a centre of excellence in tertiary education and research. The university's and polytechnic's growth accelerated. Dunedin has continued to refurbish itself, embarking on redevelopments of the [art gallery](/wiki/Art_museum), railway station and the [Toitū Otago Settlers Museum](/wiki/Toit%C5%AB_Otago_Settlers_Museum). Meanwhile, the continued blossoming of local creative writing saw the city gain UNESCO [City of Literature](/wiki/City_of_Literature) status in 2014.

Dunedin has flourishing niche industries including engineering, software engineering, [biotechnology](/wiki/Biotechnology) and fashion. [Port Chalmers](/wiki/Port_Chalmers) on the [Otago Harbour](/wiki/Otago_Harbour) provides Dunedin with deep-water facilities. It is served by the [Port Chalmers Branch](/wiki/Port_Chalmers_Branch), a [branch line](/wiki/Branch_line) railway which diverges from the [Main South Line](/wiki/Main_South_Line) and runs from [Christchurch](/wiki/Christchurch) by way of Dunedin to [Invercargill](/wiki/Invercargill). Dunedin is also home to MTF, the nationwide vehicle finance company.

The cityscape glitters with gems of Victorian and Edwardian architecture—the legacy of the city's gold-rush affluence. Many, including First Church, [Otago Boys' High School](/wiki/Otago_Boys%27_High_School) and [Larnach Castle](/wiki/Larnach_Castle) were designed by one of New Zealand's most eminent architects [R. A. Lawson](/wiki/Robert_Lawson_(architect)). Other prominent buildings include [Olveston](/wiki/Olveston_(house)) and the [Dunedin Railway Station](/wiki/Dunedin_Railway_Station). Other unusual or memorable buildings or constructions are [Baldwin Street](/wiki/Baldwin_Street), claimed to be the world's steepest residential street; the Captain Cook tavern; Cadbury Chocolate Factory ([Cadbury World](/wiki/Cadbury_World,_Dunedin)) (In 2018, both the factory and Cadbury World closed to make way for a new NZ$1.4 billion hospital to replace the existing [Dunedin Public Hospital](/wiki/Dunedin_Public_Hospital)); and the [Speight's](/wiki/Speight%27s) brewery.

The thriving tertiary student population has led to a vibrant [youth culture](/wiki/Youth_culture) (students are referred to as 'Scarfies' by people who are not students), consisting of the previously mentioned [music scene](/wiki/Indie_music_scene), and more recently a burgeoning boutique fashion industry. A strong visual arts community also exists in Dunedin, notably in [Port Chalmers](/wiki/Port_Chalmers) and the other settlements which dot the coast of the [Otago Harbour](/wiki/Otago_Harbour), and also in communities such as [Waitati](/wiki/Waitati).

Sport is catered for in Dunedin by the floodlit [rugby](/wiki/Rugby_Union) and [cricket](/wiki/Cricket) venues of [Forsyth Barr Stadium](/wiki/Forsyth_Barr_Stadium) and [University Oval, Dunedin](/wiki/University_Oval,_Dunedin), respectively, the new [Caledonian Ground](/wiki/Caledonian_Ground) football and [athletics](/wiki/Track_and_field_athletics) stadium near the university at [Logan Park](/wiki/Logan_Park,_Dunedin), the large [Edgar Centre](/wiki/The_Edgar_Centre) indoor sports centre, the [Dunedin Ice Stadium](/wiki/Dunedin_Ice_Stadium), and numerous golf courses and parks. There is also the [Wingatui](/wiki/Wingatui) horseracing course to the south of the city. St Clair Beach is a well-known [surfing](/wiki/Surfing) venue, and the harbour basin is popular with [windsurfers](/wiki/Windsurfing) and [kitesurfers](/wiki/Kitesurfing). Dunedin has four public swimming pools: [Moana Pool](/wiki/Moana_Pool), Port Chalmers Pool, [Te Puna o Whakaehu](/wiki/Te_Puna_o_Whakaehu) and St Clair Salt Water Pool.

In February 2021, the [East Otago](/wiki/East_Otago) towns of [Waikouaiti](/wiki/Waikouaiti) and [Karitane](/wiki/Karitane) in New Zealand [reported high lead levels](/wiki/2021_East_Otago_lead_poisoning_scare) in their water supplies. Local and national authorities responded by dispatching water tanks to assist local residents and providing free blood tests, fruits and vegetables. The lead poisoning scare also attracted coverage by national media. By early March 2021, the [Southern District Health Board](/wiki/Southern_District_Health_Board) confirmed that test results indicated that long-term exposure to lead in the water supply posed little risk to the local population.

In late January 2024, the [Dunedin City Council](/wiki/Dunedin_City_Council) and [Otago Regional Council](/wiki/Otago_Regional_Council) released a joint draft strategy to expand housing development and industrial land over the next thirty years to accommodate a projected 10% population growth.

## Geography

[Taiaroa Head](/wiki/Taiaroa_Head) with lighthouse.

The Dunedin City territorial authority has a land area of 3,314.8 km2 (1,279.9 sq mi), slightly larger than the American state of [Rhode Island](/wiki/Rhode_Island) or the English county of [Cambridgeshire](/wiki/Cambridgeshire), and a little smaller than [Cornwall](/wiki/Cornwall). It was the largest city in land area in New Zealand until the formation of the 5,600 km2 (2,200 sq mi) [Auckland Council](/wiki/Auckland_Council) on 1 November 2010. The Dunedin City Council boundaries since 1989 have extended to [Middlemarch](/wiki/Middlemarch,_New_Zealand) in the west, [Waikouaiti](/wiki/Waikouaiti) in the north, the Pacific Ocean in the east and south-east, and the Waipori/Taieri River and the township of [Henley](/wiki/Henley,_New_Zealand) in the south-west.

Dunedin is situated at the head of [Otago Harbour](/wiki/Otago_Harbour), a narrow inlet extending south-westward for some 15 miles. The harbour is a recent creation formed by the flooding of two river valleys. From the time of its foundation in 1848, the city has spread slowly over the low-lying [flats](/wiki/Flat_(landform)) and nearby hills and across the [isthmus](/wiki/Isthmus) to the slopes of the [Otago Peninsula](/wiki/Otago_Peninsula).

### Geology

Eastern Otago is tectonically stable, meaning that it does not experience many earthquakes. One of the few known faults near Dunedin is the [Akatore Fault](/wiki/Akatore_Fault). The first earthquake to cause widespread damage in Dunedin since its founding was the 1974 Dunedin earthquake, which had a magnitude of 4.9 and caused about $3.5 million in damages (2024 terms).

### Inner city

[Princes Street](/wiki/Princes_Street,_Dunedin) was developed during Dunedin's 1860s boom from the gold rush, and consequently is one of New Zealand's most historic streets

The central region of Dunedin is known as [the Octagon](/wiki/The_Octagon,_Dunedin). It was once a gully, filled in the mid-nineteenth century to create the present plaza. The initial settlement of the city took place to the south on the other side of [Bell Hill](/wiki/Bell_Hill,_New_Zealand), a large outcrop which had to be reduced to provide easy access between the two parts of the settlement. The central city stretches away from this point in a largely northeast–southwest direction, with the main streets of [George Street](/wiki/George_Street,_Dunedin) and Princes Street meeting at The Octagon. Here they are joined by [Stuart Street](/wiki/Stuart_Street,_Dunedin), which runs orthogonally to them, from the [Dunedin Railway Station](/wiki/Dunedin_Railway_Station) in the southeast, and steeply up to the suburb of [Roslyn](/wiki/Roslyn,_Dunedin) in the northwest. Many of the city's notable old buildings are located in the southern part of this area and on the inner ring of lower hills which surround the central city (most of these hills, such as [Maori Hill](/wiki/Maori_Hill), Pine Hill, and [Maryhill](/wiki/Maryhill,_New_Zealand), rise to some 200 metres [660 ft] above the plain). The head of the harbour includes a large area of reclaimed land ("The Southern Endowment"), much of which is used for light industry and warehousing. A large area of flat land, simply known colloquially as "The Flat" lies to the south and southwest of the city centre, and includes several larger and older suburbs, notably [South Dunedin](/wiki/South_Dunedin) and [St Kilda](/wiki/St_Kilda,_New_Zealand). These are protected from the Pacific Ocean by a long line of dunes which run east–west along the city's southern coastline and separate residential areas from [Ocean Beach](/wiki/Ocean_Beach,_Otago), which is traditionally divided into [St. Clair Beach](/wiki/St._Clair_Beach) at the western end and [St Kilda Beach](/wiki/Ocean_Beach,_Otago) to the east.

Dunedin is home to [Baldwin Street](/wiki/Baldwin_Street), which, according to the *[Guinness Book of Records](/wiki/Guinness_World_Records)*, is the steepest street in the world. Its gradient is 1 in 2.9. The long-since-abandoned Maryhill [Cablecar](/wiki/Cable_car_(railway)) route had a similar gradient close to its Mornington depot.

Beyond the inner range of hills lie Dunedin's outer suburbs, notably to the northwest, beyond Roslyn. This direction contains Taieri Road and Three Mile Hill, which between them formed the original road route to the [Taieri Plains](/wiki/Taieri_Plains). The modern [State Highway 1](/wiki/State_Highway_1_(New_Zealand)) follows a different route, passing through [Caversham](/wiki/Caversham,_New_Zealand) in the west and out past Saddle Hill. Lying between Saddle Hill and Caversham are the outer suburbs of [Green Island and Abbotsford](/wiki/Green_Island,_New_Zealand). Between Green Island and Roslyn lies the steep-sided valley of the [Kaikorai Stream](/wiki/Kaikorai_Stream), which is today a residential and light industrial area. Suburban settlements—mostly regarded as separate townships—also lie along both edges of the Otago Harbour. Notable among these are [Portobello](/wiki/Portobello,_New_Zealand) and [Macandrew Bay](/wiki/Macandrew_Bay), on the [Otago Peninsula](/wiki/Otago_Peninsula) coast, and [Port Chalmers](/wiki/Port_Chalmers) on the opposite side of the harbour. Port Chalmers provides Dunedin's main deep-water port, including the city's container port.

The Dunedin skyline is dominated by a ring of (traditionally seven) hills which form the remnants of a [volcanic crater](/wiki/Volcanic_crater). Notable among them are [Mount Cargill](/wiki/Mount_Cargill) (700 m [2,300 ft]), [Flagstaff](/wiki/Flagstaff,_Otago) (680 m [2,230 ft]), [Saddle Hill](/wiki/Saddle_Hill,_New_Zealand) (480 m [1,570 ft]), [Signal Hill](/wiki/Signal_Hill,_New_Zealand) (390 m [1,280 ft]), and [Harbour Cone](/wiki/Harbour_Cone) (320 m [1,050 ft]).

### Hinterland

Dunedin (grey area to lower left) sits close to the isthmus of the [Otago Peninsula](/wiki/Otago_Peninsula), at the end of [Otago Harbour](/wiki/Otago_Harbour)

Dunedin's hinterland encompasses a variety of different landforms. To the southwest lie the [Taieri Plains](/wiki/Taieri_Plains), the broad, fertile lowland floodplains of the [Taieri River](/wiki/Taieri_River) and its major tributary, the [Waipori](/wiki/Waipori_River). These are moderately heavily settled, and contain the towns of [Mosgiel](/wiki/Mosgiel), and [Allanton](/wiki/Allanton,_New_Zealand). They are separated from the coast by a range of low hills rising to some 300 metres (980 ft). Inland from the Taieri Plain is rough hill country. Close to the plain, much of this is forested, notably around [Berwick](/wiki/Berwick,_New_Zealand) and [Lake Mahinerangi](/wiki/Lake_Mahinerangi), and also around the [Silverpeaks](/wiki/Silverpeaks) Range which lies northwest of the Dunedin urban area. Beyond this, the land becomes drier and opens out into grass and [tussock](/wiki/Tussock_grass)-covered land. A high, broad valley, the [Strath-Taieri](/wiki/Strath-Taieri) lies in Dunedin's far northwest, containing the town of [Middlemarch](/wiki/Middlemarch,_New_Zealand), one of the area's few concentrations of population.

To the north of the city's urban area is undulating hill country containing several small, mainly coastal, settlements, including [Waitati](/wiki/Waitati), [Warrington](/wiki/Warrington,_New_Zealand), [Seacliff](/wiki/Seacliff,_New_Zealand), and [Waikouaiti](/wiki/Waikouaiti). [State Highway 1](/wiki/State_Highway_1_(New_Zealand)) winds steeply through a series of hills here, notably [The Kilmog](/wiki/The_Kilmog). These hills can be considered a coastal extension of the Silverpeaks Range.

### Environment and ecotourism

To the east of Dunedin lies the entirety of the [Otago Peninsula](/wiki/Otago_Peninsula), a long finger of land that formed the southeastern rim of the [Dunedin Volcano](/wiki/Dunedin_Volcano). The peninsula is lightly settled, almost entirely along the harbour coast, and much of it is maintained as a [natural habitat](/wiki/Habitat) by the Otago Peninsula Trust. The peninsula contains several fine beaches, and is home to a considerable number of [rare species](/wiki/Rare_species), including [yellow-eyed](/wiki/Yellow-eyed_penguin) and [little penguins](/wiki/Little_penguin), [seals](/wiki/Pinniped), and [shags](/wiki/Cormorant). [Taiaroa Head](/wiki/Taiaroa_Head), on the peninsula's northeastern point, is a site of global ecological significance, as it is home to the world's only mainland breeding colony of [royal albatross](/wiki/Southern_royal_albatross).

### Climate

The climate of Dunedin in general is temperate. Under the [Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification), Dunedin features an [oceanic climate](/wiki/Oceanic_climate). This leads to mild summers and coolish winters. Winter is not particularly frosty with around 49 frosts per year, lower than most other South Island locations, but sunny. Snowfall is not particularly common and significant snowfall is uncommon (perhaps every two or three years), except in the inland hill suburbs such as Halfway Bush and Wakari, which tend to receive a few days of snowfall each year. Spring can feature "four seasons in a day" weather, but from November to April it is generally settled and mild. Temperatures during summer can reach 30 °C (86 °F). Due to its maritime influence, Dunedin's mild summers and mild winters both stand out considering its latitude.

Dunedin has relatively low rainfall in comparison to many of New Zealand's cities, with usually only between 600 and 750 millimetres (30 in) recorded per year. However, wet weather is frequent, since much of this rainfall occurs in drizzle or light rain and heavy rain is relatively rare. Dunedin is one of the cloudiest major centres in the country, recording approximately 1,850 hours of bright sunshine per annum. [Prevailing wind](/wiki/Prevailing_wind) in the city is mainly a sometimes cool southwesterly and during late spring will alternate with northeasterlies. Warmer, dry northwest winds are also characteristic [Foehn winds from the northwest](/wiki/Nor%27west_arch). The circle of hills surrounding the [inner city](/wiki/Inner_city) shelters the inner city from much of the prevailing weather, while hills just to the west of the city can often push inclement weather around to the west of the city.

Inland, beyond the heart of the city and into inland Otago, the climate is sub-continental: winters are quite cold and dry, summers warm and dry. Thick freezing ground fogs are common in winter in the upper reaches of the [Taieri River](/wiki/Taieri_River)'s course around [Middlemarch](/wiki/Middlemarch,_New_Zealand), and in summer, the temperature occasionally reaches 30 °C (86 °F).

Religious affiliations were 27.1% [Christian](/wiki/Christianity_in_New_Zealand), 1.2% [Hindu](/wiki/Hinduism_in_New_Zealand), 1.4% [Islam](/wiki/Islam_in_New_Zealand), 0.3% [Māori religious beliefs](/wiki/M%C4%81ori_religious_beliefs), 0.8% [Buddhist](/wiki/Buddhism_in_New_Zealand), 0.6% [New Age](/wiki/New_Age), 0.1% [Jewish](/wiki/Judaism_in_New_Zealand), and 1.7% other religions. People who answered that they had [no religion](/wiki/Irreligion_in_New_Zealand) were 60.6%, and 6.4% of people did not answer the census question.

Of those at least 15 years old, 20,169 (23.3%) people had a bachelor's or higher degree, 45,513 (52.7%) had a post-high school certificate or diploma, and 14,928 (17.3%) people exclusively held high school qualifications. The median income was $31,800, compared with $41,500 nationally. 7,266 people (8.4%) earned over $100,000 compared to 12.1% nationally. The employment status of those at least 15 was that 37,755 (43.7%) people were employed full-time, 14,250 (16.5%) were part-time, and 3,099 (3.6%) were unemployed.

### Literature

In December 2014, Dunedin was designated as a [UNESCO Creative City of Literature](/wiki/City_of_Literature). Mayor of Dunedin Dave Cull said at the time, "This announcement puts our city on the world map as a first-class literary city. We keep honourable company; other cities bestowed with City of Literature status include Edinburgh, Dublin, Iowa City, Melbourne, Reykjavík, Norwich and Kraków."

Dunedin's application was driven by a steering committee and an advisory board of writers, librarians and academics from a range of Dunedin institutions. The bid highlighted the quality of the city's considerable literary heritage, its diverse combination of literary events, businesses, institutions and organisations, plus its thriving community of writers, playwrights and lyricists.

UNESCO established the Creative Cities Network to develop international co-operation among cities and encourage them to drive joint development partnerships in line with UNESCO's global priorities of 'culture and development' and 'sustainable development'. Each city in the network reflects one of UNESCO's seven Creative City themes: folk art, gastronomy, literature, design, film or music. Dunedin is New Zealand's first city to be appointed to the Creative City network.

[Paul Theroux](/wiki/Paul_Theroux) described Dunedin as "cold and frugal with its shabby streets and mock-gothic university". The university students he described as "ignorant, assertive and dirty". [Billy Connolly](/wiki/Billy_Connolly) described Dunedin as "a dreary town. It's got that Scottish Presbyterian feel about it". [Michael Palin](/wiki/Michael_Palin) in [Full Circle](/wiki/Full_Circle_with_Michael_Palin) says of Dunedin "at first glance it is a dour, damp, chilly place, its buildings heavy with ponderous Presbyterian pride...but beneath a grey and sober heart there lurks a wild heart." In 1895, [Mark Twain](/wiki/Mark_Twain) said of the Scots who settled in Dunedin, "They stopped here on their way from home to heaven – thinking they had arrived".

### Music

#### Choirs

Dunedin is home to many choirs. These include the following:

- The 140-member [City of Dunedin Choir](/wiki/City_of_Dunedin_Choir) is Dunedin's leading performer of large-scale choral works.
- The [Southern Consort of Voices](/wiki/Southern_Consort_of_Voices) is a smaller choir regularly performing Choral Works.
- The [Royal Dunedin Male Choir](/wiki/Royal_Dunedin_Male_Choir), conducted by Richard Madden, performs two concerts a year
- The Dunedin RSA Choir regularly performs concerts and has played an important and valued role in Dunedin City's commemorative celebrations of significant historical events. ANZAC, of course, is one such occasion, and the ANZAC Revue held on the evening of every ANZAC Day, occupies a special place of honour in the choir's calendar.
- The all-female Dunedin Harmony Chorus are an important part of the Dunedin culture.
- The Southern Children's Choir, based at [Marama Hall](/wiki/Marama_Hall) in the university, is Dunedin's main children's choir. Most schools in Dunedin have choirs, many having more than one.
- The Southern Youth Choir is a concert-based youth choir.
- The [University of Otago](/wiki/University_of_Otago) is home to three official choirs: the two chapel choirs (Knox and Selwyn), and the travelling Cantores choir.
- Several Dunedin Churches and Cathedrals hold choirs. Among these are [St. Joseph's Catholic Cathedral](/wiki/St._Joseph%27s_Cathedral,_Dunedin), home to two choirs: the Cathedral Choir and the Gabrieli Singers; [Knox Church](/wiki/Knox_Church,_Dunedin)'s large mixed gender choir for adults and children, the Knox Church Choir; [All Saints' Church, Dunedin](/wiki/All_Saints%27_Church,_Dunedin), has choral scholars from [Selwyn College, Otago](/wiki/Selwyn_College,_Otago), St. John's Church, Roslyn's small mixed-gender parish choir; and [St. Paul's Anglican Cathedral](/wiki/St._Paul%27s_Cathedral,_Dunedin)'s mixed-gender adult choir.
- The Dunedin [Red Cross](/wiki/Red_Cross) Choir (of New Zealand Red Cross), conducted by Eleanor Moyle, is one of only three Red Cross choirs globally. Established in 1942, this choir performs regularly in Dunedin at various Rest Homes and holds an annual concert at the Kings and Queens Performing Arts Centre.

#### Instrumental classical and jazz ensembles

The Dunedin Symphony Orchestra is a semi-professional orchestra based in Dunedin. Other instrumental ensembles include the Rare Byrds early music ensemble, the Collegiate Orchestra, and the Dunedin Youth Orchestra. Many schools also hold school orchestras and bands. There are also three brass bands in Dunedin: St. Kilda Brass, Kaikorai Brass, and Mosgiel Brass. The Otago Symphonic Band and [City of Dunedin Pipe Band](/wiki/City_of_Dunedin_Pipe_Band_(New_Zealand)) are also important Dunedin musical ensembles.

#### Popular music

Dunedin lends its name to the [Dunedin sound](/wiki/Dunedin_sound), a form of [indie rock](/wiki/Indie_rock) music which was created in the city in the 1980s. Some Dunedin bands recorded on the [Flying Nun Records](/wiki/Flying_Nun_Records) label, based in Christchurch. Among the bands with Dunedin connections were [The Chills](/wiki/The_Chills), [The Clean](/wiki/The_Clean), [The Verlaines](/wiki/The_Verlaines), [The Bats](/wiki/The_Bats_(New_Zealand_band)), [Sneaky Feelings](/wiki/Sneaky_Feelings), [The Dead C](/wiki/The_Dead_C) and [Straitjacket Fits](/wiki/Straitjacket_Fits), all of which had significant followings throughout New Zealand and on the college radio circuit in the United States and Europe.

Dunedin has been home to bands since the end of the Dunedin sound era. [Six60](/wiki/Six60), [Nadia Reid](/wiki/Nadia_Reid) and [Julian Temple Band](/wiki/Julian_Temple_Band) are Dunedin artists.

### Theatre

[Fortune Theatre](/wiki/Fortune_Theatre,_Dunedin) laid claim to being the world's southernmost professional theatre company

The city hosts a large theatre venue, the [Regent Theatre](/wiki/Regent_Theatre,_Dunedin) in the Octagon. Dunedin hosted the world's southernmost professional theatre company, the [Fortune Theatre](/wiki/Fortune_Theatre,_Dunedin), based in the [former Trinity Methodist Church](/wiki/Former_Trinity_Methodist_Church,_Dunedin), until it closed in 2018. Smaller theatres in Dunedin include the [Globe Theatre](/wiki/Globe_Theatre,_Dunedin), the [Mayfair Theatre](/wiki/Mayfair_Theatre,_Dunedin), the [New Athenaeum Theatre](/wiki/Dunedin_Athenaeum_and_Mechanics%27_Institute#New_Athenaeum_Theatre), and the [Playhouse Theatre](/wiki/Playhouse_Theatre,_Dunedin).

### Visual arts

Dunedin has a substantial public art gallery, the [Dunedin Public Art Gallery](/wiki/Dunedin_Public_Art_Gallery), in the Octagon. The city contains numerous other galleries, including over a dozen dealer galleries, many of which are found south of the Octagon along [Princes Street](/wiki/Princes_Street,_Dunedin), [Moray Place](/wiki/Moray_Place,_Dunedin) and Dowling Street. There are also several more experimental art spaces, notably the [Blue Oyster](/wiki/Blue_Oyster_Art_Project_Space) in Dowling Street.

Many notable artists have strong links with Dunedin, among them [Ralph Hotere](/wiki/Ralph_Hotere), [Frances Hodgkins](/wiki/Frances_Hodgkins), [Grahame Sydney](/wiki/Grahame_Sydney), and [Jeffrey Harris](/wiki/Jeffrey_Harris_(artist)).

### National

Dunedin is covered by two [general electorates](/wiki/New_Zealand_electorates#General_electorates), [Dunedin](/wiki/Dunedin_(New_Zealand_electorate)) and [Taieri](/wiki/Taieri_(New_Zealand_electorate)), and one [Māori electorate](/wiki/M%C4%81ori_electorates), [Te Tai Tonga](/wiki/Te_Tai_Tonga).

The city in general is a stronghold of the [New Zealand Labour Party](/wiki/New_Zealand_Labour_Party), having won the Dunedin-based electorate seats continuously since the [1978 election](/wiki/1978_New_Zealand_general_election). As of the [2023 general election](/wiki/2023_New_Zealand_general_election), both general electorates are held by the party, with [Rachel Brooking](/wiki/Rachel_Brooking) representing [Dunedin](/wiki/Dunedin_(New_Zealand_electorate)) and [Ingrid Leary](/wiki/Ingrid_Leary) representing [Taieri](/wiki/Taieri_(New_Zealand_electorate)). [Scott Willis](/wiki/Scott_Willis_(politician)) and [Mark Patterson](/wiki/Mark_Patterson_(New_Zealand_politician)) are both List MPs from the Taieri electorate.

## Media

The major daily newspaper is the *[Otago Daily Times](/wiki/Otago_Daily_Times)*, which is also the country's oldest daily newspaper and part of the [Allied Media](/wiki/Allied_Media) (formerly Allied Press) group. Weekly and bi-weekly community newspapers include *[The Star](/wiki/The_Star_(Dunedin))*, and the university student magazine *[Critic Te Ārohi](/wiki/Critic_Te_%C4%80rohi)*.

The city is served by all major national radio and television stations. The city's main terrestrial television and FM radio transmitter sits atop [Mount Cargill](/wiki/Mount_Cargill), north of the city, while the city's main AM transmitter is located at [Highcliff](/wiki/Shiel_Hill#Highcliff), east of the city centre on the Otago Peninsula. Local radio stations include [Radio Dunedin](/wiki/Radio_Dunedin), community station [Otago Access Radio](/wiki/Otago_Access_Radio) (formerly Hills AM, then Toroa Radio), and the university radio station, [Radio One](/wiki/Radio_One_(New_Zealand)).

Television broadcasts began in Dunedin on 31 July 1962 with the launch of channel [DNTV2](/wiki/DNTV2), the last of the four main centres to receive television. In November 1969, DNTV2 was networked with its counterpart stations in Auckland, Wellington and Christchurch to form NZBC TV. In 1975, the NZBC was broken up, with the Wellington and Dunedin studios taking over NZBC TV as [Television One](/wiki/TVNZ_1) (now TVNZ 1) while Auckland and Christchurch studios launched [Television Two](/wiki/TVNZ_2) (now TVNZ 2).

The city had one local television station called [Channel 39](/wiki/Channel_39_(New_Zealand)), which was owned by Allied Press. It closed on Christmas 2025.

The television news broadcaster [1News](/wiki/1News) along with [Radio New Zealand](/wiki/Radio_New_Zealand) and [NZME](/wiki/New_Zealand_Media_and_Entertainment) also have bureaus in Dunedin. In May 2021, the former television news broadcaster [Newshub](/wiki/Newshub)'s owner [Discovery, Inc.](/wiki/Discovery,_Inc.) announced that it would be closing down its Dunedin bureau as part of a restructuring process.

The city is home to several prominent media-related production companies, notably [Natural History New Zealand](/wiki/Natural_History_New_Zealand) and [Taylormade Media](/wiki/Taylormade_Media).

The city was once home to the head offices of [Radio Otago](/wiki/Radio_Otago)—now called [RadioWorks](/wiki/RadioWorks) (part of [Mediaworks](/wiki/MediaWorks_New_Zealand)) and based in Auckland. It was also formerly the home to several now-defunct newspapers, prominent among which were the *[Otago Witness](/wiki/Otago_Witness)* and the *[Evening Star](/wiki/Evening_Star_(Dunedin))*.

## Infrastructure and services

### Public health and hospitals

[Dunedin Hospital](/wiki/Dunedin_Hospital) is the main public hospital in Dunedin. Other hospitals include:

- Mercy Hospital – a private non-profit hospital opened in 1936 and relocated to Maori Hill in 1969
- [Wakari Hospital](/wiki/Wakari_Hospital)

The Dunedin Hospital and the Wakari Hospital, which are closely related, are operated by [Te Whatu Ora](/wiki/Te_Whatu_Ora). Ambulance services are provided by [St John New Zealand](/wiki/St_John_New_Zealand).

### Utilities

[Aurora Energy](/wiki/Aurora_Energy_(New_Zealand)) owns and operates the electricity distribution network servicing the city and the Taieri plains, while OtagoNet Joint Venture owns and operates the electricity distribution network in the rural areas north and west of the city. Electricity is primarily supplied from [Transpower](/wiki/Transpower_New_Zealand)'s national grid at two substations: Halfway Bush and South Dunedin, with part to the OtagoNet network also supplied from Transpower's [Naseby](/wiki/Naseby,_New_Zealand) substation in central Otago.

## Transport

### Road

The Dunedin urban area is served by two [state highways](/wiki/New_Zealand_state_highway_network), with an additional two state highways and one tourist route serving other parts of the district. The main state highway in Dunedin is [State Highway 1](/wiki/State_Highway_1_(New_Zealand)), which runs in a north to south-west direction through the middle of the city, connecting Dunedin with Invercargill to the south and Timaru and Christchurch to the north. Between The Oval and Mosgiel, State Highway 1 follows the eleven-kilometre [Dunedin Southern Motorway](/wiki/Dunedin_Southern_Motorway). [State Highway 88](/wiki/New_Zealand_State_Highway_88) connects central Dunedin to the city's port facilities at [Port Chalmers](/wiki/Port_Chalmers).

Other State Highways in the city are: [State Highway 86](/wiki/New_Zealand_State_Highway_86) connecting SH 1 at Allanton with Dunedin International Airport, [State Highway 87](/wiki/New_Zealand_State_Highway_87) connecting SH 1 at Kinmont with [SH 85](/wiki/New_Zealand_State_Highway_85) at Kyeburn via Middlemarch, serving the Dunedin city hinterland.

Dunedin is the northeastern terminus of the [Southern Scenic Route](/wiki/Southern_Scenic_Route), a tourist highway connecting Dunedin to Te Anau via [The Catlins](/wiki/The_Catlins), [Invercargill](/wiki/Invercargill) and [Fiordland](/wiki/Fiordland).

Orbus in Dunedin.

### Bus

Main article: [Buses in Dunedin](/wiki/Buses_in_Dunedin)

Buses in Dunedin are organised by the Otago Regional Council. A total of 64 buses operate on 17 weekday routes and 13 weeknight/weekend/holiday routes across the city. Buses are run by two operators, Ritchies Transport with three routes and Go Bus Transport with the remainder. Dunedin City Council-owned operator [Citibus](/wiki/Citibus_(New_Zealand)) was a major player until 2011 when Passenger Transport (New Zealand) purchased Citibus from Dunedin City Holdings, and both companies were subsequently bought by Go Bus.

### Rail

[Dunedin Railway Station](/wiki/Dunedin_Railway_Station), located east of the Octagon, is the city's main railway station. Once the nation's busiest, the decline in rail over the years saw the withdrawal of most services. Suburban services ceased in 1982, and the last regular commercial passenger train to serve Dunedin, [The Southerner](/wiki/Southerner_(New_Zealand_train)), was cancelled in February 2002. The [Taieri Gorge Railway](/wiki/Taieri_Gorge_Railway) now operates tourist-oriented services from the station, the most prominent of which is the Taieri Gorge Limited, which operates daily along the former [Otago Central Railway](/wiki/Otago_Central_Railway) through the scenic [Taieri Gorge](/wiki/Taieri_Gorge). Taieri Gorge Railway also operates to [Palmerston](/wiki/Palmerston,_New_Zealand) once weekly. The station is also sometimes visited by excursions organised by other [heritage railway](/wiki/Heritage_railway) societies, and by trains chartered by [cruise ships](/wiki/Cruise_ship) docking at Port Chalmers.

### Air

[Dunedin International Airport](/wiki/Dunedin_International_Airport) – an Air New Zealand 737 lands on the runway while an Air New Zealand A320 waits on the taxiway

[Dunedin International Airport](/wiki/Dunedin_International_Airport) is located 22 km (13.67 mi) southwest of the city, on the [Taieri Plains](/wiki/Taieri_Plains) at [Momona](/wiki/Momona). The airport operates a single terminal and 1,900-metre (6,200 ft) runway, and is the third-busiest airport in the South Island, after Christchurch and Queenstown. It is primarily used for domestic flights, with regular flights to and from [Auckland](/wiki/Auckland), [Christchurch](/wiki/Christchurch), [Wellington](/wiki/Wellington) and charter flights to and from [Queenstown](/wiki/Queenstown,_New_Zealand), [Wānaka](/wiki/W%C4%81naka), and [Invercargill](/wiki/Invercargill), but it also has international flights arriving from and departing to [Gold Coast, Queensland](/wiki/Gold_Coast,_Queensland) year round. In recent years, a decline in international passengers can be attributed to fewer international flights operating direct to the airport.

### Sea

As of 2018, a ferry operates between [Port Chalmers](/wiki/Port_Chalmers) and [Portobello](/wiki/Portobello,_New_Zealand) it started in 2018 and is the first since the early 20th century. Occasional calls have been made to revive them, and a non-profit organisation, Otago Ferries Inc., has been set up to examine the logistics of restoring one of the original ferries and again using it for this route.

In 1866, plans were made for a bridge across the [Otago Harbour](/wiki/Otago_Harbour) between Port Chalmers and Portobello, but this grand scheme for an 1140-metre structure never eventuated. Plans were also mooted during the 1870s for a canal between the Pacific coast at [Tomahawk](/wiki/Tomahawk,_New_Zealand) and [Andersons Bay](/wiki/Andersons_Bay), close to the head of the harbour. This scheme also never came to fruition.

## Events

### Annual events

- January – [Whare Flat Folk Festival](/wiki/Whare_Flat_Folk_Festival) ends
- February – New Zealand Masters Games (Biennial event)
- February – Otago University Students' Association & Otago Polytechnic [Orientation](/wiki/Student_orientation) Weeks
- February – Dunedin Summer Festival
- March – [Dunedin Fringe Festival](/wiki/Dunedin_Fringe_Festival)
- March/April – [iD Dunedin Fashion Week](/wiki/ID_Dunedin_Fashion_Week)
- May – [Capping week](/wiki/Capping_week) (University of Otago) including the [Capping Show](/wiki/Capping_Show) run by the [Otago University Students' Association](/wiki/Otago_University_Students%27_Association)
- May – International Rally of Otago
- May – [Dunedin Writers & Readers Festival](/wiki/Dunedin_Writers_%26_Readers_Festival)
- May – [Regent Theatre 24-hour book sale](/wiki/Regent_Theatre,_Dunedin) (reputedly the southern hemisphere's largest regularly held [second-hand book](/wiki/Used_book) sale)
- June – Dunedin Midwinter Carnival
- June – St. Clair [Polar Plunge](/wiki/Polar_plunge)
- July – [University Reorientation](/wiki/Student_orientation)
- July – New Zealand International Science Festival (every second year)
- July – Taste Otago Dunedin Food and Wine Festival
- July – [Dunedin International Film Festival](/wiki/New_Zealand_International_Film_Festivals#International_Film_Festival,_Dunedin)
- September – Dunedin City Marathon
- September – Dunedin Beer Festival
- October – Dunedin Arts Festival – every second year (even numbered years)
- October – [Rhododendron Week](/wiki/Dunedin_Botanic_Garden)
- December – Samstock Music Festival
- December – [Santa Parade](/wiki/Santa_Parade)
- December – [Whare Flat Folk Festival](/wiki/Whare_Flat_Folk_Festival) begins
- December – New Year's Eve Party Octagon
