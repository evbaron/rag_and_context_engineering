This article is about the capital city of England and the United Kingdom. For other uses, see [London (disambiguation)](/wiki/London_(disambiguation)).

**London** is the [capital](/wiki/Capital_city) and [largest city](/wiki/List_of_urban_areas_in_the_United_Kingdom) of [England](/wiki/England) and the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom), with a population of 9.1 million people in 2024. [Its wider metropolitan area](/wiki/London_metropolitan_area) is the largest in [Western Europe](/wiki/Western_Europe), with a population of 15.1 million. London stands on the [River Thames](/wiki/River_Thames) in southeast England, at the head of a 50-mile (80 km) [tidal estuary](/wiki/Tidal_estuary) down to the [North Sea](/wiki/North_Sea), and has been a major settlement for nearly 2,000 years. Its ancient core and [financial centre](/wiki/Financial_centre), the [City of London](/wiki/City_of_London), was founded by the [Romans](/wiki/Roman_Empire) as [Londinium](/wiki/Londinium) and has retained its medieval boundaries. The [City of Westminster](/wiki/City_of_Westminster), to the west of the City of London, has been the site of [the national government](/wiki/Government_of_the_United_Kingdom) and [parliament](/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom) for centuries. London grew rapidly [in the 19th century](/wiki/19th-century_London), becoming the world's [largest city at the time](/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history). Since the 19th century the name "London" has referred to the [metropolis](/wiki/Metropolis) around the City of London, historically split among the [counties](/wiki/Counties_of_England) of [Middlesex](/wiki/Middlesex), [Essex](/wiki/Essex), [Surrey](/wiki/Surrey), [Kent](/wiki/Kent) and [Hertfordshire](/wiki/Hertfordshire). Since 1965 it has largely comprised the administrative area of [Greater London](/wiki/Greater_London), governed by [33 local authorities](#Local_government) and the [Greater London Authority](/wiki/Greater_London_Authority).

As one of the world's major [global cities](/wiki/Global_cities), London exerts a strong influence on world art, [architecture](/wiki/Architecture_of_London), [culture](/wiki/Culture_of_London), [cinema](/wiki/London_in_film), entertainment, [fashion](/wiki/Fashion_capital), commerce, finance, [education](/wiki/Education_in_London), [healthcare](/wiki/Healthcare_in_London), [media](/wiki/Media_in_London), science, technology, [tourism](/wiki/Tourism_in_London), [transport](/wiki/Transport_in_London) and communications. London is Europe's largest city economy and [one of the world's major financial centres](/wiki/Global_Financial_Centres_Index). London is home to several of the world's leading academic institutions and hosts Europe's largest concentration of [higher education institutions](/wiki/List_of_universities_and_higher_education_colleges_in_London), comprising over 50 universities and colleges collectively enrolling more than 500,000 students in 2023. It is the [most-visited city](/wiki/List_of_cities_by_international_visitors) in Europe and has the world's [busiest city airport system](/wiki/List_of_busiest_city_airport_systems_by_passenger_traffic). The [London Underground](/wiki/London_Underground) is the world's oldest [rapid transit](/wiki/Rapid_transit) system.

London's diverse cultures encompass over 300 languages. The 2025 population of Greater London of just over 9.8 million made it Europe's [third-most populous city](/wiki/List_of_European_cities_by_population_within_city_limits), accounting for 13.1 per cent of the United Kingdom's population and 15.5 per cent of England's population. The [Greater London Built-up Area](/wiki/Greater_London_Built-up_Area) is the [fourth-most populous in Europe](/wiki/List_of_urban_areas_in_Europe), with about 9.8 million inhabitants as of 2011. The London metropolitan area is the [third-most-populous in Europe](/wiki/List_of_metropolitan_areas_in_Europe), with about 15 million inhabitants as of 2025, making London a [megacity](/wiki/Megacity).

Landmarks include [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London), [Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey), [Buckingham Palace](/wiki/Buckingham_Palace), the [London Eye](/wiki/London_Eye), [Piccadilly Circus](/wiki/Piccadilly_Circus), [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral), [Tower Bridge](/wiki/Tower_Bridge) and [Trafalgar Square](/wiki/Trafalgar_Square). The city [has the most museums](/wiki/List_of_museums_in_London), art galleries, libraries and [cultural venues](/wiki/List_of_London_venues) in the UK, including the [British Museum](/wiki/British_Museum), the [National Gallery](/wiki/National_Gallery), the [Natural History Museum](/wiki/Natural_History_Museum,_London), [Tate Modern](/wiki/Tate_Modern), the [British Library](/wiki/British_Library) and numerous [West End theatres](/wiki/West_End_theatre). [Annual sporting events](/wiki/Sport_in_London) include the [FA Cup Final](/wiki/FA_Cup_Final), the [Wimbledon Tennis Championships](/wiki/The_Championships,_Wimbledon) and the [London Marathon](/wiki/London_Marathon). It became the first city to host three [Summer Olympic Games](/wiki/Summer_Olympic_Games) upon hosting the [2012 Summer Olympics](/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics).

## Etymology

Main article: [Etymology of *London*](/wiki/Etymology_of_London)

The name *London* is attested in the first century AD, usually in the [Latinised](/wiki/Latinisation_of_names) form *[Londinium](/wiki/Londinium)*. Modern scientific analyses of the name must account for the origins of the different forms that are found in early sources, such as [Latin](/wiki/Latin) *Londinium*, [Old English](/wiki/Old_English) *Lunden*, and [Welsh](/wiki/Welsh_language) *Llundein*, with reference to the known developments over time of sounds in those different languages. The name came into these languages from [Common Brythonic](/wiki/Common_Brittonic); the lost Celtic form of the name was most likely \**Londonjon*.

Until 1889, the name "London" applied officially only to the [City of London](/wiki/City_of_London), but since then it has also denoted the [County of London](/wiki/County_of_London) and [Greater London](/wiki/Greater_London).

## History

Main article: [History of London](/wiki/History_of_London)

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of London](/wiki/Timeline_of_London).

### Prehistory

In 1993 remains of a [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age) bridge were found on the south River Thames [foreshore](/wiki/Foreshore), upstream from [Vauxhall Bridge](/wiki/Vauxhall_Bridge). Two of the timbers were [radiocarbon dated](/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating) to 1750–1285 BC. In 2010 foundations of a large timber structure, dated to 4800–4500 BC, were found on the Thames' south foreshore downstream from Vauxhall Bridge. Both are on the south bank of the Thames, where the now-underground [River Effra](/wiki/River_Effra) flows into the Thames.

### Roman London

Main article: [Londinium](/wiki/Londinium)

Inscription from [Londinium](/wiki/Londinium), held at the Museum of London, with the Romans' first mention of the *Londiniensi* ('Londoners')

A surviving section of the 3rd-century [London Wall](/wiki/London_Wall) behind [Tower Hill](/wiki/Tower_Hill)

Despite the evidence of scattered [Brythonic](/wiki/Celtic_Britons) settlements in the area, the first major settlement was founded by the [Romans](/wiki/Ancient_Rome) around AD 47, about 4 years after their invasion of AD 43. This only lasted until about AD 61, when the [Iceni](/wiki/Iceni) tribe led by [Queen Boudica](/wiki/Boudica) stormed it and burnt it to the ground.

The next planned incarnation of [Londinium](/wiki/Londinium) prospered, superseding [Colchester](/wiki/Colchester) as the principal city of the [Roman province](/wiki/Roman_province) of [Britannia](/wiki/Roman_Britain) in 100. At its height in the 2nd century, Roman London had a population of about 60,000.

### Anglo-Saxon and Viking-period London

Main article: [Anglo-Saxon London](/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_London)

With the early-5th-century collapse of Roman rule, the walled city of Londinium was effectively abandoned, although [Roman civilisation](/wiki/Roman_civilisation) continued around [St Martin-in-the-Fields](/wiki/St_Martin-in-the-Fields) until about 450. From about 500 an [Anglo-Saxon](/wiki/Anglo-Saxon) settlement known as [Lundenwic](/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_London) developed slightly west of the old Roman city. By about 680 the city had become a major port again, but there is little evidence of large-scale production. From the 820s repeated [Viking](/wiki/Viking) assaults brought decline.

The Vikings applied [Danelaw](/wiki/Danelaw) over much of [eastern](/wiki/East_of_England) and [northern England](/wiki/Northern_England) from 886; The *[Anglo-Saxon Chronicle](/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle)* records that Alfred "refounded" London that year. This involved the abandonment of Lundenwic and a revival of life and trade within the old [Roman walls](/wiki/London_Wall). London then grew slowly until a dramatic increase in about 950.

By the 11th century, London was clearly the largest town in England. [Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey), rebuilt in [Romanesque](/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) style by King [Edward the Confessor](/wiki/Edward_the_Confessor), was one of the grandest churches in Europe. [Winchester](/wiki/Winchester) had been the capital of [Anglo-Saxon England](/wiki/History_of_Anglo-Saxon_England), but from this time, London became the main forum for foreign traders and the base for defence in time of war. In the view of [Frank Stenton](/wiki/Frank_Stenton): "It had the resources, and it was rapidly developing the dignity and the political self-consciousness appropriate to a national capital."

### Middle Ages

[Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey), as seen in this painting ([Canaletto](/wiki/Canaletto), 1749), is a [World Heritage Site](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site) and one of London's oldest buildings.

After winning the [Battle of Hastings](/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings), [William, Duke of Normandy](/wiki/William_the_Conqueror), was crowned [King of England](/wiki/King_of_England) as William I in the newly completed [Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey) on Christmas Day 1066. He built the [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London) to intimidate the inhabitants. In 1097 [William II](/wiki/William_II_of_England) began building [Westminster Hall](/wiki/Westminster_Hall), near the abbey. It became the basis of a new [Palace of Westminster](/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster).

In the 12th century the institutions of [central government](/wiki/Central_government), which had followed the royal English court around the country, grew in size and sophistication and became increasingly fixed, for most purposes at Westminster, although the royal treasury came to rest in the Tower of London. While the City of Westminster developed into a true governmental capital, its distinct neighbour, the City of London, remained England's largest city and principal commercial centre and flourished under its own unique administration, the [Corporation of London](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation). In 1100, its population was some 18,000; by 1300, it had grown to nearly 100,000. With the [Black Death](/wiki/Black_Death) in the mid-14th century, London lost nearly a third of its population. London was the focus of the [Peasants' Revolt](/wiki/Peasants%27_Revolt) in 1381.

London was a centre of England's [Jewish population](/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_England_(1066%E2%80%931290)) before [their expulsion](/wiki/Edict_of_Expulsion) by [Edward I](/wiki/Edward_I_of_England) in 1290. Violence against Jews occurred in 1190, when it was rumoured that [Richard I](/wiki/Richard_I_of_England) had ordered their massacre after some of their leaders had presented themselves at his coronation. In 1264, during the [Second Barons' War](/wiki/Second_Barons%27_War), [Simon de Montfort](/wiki/Simon_de_Montfort,_6th_Earl_of_Leicester)'s rebels killed 500 Jews while attempting to seize records of debts.

### Early modern

The [Reformation](/wiki/English_Reformation) produced a shift to [Protestantism](/wiki/Protestantism). Much property passed into private ownership, accelerating business in the city. In 1475 the [Hanseatic League](/wiki/Hanseatic_League) set up a trading base. [Woollen](/wiki/Woollen) cloth was shipped undyed from 14th- and 15th-century London to the [Low Countries](/wiki/Low_Countries). The reopening of the Netherlands to English shipping in January 1565 spurred a burst of commercial activity. [Mercantilism](/wiki/Mercantilism) grew and monopoly traders such as the [East India Company](/wiki/East_India_Company) were founded as trade expanded to the [New World](/wiki/New_World). London became the main [North Sea](/wiki/North_Sea) port, with migrants from England and abroad. The population rose from about 50,000 in 1530 to about 225,000 in 1605. [Shakespeare](/wiki/William_Shakespeare) built his [Globe Theatre](/wiki/Globe_Theatre) in [Southwark](/wiki/Southwark) in 1599. Stage performances halted when [Puritan](/wiki/Puritan) authorities [shut down the theatres](/wiki/London_theatre_closure_1642) in the 1640s. The ban was lifted during [the 1660 Restoration](/wiki/Stuart_Restoration); London's oldest theatre, [Drury Lane](/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Drury_Lane), opened in 1663. By the end of the Tudor period in 1603, London was still compact. There was an assassination attempt on [James I](/wiki/James_I_of_England) in Westminster, in the [Gunpowder Plot](/wiki/Gunpowder_Plot) of 5 November 1605. In 1637 the government of [Charles I](/wiki/Charles_I_of_England) attempted to reform London's administration; the Corporation made "The Great Refusal", explaining the City of London's unique governmental status.

In the [English Civil War](/wiki/English_Civil_War), most Londoners supported the [Roundheads](/wiki/Roundhead). After an advance by the [Cavaliers](/wiki/Cavaliers) in 1642, culminating in the battles of [Brentford](/wiki/Battle_of_Brentford_(1642)) and [Turnham Green](/wiki/Battle_of_Turnham_Green), London was surrounded by [a defensive perimeter wall](/wiki/Lines_of_Communication_(London)). The fortifications failed their only test when the [New Model Army](/wiki/New_Model_Army) entered London in 1647. London suffered the [Great Plague](/wiki/Great_Plague_of_London) of 1665–1666, killing some 100,000 people, a fifth of the population. In 1666, the [Great Fire](/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London) destroyed much of the wooden-built city. Rebuilding took over 10 years, supervised by the polymath [Robert Hooke](/wiki/Robert_Hooke).

In 1710, [Christopher Wren](/wiki/Christopher_Wren)'s masterpiece [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral) was completed, replacing its medieval predecessor, lost in the Great Fire. Its dome dominated the [London skyline](/wiki/London_skyline) for centuries, inspiring [William Blake](/wiki/William_Blake), whose 1789 poem "[Holy Thursday](/wiki/Holy_Thursday_(Songs_of_Innocence))" mentions 'the high dome of Pauls'. During the [Georgian era](/wiki/Georgian_era) new districts such as [Mayfair](/wiki/Mayfair) were formed in the west, while new bridges encouraged development in [South London](/wiki/South_London). In the east, the [Port of London](/wiki/Port_of_London) expanded downstream, while the [financial centre](/wiki/Financial_centre) matured. In 1762 [George III](/wiki/George_III_of_Great_Britain) acquired [Buckingham House](/wiki/Buckingham_Palace). During the 18th century London was dogged by crime, and the [Bow Street Runners](/wiki/Bow_Street_Runners) were established in 1750 as a professional police force. Epidemics during the 1720s and 30s saw most children born in the city die before reaching their fifth birthday. Growing [literacy](/wiki/Literacy) and printed papers made news widely available; [Fleet Street](/wiki/Fleet_Street) became the centre of the British press, and Londoners debated in [coffeehouses](/wiki/Coffeehouse). [Napoleon](/wiki/Napoleon)'s invasion of [Amsterdam](/wiki/Amsterdam) led many financiers to relocate to London and the first London international issue was arranged in 1817. The [Royal Navy](/wiki/Royal_Navy) became the world's leading war fleet, acting as a deterrent to economic adversaries. Following an 1838 fire, the [Royal Exchange](/wiki/Royal_Exchange,_London) was rebuilt in 1844. The 1846 repeal of the [Corn Laws](/wiki/Corn_Laws), meant to weaken Dutch economic power, enabled London to overtake Amsterdam as the leading international financial centre.

### Late modern and contemporary

The [Industrial Revolution](/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) saw unprecedented [urbanisation](/wiki/Urbanisation). London was the world's [largest city from about 1831 to 1925](/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history), with a population density of 132 per acre (325 per hectare). [Harding, Howell & Co.](/wiki/Harding,_Howell_%26_Co.) on [Pall Mall](/wiki/Pall_Mall,_London) was one of the first [department stores](/wiki/Department_store), alongside many [street sellers](/wiki/Hawker_(trade)). The [London Underground](/wiki/London_Underground), the world's first [urban rail network](/wiki/Rapid_transit), was created in response to [traffic congestion](/wiki/Traffic_congestion). Overcrowding led to [cholera](/wiki/Cholera) epidemics, claiming 14,000 lives in 1848, and 6,000 in 1866. A modern [sewage system](/wiki/History_of_water_supply_and_sanitation#Sewer_systems) was created by the [Metropolitan Board of Works](/wiki/Metropolitan_Board_of_Works); it diverted waste to the Thames Estuary, and by the 1890s used biological [treatment of sewage](/wiki/Sewage_treatment#History) to oxidise the waste. The Metropolitan Board of Works oversaw infrastructure expansion in the capital and some surrounding counties. [Lyons](/wiki/J._Lyons_and_Co.) opened the first of their [chain](/wiki/Chain_store) of teashops in [Piccadilly](/wiki/Piccadilly) in 1894. Tearooms became popular meeting places for women from the suffrage movement. [Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey) and [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral) were bombed in the 1912–14 [suffragette bombing and arson campaign](/wiki/Suffragette_bombing_and_arson_campaign).

A bombed-out London street during [the Blitz](/wiki/The_Blitz) in 1940

London was [bombed by the Germans](/wiki/German_strategic_bombing_during_World_War_I) in the [First World War](/wiki/First_World_War). During the [Second World War](/wiki/Second_World_War), [the Blitz](/wiki/The_Blitz) killed over 30,000 Londoners, destroying many buildings. The tomb of [the Unknown Warrior](/wiki/The_Unknown_Warrior) was created in Westminster Abbey on 11 November 1920 and [The Cenotaph](/wiki/The_Cenotaph), in [Whitehall](/wiki/Whitehall), was unveiled on the same day; it is the focus the [National Service of Remembrance](/wiki/National_Service_of_Remembrance) held annually on [Remembrance Sunday](/wiki/Remembrance_Sunday). The [1948 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1948_Summer_Olympics) were held at the original [Wembley Stadium](/wiki/Wembley_Stadium_(1923)), while London was still recovering from the war. From the 1940s, London became home to many immigrants, primarily from [Commonwealth](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations) countries such as Jamaica, India, Bangladesh and Pakistan, making London one of the most [multiracial](/wiki/Multiracialism) cities in the world. In 1951 the [Festival of Britain](/wiki/Festival_of_Britain) was held on the [South Bank](/wiki/South_Bank). The [Great Smog](/wiki/Great_Smog) of 1952 led to the [Clean Air Act 1956](/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_1956), which ended the "[pea soup fogs](/wiki/Pea_soup_fog)" which had earned London the nickname the "Big Smoke".

Starting in the mid-1960s, London became a centre for worldwide [youth culture](/wiki/Youth_culture), exemplified by the [Swinging London](/wiki/Swinging_London) sub-culture of the [King's Road](/wiki/King%27s_Road), [Chelsea](/wiki/Chelsea,_London) and [Carnaby Street](/wiki/Carnaby_Street). The role of trendsetter revived in the [punk](/wiki/Punk_rock) era. In 1965 London's political boundaries were expanded in response to the city's growth, and a new [Greater London Council](/wiki/Greater_London_Council) was created. During [the Troubles](/wiki/The_Troubles) in [Northern Ireland](/wiki/Northern_Ireland), London was hit from 1973 by bomb attacks by the [Provisional Irish Republican Army](/wiki/Provisional_Irish_Republican_Army). These attacks lasted for two decades, starting with the [Old Bailey bombing](/wiki/Old_Bailey_bombing). Greater London's population declined after the Second World War, from an estimated peak of 8.6 million in 1939 to around 6.8 million in the 1980s. By January 2015 Greater London's population had increased again to 8.63 million. The principal ports for London moved downstream to [Felixstowe](/wiki/Port_of_Felixstowe) and [Tilbury](/wiki/Port_of_Tilbury), with the [London Docklands](/wiki/London_Docklands) area becoming a focus for regeneration. 2 miles (3.2 km) east of central London, the [Thames Barrier](/wiki/Thames_Barrier) was completed in 1982 to protect London against tidal surges from the [North Sea](/wiki/North_Sea).

The Greater London Council was abolished in 1986, leaving London with no central administration until the creation of the [Greater London Authority](/wiki/Greater_London_Authority) in 2000. To mark the 21st century the [Millennium Dome](/wiki/Millennium_Dome), the [London Eye](/wiki/London_Eye) and the [Millennium Bridge](/wiki/Millennium_Bridge_(London)) were constructed. On 7 July 2005, three London Underground trains and a double-decker bus were bombed in [a series of terrorist attacks](/wiki/7_July_2005_London_bombings). In 2008 *[Time](/wiki/Time_(magazine))* magazine hailed London as one of the world's three most influential [global cities](/wiki/Global_cities). In terms of international connectedness, as of 2024, London was one of two cities worldwide classified as an "Alpha++" city by the [Globalization and World Cities Research Network](/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network).

## Administration

### Local government and services

Main articles: [Local government in London](/wiki/Local_government_in_London), [History of local government in London](/wiki/History_of_local_government_in_London), and [List of heads of London government](/wiki/List_of_heads_of_London_government)

[City Hall](/wiki/City_Hall,_London_(Newham)) seen across Royal Victoria Dock, headquarters of the [Greater London Authority](/wiki/Greater_London_Authority) (GLA) since 2022

The administration of London is formed of two tiers: a citywide, strategic tier coordinated by the [Greater London Authority](/wiki/Greater_London_Authority) (GLA), and a local tier with 33 local administrations. The GLA consists of the [mayor of London](/wiki/Mayor_of_London), who has [executive powers](/wiki/Executive_powers), and the [London Assembly](/wiki/London_Assembly), which scrutinises the mayor's decisions and can accept or reject the mayor's annual budget proposals. The GLA is responsible for most of London's transport system through [Transport for London](/wiki/Transport_for_London) (TfL), overseeing the city's police and fire services, and setting a strategic vision for London. The headquarters of the GLA is [City Hall, Newham](/wiki/City_Hall,_Newham). The mayor since 2016 is [Sadiq Khan](/wiki/Sadiq_Khan).

[Arms of the Corporation of the City of London](/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_the_City_of_London) since 1381

The local authorities are the councils of the 32 [London boroughs](/wiki/London_boroughs) and the [City of London Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation). They are responsible for most local services, including local planning, schools, libraries, leisure and recreation, [social services](/wiki/Social_work), local roads and refuse collection. Some functions, such as [waste management](/wiki/Waste_disposal_authorities_in_London), are provided through joint arrangements. In 2009–2010 the revenue expenditure by London councils and the GLA amounted to just over £22 billion (£14.7 billion for the boroughs and £7.4 billion for the GLA).

The [London Fire Brigade](/wiki/London_Fire_Brigade) is the [statutory](/wiki/Statute) [fire and rescue service](/wiki/Fire_service_in_the_UK) for Greater London, run by the [London Fire and Emergency Planning Authority](/wiki/London_Fire_and_Emergency_Planning_Authority). [National Health Service](/wiki/National_Health_Service) [ambulance services](/wiki/Emergency_medical_services) are provided by the [London Ambulance Service (LAS) NHS Trust](/wiki/London_Ambulance_Service). The [London Air Ambulance](/wiki/London_Air_Ambulance) charity operates in conjunction with the LAS where required. [His Majesty's Coastguard](/wiki/HM_Coastguard) and the [Royal National Lifeboat Institution](/wiki/Royal_National_Lifeboat_Institution) operate on the [River Thames](/wiki/River_Thames), under the jurisdiction of the [Port of London Authority](/wiki/Port_of_London_Authority) from [Teddington Lock](/wiki/Teddington_Lock) to the sea.

Policing in Greater London, with the exception of the [City of London](/wiki/City_of_London), is provided by the [Metropolitan Police](/wiki/Metropolitan_Police) ("The Met"), overseen by the mayor through the [Mayor's Office for Policing and Crime](/wiki/Mayor%27s_Office_for_Policing_and_Crime). The [British Transport Police](/wiki/British_Transport_Police) are responsible for police services on [National Rail](/wiki/National_Rail), [London Underground](/wiki/London_Underground), [Docklands Light Railway](/wiki/Docklands_Light_Railway) and [Tramlink](/wiki/Tramlink) services. The [Ministry of Defence Police](/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_Police) is a specialised police force in London.

### National government

[10 Downing Street](/wiki/10_Downing_Street), official residence of the [Prime Minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom)

London is the seat of the [Government of the United Kingdom](/wiki/Government_of_the_United_Kingdom). Many government departments and the [prime minister's](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom) residence at [10 Downing Street](/wiki/10_Downing_Street) are based close to the [Palace of Westminster](/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster), particularly along [Whitehall](/wiki/Whitehall). There are 75 [members of Parliament](/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_(United_Kingdom)) from London; as at [June 2024](/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election), 59 are from the [Labour Party](/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK)), 9 are [Conservatives](/wiki/Conservative_Party_(UK)), 6 are [Liberal Democrats](/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_(UK)) and one constituency is held by an [independent](/wiki/Independent_politician). The ministerial post of [Minister for London](/wiki/Minister_for_London) was created in 1994; it has been vacant since July 2024.

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of London](/wiki/Geography_of_London)

### Scope

[Greater London](/wiki/Greater_London) is one of nine [regions of England](/wiki/Regions_of_England) and the top subdivision covering most of the [metropolis](/wiki/Metropolis). The [City of London](/wiki/City_of_London) at its core once comprised the whole settlement; as its urban area grew, the [Corporation of London](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation) resisted attempts to amalgamate the city with its [suburbs](/wiki/Suburbs), causing "London" to be defined several ways.

Satellite view of London in June 2018

Forty per cent of Greater London is covered by the [London post town](/wiki/London_postal_district), in which "London" forms part of postal addresses. The London telephone [area code](/wiki/Area_code) (020) covers an area similar in size to Greater London, although some outer districts are excluded and some just outside included. The Greater London boundary has been [aligned to the M25 motorway](/wiki/List_of_Greater_London_boundary_changes) in places.

Urban expansion is prevented by the [Metropolitan Green Belt](/wiki/Metropolitan_Green_Belt), although the built-up area extends beyond the boundary in places, producing a separately defined [Greater London Urban Area](/wiki/Greater_London_Urban_Area). Beyond this is the [London commuter belt](/wiki/London_commuter_belt). Greater London is split for some purposes into [Inner London](/wiki/Inner_London) and [Outer London](/wiki/Outer_London), and by the River Thames into [North](/wiki/North_London) and [South](/wiki/South_London), with an informal [Central London](/wiki/Central_London) area. The coordinates of the nominal "centre of London", traditionally the original [Eleanor Cross](/wiki/Eleanor_Cross) at [Charing Cross](/wiki/Charing_Cross) near the junction of [Trafalgar Square](/wiki/Trafalgar_Square) and [Whitehall](/wiki/Whitehall), are about [51°30′26″N 00°07′39″W / 51.50722°N 0.12750°W / 51.50722; -0.12750](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=London&params=51_30_26_N_00_07_39_W_type:city(7,000,000)_region:GB).

### Status

Within London, the City of London and the [City of Westminster](/wiki/City_of_Westminster) have [city status](/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom). The City of London and the remainder of Greater London are [ceremonial counties](/wiki/Ceremonial_counties_of_England). [Greater London](/wiki/Metropolitan_and_non-metropolitan_counties_of_England#Greater_London) includes parts of the [historic counties](/wiki/Historic_counties_of_England) of [Middlesex](/wiki/Middlesex), [Kent](/wiki/Kent), [Surrey](/wiki/Surrey), [Essex](/wiki/Essex) and [Hertfordshire](/wiki/Hertfordshire). Greater London has been defined as a [region of England](/wiki/Regions_of_England).

London is the capital of the United Kingdom and of England by [convention](/wiki/Constitutional_convention_(political_custom)). The capital of England was moved from [Winchester](/wiki/Winchester) as the [Palace of Westminster](/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster) developed in the 12th and 13th centuries to become the home of the [royal court](/wiki/Noble_court), and thus the nation's political capital. The city has four [World Heritage Sites](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site): [Kew Gardens](/wiki/Kew_Gardens); the [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London); the site featuring the [Palace of Westminster](/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster), the [Church of St Margaret](/wiki/St_Margaret%27s,_Westminster), and [Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey); and the historic settlement in [Greenwich](/wiki/Greenwich) where the [Royal Observatory](/wiki/Royal_Observatory,_Greenwich) defines the [prime meridian](/wiki/Prime_meridian_(Greenwich)) (0° [longitude](/wiki/Longitude)) and [Greenwich Mean Time](/wiki/Greenwich_Mean_Time).

### Topography

London from [Primrose Hill](/wiki/Primrose_Hill)

Greater London has an area of 611 square miles (1,583 km2), while the London Metropolitan Region has an area of 3,236 square miles (8,382 km2).

Modern London stands on the [Thames](/wiki/River_Thames), its primary geographical feature, a navigable river which crosses the city from the south-west to the east. The [Thames Valley](/wiki/Thames_Valley) is a [flood plain](/wiki/Floodplain) surrounded by gently rolling hills including [Parliament Hill](/wiki/Parliament_Hill,_London), [Addington Hills](/wiki/Addington_Hills), and [Primrose Hill](/wiki/Primrose_Hill). Historically, London grew up at the river's [lowest bridging point](/wiki/Lowest_bridging_point). The Thames was once much broader and shallower with extensive wetlands; at high tide, its shores reached five times their present width.

Since the [Victorian era](/wiki/Victorian_era) the Thames has been [embanked](/wiki/Thames_Embankment), and many of its London [tributaries](/wiki/Tributaries_of_the_River_Thames) now flow [underground](/wiki/Subterranean_rivers_of_London). The Thames is tidal, and London is vulnerable to flooding. The threat has increased because of a continuous rise in [high water](/wiki/Tide) level caused by [climate change](/wiki/Climate_change) and by the slow 'tilting' of the British Isles through [post-glacial rebound](/wiki/Post-glacial_rebound).

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of London](/wiki/Climate_of_London)

London has a temperate [oceanic climate](/wiki/Oceanic_climate) ([Köppen](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): *Cfb*). The average annual precipitation is about 600mm. Despite relatively low annual precipitation, London receives 109.6 rainy days on the 1.0mm threshold annually. London is vulnerable to [climate change](/wiki/Climate_change_in_the_United_Kingdom), and experts are concerned that households may run out of water before 2050.

Summers are generally warm, sometimes hot. London's average July high is 23.5 °C (74.3 °F). On average each year, London experiences 31 days above 25 °C (77.0 °F) and 4.2 days above 30.0 °C (86.0 °F). Winters are generally cool with little temperature variation. Heavy snow is rare, but snow usually falls at least once each winter. As a large city, London has a considerable [urban heat island](/wiki/Urban_heat_island) effect, making the centre of London at times 5 °C (9 °F) warmer than the suburbs and outskirts.

### Areas

Main articles: [List of areas of London](/wiki/List_of_areas_of_London) and [London boroughs](/wiki/London_boroughs)

The [West End theatre](/wiki/West_End_theatre) district in 2016

Places within London are identified using area names, such as [Mayfair](/wiki/Mayfair), [Southwark](/wiki/Southwark), and [Whitechapel](/wiki/Whitechapel). These are informal designations, reflect the names of villages that have been absorbed by sprawl, or are superseded administrative units. Since 1965, Greater London has been divided into 32 [London boroughs](/wiki/London_borough) in addition to its [once walled](/wiki/London_Wall) ancient core, the City of London. The City of London is the main financial district, and [Canary Wharf](/wiki/Canary_Wharf) has developed into a new financial and commercial hub in the [Docklands](/wiki/London_Docklands). The [West End](/wiki/West_End_of_London) is an area of inner [West London](/wiki/West_London) with the main shopping and entertainment areas, including [West End theatre](/wiki/West_End_theatre), and attracts many tourists. Its residential areas where properties can sell for tens of millions of pounds. The [East End](/wiki/East_End_of_London) is the part of [East London](/wiki/East_London) closest to the City of London. It has a high immigrant population and is one of the poorest areas of the capital. It once included the main northern part of the [Port of London](/wiki/Port_of_London) and extensive industry. In the late 20th century, deindustrialisation and the closure of the docks brought hardship. Large redeveloped brownfield areas include [London Riverside](/wiki/London_Riverside) and the [Lower Lea Valley](/wiki/Lower_Lea_Valley), which was developed into the [Olympic Park](/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_Olympic_Park) for the [2012 Olympics](/wiki/London_2012_Olympic_bid).

### Architecture

Main articles: [Architecture of London](/wiki/Architecture_of_London), [List of tallest buildings and structures in London](/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_and_structures_in_London), and [List of demolished buildings and structures in London](/wiki/List_of_demolished_buildings_and_structures_in_London)

London's buildings are of many architectural styles and ages. Many grand houses and public buildings, such as the [National Gallery](/wiki/National_Gallery), are constructed from [Portland stone](/wiki/Portland_stone). Some areas, particularly just west of the centre, are characterised by white [stucco](/wiki/Stucco) or whitewashed buildings. Few structures in central London pre-date the [Great Fire](/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London) of 1666, other than the [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London). Further out is the Tudor-period [Hampton Court Palace](/wiki/Hampton_Court_Palace). The 17th-century is represented by [Christopher Wren](/wiki/Christopher_Wren) churches alongside neoclassical financial institutions such as the [Royal Exchange](/wiki/Royal_Exchange,_London) and the [Bank of England](/wiki/Bank_of_England), and the 1960s [Barbican Estate](/wiki/Barbican_Estate). The 1939 [Battersea Power Station](/wiki/Battersea_Power_Station) by the river in the south-west is a local landmark, while the [St. Pancras](/wiki/St_Pancras_railway_station) and [Paddington](/wiki/Paddington_railway_station) railway termini exemplify [Victorian architecture](/wiki/Victorian_architecture).

- The [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London), a medieval castle, dating in part to 1078
- The east wing public façade of [Buckingham Palace](/wiki/Buckingham_Palace) was built between 1847 and 1850; it was remodelled to its present form in 1913.
- [Trafalgar Square](/wiki/Trafalgar_Square) and its fountains, with [Nelson's Column](/wiki/Nelson%27s_Column) on the right

The [Monument to the Great Fire of London](/wiki/Monument_to_the_Great_Fire_of_London) in the City of London provides views of the surrounding area while commemorating the [Great Fire of London](/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London), which originated nearby. [Marble Arch](/wiki/Marble_Arch) and [Wellington Arch](/wiki/Wellington_Arch), at the north and south ends of [Park Lane](/wiki/Park_Lane_(road)), have royal connections, as do the [Albert Memorial](/wiki/Albert_Memorial) and [Royal Albert Hall](/wiki/Royal_Albert_Hall) in [Kensington](/wiki/Kensington). [Nelson's Column](/wiki/Nelson%27s_Column) in [Trafalgar Square](/wiki/Trafalgar_Square) is a focal point. Older buildings are mainly brick, commonly the yellow [London stock brick](/wiki/London_stock_brick).

In the dense areas, most of the concentration is via medium- and high-rise buildings. London's skyscrapers, such as [30 St Mary Axe](/wiki/30_St_Mary_Axe), [Tower 42](/wiki/Tower_42), the [Broadgate Tower](/wiki/Broadgate_Tower) and [One Canada Square](/wiki/One_Canada_Square), are mostly in the two financial districts, the City of London and [Canary Wharf](/wiki/Canary_Wharf). High-rise development has been restricted since 1937 if it would obstruct [protected views](/wiki/Protected_view) of [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral). There are [skyscrapers](/wiki/Tall_buildings_in_London) in Central London, including the 95-storey [Shard London Bridge](/wiki/Shard_London_Bridge), the [tallest building in the UK](/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_the_United_Kingdom) and Western Europe. Modern buildings include [The Scalpel](/wiki/The_Scalpel), [20 Fenchurch Street](/wiki/20_Fenchurch_Street), the former [City Hall](/wiki/City_Hall,_London_(Southwark)) in [Southwark](/wiki/Southwark), the [Art Deco](/wiki/Art_Deco) [BBC Broadcasting House](/wiki/BBC_Broadcasting_House), the [Postmodernist](/wiki/Postmodernism) [British Library](/wiki/British_Library) near [Kings Cross](/wiki/Kings_Cross,_London), [No 1 Poultry](/wiki/No_1_Poultry), the [BT Tower](/wiki/BT_Tower) and [The O2 Arena](/wiki/The_O2_Arena).

The [Houses of Parliament](/wiki/Houses_of_Parliament) and [Elizabeth Tower](/wiki/Big_Ben) (Big Ben) on the right foreground, the [London Eye](/wiki/London_Eye) on the left foreground and [The Shard](/wiki/The_Shard) with [Canary Wharf](/wiki/Canary_Wharf) in the background; seen in September 2014

### Natural history

See also: [List of local nature reserves in Greater London](/wiki/List_of_local_nature_reserves_in_Greater_London)

London has some 10,000 [red foxes](/wiki/Red_fox).

The [London Natural History Society](/wiki/London_Natural_History_Society) suggests that London is "one of the World's Greenest Cities" with more than 40% green space or open water, 2000 species of flowering plant, and 120 species of fish. Over 60 species of bird nest in [central London](/wiki/Central_London), with 47 species of butterfly, 1,173 moths and more than 270 kinds of spider. London's [wetland](/wiki/Wetland) areas support nationally important populations of water birds. In 2024 there were some 3 million pigeons in London. At least [six ravens are kept at the Tower of London](/wiki/Ravens_of_the_Tower_of_London). London has 38 [Sites of Special Scientific Interest](/wiki/List_of_Sites_of_Special_Scientific_Interest_in_Greater_London) (SSSIs), two [national nature reserves](/wiki/National_nature_reserve_(United_Kingdom)) and 76 [local nature reserves](/wiki/List_of_local_nature_reserves_in_Greater_London).

[Amphibians](/wiki/Amphibians) include [smooth newts](/wiki/Smooth_newt), [frogs](/wiki/Common_frog), [toadss](/wiki/Common_toad) [palmate newts](/wiki/Palmate_newt), and [great crested newts](/wiki/Great_crested_newt). Native reptiles such as [slowworms](/wiki/Slowworm), [lizards](/wiki/Common_lizard), [grass snakes](/wiki/Barred_grass_snake) and [adders](/wiki/Vipera_berus), are mostly seen only in [Outer London](/wiki/Outer_London).

London has some 10,000 [red foxes](/wiki/Red_fox), as well as [hedgehogs](/wiki/European_hedgehog), mice, [rabbits](/wiki/European_rabbit), [shrews](/wiki/Shrew), [voles](/wiki/Vole) and [grey squirrels](/wiki/Grey_squirrel). In wilder areas of Outer London, such as [Epping Forest](/wiki/Epping_Forest), there are also [hare](/wiki/European_hare), [badger](/wiki/European_badger), voles, [wood mouse](/wiki/Wood_mouse), [yellow-necked mouse](/wiki/Yellow-necked_mouse), [mole](/wiki/European_mole), [shrew](/wiki/Shrew) and [weasel](/wiki/Least_weasel). [Otters](/wiki/Eurasian_otter) are starting to return after a hundred years absent from the city. Ten of England's eighteen species of bats have been recorded in Epping Forest.

Herds of [red](/wiki/Red_deer) and [fallow deer](/wiki/Fallow_deer) roam in [Richmond](/wiki/Richmond_Park) and [Bushy Parks](/wiki/Bushy_Park). They are culled each November and February to keep their populations sustainable. Epping Forest is known for its fallow deer, which can be seen in herds to the north of the Forest. A rare population of [melanistic](/wiki/Melanistic), black fallow deer is maintained at the Deer Sanctuary near [Theydon Bois](/wiki/Theydon_Bois), while [muntjac](/wiki/Reeve%27s_muntjac) are found in the forest. While Londoners are accustomed to birds and foxes sharing the city, urban deer have become a regular feature, and herds of fallow deer come into residential areas at night.

## Demographics

Main article: [Demographics of London](/wiki/Demographics_of_London)

Population density map (%)

London's continuous urban area held 9,787,426 people in 2011, while its wider [metropolitan area](/wiki/London_metropolitan_area) held 15.1 million in 2025. A net 726,000 immigrants arrived in the period 1991–2001. The region's 610 square miles (1,579 km2) had a population density of 13,410 inhabitants per square mile (5,177/km2) more than ten times that of any other [British region](/wiki/ITL_(UK)).
London's median age in 2018 was 36.5 years old, younger than the UK median of 40.3.
In the 2021 census, 3,575,739 people (40.6%) of London's population were [foreign-born](/wiki/Foreign-born_population_of_the_United_Kingdom). About 56.8 per cent of children born in London in 2021 were born to a mother who was born abroad.

### Ethnicity

Main article: [Ethnic groups in London](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_London)

Maps of Greater London showing percentage distribution of selected ethnic groups according to the 2001-21 Census

[White](/wiki/White_people_in_the_United_Kingdom) (53.8%, 2021)

[Asian](/wiki/British_Asian) (20.8%, 2021)

[Black](/wiki/Black_British_people) (13.5%, 2021)

The 2021 census reported that 53.8% of the 8,173,941 inhabitants of London were [White](/wiki/White_people). 22.2% of Londoners were of [Asian](/wiki/British_Asians) or mixed-Asian descent, with 20.8% of full Asian descent and 1.4% of mixed-Asian heritage. [Indians](/wiki/British_Indian) accounted for 7.5% of the population, followed by [Bangladeshis](/wiki/British_Bangladeshis) and [Pakistanis](/wiki/British_Pakistanis) at 3.7% and 3.3% respectively. 15.9% of London's population were of [Black](/wiki/Black_British_people) or mixed-Black descent. As of 2021, the majority of London's school pupils come from ethnic minority backgrounds, with only 23.9% White British. A 2005 survey claimed that more than 300 languages were spoken in London and more than 50 non-indigenous communities had populations of more than 10,000. At the 2021 census, 78.4% of Londoners spoke English as their first language. The 5 biggest languages outside English were Romanian, Spanish, Polish, Bengali and Portuguese.

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in London](/wiki/Religion_in_London)

See also: [List of churches in London](/wiki/List_of_churches_in_London)

According to the [2021 Census](/wiki/2021_United_Kingdom_census), the largest religious groupings were [Christians](/wiki/British_Christianity) (40.66%), followed by those of no religion (20.7%), [Muslims](/wiki/Islam_in_London) (15%), no response (8.5%), [Hindus](/wiki/Hinduism_in_the_United_Kingdom) (5.15%), Jews (1.65%), [Sikhs](/wiki/Sikhism_in_the_United_Kingdom) (1.64%), [Buddhists](/wiki/Buddhism_in_the_United_Kingdom) (1.0%) and other (0.8%). London has traditionally been [Christian](/wiki/Christian), with [many churches](/wiki/List_of_churches_in_London), particularly in the City of London. [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral) in the City and [Southwark Cathedral](/wiki/Southwark_Cathedral) south of the river are [Anglican](/wiki/Anglicanism) administrative centres, while the [Archbishop of Canterbury](/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury)'s main residence is [Lambeth Palace](/wiki/Lambeth_Palace). National and royal ceremonies are shared between [St Paul's](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral) and [Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey). [Westminster Cathedral](/wiki/Westminster_Cathedral) is the largest [Roman Catholic](/wiki/Roman_Catholic) cathedral in [England and Wales](/wiki/England_and_Wales). There are large [Bengali Muslim](/wiki/Bengali_Muslim) communities in the eastern boroughs of [Tower Hamlets](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Tower_Hamlets) and [Newham](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Newham). [Harrow](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Harrow) and [Brent](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Brent) have large Hindu communities, the latter hosting [a large Hindu temple](/wiki/Neasden_Temple). London is home to 44 Hindu temples. Southall is home to a Sikh community and the largest Sikh temple outside India. Most [British Jews](/wiki/British_Jews) live in North London, especially in and around [Golders Green](/wiki/Golders_Green). Opened in 1701, [Bevis Marks Synagogue](/wiki/Bevis_Marks_Synagogue) in the [City of London](/wiki/City_of_London) is affiliated to London's historic [Sephardic](/wiki/Sephardic) Jewish community. It is the oldest continually active synagogue in Europe. [Stanmore and Canons Park Synagogue](/wiki/Stanmore_and_Canons_Park_Synagogue) has the largest membership of any Orthodox synagogue in Europe.

### Accents

Traditionally, anyone born within earshot of the bells of [St Mary-le-Bow](/wiki/St_Mary-le-Bow) church was considered to be a true [Cockney](/wiki/Cockney).

[Cockney](/wiki/Cockney) is an accent heard across London, mainly spoken by [working-class](/wiki/Working_class) and [lower-middle-class](/wiki/Lower_middle_class) people of East London. [Estuary English](/wiki/Estuary_English) is an intermediate accent between Cockney and [Received Pronunciation](/wiki/Received_Pronunciation), widely spoken by people of all classes. [Multicultural London English](/wiki/Multicultural_London_English) is a [multiethnolect](/wiki/Multiethnolect) in multicultural areas amongst young, working-class people from diverse backgrounds, especially Afro-Caribbean and South Asian, with Cockney influence. Received Pronunciation is the accent regarded as standard for [British English](/wiki/British_English). It is mainly spoken by [upper-class](/wiki/Upper_class) and [upper-middle class](/wiki/Upper_middle_class) Londoners.

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of London](/wiki/Economy_of_London)

London's [gross regional product](/wiki/Gross_regional_product) in 2023 was £617.92 [billion](/wiki/1,000,000,000) ([US$](/wiki/USD)846.46 [billion](/wiki/1,000,000,000))or around a quarter of [UK GDP](/wiki/Economy_of_the_United_Kingdom). London has five major business districts: the City, Westminster, Canary Wharf, Camden & Islington, and Lambeth & Southwark. Greater London had 27 million m2 of office space in 2001, and the City contains the most space, with 8 million m2 of office space. London has some of the highest real estate prices in the world.

### Finance

London's finance industry is based in the [City of London](/wiki/City_of_London) and [Canary Wharf](/wiki/Canary_Wharf), the two major [business districts](/wiki/Central_business_district). London took over as a major financial centre shortly after 1795 when the [Dutch Republic](/wiki/Dutch_Republic) collapsed before the Napoleonic armies. This caused many bankers established in [Amsterdam](/wiki/Amsterdam) to move to London. London's market-centred system grew more dominant in the 18th century. This economic strength of the city was attributed to its diversity. By the mid-19th century, London was the leading global financial centre; at the end of the century, over half the world's trade was financed in British currency. As of 2023, London ranks second in the world rankings on the [Global Financial Centres Index](/wiki/Global_Financial_Centres_Index) and ranked second in A.T. Kearney's 2018 Global Cities Index.

Finance is London's largest industry; its [financial exports](/wiki/Financial_export) make it a large contributor to the UK's [balance of payments](/wiki/Balance_of_payments). It is the world's biggest currency trading centre, accounting for some 37% of the $5.1 trillion average daily volume. The City of London is home to the [Bank of England](/wiki/Bank_of_England), the London Stock Exchange, and [Lloyd's of London](/wiki/Lloyd%27s_of_London) insurance market. Over half the UK's top 100 listed companies (the [FTSE 100](/wiki/FTSE_100)) and over 100 of Europe's 500 largest companies have their headquarters in central London. Over 70% of the FTSE 100 are within London's metropolitan area, and 75% of [Fortune 500](/wiki/Fortune_500) companies have offices in London.

- The [City of London](/wiki/City_of_London), one of the largest [financial centres](/wiki/Financial_centre) in the world
- The [London Stock Exchange](/wiki/London_Stock_Exchange) at [Paternoster Square](/wiki/Paternoster_Square) and [Temple Bar](/wiki/Temple_Bar,_London)
- The [Bank of England](/wiki/Bank_of_England), established in 1694, is the model on which most modern central banks are based.

### Media and technology

Main article: [Media in London](/wiki/Media_in_London)

[Broadcasting House](/wiki/Broadcasting_House), headquarters of the [BBC](/wiki/BBC)

Media companies are concentrated in London, and the media distribution industry is London's second most competitive sector. The [BBC](/wiki/BBC), the world's oldest national broadcaster, is a significant employer, while broadcasters including [ITV](/wiki/ITV_(TV_network)), [Channel 4](/wiki/Channel_4), [Channel 5](/wiki/Channel_5_(UK)) and [Sky](/wiki/Sky_UK) have headquarters around the city. Many [national newspapers](/wiki/List_of_newspapers_in_the_United_Kingdom) are edited in London; the term [Fleet Street](/wiki/Fleet_Street) (where most national newspapers operated) remains a [metonym](/wiki/Metonym) for the British national press.

A large number of technology companies are based in London, notably in [East London Tech City](/wiki/East_London_Tech_City), known as Silicon Roundabout. As of April 2025, London is the 3rd largest game development hub in the world after [Los Angeles](/wiki/Los_Angeles) and [San Francisco](/wiki/San_Francisco). The gas and electricity distribution networks of towers, cables and pressure systems that deliver energy to consumers across the city are managed by [National Grid plc](/wiki/National_Grid_plc), [SGN](/wiki/SGN_(company)) and [UK Power Networks](/wiki/UK_Power_Networks).

### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in London](/wiki/Tourism_in_London)

The [British Museum](/wiki/British_Museum)

The [National Gallery](/wiki/National_Gallery)

London is a leading tourist destination. In 2015, it was the top city in the world by visitor cross-border spending, estimated at US$20.23 billion. Tourism is one of London's prime industries, employing 700,000 full-time workers in 2016, and contributes £36 billion a year to the economy. The city accounts for 54% of all inbound visitor spending in the UK.

In 2024, nine of the ten most-visited attractions in the UK were in London. The most visited attraction was the [British Museum](/wiki/British_Museum) at 6,479,952 visitors, followed by the [Natural History Museum](/wiki/Natural_History_Museum,_London), [Windsor Great Park](/wiki/Windsor_Great_Park), [Tate Modern](/wiki/Tate_Modern) and the [Southbank Centre](/wiki/Southbank_Centre). The number of hotel rooms in London in 2023 stood at 155,700 and is expected to grow to 183,600 rooms, the most of any city outside China.

## Transport

Main article: [Transport in London](/wiki/Transport_in_London)

Transport is one of the four main areas of policy administered by the [Mayor of London](/wiki/Mayor_of_London), but the mayor's financial control does not extend to the longer-distance rail network that enters London. In 2007, the Mayor of London assumed responsibility for some local lines, which now form the [London Overground](/wiki/London_Overground) network, adding to the existing responsibility for the [London Underground](/wiki/London_Underground), [trams](/wiki/Tramlink) and [buses](/wiki/London_Buses). The public transport network is administered by [Transport for London](/wiki/Transport_for_London) (TfL).

The lines that formed the London Underground, as well as trams and buses, became part of an integrated transport system in 1933 when the [London Passenger Transport Board](/wiki/London_Passenger_Transport_Board) or *[London Transport](/wiki/London_Transport_(brand))* was created. Transport for London is now the statutory corporation responsible for most aspects of the transport system in [Greater London](/wiki/Greater_London), and is run by a board and a commissioner appointed by the Mayor of London.

### Aviation

Main article: [Airports of London](/wiki/Airports_of_London)

[Heathrow Airport](/wiki/Heathrow_Airport) is the [busiest airport in Europe](/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_Europe) as well as the [second busiest in the world](/wiki/List_of_busiest_airports_by_international_passenger_traffic) for international passenger traffic ([Terminal 5](/wiki/Heathrow_Terminal_5)C is pictured).

London is a major international air transport hub with the [busiest city airspace](/wiki/List_of_busiest_city_airport_systems_by_passenger_traffic) in the world. Eight airports use the word *London* in their name, but most traffic passes through six of these. Additionally, [various other airports](/wiki/Airports_of_London) serve London, catering primarily to [general aviation](/wiki/General_aviation) flights.

- [Heathrow Airport](/wiki/Heathrow_Airport), in [Hillingdon](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Hillingdon), West London, was for many years the [busiest airport](/wiki/World%27s_busiest_airport) in the world for international traffic, and is the major hub of the nation's flag carrier, [British Airways](/wiki/British_Airways). In March 2008 its fifth terminal was opened.
- [Gatwick Airport](/wiki/Gatwick_Airport), south of London in [West Sussex](/wiki/West_Sussex), handles flights to more destinations than any other UK airport and is the main base of [easyJet](/wiki/EasyJet), the UK's largest airline by number of passengers.
- [London Stansted Airport](/wiki/London_Stansted_Airport), north-east of London in [Essex](/wiki/Essex), has flights that serve the greatest number of European destinations of any UK airport and is the main base of [Ryanair](/wiki/Ryanair), the world's largest international airline by number of international passengers.
- [Luton Airport](/wiki/Luton_Airport), to the north of London in [Bedfordshire](/wiki/Bedfordshire), is used by several budget airlines (especially easyJet and [Wizz Air](/wiki/Wizz_Air)) for short-haul flights.
- [London City Airport](/wiki/London_City_Airport), the most central airport and the one with the shortest runway, in [Newham](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Newham), East London, is focused on business travellers, with a mixture of full-service short-haul scheduled flights and considerable [business jet](/wiki/Business_jet) traffic.
- [London Southend Airport](/wiki/London_Southend_Airport), east of London in Essex, is a smaller, regional airport that caters to short-haul flights on a limited, though growing, number of airlines. In 2017, international passengers made up over 95% of the total at Southend, the highest proportion of any London airport.

### Rail

Opened in 1863, the [London Underground](/wiki/London_Underground) ("the Tube") is the oldest and third-longest [metro](/wiki/Rapid_transit) system in the world. The system serves [272 stations](/wiki/List_of_London_Underground_stations), and was formed from several private companies, including the [world's first underground railway](/wiki/City_and_South_London_Railway), which opened in 1890. Over 4 million journeys are made every day on the Underground network. The [Docklands Light Railway](/wiki/Docklands_Light_Railway), opened in 1987, is a [local metro system](/wiki/Medium-capacity_rail_transport_system) serving the [Docklands](/wiki/London_Docklands) and Southeast London.

There are [368 railway stations](/wiki/List_of_London_railway_stations) in the [London fare zones](/wiki/London_fare_zones) on an extensive above-ground suburban railway network, especially in South London. Most rail lines run into [the city centre's main stations](/wiki/London_station_group). London has Britain's busiest station by number of passengers, [Waterloo](/wiki/London_Waterloo_station), with over 184 million people annually. [Clapham Junction](/wiki/Clapham_Junction_railway_station) is one of Europe's busiest rail interchanges. The east–west [Elizabeth line](/wiki/Elizabeth_line) opened in May 2022. It was Europe's biggest construction project, with a £15 billion projected cost.

70% of [National Rail](/wiki/National_Rail) journeys start or end in London. [King's Cross station](/wiki/London_King%27s_Cross_railway_station) and [Euston station](/wiki/Euston_railway_station), both in London, are the starting points of the [East Coast Main Line](/wiki/East_Coast_Main_Line) and the [West Coast Main Line](/wiki/West_Coast_Main_Line). *[The Flying Scotsman](/wiki/Flying_Scotsman_(train))* express has operated between London and Edinburgh since 1862. The opening of the [Channel Tunnel](/wiki/Channel_Tunnel) in 1994 connected London directly to the continental rail network, allowing [Eurostar](/wiki/Eurostar) services to begin. Since 2007, high-speed trains link [St. Pancras International](/wiki/St_Pancras_railway_station) with European tourist destinations via the [High Speed 1](/wiki/High_Speed_1) rail link and the Channel Tunnel. The first [high-speed domestic](/wiki/Southeastern_(train_operating_company)) trains started in June 2009, linking [Kent](/wiki/Kent) to London. There are plans for a [second high speed line](/wiki/High_Speed_2) linking London to the Midlands, North West England, and Yorkshire.

- The [London Underground](/wiki/London_Underground), opened in January 1863, is the world's oldest and third-longest [rapid transit](/wiki/Rapid_transit) system.
- The [London Underground's roundel symbol](/wiki/London_Underground#Roundel) appeared in 1917.
- [St Pancras International](/wiki/St_Pancras_railway_station) is the main terminal for high-speed [Eurostar](/wiki/Eurostar) and [High Speed 1](/wiki/High_Speed_1) services.

### Buses, coaches and trams

The [New Routemaster](/wiki/New_Routemaster) is the spiritual successor to the [AEC Routemaster](/wiki/AEC_Routemaster). First appearing in 1947, the red double-decker bus is an emblematic symbol of London.

London's [bus network](/wiki/London_Buses) runs 24 hours a day with about 9,300 vehicles, over 675 bus routes and about 19,000 bus stops. In 2019, the network had over 2 billion commuter trips per year. Since 2010 an average of £1.2 billion is taken in revenue each year. London has one of the largest wheelchair-accessible networks in the world, which, from 2009, became more accessible to hearing and visually impaired passengers as audio-visual announcements were introduced. An emblem of London, the red [double-decker bus](/wiki/Double-decker_bus) first appeared in the city in 1947 with the [AEC Regent III RT](/wiki/AEC_Regent_III_RT) (predecessor to the [AEC Routemaster](/wiki/AEC_Routemaster)). London's coach hub is [Victoria Coach Station](/wiki/Victoria_Coach_Station), opened in 1932. Nationalised in 1970 and then purchased by London Transport (now [Transport for London](/wiki/Transport_for_London)), Victoria Coach Station has over 14 million passengers a year and provides services across the UK and continental Europe. A modern tram network, [Tramlink](/wiki/Tramlink), serves South London.

### Cars

The [hackney carriage](/wiki/Hackney_carriage) (black cab) is a common sight on London streets. Black has been its standard colour since the [Austin FX3](/wiki/Austin_FX3) model in 1948.

Car travel is common in the suburbs. The [inner ring road](/wiki/London_Inner_Ring_Road), the [North](/wiki/A406_road) and [South Circular](/wiki/A205_road) roads (just within the suburbs), and the outer orbital motorway (the [M25](/wiki/M25_motorway), just outside the built-up area in most places) encircle the city and are intersected by busy radial routes, but few motorways penetrate into [inner London](/wiki/Inner_London). The M25 is the second-longest ring-road motorway in Europe at 117 miles (188 km) long. The [A1](/wiki/A1_road_(Great_Britain)) and [M1](/wiki/M1_motorway) connect London to [Leeds](/wiki/Leeds), and [Newcastle](/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne) and [Edinburgh](/wiki/Edinburgh).
The [Austin Motor Company](/wiki/Austin_Motor_Company) began making [hackney carriages](/wiki/Hackney_carriage) (London taxis) in 1929; the black cabs have become part of the city's tradition. Although traditionally black, some are painted in other colours or bear advertising.

London is notorious for traffic congestion; in 2009, the average rush hour speed was 10.6 mph (17.1 km/h). In 2003, a [congestion charge](/wiki/London_congestion_charge) was introduced to reduce traffic volumes in the city centre. Residents of the defined zone can buy a cheaper season pass. The average number of cars entering the centre of London on a weekday was reduced from 195,000 to 125,000. [Zebra crossings](/wiki/Zebra_crossing), the world's first marked [pedestrian crossings](/wiki/Pedestrian_crossing), appeared across London in 1951. [Low Traffic Neighbourhoods](/wiki/Low_Traffic_Neighbourhood) were introduced in 2020.

### Other modes

Further information: [Cycling in London](/wiki/Cycling_in_London)

Santander Cycle Hire, near [Victoria](/wiki/Victoria,_London) in Central London

London's only cable car is the [London Cable Car](/wiki/London_Cable_Car), which opened in June 2012. The cable car crosses the Thames and links [Greenwich Peninsula](/wiki/Greenwich_Peninsula) with the [Royal Docks](/wiki/Royal_Docks) in the east of the city. It is able to carry up to 2,500 passengers per hour in each direction at peak times. In the Greater London Area, around 670,000 people use a bicycle every day, meaning around 7% of the total population of around 8.8 million use a bike on an average day.

The [Port of London](/wiki/Port_of_London), once the largest in the world, is now only the second-largest in the United Kingdom, handling 45 million tonnes of cargo each year as of 2009. Most of this cargo passes through the [Port of Tilbury](/wiki/Port_of_Tilbury), outside the boundary of Greater London. London has river boat services on the Thames known as [Thames Clippers](/wiki/Thames_Clippers), which offer both commuter and tourist boat services. At major piers including [Canary Wharf](/wiki/Canary_Wharf_Pier), [London Bridge City](/wiki/London_Bridge_City_Pier), [Battersea Power Station](/wiki/Battersea_Power_Station) and [London Eye](/wiki/London_Eye_Pier) (Waterloo), services depart at least every 20 minutes during commuter times. The [Woolwich Ferry](/wiki/Woolwich_Ferry), with 2.5 million passengers every year, is a frequent service linking the [North](/wiki/North_Circular_Road,_London) and [South Circular](/wiki/South_Circular_Road,_London) Roads.

## Education

Main article: [Education in London](/wiki/Education_in_London)

### Tertiary education

See also: [List of universities and higher education colleges in London](/wiki/List_of_universities_and_higher_education_colleges_in_London)

London is a major global centre of higher education, teaching and research and has the largest concentration of higher education institutes in Europe. According to the QS World University Rankings 2015/16, London has the greatest concentration of top class universities in the world and its international student population of around 110,000 is larger than any other city in the world. In the 2022 *[QS World University Rankings](/wiki/QS_World_University_Rankings)*, [Imperial College London](/wiki/Imperial_College_London) is ranked No. 6 in the world, [University College London](/wiki/University_College_London) (UCL) is ranked 8th, and [King's College London](/wiki/King%27s_College_London) (KCL) is ranked 37th. All are regularly ranked highly, with Imperial College being the UK's leading university in the [Research Excellence Framework](/wiki/Research_Excellence_Framework) ranking 2021. The [London School of Economics](/wiki/London_School_of_Economics) has been described as the world's leading social science institution for both teaching and research. The [London Business School](/wiki/London_Business_School) is one of the world's leading business schools. The city is home to three of the world's top ten performing arts schools (as ranked by the 2020 QS World University Rankings): the [Royal College of Music](/wiki/Royal_College_of_Music) (ranking 2nd in the world), the [Royal Academy of Music](/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Music) (ranking 4th) and the [Guildhall School of Music and Drama](/wiki/Guildhall_School_of_Music_and_Drama) (ranking 6th).

With students in London and around 48,000 in [University of London Worldwide](/wiki/University_of_London_Worldwide), the federal [University of London](/wiki/University_of_London) is the largest contact teaching university in the UK. [London has multiple universities](/wiki/List_of_universities_and_higher_education_colleges_in_London) outside the University of London system.

London is home to [five major medical schools](/wiki/United_Hospitals) and many affiliated teaching hospitals. It is a major centre for biomedical research, and three of the UK's eight [academic health science centres](/wiki/Academic_health_science_centre) are based in the city. Many biomedical and biotechnology spin out companies from these research institutions are based around the city, most prominently in [White City](/wiki/White_City,_London). Founded by [Florence Nightingale](/wiki/Florence_Nightingale) at [St Thomas' Hospital](/wiki/St_Thomas%27_Hospital) in 1860, the [first nursing school](/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Faculty_of_Nursing_and_Midwifery) is now part of King's College London.

- [Imperial College London](/wiki/Imperial_College_London), a technical research university
- The [London School of Economics](/wiki/London_School_of_Economics) was established in 1895.
- [King's College London](/wiki/King%27s_College_London)'s [Guy's Campus](/wiki/Guy%27s_Campus), home to its faculty of medicine

### Royal Observatory, Greenwich and learned societies

Tourists queuing to take pictures on the line of the historic [prime meridian](/wiki/Prime_meridian_(Greenwich)) at the [Royal Observatory, Greenwich](/wiki/Royal_Observatory,_Greenwich)

Founded in 1675, the [Royal Observatory, Greenwich](/wiki/Royal_Observatory,_Greenwich) was established to address the problem of calculating [longitude](/wiki/Longitude) for navigation. This work featured in astronomer royal [Nevil Maskelyne](/wiki/Nevil_Maskelyne)'s *[Nautical Almanac](/wiki/Nautical_Almanac)* which made the Greenwich meridian the universal reference point, leading to the international adoption of Greenwich as the [prime meridian](/wiki/Prime_meridian) (0° longitude) in 1884.

Scientific [learned societies](/wiki/Learned_societies) based in London include the [Royal Society](/wiki/Royal_Society) - the UK's national [academy of sciences](/wiki/Academy_of_sciences) and the oldest national scientific institution in the world - founded in 1660. Since 1825, scientists have presented their subjects to a general audience at the [Royal Institution Christmas Lectures](/wiki/Royal_Institution_Christmas_Lectures).

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of London](/wiki/Culture_of_London)

### Leisure and entertainment

See also: [List of annual events in London](/wiki/List_of_annual_events_in_London) and [West End theatre](/wiki/West_End_theatre)

A scene of the annual [Notting Hill Carnival](/wiki/Notting_Hill_Carnival), 2014

According to a 2003 report, London has a quarter of the UK's leisure economy. The city is one of the four [fashion capitals](/wiki/Fashion_capital) of the world, is the world's third-busiest film production centre, presents more live comedy than any other city, and has the biggest theatre audience of any city in the world. Alongside [New York](/wiki/Culture_of_New_York_City), London has been described as the cultural capital of the world.

The entertainment district of the [West End](/wiki/West_End_of_London) has its focus around [Leicester Square](/wiki/Leicester_Square) for film [premieres](/wiki/Premiere), and [Piccadilly Circus](/wiki/Piccadilly_Circus), with its giant electronic advertisements. It contains London's [theatre district](/wiki/West_End_theatre) and [Chinatown](/wiki/Chinatown,_London) district. [Agatha Christie](/wiki/Agatha_Christie)'s *[The Mousetrap](/wiki/The_Mousetrap)*, the world's longest-running play, has performed in the West End [since 1952](/wiki/List_of_the_longest-running_West_End_shows#List). Opera and ballet are performed at the [Royal Opera House](/wiki/Royal_Opera_House), the [London Coliseum](/wiki/London_Coliseum), [Sadler's Wells Theatre](/wiki/Sadler%27s_Wells_Theatre) and the [Royal Albert Hall](/wiki/Royal_Albert_Hall). London was the centre of [music halls](/wiki/Music_hall) such as the [Hackney Empire](/wiki/Hackney_Empire).

[Shakespeare's Globe](/wiki/Shakespeare%27s_Globe) is a modern reconstruction of the Globe Theatre on the south bank of the Thames.

[Islington](/wiki/Islington)'s 1 mile (1.6 km) long [Upper Street](/wiki/Upper_Street) has more bars and restaurants than any other British street. Europe's busiest shopping area and England's longest shopping street is [Oxford Street](/wiki/Oxford_Street), nearly 1 mile (1.6 km) long. One of the world's largest retail destinations, London ranks near the top of retail sales of any city. In 2017, London was ranked the top city for luxury store openings. [London Fashion Week](/wiki/London_Fashion_Week) takes place in February and September.

Gastronomic centres include [Brick Lane](/wiki/Brick_Lane) for [Bangladeshi](/wiki/Bangladeshi_cuisine) and [Chinatown](/wiki/Chinatown,_London) for [Chinese food](/wiki/Chinese_food). Around 1860, the first [fish and chips](/wiki/Fish_and_chips) shop in London opened in [Bow](/wiki/Bow,_London). Many [cafes](/wiki/Cafe_(British)) serve a [full English breakfast](/wiki/Full_English_breakfast) all day. London has five 3-[Michelin star](/wiki/Michelin_star) restaurants. Many hotels provide a traditional [afternoon tea](/wiki/Afternoon_tea) service. The nation's most popular [biscuit](/wiki/Biscuit) to [dunk](/wiki/Dunking_(biscuit)) in tea, [chocolate digestives](/wiki/Chocolate_digestive), have been manufactured in [Harlesden](/wiki/Harlesden) by [McVitie's](/wiki/McVitie%27s) since 1925.

[Annual events](/wiki/List_of_annual_events_in_London) begin with the [New Year's Day Parade](/wiki/New_Year%27s_Day_Parade) and a fireworks display at the [London Eye](/wiki/London_Eye). The world's second largest [street party](/wiki/Street_party), the [Notting Hill Carnival](/wiki/Notting_Hill_Carnival), is held on the late [August Bank Holiday](/wiki/August_Bank_Holiday) each year. Traditional parades include November's [Lord Mayor's Show](/wiki/Lord_Mayor%27s_Show) celebrating the appointment of a new [Lord Mayor](/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_the_City_of_London), while June's [Trooping the Colour](/wiki/Trooping_the_Colour), a formal military pageant and [flypast](/wiki/Flypast), celebrates the [King's Official Birthday](/wiki/King%27s_Official_Birthday). The [Boishakhi Mela](/wiki/Boishakhi_Mela), the largest open-air Asian festival in Europe, celebrates the [Bengali New Year](/wiki/Bengali_New_Year). First held in 1862, the [RHS Chelsea Flower Show](/wiki/Chelsea_Flower_Show) (run by the [Royal Horticultural Society](/wiki/Royal_Horticultural_Society)) takes place in May.

### LGBTQ scene

Main article: [LGBTQ culture in London](/wiki/LGBTQ_culture_in_London)

[Comptons of Soho](/wiki/Comptons_of_Soho) during [London Pride](/wiki/Pride_in_London) in 2010

The first [gay bar](/wiki/Gay_bar) in London in the modern sense was [The Cave of the Golden Calf](/wiki/The_Cave_of_the_Golden_Calf), established as a [nightclub](/wiki/Nightclub) in an underground location at 9 [Heddon Street](/wiki/Heddon_Street), just off [Regent Street](/wiki/Regent_Street), in 1912 and "which developed a reputation for sexual freedom and tolerance of same-sex relations". While London has been an LGBT tourism destination, after [homosexuality was decriminalised](/wiki/Sexual_Offences_Act_1967) in England in 1967 gay bar culture became more visible, and from the early 1970s [Soho](/wiki/Soho) (and in particular [Old Compton Street](/wiki/Old_Compton_Street)) became the centre of the [London LGBTQ community](/wiki/LGBTQ_culture_in_London).

### Literature, film and television

Main articles: [London in fiction](/wiki/London_in_fiction), [London in film](/wiki/London_in_film), [List of television shows set in London](/wiki/List_of_television_shows_set_in_London), and [London Television Archive](/wiki/London_Television_Archive)

[Sherlock Holmes Museum](/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes_Museum) in [Baker Street](/wiki/Baker_Street), bearing the number 221B

London has been the setting for many works of literature. The pilgrims in [Geoffrey Chaucer](/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer)'s late 14th-century *[Canterbury Tales](/wiki/The_Canterbury_Tales)* set out for [Canterbury](/wiki/Canterbury) from London. [William Shakespeare](/wiki/William_Shakespeare) spent a large part of his life living and working in London; his contemporary [Ben Jonson](/wiki/Ben_Jonson) was based there, and his play *[The Alchemist](/wiki/The_Alchemist_(play))* was set in the city. *[A Journal of the Plague Year](/wiki/A_Journal_of_the_Plague_Year)* (1722) by [Daniel Defoe](/wiki/Daniel_Defoe) is a fictionalisation of the events of the 1665 [Great Plague](/wiki/Great_Plague_of_London).

The literary centres of London were hilly [Hampstead](/wiki/Hampstead) and [Bloomsbury](/wiki/Bloomsbury). Writers included the diarist [Samuel Pepys](/wiki/Samuel_Pepys), recording the [Great Fire](/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London); [Charles Dickens](/wiki/Charles_Dickens), describing foggy, grimy [Victorian](/wiki/Victorian_era) London; and the modernist [Virginia Woolf](/wiki/Virginia_Woolf). [Letitia Elizabeth Landon](/wiki/Letitia_Elizabeth_Landon) wrote *Calendar of the London Seasons* in 1834, while [Arthur Conan Doyle](/wiki/Arthur_Conan_Doyle) wrote the [Sherlock Holmes](/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes) stories. [Robert Louis Stevenson](/wiki/Robert_Louis_Stevenson) narrated the *[Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde](/wiki/Strange_Case_of_Dr_Jekyll_and_Mr_Hyde)*, a [gothic](/wiki/Gothic_fiction) London novella. In 1898 [H. G. Wells](/wiki/H._G._Wells)' [science fiction](/wiki/Science_fiction) novel *[The War of the Worlds](/wiki/The_War_of_the_Worlds)* sees London invaded by Martians. In 1925, [Winnie-the-Pooh](/wiki/Winnie-the-Pooh) debuted in London's *[Evening News](/wiki/The_Evening_News_(London_newspaper))*. In the 1940s, [George Orwell](/wiki/George_Orwell) wrote essays in the *[London Evening Standard](/wiki/Evening_Standard)*, including "[The Moon Under Water](/wiki/The_Moon_Under_Water)" (an ideal [pub](/wiki/Pub)). [Wartime evacuation of children](/wiki/Evacuations_of_civilians_in_Britain_during_World_War_II) is depicted in [C. S. Lewis](/wiki/C._S._Lewis)'s *[The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe](/wiki/The_Lion,_the_Witch_and_the_Wardrobe)*. In 1958 [Michael Bond](/wiki/Michael_Bond) created the refugee [Paddington Bear](/wiki/Paddington_Bear). Buckingham Palace appears in [Roald Dahl](/wiki/Roald_Dahl)'s *[The BFG](/wiki/The_BFG)*.

Opened in 1937, the [Odeon cinema](/wiki/Odeon_Luxe_Leicester_Square) in [Leicester Square](/wiki/Leicester_Square) hosts European and world film premieres.

London's film studios have produced many films including the *[James Bond](/wiki/James_Bond)* and *[Harry Potter](/wiki/Harry_Potter_(film_series))* series. A [post-production](/wiki/Post-production) community is centred in [Soho](/wiki/Soho), and London houses six of the world's largest [visual effects](/wiki/Visual_effects) companies, such as [Framestore](/wiki/Framestore). [The Imaginarium](/wiki/The_Imaginarium), a digital [performance-capture](/wiki/Performance-capture) studio, was founded by [Andy Serkis](/wiki/Andy_Serkis). London has been the [setting for numerous films](/wiki/London_in_film). The largest cinema chain in the country, [Odeon Cinemas](/wiki/Odeon_Cinemas), was founded in London in 1928 by [Oscar Deutsch](/wiki/Oscar_Deutsch). The [BFI IMAX](/wiki/BFI_IMAX) on the [South Bank](/wiki/South_Bank) is the largest cinema screen in the UK. The [British Academy Film Awards](/wiki/British_Academy_Film_Awards) have been held in London since [1949](/wiki/1st_British_Academy_Film_Awards), with the [BAFTA Fellowship](/wiki/BAFTA_Fellowship) the Academy's highest accolade. Founded in 1957, the [BFI London Film Festival](/wiki/BFI_London_Film_Festival) takes place over two weeks every October. London is home to several television production companies which produce several programmes.

### Museums, art galleries and libraries

Aerial view of [Albertopolis](/wiki/Albertopolis). The [Albert Memorial](/wiki/Albert_Memorial), [Royal Albert Hall](/wiki/Royal_Albert_Hall), [Royal Geographical Society](/wiki/Royal_Geographical_Society), and [Royal College of Art](/wiki/Royal_College_of_Art) are near the top; [V&A](/wiki/Victoria_and_Albert_Museum) and [Natural History Museum](/wiki/Natural_History_Museum,_London) at the lower end; [Imperial](/wiki/Imperial_College_London), [Royal College of Music](/wiki/Royal_College_of_Music), and [Science Museum](/wiki/Science_Museum,_London) in between.

London is [home to many museums](/wiki/List_of_museums_in_London), galleries, and other institutions, many of which are free of admission charges and are major [tourist attractions](/wiki/Tourist_attraction) as well as playing a research role. The first to be established was the [British Museum](/wiki/British_Museum) in [Bloomsbury](/wiki/Bloomsbury), in 1753. Originally containing antiquities, natural history specimens, and the national library, the museum now has 7 million artefacts from around the globe. In 1824 the [National Gallery](/wiki/National_Gallery) was founded to house the British national collection of Western paintings and is located in [Trafalgar Square](/wiki/Trafalgar_Square).

The [British Library](/wiki/British_Library) is the [second largest library](/wiki/List_of_largest_libraries) in the world, and the UK's [national library](/wiki/National_library). Research libraries include the [Wellcome Library](/wiki/Wellcome_Library) and [Dana Centre](/wiki/Dana_Library_and_Research_Centre), as well as [university libraries](/wiki/Academic_library) like the [British Library of Political and Economic Science](/wiki/British_Library_of_Political_and_Economic_Science) at [LSE](/wiki/London_School_of_Economics), the [Abdus Salam Library](/wiki/Imperial_College_London_Abdus_Salam_Library) at [Imperial](/wiki/Imperial_College_London), the [Maughan Library](/wiki/Maughan_Library) at [King's](/wiki/King%27s_College_London), and the [Senate House Libraries](/wiki/Senate_House_Libraries) at the [University of London](/wiki/University_of_London).

In the 19th century, [South Kensington](/wiki/South_Kensington) was developed as "[Albertopolis](/wiki/Albertopolis)", a cultural and scientific quarter. Three major national museums are there: the [Victoria and Albert Museum](/wiki/Victoria_and_Albert_Museum), the [Natural History Museum](/wiki/Natural_History_Museum,_London) and the [Science Museum](/wiki/London_Science_Museum). The [National Portrait Gallery](/wiki/National_Portrait_Gallery,_London) was founded in 1856 to house depictions of figures from British history; its holdings now comprise the world's most extensive collection of portraits. The national gallery of British art is at [Tate Britain](/wiki/Tate_Britain), originally established as an annexe of the National Gallery in 1897. The Tate Gallery became a major centre for modern art. In 2000 this collection moved to [Tate Modern](/wiki/Tate_Modern), a new gallery housed in the former [Bankside Power Station](/wiki/Bankside_Power_Station).

### Music

The [Royal Albert Hall](/wiki/Royal_Albert_Hall) in [South Kensington](/wiki/South_Kensington) hosts concerts and musical events, including the classical music festival [The Proms](/wiki/BBC_Proms) which are held every summer, as well as cinema screenings of films accompanied with live orchestral music.

London is one of the major classical and [popular music](/wiki/Popular_music) capitals of the world and hosts major music corporations, such as [Universal Music Group International](/wiki/Universal_Music_Group) and [Warner Music Group](/wiki/Warner_Music_Group), and countless bands, musicians and industry professionals. The city contains many orchestras and concert halls, such as the [Barbican Arts Centre](/wiki/Barbican_Arts_Centre), the [Southbank Centre](/wiki/Southbank_Centre), [Cadogan Hall](/wiki/Cadogan_Hall) and the [Royal Albert Hall](/wiki/Royal_Albert_Hall). London's two main opera houses are the [Royal Opera House](/wiki/Royal_Opera_House) and the [London Coliseum](/wiki/London_Coliseum). Several [conservatoires](/wiki/Conservatoire) are within the city: [Royal Academy of Music](/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Music), [Royal College of Music](/wiki/Royal_College_of_Music), [Guildhall School of Music and Drama](/wiki/Guildhall_School_of_Music_and_Drama), and [Trinity Laban](/wiki/Trinity_College_of_Music). The record label [EMI](/wiki/EMI) was formed in the city in 1931, and an early employee for the company, [Alan Blumlein](/wiki/Alan_Blumlein), created [stereo sound](/wiki/Stereo_sound) that year. The guitar amp engineer [Jim Marshall](/wiki/Jim_Marshall_(businessman)) founded [Marshall Amplification](/wiki/Marshall_Amplification) in London in 1962.

[Abbey Road Studios](/wiki/Abbey_Road_Studios)

London has numerous venues for rock and pop concerts, including the world's busiest indoor venue, [the O2 Arena](/wiki/The_O2_Arena), and [Wembley Arena](/wiki/Wembley_Arena), as well as many mid-sized venues, such as [Brixton Academy](/wiki/Brixton_Academy), the [Hammersmith Apollo](/wiki/Hammersmith_Apollo) and the [Shepherd's Bush Empire](/wiki/Shepherd%27s_Bush_Empire). Several [music festivals](/wiki/Music_festivals), including the [Wireless Festival](/wiki/Wireless_Festival), [Lovebox](/wiki/Lovebox_Festival), and [Hyde Park](/wiki/Hyde_Park,_London)'s [British Summer Time](/wiki/British_Summer_Time_(concerts)), are held in London. The city is home to the original [Hard Rock Cafe](/wiki/Hard_Rock_Cafe) and the [Abbey Road Studios](/wiki/Abbey_Road_Studios), where [the Beatles](/wiki/The_Beatles) recorded many of their hits.

London has been instrumental in the development of [punk music](/wiki/Punk_music) and other genres. Artists from London played a prominent role in the development of [synth-pop](/wiki/Synth-pop), including [Eurythmics](/wiki/Eurythmics), whose "[Sweet Dreams (Are Made of This)](/wiki/Sweet_Dreams_(Are_Made_of_This))" was recorded in the attic of their north London home, heralding a trend for home recording methods. Many alternative artists have been based in London; others have brought a Caribbean influence. London is a centre for urban music. In particular the genres [UK garage](/wiki/UK_garage), [drum and bass](/wiki/Drum_and_bass), [dubstep](/wiki/Dubstep), and [grime](/wiki/Grime_(music)) evolved in the city from the foreign genres of [house](/wiki/House_music), [hip hop](/wiki/Hip_hop_music) and reggae, alongside local [drum and bass](/wiki/Drum_and_bass). Music station [BBC Radio 1Xtra](/wiki/BBC_Radio_1Xtra) was set up to support the rise of local [urban contemporary](/wiki/Urban_contemporary) music. The [British Phonographic Industry](/wiki/British_Phonographic_Industry)'s annual popular music awards, the [Brit Awards](/wiki/Brit_Awards), are held in London.

## Recreation

### Parks and open spaces

Main articles: [Parks and open spaces in London](/wiki/Parks_and_open_spaces_in_London) and [Royal Parks of London](/wiki/Royal_Parks_of_London)

See also: [List of Sites of Special Scientific Interest in Greater London](/wiki/List_of_Sites_of_Special_Scientific_Interest_in_Greater_London) and [List of local nature reserves in Greater London](/wiki/List_of_local_nature_reserves_in_Greater_London)

[Hyde Park](/wiki/Hyde_Park,_London) (with [Kensington Gardens](/wiki/Kensington_Gardens) in the foreground) has been a popular public space since it opened in 1637.

A 2013 report by the [City of London Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation) said that London is the "greenest city" in Europe with 35,000 acres (14,164 hectares) of public parks, woodlands and gardens. The city is home to nearly half of its area covered in green and blue spaces, featuring around 3,000 parks, over 8 million trees, and 150 nature reserves.

The largest parks in the [central area of London](/wiki/Central_London) are three of the eight [Royal Parks](/wiki/Royal_Parks_of_London), namely [Hyde Park](/wiki/Hyde_Park,_London) and its neighbour [Kensington Gardens](/wiki/Kensington_Gardens) in the west, and [Regent's Park](/wiki/Regent%27s_Park) to the north. Hyde Park is popular for [sports](#Sport) and sometimes hosts open-air concerts. Regent's Park contains [London Zoo](/wiki/London_Zoo), the world's oldest scientific zoo. [Primrose Hill](/wiki/Primrose_Hill) is a popular spot from which to view the city skyline. Close to Hyde Park are smaller Royal Parks, [Green Park](/wiki/Green_Park) and [St James's Park](/wiki/St_James%27s_Park). Several large parks lie outside the city centre, including [Hampstead Heath](/wiki/Hampstead_Heath) and the remaining Royal Parks of [Greenwich Park](/wiki/Greenwich_Park) to the southeast, and [Bushy Park](/wiki/Bushy_Park) and [Richmond Park](/wiki/Richmond_Park) (the largest) to the southwest. [Crystal Palace Park](/wiki/Crystal_Palace_Park) in south-east London was laid out as a [pleasure ground](/wiki/Pleasure_ground), and, reflecting the public enthusiasm for dinosaurs that first developed in Victorian England, it contains [life-sized models of dinosaurs](/wiki/Crystal_Palace_Dinosaurs).

Close to Richmond Park is [Kew Gardens](/wiki/Kew_Gardens), which has the world's largest collection of living plants. Many parks are administered by borough Councils, including [Victoria Park](/wiki/Victoria_Park,_Tower_Hamlets) in the [East End](/wiki/East_End) and [Battersea Park](/wiki/Battersea_Park) in the centre. Some more informal, semi-natural open spaces, including [Hampstead Heath](/wiki/Hampstead_Heath) and [Epping Forest](/wiki/Epping_Forest), are controlled by the [City of London Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation). Hampstead Heath incorporates [Kenwood House](/wiki/Kenwood_House), a former [stately home](/wiki/Stately_home) and a popular location in the summer months when classical musical concerts are held by the lake. Epping Forest is a popular venue for various outdoor activities, including mountain biking, walking, horse riding, golf, angling, and orienteering. Three of the UK's most-visited theme parks, [Thorpe Park](/wiki/Thorpe_Park) near Staines-upon-Thames, [Chessington World of Adventures](/wiki/Chessington_World_of_Adventures) in Chessington and [Legoland Windsor](/wiki/Legoland_Windsor_Resort), are located within 20 miles (32 km) of London.

### Walking

Main article: [Walking in London](/wiki/Walking_in_London)

See also: [Walking in the United Kingdom](/wiki/Walking_in_the_United_Kingdom)

The Horse Ride is a [tree tunnel](/wiki/Tree_tunnel) (route overhung by trees) on the western side of [Wimbledon Common](/wiki/Wimbledon_Common).

Areas that provide for walks include [Wimbledon Common](/wiki/Wimbledon_Common), [Epping Forest](/wiki/Epping_Forest), [Hampton Court Park](/wiki/Hampton_Court_Park), [Hampstead Heath](/wiki/Hampstead_Heath), the eight [Royal Parks](/wiki/Royal_Parks), [Regents Canal](/wiki/Regents_Canal) Walk, canals and disused railway tracks. Access to canals and rivers has improved recently, including the creation of the [Thames Path](/wiki/Thames_Path), some 28 miles (45 km) of which is within [Greater London](/wiki/Greater_London), and the [Wandle Trail](/wiki/Wandle_Trail) along the [River Wandle](/wiki/River_Wandle).

Other [long-distance paths](/wiki/Long-distance_path), linking green spaces, have been created, including the [Capital Ring](/wiki/Capital_Ring), the [Green Chain Walk](/wiki/Green_Chain_Walk), [London Outer Orbital Path](/wiki/London_Outer_Orbital_Path) ("Loop"), [Jubilee Walkway](/wiki/Jubilee_Walkway), [Lea Valley Walk](/wiki/Lea_Valley_Walk) and the [Diana, Princess of Wales Memorial Walk](/wiki/Diana,_Princess_of_Wales_Memorial_Walk).

## Sport

Main article: [Sport in London](/wiki/Sport_in_London)

See also: [Football in London](/wiki/Football_in_London) and [Rugby union in London](/wiki/Rugby_union_in_London)

London has hosted the [Summer Olympics](/wiki/Summer_Olympic_Games) in [1908](/wiki/1908_Summer_Olympics), [1948](/wiki/1948_Summer_Olympics), and [2012](/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics), making it the first city to host the modern Games three times. The city was host to the [British Empire Games](/wiki/Commonwealth_Games) in [1934](/wiki/1934_British_Empire_Games). In 2017, London hosted the [World Championships in Athletics](/wiki/2017_World_Championships_in_Athletics) for the first time.

London's [most popular sport](/wiki/Sport_in_the_United_Kingdom#Popularity) is [association football](/wiki/Association_football), with seven clubs in the [Premier League](/wiki/Premier_League) in the [2025–26 season](/wiki/2025%E2%80%9326_Premier_League): [Arsenal](/wiki/Arsenal_F.C.), [Brentford](/wiki/Brentford_F.C.), [Chelsea](/wiki/Chelsea_F.C.), [Crystal Palace](/wiki/Crystal_Palace_F.C.), [Fulham](/wiki/Fulham_F.C.), [Tottenham Hotspur](/wiki/Tottenham_Hotspur_F.C.) and [West Ham United](/wiki/West_Ham_United_F.C.). Five London-based teams are in the [Women's Super League](/wiki/Women%27s_Super_League): [Arsenal](/wiki/Arsenal_W.F.C.), [Chelsea](/wiki/Chelsea_F.C._Women), [London City Lionesses](/wiki/London_City_Lionesses), [Tottenham](/wiki/Tottenham_Hotspur_F.C._Women) and [West Ham United](/wiki/West_Ham_United_F.C._Women).

Two [Premiership Rugby](/wiki/Premiership_Rugby) union teams are based in Greater London: [Harlequins](/wiki/Harlequin_F.C.) and [Saracens](/wiki/Saracens_F.C.). [Ealing Trailfinders](/wiki/Ealing_Trailfinders_Rugby_Club) and [London Scottish](/wiki/London_Scottish_F.C.) play in the [RFU Championship](/wiki/RFU_Championship); other rugby union clubs in the city include [Richmond](/wiki/Richmond_F.C.), [Rosslyn Park](/wiki/Rosslyn_Park_F.C.), [Westcombe Park](/wiki/Westcombe_Park_RFC), and [Blackheath](/wiki/Blackheath_F.C.). [Twickenham Stadium](/wiki/Twickenham_Stadium) in south-west London hosts home matches for the [England national rugby union team](/wiki/England_national_rugby_union_team). While [rugby league](/wiki/Rugby_league) is more popular in the north of England, the sport has one professional club in London – the [London Broncos](/wiki/London_Broncos), while the [Challenge Cup](/wiki/Challenge_Cup) final often takes place in the city.

The [Wimbledon Tennis Championships](/wiki/Wimbledon_Championships) is held at the [All England Club](/wiki/All_England_Lawn_Tennis_and_Croquet_Club) in the south-western suburb of [Wimbledon](/wiki/Wimbledon,_London) since 1877. Played in late June to early July, it is the oldest tennis tournament in the world and widely considered the most prestigious.

London has two [Test cricket](/wiki/Test_cricket) grounds which host the [England cricket team](/wiki/England_cricket_team), [Lord's](/wiki/Lord%27s) (home of [Middlesex C.C.C.](/wiki/Middlesex_County_Cricket_Club)) and [the Oval](/wiki/The_Oval) (home of [Surrey C.C.C.](/wiki/Surrey_County_Cricket_Club)). Lord's has hosted four finals of the [Cricket World Cup](/wiki/Cricket_World_Cup) and is known as the "Home of Cricket". In golf, the [Wentworth Club](/wiki/Wentworth_Club) is located in [Virginia Water](/wiki/Virginia_Water), Surrey on the south-west fringes of London, while the closest venue to London that is used as one of the courses for [the Open Championship](/wiki/The_Open_Championship), the oldest major and tournament in golf, is [Royal St George's](/wiki/Royal_St_George%27s_Golf_Club) in Sandwich, Kent. The rules of modern boxing, the [Queensberry Rules](/wiki/Queensberry_Rules), were formulated in London in 1867, and since then fights have taken place at [York Hall](/wiki/York_Hall) (the spiritual home of British boxing), and larger arenas and stadiums across the city. [Alexandra Palace](/wiki/Alexandra_Palace) in north London hosts the [PDC World Darts Championship](/wiki/PDC_World_Darts_Championship) and the [Masters snooker](/wiki/Masters_(snooker)) tournament. Other key annual events are the mass-participation [London Marathon](/wiki/London_Marathon) and the [University Boat Race](/wiki/University_Boat_Race) on the Thames contested between [Oxford](/wiki/Oxford_University_Boat_Club) and [Cambridge](/wiki/Cambridge_University_Boat_Club).

[Wembley Stadium](/wiki/Wembley_Stadium), home of the England [men](/wiki/England_national_football_team) and [women's football team](/wiki/England_women%27s_national_football_team) and the [FA Cup Final](/wiki/FA_Cup_Final), has a seating capacity of 90,000. It is the UK's biggest stadium.

[Centre Court](/wiki/Centre_Court) at [Wimbledon](/wiki/The_Championships,_Wimbledon). Held every June and July, Wimbledon is the oldest tennis tournament in the world, and the only major played on grass.

[Twickenham](/wiki/Twickenham_Stadium), home of the [England national rugby union team](/wiki/England_national_rugby_union_team), has a capacity of 82,000 seats.
