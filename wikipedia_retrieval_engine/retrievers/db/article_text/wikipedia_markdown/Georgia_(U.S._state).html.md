"State of Georgia" and "Georgia, United States" redirect here. For the country, see [Georgia (country)](/wiki/Georgia_(country)). For other uses, see [Georgia (disambiguation)](/wiki/Georgia_(disambiguation)).

**Georgia** ([/ˈdʒɔːrdʒə/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [ⓘ](/wiki/File:En-us-Georgia.ogg) [*JOR-jə*](/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key)) is a [state](/wiki/U.S._state) in the [Southeastern](/wiki/Southeastern_United_States), [South Atlantic](/wiki/South_Atlantic_states), and [Deep South](/wiki/Deep_South) regions of the United States. It borders [Tennessee](/wiki/Tennessee) to the northwest, [North Carolina](/wiki/North_Carolina) and [South Carolina](/wiki/South_Carolina) to the northeast, the [Atlantic Ocean](/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean) to the east, [Florida](/wiki/Florida) to the south, and [Alabama](/wiki/Alabama) to the west. Of the [50 U.S. states](/wiki/List_of_states_and_territories_of_the_United_States), Georgia is the [24th-largest by area](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_area) and [eighth-most populous](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_population). According to the [U.S. Census Bureau](/wiki/U.S._Census_Bureau), its 2025 estimated population was 11,302,748. [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta), a [global city](/wiki/Global_city), is both the state's [capital](/wiki/List_of_capitals_in_the_United_States) and its largest city. The [Atlanta metropolitan area](/wiki/Atlanta_metropolitan_area), with a population greater than 6.3 million people in 2023, is the [eighth most populous metropolitan area](/wiki/List_of_metropolitan_statistical_areas) in the United States and contains about 57% of Georgia's entire population. Other major metropolitan areas in the state include [Augusta](/wiki/Augusta_metropolitan_area), [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah_metropolitan_area), [Columbus](/wiki/Columbus_metropolitan_area,_Georgia), and [Macon](/wiki/Macon_metropolitan_area,_Georgia).

The [Province of Georgia](/wiki/Province_of_Georgia) was established in 1732, with its first settlement occurring in 1733 when [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia) was founded. By 1752, Georgia had transitioned into a British [royal colony](/wiki/Crown_colony), making it the last and southernmost of the original [Thirteen Colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies). On January 2, 1788, Georgia became the fourth state to [ratify](/wiki/Ratification) the [United States Constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_States). Between 1802 and 1804, a portion of western Georgia was carved out to create the [Mississippi Territory](/wiki/Mississippi_Territory), which eventually became the U.S. states of [Alabama](/wiki/Alabama) and [Mississippi](/wiki/Mississippi). Georgia declared its [secession](/wiki/Secession_in_the_United_States) from the Union on January 19, 1861, joining the ranks of the original seven [Confederate States](/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America). After the [Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War), it was the last state to be readmitted to the Union on July 15, 1870.

In the late 19th century, during the post-[Reconstruction](/wiki/Reconstruction_era) period, Georgia's economy underwent significant changes, driven by a coalition of influential politicians, business leaders, and journalists, notably [Henry W. Grady](/wiki/Henry_W._Grady), who promoted the "[New South](/wiki/New_South)" ideology focused on reconciliation and [industrialization](/wiki/Industrialization_in_the_United_States). In the mid-20th century, several notable figures from Georgia, including [Martin Luther King Jr.](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.), emerged as key leaders in the [civil rights movement](/wiki/Civil_rights_movement). Since 1945, Georgia has experienced significant population and economic expansion, aligning with the larger [Sun Belt](/wiki/Sun_Belt) trend.

Georgia is defined by a diversity of landscapes, flora, and fauna, such as the [Blue Ridge Mountains](/wiki/Blue_Ridge_Mountains) (part of the broader [Appalachian Mountain](/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains) range) and the [Piedmont](/wiki/Piedmont_(United_States)) plateau. Except for some elevated areas in the Blue Ridge, Georgia predominantly experiences a [humid subtropical climate](/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate).

## History

Main article: [History of Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/History_of_Georgia_(U.S._state))

### Pre-settlement

Before settlement by [European colonists](/wiki/European_colonization_of_the_Americas), Georgia was governed by [mound building](/wiki/Mound_Builders) polities such as [Ocute](/wiki/Ocute) and [Coosa](/wiki/Coosa_chiefdom).

### Colonial era and Revolutionary War

Main article: [Province of Georgia](/wiki/Province_of_Georgia)

On February 12, 1733, a year after Georgia was established as a British colony, the [Province of Georgia](/wiki/Province_of_Georgia) was established in [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia) by British General [James Oglethorpe](/wiki/James_Oglethorpe). It was administered by the [Trustees for the Establishment of the Colony of Georgia in America](/wiki/Trustees_for_the_Establishment_of_the_Colony_of_Georgia_in_America) under a charter issued by (and named for) [King George II](/wiki/George_II_of_Great_Britain). The Trustees implemented an elaborate plan for the colony's settlement, known as the [Oglethorpe Plan](/wiki/Oglethorpe_Plan), which envisioned an agrarian society of [yeoman farmers](/wiki/Yeoman) and prohibited slavery. The colony was [invaded by the Spanish](/wiki/Invasion_of_Georgia_(1742)) in 1742, during the [War of Jenkins' Ear](/wiki/War_of_Jenkins%27_Ear). In 1752, after the government failed to renew subsidies that had helped support the colony, the Trustees turned over control to the [crown](/wiki/Monarchy_of_the_United_Kingdom). Georgia became a [crown colony](/wiki/Crown_colony#History), with a governor appointed by the king of [Great Britain](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain).

The Province of Georgia was one of the [Thirteen Colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) that revolted against [British rule](/wiki/British_Empire) in the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution). Its delegates to the [Second Continental Congress](/wiki/Second_Continental_Congress), which convened in present-day [Independence Hall](/wiki/Independence_Hall) in [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia), joined other delegates in unanimously approving the [Declaration of Independence](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence), which declared the Thirteen Colonies free and independent from British colonial rule.

### Independence

Georgia's first constitution was ratified in February 1777. Georgia was the 10th state to ratify the Articles of Confederation on July 24, 1778, and was the 4th state to ratify the [United States Constitution](/wiki/United_States_Constitution) on January 2, 1788.

Cotton slaves in Georgia, c. 1850

After the [Creek War (1813–1814)](/wiki/Creek_War), General [Andrew Jackson](/wiki/Andrew_Jackson) forced the [Muscogee (Creek) tribes](/wiki/Muscogee_Nation) to surrender land to Georgia, including in the [Treaty of Fort Jackson (1814)](/wiki/Treaty_of_Fort_Jackson), surrendering 21 million acres in what is now southern Georgia and central Alabama, and the [Treaty of Indian Springs (1825)](/wiki/Treaty_of_Indian_Springs_(1825)). In 1829, gold was discovered in the [North Georgia mountains](/wiki/North_Georgia_mountains) leading to the [Georgia Gold Rush](/wiki/Georgia_Gold_Rush) and establishment of a [federal mint](/wiki/United_States_Mint) in [Dahlonega](/wiki/Dahlonega,_Georgia), which continued in operation until 1861. The resulting influx of [American settlers](/wiki/American_pioneer) put pressure on the [federal U.S. government](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States) to take land from the [Cherokee Nation](/wiki/Cherokee_Nation_(1794%E2%80%931907)). In 1830, President [Andrew Jackson](/wiki/Andrew_Jackson) signed into law the [Indian Removal Act](/wiki/Indian_Removal_Act), sending many eastern Indian nations to [reservations](/wiki/Indian_reservation) in present-day Oklahoma, including all of Georgia's tribes. Despite the Supreme Court's ruling in *[Worcester v. Georgia](/wiki/Worcester_v._Georgia)* (1832) that U.S. states were not permitted to redraw Indian boundaries, President Jackson and the state of Georgia ignored the ruling. In 1838, his successor, [Martin Van Buren](/wiki/Martin_Van_Buren), dispatched federal troops to gather the tribes and deport them west of the [Mississippi](/wiki/Mississippi_River). This forced relocation, known as the [Trail of Tears](/wiki/Trail_of_Tears), led to the death of more than four thousand Cherokees.

### American Civil War

The [Battle of Kennesaw Mountain](/wiki/Battle_of_Kennesaw_Mountain) in 1864

In early 1861, as the [American Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War) commenced, Georgia chose to leave the [Union](/wiki/Union_(American_Civil_War)) to join the [Confederacy](/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America). Support for secession from the Union enjoyed a majority among the state's delegates, which grew during successive votes, and the state ultimately became one of several major [military theaters](/wiki/Theater_(warfare)) during the Civil War.

Major battles took place at [Chickamauga](/wiki/Battle_of_Chickamauga), [Kennesaw Mountain](/wiki/Battle_of_Kennesaw_Mountain), and [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta). In December 1864, a large swath of the state from Atlanta to [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia), was destroyed during General [William Tecumseh Sherman](/wiki/William_Tecumseh_Sherman)'s [March to the Sea](/wiki/Sherman%27s_March_to_the_Sea), during which 18,253 Georgian soldiers were killed, representing roughly one of every five then in service of the Confederacy. One of the most notorious Civil War sites in the state was the [Andersonville Prison](/wiki/Andersonville_Prison), where nearly 13,000 Union [prisoners of war](/wiki/Prisoners_of_war) died because of inhumane conditions and ill treatment. Following the war, the camp's commander [Henry Wirz](/wiki/Henry_Wirz) was sentenced to death for [war crimes](/wiki/War_crimes) and hanged, making him the highest-ranking Confederate official to be executed.

### Reconstruction and civil rights

A so-called "Colored" waiting room sign in 1943 at a bus terminal in [Rome, Georgia](/wiki/Rome,_Georgia), where [Jim Crow laws](/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws) created "[de jure](/wiki/De_jure)" legally required [segregation](/wiki/Racial_segregation_in_the_United_States)

Georgia did not re-enter the Union until July 15, 1870, as the last of the former Confederate states to be re-admitted. Federal troops would continue to be stationed in the state until the end of the [Reconstruction era](/wiki/Reconstruction_era) in 1877. With white Democrats having regained power in the state legislature, they passed a [poll tax](/wiki/Poll_tax_(United_States)) that year which [disenfranchised](/wiki/Disfranchisement_after_Reconstruction_era) many poor black (and some white) people, preventing them from registering. In 1908, the state established a [white primary](/wiki/White_primary); with the only competitive contests within the Democratic Party, it was another way to exclude black people from politics. They constituted 46.7% of the state's population in 1900, but the proportion of Georgia's population that was African American dropped thereafter to 28%, primarily due to tens of thousands leaving the state during the [Great Migration](/wiki/Great_Migration_(African_American)).

According to the [Equal Justice Initiative](/wiki/Equal_Justice_Initiative)'s 2015 report on lynching in the United States (1877–1950), Georgia had 531 deaths, the second-highest total of these extralegal executions of any state in the South. The overwhelming number of victims were black and male. Many of the killings were committed by the [white supremacist](/wiki/White_supremacist) hate group the [Ku Klux Klan](/wiki/Ku_Klux_Klan) (KKK), whose second iteration was formed at Georgia's [Stone Mountain](/wiki/Stone_Mountain,_Georgia) by [William Joseph Simmons](/wiki/William_Joseph_Simmons) on November 25, 1915. The Klan's revival was spurned in part by the 1913 murder of 13-year-old Mary Phagan and the lynching two years later of her convicted killer, Jewish pencil factory supervisor and [B'nai B'rith](/wiki/B%27nai_B%27rith) Atlanta chapter president [Leo Frank](/wiki/Leo_Frank). The affair led to the creation of the [Anti-Defamation League](/wiki/Anti-Defamation_League), which successfully lobbied for Frank to be posthumously pardoned in 1986. Political disfranchisement persisted through the mid-1960s, until after Congress passed the [Voting Rights Act of 1965](/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965).

[Martin Luther King Jr.](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.)'s tomb at [Martin Luther King Jr. National Historical Park](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr._National_Historical_Park) in [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta)

[Martin Luther King Jr.](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.), an Atlanta-born [Baptist minister](/wiki/Baptists) who was part of the educated middle class that had developed in the city's African-American community, emerged as a national leader in the [civil rights movement](/wiki/Civil_rights_movement) in the 1950s. King joined with others to form the [Southern Christian Leadership Conference](/wiki/Southern_Christian_Leadership_Conference) (SCLC) in Atlanta in 1957 to provide political leadership for the civil rights movement across the South. In 1956, riots occurred at the [Sugar Bowl](/wiki/1956_Sugar_Bowl) in Atlanta following a clash between [Georgia Tech](/wiki/Georgia_Tech)'s president [Blake R. Van Leer](/wiki/Blake_R._Van_Leer) and Governor [Marvin Griffin](/wiki/Marvin_Griffin).

On February 5, 1958, during a training mission flown by a [B-47](/wiki/Boeing_B-47_Stratojet), a [Mark 15 nuclear bomb](/wiki/Mark_15_nuclear_bomb), also known as the [Tybee Bomb](/wiki/Tybee_Bomb), was lost off the coast of [Tybee Island](/wiki/Tybee_Island,_Georgia) near Savannah. The bomb was thought by the [Department of Energy](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy) to lie buried in silt at the bottom of [Wassaw Sound](/wiki/Wassaw_Sound).

By the 1960s, the proportion of African Americans in Georgia had declined to 28% of the state's population, after waves of migration to the North and some immigration by whites. With their voting power diminished, it took some years for African Americans to win a state-wide office. [Julian Bond](/wiki/Julian_Bond), a civil rights leader, was elected to the Georgia's House of Representatives in 1965, and served multiple terms there and subsequently in Georgia's State Senate.

Atlanta mayor [Ivan Allen Jr.](/wiki/Ivan_Allen_Jr.) testified before [Congress](/wiki/United_States_Congress) in support of the Civil Rights Act, and Governor [Carl Sanders](/wiki/Carl_Sanders) worked with the [Kennedy administration](/wiki/Presidency_of_John_F._Kennedy) charged with ensuring the state's compliance. [Ralph McGill](/wiki/Ralph_McGill), editor and syndicated columnist at the *[Atlanta Constitution](/wiki/Atlanta_Constitution)*, wrote supportively of civil rights movement. In 1970, [Jimmy Carter](/wiki/Jimmy_Carter), who was recently elected the state's governor, declared in his inaugural address that the era of racial segregation had ended. In 1972, Georgians elected [Andrew Young](/wiki/Andrew_Young) to Congress as the first African American Congressman since the [Reconstruction era](/wiki/Reconstruction_era).

### Other early 20th century

In 1910, a secret meeting was held on [Jekyll Island](/wiki/Jekyll_Island), off Georgia's Atlantic coast, to plan for the creation of an American central banking system. The decisions made at the meeting [led toward](/wiki/Panic_of_1907#Aftermath) the passing of the [Federal Reserve Act](/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Act) of 1913.

### Late 20th and early 21st centuries

In 1980, construction was completed on an expansion of what is now named [Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport](/wiki/Hartsfield%E2%80%93Jackson_Atlanta_International_Airport) (ATL). The busiest and most efficient airport in the world, it accommodates more than a hundred million passengers annually. Employing more than 60,000 people, the airport became a major engine for economic growth. With the advantages of cheap real estate, low taxes, [right-to-work laws](/wiki/Right-to-work_law) and a regulatory environment limiting government interference, the Atlanta metropolitan area became a national center of finance, insurance, technology, manufacturing, real estate, logistics, and transportation companies, as well as the film, convention, and trade show businesses. As a testament to the city's growing international profile, in 1990 the [International Olympic Committee](/wiki/International_Olympic_Committee) selected [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta) as the site of the [1996 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1996_Summer_Olympics). Taking advantage of Atlanta's status as a transportation hub, in 1991, [UPS](/wiki/United_Parcel_Service) established its headquarters in the suburb of [Sandy Springs](/wiki/Sandy_Springs). In 1992, construction finished on [Bank of America Plaza](/wiki/Bank_of_America_Plaza_(Atlanta)), the tallest building in the U.S. outside of New York City or Chicago.

In 2024, it was announced that Atlanta would host multiple games during the [2026 FIFA World Cup](/wiki/2026_FIFA_World_Cup), which further substantiated the economic investment and growth in the city and state.

### Boundaries

Beginning from the Atlantic Ocean, the state's eastern border with South Carolina runs up the [Savannah River](/wiki/Savannah_River), northwest to its origin at the [confluence](/wiki/Confluence) of the [Tugaloo](/wiki/Tugaloo_River) and [Seneca Rivers](/wiki/Seneca_River_(South_Carolina)). It then continues up the Tugaloo (originally Tugalo) and into the [Chattooga River](/wiki/Chattooga_River), its most significant [tributary](/wiki/Tributary). These bounds were decided in the 1797 [Treaty of Beaufort](/wiki/Treaty_of_Beaufort), and tested in the [U.S. Supreme Court](/wiki/U.S._Supreme_Court) in the two *[Georgia v. South Carolina](/wiki/Georgia_v._South_Carolina)* cases in 1923 and 1989.

The border then takes a sharp turn around the tip of [Rabun County](/wiki/Rabun_County,_Georgia), at [latitude 35°N](/wiki/35th_parallel_north), though from this point it diverges slightly south (due to inaccuracies in the original survey, conducted in 1818). This northern border was originally the Georgia and North Carolina border all the way to the [Mississippi River](/wiki/Mississippi_River), until [Tennessee](/wiki/Tennessee) was divided from [North Carolina](/wiki/North_Carolina), and the Yazoo companies induced the legislature of Georgia to pass an act, approved by the governor in 1795, to sell the greater part of Georgia's territory presently comprising [Alabama](/wiki/Alabama) and [Mississippi](/wiki/Mississippi).

The state's western border runs in a straight line south-southeastward from a point southwest of [Chattanooga](/wiki/Chattanooga,_Tennessee), to meet the [Chattahoochee River](/wiki/Chattahoochee_River) near [West Point](/wiki/West_Point,_Georgia). It continues downriver to the point where it joins the [Flint River](/wiki/Flint_River_(Georgia)) (the confluence of the two forming Florida's [Apalachicola River](/wiki/Apalachicola_River)); the southern border goes almost due east and very slightly south, in a straight line to the [St. Mary's River](/wiki/St._Marys_River_(Florida/Georgia)), which then forms the remainder of the boundary back to the ocean.

The water boundaries are still set to be the original [thalweg](/wiki/Thalweg) of the rivers. Since then, several have been inundated by lakes created by dams, including the [Apalachicola/Chattahoochee/Flint](/wiki/ACF_River_Basin) point now under [Lake Seminole](/wiki/Lake_Seminole).

An 1818 survey erroneously placed Georgia's border with Tennessee one mile (1.6 km) south of the intended location of the 35th parallel north. State legislators [still dispute this placement](/wiki/Tennessee%E2%80%93Georgia_water_dispute), as correction of this inaccuracy would allow Georgia access to water from the [Tennessee River](/wiki/Tennessee_River).

### Geology and terrain

Main article: [Geology of Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Geology_of_Georgia_(U.S._state))

USGS map of Georgia elevations

Georgia consists of five principal [physiographic regions](/wiki/Physiographic_regions_of_the_United_States): The [Cumberland Plateau](/wiki/Cumberland_Plateau), [Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians](/wiki/Ridge-and-Valley_Appalachians), [Blue Ridge Mountains](/wiki/Blue_Ridge_Mountains), [Piedmont](/wiki/Piedmont_(United_States)), and the [Atlantic coastal plain](/wiki/Atlantic_coastal_plain). Each region has its own distinctive characteristics. For instance, the region, which lies in the northwest corner of the state, includes [limestone](/wiki/Limestone), [sandstone](/wiki/Sandstone), [shale](/wiki/Shale), and other [sedimentary rocks](/wiki/Sedimentary_rock), which have yielded construction-grade limestone, [barite](/wiki/Baryte), [ocher](/wiki/Ochre), and small amounts of [coal](/wiki/Coal).

### Ecology

Main article: [Ecology of Georgia](/wiki/Ecology_of_Georgia_(U.S._state))

The state of Georgia has approximately 250 tree species and 58 protected plants. Georgia's native trees include [red cedar](/wiki/Juniperus_virginiana), a variety of [pines](/wiki/Pine), [oaks](/wiki/Oak), [hollies](/wiki/Holly), [cypress](/wiki/Taxodium), [sweetgum](/wiki/Liquidambar_styraciflua), scaly-bark and white [hickories](/wiki/Hickory), and [sabal palmetto](/wiki/Sabal_palmetto). East Georgia is in the subtropical coniferous forest biome and conifer species as other broadleaf evergreen flora make up the majority of the southern and coastal regions. [Yellow jasmine](/wiki/Gelsemium_sempervirens) and [mountain laurel](/wiki/Kalmia_latifolia) make up just a few of the flowering shrubs in the state.

[White-tailed deer](/wiki/White-tailed_deer) are found in nearly all counties of Georgia. The [northern mockingbird](/wiki/Northern_mockingbird) and [brown thrasher](/wiki/Brown_thrasher) are among the 160 bird species that live in the state.

Reptiles include the [eastern diamondback](/wiki/Eastern_diamondback), [copperhead](/wiki/Agkistrodon_contortrix), and [cottonmouth](/wiki/Agkistrodon_piscivorus) snakes as well as alligators; amphibians include [salamanders](/wiki/Salamander), [frogs](/wiki/Frog) and [toads](/wiki/Toad). There are about 79 species of reptile and 63 amphibians known to live in Georgia. The Argentine black and white tegu is currently an invasive species in Georgia. It poses a problem to local wildlife by chasing down and killing many native species and dominating habitats.

The most popular freshwater game fish are [trout](/wiki/Trout), [bream](/wiki/Bream), [bass](/wiki/Bass_(fish)), and [catfish](/wiki/Catfish), all but the last of which are produced in state hatcheries for restocking. Popular saltwater game fish include [red drum](/wiki/Red_drum), [spotted seatrout](/wiki/Spotted_seatrout), [flounder](/wiki/Flounder), and [tarpon](/wiki/Tarpon). [Porpoises](/wiki/Porpoise), whales, [shrimp](/wiki/Shrimp), [oysters](/wiki/Oyster), and [blue crabs](/wiki/Callinectes_sapidus) are found inshore and offshore of the Georgia coast.

In 2008, a Georgia man was convicted for illegally killing a vagrant [Florida panther](/wiki/Florida_panther) that had walked 960 km to [Troup County](/wiki/Troup_County) from SW Florida.

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Climate_of_Georgia_(U.S._state))

Further information: [Climate change in Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Climate_change_in_Georgia_(U.S._state))

The majority of the state is primarily a [humid subtropical climate](/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate). Hot and humid summers are typical, except at the highest elevations. The entire state, including the [North Georgia mountains](/wiki/North_Georgia_mountains), receives moderate to heavy precipitation, which varies from 45 inches (1,100 mm) in central Georgia to approximately 75 inches (1,900 mm) around the northeast part of the state. The degree to which the weather of a certain region of Georgia is subtropical depends on the latitude, its proximity to the Atlantic Ocean or [Gulf of Mexico](/wiki/Gulf_of_Mexico), and the elevation. The latter factor is felt chiefly in the mountainous areas of the northern part of the state, which are farther away from the ocean and can be 4,500 feet (1,400 m) above sea level. The USDA plant [hardiness zones](/wiki/Hardiness_zone) for Georgia range from zone 6b (no colder than −5 °F (−21 °C)) in the [Blue Ridge Mountains](/wiki/Blue_Ridge_Mountains) to zone 8b (no colder than 15 °F (−9 °C) ) along the [Atlantic](/wiki/Atlantic) coast and Florida border.

Image of March [1993 Storm of the Century](/wiki/1993_Storm_of_the_Century) covering the length of the east coast; the outline of Georgia is discernible in the center of the image

The highest temperature ever recorded is 112 °F (44 °C) in [Louisville](/wiki/Louisville,_Georgia) on July 24, 1952, while the lowest is −17 °F (−27 °C) in northern [Floyd County](/wiki/Floyd_County,_Georgia) on January 27, 1940. Georgia is one of the leading states in frequency of [tornadoes](/wiki/Tornado), though they are rarely stronger than [EF1](/wiki/Enhanced_Fujita_scale). Although tornadoes striking the city are very rare, an EF2 tornado hit down
town Atlanta on March 14, 2008, causing moderate to severe damage to various buildings. With a coastline on the Atlantic Ocean, Georgia is also vulnerable to [hurricanes](/wiki/Hurricane), although direct hurricane strikes were rare during the 20th century. Georgia often is affected by hurricanes that strike the [Florida Panhandle](/wiki/Florida_Panhandle), weaken over land, and bring strong [tropical storm](/wiki/Tropical_storm) winds and heavy rain to the interior, a recent example being [Hurricane Michael](/wiki/Hurricane_Michael), as well as hurricanes that come close to the Georgia coastline, brushing the coast on their way north without ever making landfall. [Hurricane Matthew of 2016](/wiki/Hurricane_Matthew) and [Hurricane Dorian of 2019](/wiki/Hurricane_Dorian) did just that.

[Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta), located in north-central Georgia at the [Eastern Continental Divide](/wiki/Eastern_Continental_Divide), has been Georgia's [capital city](/wiki/List_of_capitals_in_the_United_States) since 1868. It is the most populous city in Georgia, with a 2020 U.S. census population of just over 498,000. The state has seventeen cities with populations over 50,000, based on official 2020 U.S. census data.

Along with the rest of the Southeast, Georgia's population continues to grow rapidly, with primary gains concentrated in urban areas. Metropolitan Atlanta is the cultural and economic center of the [Southeast](/wiki/Southeastern_United_States); its official population in 2020 was over 6 million, or 57% of Georgia's total population.

The [United States Census Bureau](/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau) reported Georgia's official population to be 10,711,908 as of the [2020 United States census](/wiki/2020_United_States_census). This was an increase of 1,024,255, or 10.57% over the [2010](/wiki/2010_United_States_census) figure of 9,687,653 residents. The state made up 3.14% of the total US population in 2010 and 3.23% in 2020.

As of 2010, the number of [illegal immigrants](/wiki/Illegal_immigration_to_the_United_States) living in Georgia more than doubled to 480,000 from January 2000 to January 2009, according to a federal report. That gave Georgia the greatest percentage increase among the 10 states with the biggest illegal immigrant populations during those years. Georgia has banned [sanctuary cities](/wiki/Sanctuary_city).

In 2018, the top countries of origin for Georgia's immigrants were [Mexico](/wiki/Mexico), [India](/wiki/India), [Jamaica](/wiki/Jamaica), [Korea](/wiki/Korea), and [Guatemala](/wiki/Guatemala).

There were 743,000 [veterans](/wiki/Veteran) in 2009.

According to [HUD](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Housing_and_Urban_Development)'s 2022 [Annual Homeless Assessment Report](/wiki/Annual_Homeless_Assessment_Report_to_Congress), there were an estimated 10,689 [homeless](/wiki/Homelessness) people in Georgia.

In the [1980 census](/wiki/1980_United_States_census), 1,584,303 people from Georgia claimed [English](/wiki/English_American) ancestry out of a total state population of 3,994,817, making them 40% of the state, and the largest ethnic group at the time. Today, many of these same people claim they are of "American" ancestry, as do many of [Scots-Irish](/wiki/Scottish-Irish_American) descent; however, their families have lived in the state for so long, in many cases since the colonial period, that they choose to identify simply as having "American" ancestry or do not in fact know their own ancestry.

Historically, about half of Georgia's population was composed of African Americans who, before the [American Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War), were almost exclusively enslaved. The [Great Migration](/wiki/Great_Migration_(African_American)) of hundreds of thousands of blacks from the rural South to the industrial North from 1914 to 1970 reduced the African American population. However, the proportion of Georgia's population that is Black has increased since 1990 and today the state is third in percent of the total population that is African American (after [Mississippi](/wiki/Mississippi) and [Louisiana](/wiki/Louisiana)) and third in numeric Black population after New York and Florida.

Georgia had the second-fastest-growing Asian population growth in the U.S. from 1990 to 2000, more than doubling in size during the ten-year period. Indian people and Chinese people are the largest Asian groups in Georgia. Georgia also has a sizeable Hispanic population. Many are of Mexican descent.

Georgia is the state with the third-lowest percentage of older people (65 or older), at 12.8 percent (as of 2015). As of 2011, 58.8% of Georgia's population younger than 1 were minorities (meaning they had at least one parent who was not non-Hispanic white) compared to other states like California with 75.1%, Texas with 69.8%, and New York with 55.6%.

The colonial settlement of large numbers of [Scottish American](/wiki/Scottish_American), [English American](/wiki/English_American) and [Scotch-Irish Americans](/wiki/Scotch-Irish_American) in the mountains and Piedmont, and coastal settlement by some [English Americans](/wiki/English_American) and African Americans, have strongly influenced the state's culture in food, language and music. The concentration of African slaves repeatedly "imported" to coastal areas in the 18th century from rice-growing regions of West Africa led to the development of [Gullah](/wiki/Gullah)-Geechee language and culture in the Low Country among African Americans. They share a unique heritage in which many African traditions of food, religion and culture were retained. In the creolization of Southern culture, their foodways became an integral part of Low Country cooking. Sephardic Jews, French-speaking Swiss people, Moravians, Irish convicts, Piedmont Italians and Russian people immigrated to the state during the colonial era.

The largest European ancestry groups as of 2011 were: [English](/wiki/English_American) 8.1%, [Irish](/wiki/Irish_American) 8.1%, and [German](/wiki/German_American) 7.2%.

### Native American tribes

Tribes which historically lived in what is now Georgia include the [Muscogee](/wiki/Muscogee) (including the [Hitchiti](/wiki/Hitchiti) subgroup), the [Cherokee](/wiki/Cherokee), the [Oconi](/wiki/Oconi), the [Guale](/wiki/Guale), the [Yamasee](/wiki/Yamasee) and the [Apalachee](/wiki/Apalachee). Other tribes which at various times lived in or migrated through Georgia include the [Apalachicola](/wiki/Apalachicola_(tribal_town)), the [Chatot](/wiki/Chacato), the [Yuchi](/wiki/Yuchi), the Chiaha, the [Chickasaw](/wiki/Chickasaw), the Okmulgee, the [Shawnee](/wiki/Shawnee) and the [Timucua](/wiki/Timucua). Today there are no [federally recognized](/wiki/List_of_federally_recognized_tribes_by_state) tribes in Georgia, but there are three [state-recognized](/wiki/State-recognized_tribes_in_the_United_States) tribes. Many inhabitants of Georgia identify as being Native American alone (32,151 people in 2010 census and 50,618 in 2020) or Native American in combination with one or more other races (51,873 people in 2010 census and 163,423 in 2020). Many Georgians also reported belonging to various Native American tribes in 2010 census, the largest of which was the Cherokee (21,525 people). Other tribes reported in Georgia in 2010 included for example the Muscogee (2,370 people), the Choctaw (1,419), the Sioux (1,027), the Seminole (664) and more.

## Economy

See also: [Georgia (U.S. state) locations by per capita income](/wiki/Georgia_(U.S._state)_locations_by_per_capita_income)

According to the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, in 2025, Georgia's total [gross state product](/wiki/Gross_state_product) was $924.8 billion and its per capita personal income was $65,382. For years Georgia as a state has had the highest [credit rating](/wiki/Credit_rating) by [Standard & Poor's](/wiki/Standard_%26_Poor%27s) (AAA) and is one of only 15 states with a AAA rating. If Georgia were a stand-alone country, it would be the 28th-largest economy in the world, based on data from 2005. As of 2025, Georgia has been noted by *Area Development Magazine* as the Top State for Doing Business for 12 consecutive years. As of 2026, Georgia was a top ten exporting state out of all U.S. states, doubling the national average for export growth, and surpassing its export record five years in a row. In 2025, 99.7% of businesses in Georgia were [small businesses](/wiki/Small_business), and employed 42.5% of the state's work force.

There are many [Fortune 500](/wiki/Fortune_500) companies with headquarters in Georgia, including [Home Depot](/wiki/Home_Depot), [UPS](/wiki/United_Parcel_Service), [Coca-Cola](/wiki/The_Coca-Cola_Company), [TSYS](/wiki/TSYS), [Delta Air Lines](/wiki/Delta_Air_Lines), [Aflac](/wiki/Aflac), [Southern Company](/wiki/Southern_Company), and [Elevance Health](/wiki/Elevance_Health). Atlanta boasts the world's [busiest airport](/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports), as measured both by passenger traffic and by aircraft traffic. In addition, the [Port of Savannah](/wiki/Port_of_Savannah) is the fourth-largest [seaport](/wiki/Seaport) and fastest-growing container seaport in North America, importing and exporting a total of 2.3 million [TEUs](/wiki/Twenty-foot_equivalent_unit) per year.

Atlanta has a significant effect on the state of Georgia, the Southeastern United States, and beyond. It has been the site of growth in finance, insurance, technology, manufacturing, real estate, [service](/wiki/Tertiary_sector_of_the_economy), logistics, transportation, film, communications, [convention and trade show](/wiki/Convention_center) businesses and industries, while tourism is important to the economy. [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta) is a [global city](/wiki/Global_city), also called *world city* or sometimes *alpha city* or *world center*, as a city generally considered to be an important node in the global economic system.

For the five years through November 2017, Georgia has been ranked the top state (number 1) in the nation to do business, and has been recognized as number 1 for business and labor climate in the nation, number 1 in business climate in the nation, number 1 in the nation in workforce training and as having a "Best in Class" state economic development agency.

In 2016, Georgia had a median annual income per person of between $50,000 and $59,999, which is in inflation-adjusted dollars for 2016. The U.S. median annual income for the entire nation is $57,617. This lies within the range of Georgia's median annual income.

A 2024 study listed Georgia in the top 20 of states for an affordable [cost of living](/wiki/Cost_of_living). However, studies for 2025 have shown Georgia is the leading state for personal loan searches, with nearly twice the volume of most other states. As of May 2025, the [unemployment rate](/wiki/Unemployment_rate) was 3.5 percent.

### Manufacturing

While many textile jobs moved overseas, there is still a textile industry located around the cities of [Rome](/wiki/Rome,_Georgia), [Columbus](/wiki/Columbus,_Georgia), [Augusta](/wiki/Augusta,_Georgia), [Macon](/wiki/Macon,_Georgia) and along the I-75 corridor between Atlanta and [Chattanooga, Tennessee](/wiki/Chattanooga,_Tennessee). Historically it started along the fall line in the Piedmont, where factories were powered by waterfalls and rivers. It includes the towns of [Cartersville](/wiki/Cartersville,_Georgia), [Calhoun](/wiki/Calhoun,_Georgia), [Ringgold](/wiki/Ringgold,_Georgia) and [Dalton](/wiki/Dalton,_Georgia).

In November 2009, the South Korean automaker [Kia Corporation](/wiki/Kia_Corporation) began production in Georgia. The first Kia plant built in the U.S., [Kia Motors Manufacturing Georgia](/wiki/Kia_Design_and_Manufacturing_Facilities#Kia_Motors_Manufacturing_Georgia_(KMMG)), is located in [West Point](/wiki/West_Point,_Georgia). [Rivian](/wiki/Rivian), an [electric vehicle](/wiki/Electric_vehicle) manufacturer, plans to begin production at a facility in [Social Circle](/wiki/Social_Circle,_Georgia) in 2024.

Industrial products include textiles and [apparel](/wiki/Apparel), transportation equipment, food processing, paper products, [chemicals](/wiki/Chemical) and products, and electric equipment.

### Agriculture

A cotton field in southern Georgia

Widespread farms produce peanuts, corn, and [soybeans](/wiki/Soybean) across middle and south Georgia. The state is the number one producer of [pecans](/wiki/Pecans) in the world, thanks to [Naomi Chapman Woodroof](/wiki/Naomi_Chapman_Woodroof) regarding peanut breeding, with the region around [Albany](/wiki/Albany,_Georgia) in [southwest Georgia](/wiki/Southwest_Georgia) being the center of Georgia's pecan production. Georgia produces the most chickens for [poultry](/wiki/Poultry) of any state; [Gainesville](/wiki/Gainesville,_Georgia), in northeast Georgia, touts itself as the "Poultry Capital of the World". Georgia is in the top five [blueberry](/wiki/Blueberry) producers in the United States.

### Energy

See also: [List of gold mines in Georgia](/wiki/List_of_gold_mines_in_Georgia), [Georgia Gold Belt](/wiki/Georgia_Gold_Belt), and [Georgia Gold Rush](/wiki/Georgia_Gold_Rush)

Georgia electricity production by type

Georgia's electricity generation and consumption are among the highest in the United States, with natural gas being the primary electrical generation fuel, followed by coal. The state also has two nuclear power facilities, [Plant Hatch](/wiki/Plant_Hatch) and [Plant Vogtle](/wiki/Plant_Vogtle), which contribute almost one fourth of Georgia's electricity generation, and two additional nuclear reactors are being built at Vogtle as of 2022. In 2013, the generation mix was 39% gas, 35% coal, 23% nuclear, 3% hydro and other renewable sources. The leading area of energy consumption is the industrial sector because Georgia "is a leader in the energy-intensive wood and paper products industry". Solar generated energy is becoming more in use with solar energy generators currently installed ranking Georgia 15th in the country in installed solar capacity. In 2013, $189 million was invested in Georgia to install solar for home, business and utility use representing a 795% increase over the previous year.

Major products in the mineral industry include a variety of clays, stones, sands and the clay [palygorskite](/wiki/Palygorskite), known as attapulgite.

### Logistics

The [Port of Savannah](/wiki/Port_of_Savannah) and [intermodal terminal](/wiki/Container_port)

In 2017, Georgia ranked second among all states for infrastructure and global access by *Area Development* magazine.

The [Georgia Ports Authority](/wiki/Georgia_Ports_Authority) owns and operates four ports in the state: [Port of Savannah](/wiki/Port_of_Savannah), [Port of Brunswick](/wiki/Port_of_Brunswick), [Port Bainbridge](/wiki/Port_Bainbridge), and Port Columbus. The [Port of Savannah](/wiki/Port_of_Savannah) is the third-busiest [seaport](/wiki/Seaport) in the United States, importing and exporting a total of 4.9 million [TEUs](/wiki/Twenty-foot_equivalent_unit) for 2023. The Port of Savannah's Garden City Terminal is the largest single container terminal in North America. Several major companies including [Target](/wiki/Target_Corporation), [IKEA](/wiki/IKEA), and [Heineken](/wiki/Heineken) operate distribution centers in close proximity to the Port of Savannah.

Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport moves over 650,000 tons of cargo annually through three cargo complexes (2 million square feet or 200,000 square meters of floor space). It has nearby cold storage for perishables; it is the only airport in the Southeast with USDA-approved cold-treatment capabilities. Delta Air Lines also offers an on-airport refrigeration facility for perishable cargo, and a 250-acre Foreign Trade Zone is located at the airport.

Georgia is a major railway hub, has the most extensive rail system in the Southeast, and has the service of two Class I railroads, CSX and Norfolk Southern, plus 24 short-line railroads. Georgia is ranked the No. 3 state in the nation for rail accessibility. Rail shipments include intermodal, bulk, automotive and every other type of shipment.

Georgia has an extensive interstate highway system including 1,200 miles (1,900 kilometers) of interstate highway and 20,000 miles (32,000 kilometers) of federal and state highways that facilitate the efficient movement of more than $620 billion of cargo by truck each year. Georgia's six interstates connect to 80 percent of the U.S. population within a two-day truck drive. More than $14 billion in funding has been approved for new roadway infrastructure.

Savannah's [River Street](/wiki/River_Street_(Savannah,_Georgia)) is a popular tourist destination.

Several sites honor the lives and careers of noted American leaders: the [Little White House](/wiki/Little_White_House) in [Warm Springs](/wiki/Warm_Springs,_Georgia), which served as the summer residence of President [Franklin Delano Roosevelt](/wiki/Franklin_Delano_Roosevelt) while he was being treated for [polio](/wiki/Polio); President [Jimmy Carter](/wiki/Jimmy_Carter)'s hometown of [Plains](/wiki/Plains,_Georgia) and the [Carter Presidential Center](/wiki/Jimmy_Carter_Library_and_Museum) in Atlanta; the [Martin Luther King Jr. National Historical Park](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr._National_Historical_Park) in Atlanta, which is the final resting place of [Martin Luther King Jr.](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.) and [Coretta Scott King](/wiki/Coretta_Scott_King); and Atlanta's [Ebenezer Baptist Church](/wiki/Ebenezer_Baptist_Church_(Atlanta,_Georgia)), where King preached.

### Taxes

Georgia has a [progressive income tax](/wiki/Progressive_income_tax) structure with six brackets of state [income tax](/wiki/Income_tax) rates that range from 1% to 6%. In 2009, Georgians paid 9% of their income in state and local taxes, compared to the U.S. average of 9.8% of income. This ranks Georgia 25th among the states for total state and local tax burden. The state [sales tax](/wiki/Sales_tax) in Georgia is 4% with additional percentages added through local options (e.g. [special-purpose local-option sales tax](/wiki/Special-purpose_local-option_sales_tax) or SPLOST), but there is no sales tax on [prescription drugs](/wiki/Prescription_drug), certain medical devices, or food items for home consumption.

The [state legislature](/wiki/Georgia_Legislature) may allow municipalities to institute local sales taxes and special local taxes, such as the 2% SPLOST tax and the 1% sales tax for [MARTA](/wiki/Metropolitan_Atlanta_Rapid_Transit_Authority) serviced counties. [Excise taxes](/wiki/Excise) are levied on alcohol, tobacco, and [motor fuel](/wiki/Motor_fuel). Owners of [real property](/wiki/Real_property) in Georgia pay [property tax](/wiki/Property_tax) to their county. All taxes are collected by the [Georgia Department of Revenue](/wiki/Georgia_Department_of_Revenue) and then properly distributed according to any agreements that each county has with its cities.

## Education

Main article: [Education in Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Education_in_Georgia_(U.S._state))

[Georgia Tech](/wiki/Georgia_Institute_of_Technology)'s [Tech Tower](/wiki/Tech_Tower)

Georgia county and city public school systems are administered by school boards with members elected at the local level. As of 2013, all but 19 of 181 boards are elected from [single-member districts](/wiki/Single-member_districts). Residents and activist groups in [Fayette County](/wiki/Fayette_County,_Georgia) sued the board of commissioners and school board for maintaining an election system based on [at-large](/wiki/At-large) voting, which tended to increase the power of the majority and effectively prevented minority participation on elected local boards for nearly 200 years. A change to single-member districts has resulted in the African-American minority being able to elect representatives of its choice.

Georgia high schools (grades nine through twelve) are required to administer a [standardized](/wiki/Standardized_test), [multiple choice](/wiki/Multiple_choice) [End of Course Test](/wiki/End_of_Course_Test), or EOCT, in each of eight core subjects: [algebra](/wiki/Algebra), [geometry](/wiki/Geometry), [U.S. history](/wiki/U.S._history), economics, [biology](/wiki/Biology), [physical science](/wiki/Physical_science), ninth-grade literature and [composition](/wiki/Composition_(language)), and [American literature](/wiki/American_literature). The official purpose of the tests is to assess "specific content knowledge and skills". Although a minimum test score is not required for the student to receive [credit](/wiki/Carnegie_Unit_and_Student_Hour) in the course, completion of the test is mandatory. The EOCT score accounts for 15% of a student's grade in the course. The *Georgia Milestone* evaluation is taken by public school students in the state. In 2020, because of the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic, the Georgia State BOE agreed to state superintendent Richard Woods' proposal to change the weight of the EOCT test to only count for 0.01% of the Student's course grade. This change is currently only in effect for the 2020–21 school year.

The Arch at the entrance to the [University of Georgia](/wiki/University_of_Georgia)'s North Campus

Georgia has 85 public colleges, universities, and technical colleges in addition to more than 45 private institutes of higher learning. Among Georgia's public universities is the [flagship](/wiki/Flagship_university) [research university](/wiki/Research_university), the [University of Georgia](/wiki/University_of_Georgia), founded in 1785 as the country's oldest state-chartered university and the birthplace of the American system of public higher education. The [University System of Georgia](/wiki/University_System_of_Georgia) is the presiding body over public post-secondary education in the state. The system includes 26 institutions of higher learning and is governed by the [Georgia Board of Regents](/wiki/Georgia_Board_of_Regents). Georgia's workforce of more than 6.3 million is constantly refreshed by the growing number of people who move there along with the 90,000 graduates from the universities, colleges and technical colleges across the state, including the highly ranked [University of Georgia](/wiki/University_of_Georgia), [Georgia Institute of Technology](/wiki/Georgia_Institute_of_Technology), [Georgia State University](/wiki/Georgia_State_University), [Morehouse College](/wiki/Morehouse_College), [Spelman College](/wiki/Spelman_College), [Agnes Scott College](/wiki/Agnes_Scott_College), and [Emory University](/wiki/Emory_University).

The [HOPE Scholarship](/wiki/HOPE_Scholarship), funded by the [state lottery](/wiki/Georgia_Lottery), is available to all Georgia residents who have graduated from high school or earned a [General Educational Development](/wiki/General_Educational_Development) certificate. The student must maintain a 3.0 or higher grade point average and attend a public college or university in the state.

The [Georgia Historical Society](/wiki/Georgia_Historical_Society), an independent educational and research institution, has a research center located in [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia). The research center's library and archives hold the oldest collection of materials related to Georgia history in the nation.

As with all other U.S. states and the federal government, Georgia's government is based on the [separation of legislative, executive, and judicial power](/wiki/Separation_of_powers). Executive authority in the state rests with the [governor](/wiki/Governor_(United_States)), currently [Brian Kemp](/wiki/Brian_Kemp) ([Republican](/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States))). Both the [Governor of Georgia](/wiki/List_of_Governors_of_Georgia) and [lieutenant governor](/wiki/Lieutenant_Governor_of_Georgia) are elected on separate ballots to four-year terms of office. Unlike the federal government, but like many other U.S. States, most of the executive officials who comprise the governor's cabinet are elected by the citizens of Georgia rather than appointed by the governor.

Legislative authority resides in the [General Assembly](/wiki/Georgia_General_Assembly), composed of the [Senate](/wiki/Georgia_State_Senate) and [House of Representatives](/wiki/Georgia_House_of_Representatives). The lieutenant governor [presides over the Senate](/wiki/President_of_the_Senate), while members of the House of Representatives select their own Speaker. The [Georgia Constitution](/wiki/Georgia_(U.S._state)_Constitution) mandates a maximum of 56 senators, elected from single-member districts, and a minimum of 180 representatives, apportioned among representative districts (which sometimes results in more than one representative per district); there are currently 56 senators and 180 representatives. The term of office for senators and representatives is two years. The laws enacted by the General Assembly are codified in the [Official Code of Georgia Annotated](/wiki/Official_Code_of_Georgia_Annotated).

State judicial authority rests with the state [Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Georgia_(U.S._state)) and [Court of Appeals](/wiki/Georgia_Court_of_Appeals), which have statewide authority. In addition, there are smaller courts which have more limited geographical jurisdiction, including Superior Courts, State Courts, Juvenile Courts, Magistrate Courts and Probate Courts. Justices of the Supreme Court and judges of the Court of Appeals are elected statewide by the citizens in non-partisan elections to six-year terms. Judges for the smaller courts are elected to four-year terms by the state's citizens who live within that court's jurisdiction.

### Local government

Further information: [List of counties in Georgia](/wiki/List_of_counties_in_Georgia) and [List of municipalities in Georgia](/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_Georgia_(U.S._state))

Georgia consists of 159 [counties](/wiki/County_(United_States)), second only to Texas, with 254. Georgia had 161 counties until the end of 1931, when [Milton](/wiki/Milton_County,_Georgia) and [Campbell](/wiki/Campbell_County,_Georgia) were merged into the existing [Fulton](/wiki/Fulton_County,_Georgia). Some counties have been named for prominent figures in both American and Georgian history, and many bear names with Native American origin. Counties in Georgia have their own elected legislative branch, usually called the Board of Commissioners, which usually also has executive authority in the county. Several counties have a [sole commissioner](/wiki/Sole_commissioner) form of government, with legislative and executive authority vested in a single person. Georgia is the only state with current sole-commissioner counties. Georgia's Constitution provides all counties and cities with "[home rule](/wiki/Home_rule_in_the_United_States)" authority. The county commissions have considerable power to pass legislation within their county, as a municipality would.

Georgia recognizes all local units of government as cities, so every incorporated town is legally a city. Georgia does not provide for [townships](/wiki/Township_(United_States)) or [independent cities](/wiki/Independent_city_(United_States)), though there have been bills proposed in the Legislature to provide for townships; it does allow [consolidated city-county](/wiki/Consolidated_city-county) governments by local [referendum](/wiki/Referendum). All of Georgia's second-tier cities except [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia) have now formed consolidated city-county governments by referendum: [Columbus](/wiki/Columbus,_Georgia) (in 1970), [Athens](/wiki/Athens,_Georgia) (1990), [Augusta](/wiki/Augusta,_Georgia) (1995), and [Macon](/wiki/Macon,_Georgia) (2012). (Augusta and Athens have excluded one or more small, incorporated towns within their consolidated boundaries; Columbus and Macon eventually absorbed all smaller incorporated entities within their consolidated boundaries.) The small town of [Cusseta](/wiki/Cusseta,_Georgia) adopted a consolidated city-county government after it merged with unincorporated [Chattahoochee County](/wiki/Chattahoochee_County,_Georgia) in 2003. Three years later, in 2006, the town of [Georgetown](/wiki/Georgetown,_Quitman_County,_Georgia) consolidated with the rest of [Quitman County](/wiki/Quitman_County,_Georgia).

There is no true [metropolitan government](/wiki/Metropolitan_government) in Georgia, though the [Atlanta Regional Commission](/wiki/Atlanta_Regional_Commission) (ARC) and [Georgia Regional Transportation Authority](/wiki/Georgia_Regional_Transportation_Authority) do provide some services, and the ARC must review all major [land development](/wiki/Land_development) projects in the [Atlanta metropolitan area](/wiki/Atlanta_metropolitan_area).

### Elections

Main article: [Elections in Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Elections_in_Georgia_(U.S._state))

See also: [Political party strength in Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Political_party_strength_in_Georgia_(U.S._state))

Georgia voted [Republican](/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States)) in six consecutive presidential elections from [1996](/wiki/1996_United_States_presidential_election_in_Georgia) to [2016](/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election_in_Georgia), a streak that was broken when the state went for [Democratic](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)) candidate [Joe Biden](/wiki/Joe_Biden_2020_presidential_campaign) in [2020](/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election_in_Georgia).

Until 1964, Georgia's state government had the longest unbroken record of single-party dominance, by the [Democratic Party](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)), of any state in the Union. This record was established largely due to the [disenfranchisement of most blacks](/wiki/Disfranchisement_after_the_Reconstruction_era) and many poor whites by the state in its constitution and laws in the early 20th century. Some elements, such as requiring payment of poll taxes and passing literacy tests, prevented blacks from registering to vote; their exclusion from the political system lasted into the 1960s and reduced the Republican Party to a non-competitive status in the early 20th century.

White Democrats regained power after Reconstruction due in part to the efforts of some using intimidation and violence, but this method came into disrepute. In 1900, shortly before Georgia adopted a disfranchising constitutional amendment in 1908, blacks comprised 47% of the state's population.

The whites dealt with this problem of potential political power by the 1908 amendment, which in practice disenfranchised blacks and poor whites, nearly half of the state population. It required that any male at least 21 years of age wanting to register to vote must also be of good character and able to pass a test on citizenship, be able to read and write provisions of the U.S. and Georgia constitutions, or own at least forty acres of land or $500 in property. Any Georgian who had fought in any war from the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution) through the [Spanish–American War](/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War) was exempted from these additional qualifications. More importantly, any Georgian descended from a veteran of any of these wars also was exempted. Because, by 1908, many white Georgia males were grandsons of veterans or owned the required property, the exemption and the property requirement basically allowed only well-to-do whites to vote. The qualifications of good character, citizenship knowledge, literacy (all determined subjectively by white registrars), and property ownership were used to disqualify most blacks and poor whites, preventing them from registering to vote. The voter rolls dropped dramatically. In the early 20th century, [Progressives](/wiki/Progressivism) promoted electoral reform and reducing the power of ward bosses to clean up politics. Their additional rules, such as the [eight box law](/wiki/Eight_box_law), continued to effectively close out people who were illiterate. White one-party rule was solidified.

For more than 130 years, from 1872 to 2003, Georgians nominated and elected only white Democratic governors, and white Democrats held the majority of seats in the General Assembly. Most of the Democrats elected throughout these years were [Southern Democrats](/wiki/Southern_Democrats), who were fiscally and socially conservative by national standards. This voting pattern continued after the segregationist period.

Legal segregation was ended by passage of federal legislation in the 1960s. According to the 1960 census, the proportion of Georgia's population that was African American was 28%; hundreds of thousands of blacks had left the state in the [Great Migration](/wiki/Great_Migration_(African_American)) to the North and Midwest. New white residents arrived through migration and immigration. Following support from the national Democratic Party for the civil rights movement and especially civil rights legislation of 1964 and 1965, most African-American voters, as well as other minority voters, have largely supported the Democratic Party in Georgia.

In 2002, incumbent moderate Democratic governor [Roy Barnes](/wiki/Roy_Barnes) was defeated by Republican [Sonny Perdue](/wiki/Sonny_Perdue), a state legislator and former Democrat. While Democrats retained control of the State House, they lost their majority in the Senate when four Democrats switched parties. They lost the House in the 2004 election. Republicans then controlled all three partisan elements of the state government.

Even before 2002, the state had become increasingly supportive of Republicans in presidential elections. It has supported a Democrat for president only four times since 1960. In 1976 and 1980, native son [Jimmy Carter](/wiki/Jimmy_Carter) carried the state; in 1992, the former Arkansas governor [Bill Clinton](/wiki/Bill_Clinton) narrowly won the state; and in 2020, [Joe Biden](/wiki/Joe_Biden) narrowly carried the state. Generally, Republicans were strongest in the predominantly white suburban (especially the Atlanta suburbs) and rural portions of the state. Many of these areas were represented by conservative Democrats in the state legislature well into the 21st century. One of the most conservative of these was U.S. congressman [Larry McDonald](/wiki/Larry_McDonald), former head of the [John Birch Society](/wiki/John_Birch_Society), who died when the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) shot down [KAL 007](/wiki/KAL_007) near [Sakhalin Island](/wiki/Sakhalin_Island). Democratic candidates have tended to win a higher percentage of the vote in the areas where black voters are most numerous, as well as in the cities among liberal urban populations (especially Atlanta and Athens), and the central and southwestern portion of the state.

The ascendancy of the Republican Party in Georgia and in the South in general resulted in Georgia [U.S. House of Representatives](/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) member [Newt Gingrich](/wiki/Newt_Gingrich) being elected as [Speaker of the House](/wiki/Speaker_of_the_United_States_House_of_Representatives) following the election of a Republican majority in the House in 1994. Gingrich served as Speaker until 1999, when he resigned in the aftermath of the loss of House seats held by members of the GOP. Gingrich mounted an unsuccessful bid for president in the 2012 election, but withdrew after winning only the South Carolina and Georgia primaries.

In 2008, Democrat [Jim Martin](/wiki/Jim_Martin_(Georgia_politician)) ran against incumbent Republican senator [Saxby Chambliss](/wiki/Saxby_Chambliss). Chambliss failed to acquire the necessary 50 percent of votes due to a Libertarian Party candidate receiving the remainder of votes. In the [runoff election](/wiki/Runoff_election) held on December 2, 2008, Chambliss became the second Georgia Republican to be reelected to the U.S. Senate.

In the 2018 elections, the [governorship](/wiki/2018_Georgia_gubernatorial_election) remained under control by a Republican (by 54,723 votes against a Democrat, [Stacey Abrams](/wiki/Stacey_Abrams)), Republicans lost eight seats in the [Georgia House of Representatives](/wiki/Georgia_House_of_Representatives) (winning 106), while Democrats gained ten (winning 74), Republicans lost two seats in the [Georgia Senate](/wiki/Georgia_State_Senate) (winning 35 seats), while Democrats gained two seats (winning 21), and five Democrat [U.S. representatives](/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) were elected with Republicans winning nine seats (one winning with just 419 votes over the Democratic challenger, and one seat being lost).

In the three presidential elections up to and including 2016, the Republican candidate has won Georgia by approximately five to eight points over the Democratic nominee, at least once for each election being narrower than margins recorded in some states that have flipped within that timeframe, such as [Michigan](/wiki/United_States_presidential_elections_in_Michigan), [Ohio](/wiki/United_States_presidential_elections_in_Ohio) and [Wisconsin](/wiki/United_States_presidential_elections_in_Wisconsin). This trend led to the state narrowly electing Democrat [Joe Biden](/wiki/Joe_Biden) for president in 2020, and it coming to be regarded as a [swing state](/wiki/Swing_state).

In a 2020 study, Georgia was ranked as 49th on the "Cost of Voting Index" with only Texas ranking higher. In 2022, Georgia swung substantially back to the right towards Republicans with incumbent Republican governor [Brian Kemp](/wiki/Brian_Kemp) winning reelection by 7.5% over Democrat [Stacey Abrams](/wiki/Stacey_Abrams) with a raw vote margin of over 300,000 votes in the [2022 Georgia gubernatorial election](/wiki/2022_Georgia_gubernatorial_election); the largest amount since the early 2000s, and every other Republican statewide getting elected by a 5–10% margin of victory.

### Politics

Main article: [Politics of Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Politics_of_Georgia_(U.S._state))

During the 1960s and 1970s, Georgia made significant changes in civil rights and governance. As in many other states, its legislature had not reapportioned congressional districts according to population from 1931 to after the 1960 census. Problems of malapportionment in the state legislature, where rural districts had outsize power in relation to urban districts, such as Atlanta's, were corrected after the U.S. Supreme Court ruling in *[Wesberry v. Sanders](/wiki/Wesberry_v._Sanders)* (1964). The court ruled that congressional districts had to be reapportioned to have essentially equal populations.

A related case, *[Reynolds v. Sims](/wiki/Reynolds_v._Sims)* (1964), required state legislatures to end their use of geographical districts or counties in favor of "one man, one vote"; that is, districts based upon approximately equal populations, to be reviewed and changed as necessary after each census. These changes resulted in residents of Atlanta and other urban areas gaining political power in Georgia in proportion to their populations. From the mid-1960s, the voting electorate increased after African Americans' rights to vote were enforced under civil rights law.

Economic growth through this period was dominated by Atlanta and its region. It was a bedrock of the emerging "[New South](/wiki/New_South)". From the late 20th century, Atlanta attracted headquarters and relocated workers of national companies, becoming more diverse, liberal and cosmopolitan than many areas of the state.

In the 21st century, many conservative Democrats, including former U.S. senator and governor [Zell Miller](/wiki/Zell_Miller), decided to support Republicans. The state's then-socially conservative bent resulted in wide support for measures such as restrictions on abortion. In 2004, a state constitutional amendment banning [same-sex marriages](/wiki/Same-sex_marriage) was approved by 76% of voters. However, after the United States Supreme Court issued its ruling in *[Obergefell v. Hodges](/wiki/Obergefell_v._Hodges)*, all Georgia counties came into full compliance, recognizing the rights of same-sex couples to marry in the state.

In [presidential elections](/wiki/United_States_presidential_election), Georgia voted solely Democratic in every election from [1900](/wiki/1900_United_States_presidential_election) to [1960](/wiki/1960_United_States_presidential_election). In [1964](/wiki/1964_United_States_presidential_election), it was one of only a handful of states to vote for Republican [Barry Goldwater](/wiki/Barry_Goldwater) over Democrat [Lyndon B. Johnson](/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson). In [1968](/wiki/1968_United_States_presidential_election), it did not vote for either of the two parties, but rather the [American Independent Party](/wiki/American_Independent_Party) and its nominee, [Alabama](/wiki/Alabama) Governor [George Wallace](/wiki/George_Wallace). In [1972](/wiki/1972_United_States_presidential_election), the state returned to Republicans as part of a landslide victory for [Richard Nixon](/wiki/Richard_Nixon). In [1976](/wiki/1976_United_States_presidential_election) and [1980](/wiki/1980_United_States_presidential_election), it voted for Democrat and former Georgia governor [Jimmy Carter](/wiki/Jimmy_Carter). The state returned to Republicans in [1984](/wiki/1984_United_States_presidential_election) and [1988](/wiki/1988_United_States_presidential_election), before going Democratic once again in [1992](/wiki/1992_United_States_presidential_election). For every election between that year and [2020](/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election), Georgia voted heavily Republican, in line with many of its neighbors in the [Deep South](/wiki/Deep_South). In [2020](/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election), it voted Democratic for the first time in 28 years, carried by [Joe Biden](/wiki/Joe_Biden) by 11,779 votes in his national defeat of incumbent Republican [Donald Trump](/wiki/Donald_Trump).

Though Republicans had continued to regularly win state and federal elections, in the years prior to 2020, their margins of victory tended to decrease, and that year, many election forecasts ranked Georgia as a [swing state](/wiki/Swing_state). Concurrent with the 2020 presidential election were elections for both of Georgia's United States Senate seats; when no candidate in either race received a majority of the vote, both went to run-offs, which Democrats [Jon Ossoff](/wiki/Jon_Ossoff) and [Raphael Warnock](/wiki/Raphael_Warnock) won. Ossoff is the state's first Jewish senator, and Warnock is the state's first Black senator. The Democratic wins were attributed to the rapid [diversification](/wiki/Historical_racial_and_ethnic_demographics_of_the_United_States) of the suburbs of Atlanta and increased turnout of younger African-American voters, particularly around the suburbs of Atlanta and in [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia).

However, Republicans rebounded as Governor [Brian Kemp](/wiki/Brian_Kemp) won [re-election in 2022](/wiki/2022_Georgia_gubernatorial_election) by a comfortable margin, and Donald Trump carried the state by 115,000 votes as part of his victory in the [2024 presidential election](/wiki/2024_United_States_presidential_election).

## Parks and recreational activities

Main article: [Protected areas of Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Protected_areas_of_Georgia_(U.S._state))

There are 48 [state parks](/wiki/List_of_Georgia_state_parks), 15 historic sites, and numerous wildlife preserves under supervision of the [Georgia Department of Natural Resources](/wiki/Georgia_Department_of_Natural_Resources). Other historic sites and parks are supervised by the [National Park Service](/wiki/National_Park_Service) and include the [Andersonville National Historic Site](/wiki/Andersonville_National_Historic_Site) in [Andersonville](/wiki/Andersonville,_Georgia); [Appalachian National Scenic Trail](/wiki/Appalachian_National_Scenic_Trail); [Chattahoochee River National Recreation Area](/wiki/Chattahoochee_River_National_Recreation_Area) near Atlanta; [Chickamauga and Chattanooga National Military Park](/wiki/Chickamauga_and_Chattanooga_National_Military_Park) at [Fort Oglethorpe](/wiki/Fort_Oglethorpe,_Georgia); [Cumberland Island National Seashore](/wiki/Cumberland_Island_National_Seashore) near [St. Marys](/wiki/St._Marys,_Georgia); [Fort Frederica National Monument](/wiki/Fort_Frederica_National_Monument) on [St. Simons Island](/wiki/St._Simons,_Georgia); [Fort Pulaski National Monument](/wiki/Fort_Pulaski_National_Monument) in [Savannah](/wiki/Savannah,_Georgia); [Jimmy Carter National Historic Site](/wiki/Jimmy_Carter_National_Historic_Site) near [Plains](/wiki/Plains,_Georgia); [Kennesaw Mountain National Battlefield Park](/wiki/Kennesaw_Mountain_National_Battlefield_Park) near [Kennesaw](/wiki/Kennesaw,_Georgia); [Martin Luther King Jr. National Historical Park](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr._National_Historical_Park) in Atlanta; [Ocmulgee National Monument](/wiki/Ocmulgee_National_Monument) at [Macon](/wiki/Macon,_Georgia); [Trail of Tears National Historic Trail](/wiki/Trail_of_Tears_National_Historic_Trail); and the [Okefenokee Swamp](/wiki/Okefenokee_Swamp) in [Waycross, Georgia](/wiki/Waycross,_Georgia).

Outdoor recreational activities include hiking along the [Appalachian Trail](/wiki/Appalachian_Trail); Civil War Heritage Trails; [rock climbing](/wiki/Rock_climbing) and [whitewater kayaking](/wiki/Whitewater_kayaking). Other outdoor activities include hunting and fishing.

## Infrastructure

### Transportation

Main article: [Transportation in Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/Transportation_in_Georgia_(U.S._state))

The [Port of Brunswick](/wiki/Port_of_Brunswick) and the Sidney Lanier Bridge

Transportation in Georgia is overseen by the [Georgia Department of Transportation](/wiki/Georgia_Department_of_Transportation), a part of the executive branch of the [state government](/wiki/Government_of_Georgia_(U.S._state)). Georgia's major [Interstate Highways](/wiki/Interstate_Highway) are [I-20](/wiki/Interstate_20_in_Georgia), [I-75](/wiki/Interstate_75_in_Georgia), [I-85](/wiki/Interstate_85_in_Georgia), and [I-95](/wiki/Interstate_95_in_Georgia). On March 18, 1998, the Georgia House of Representatives passed a resolution naming the portion of Interstate 75, which runs from the Chattahoochee River northward to the Tennessee state line the [Larry McDonald](/wiki/Larry_McDonald) Memorial Highway. Larry McDonald, a Democratic member of the House of Representatives, had been on [Korean Air Lines Flight 007](/wiki/Korean_Air_Lines_Flight_007) when it was shot down by the Soviets on September 1, 1983.

[MARTA](/wiki/MARTA) (rapid transit) train

Georgia's primary commercial airport is Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ATL), the world's busiest airport. In addition to Hartsfield–Jackson, there are eight other airports serving major commercial traffic in Georgia. [Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport](/wiki/Savannah/Hilton_Head_International_Airport) is the second-busiest airport in the state as measured by passengers served, and is the only additional international airport. Other commercial airports (ranked in order of passengers served) are located in [Augusta](/wiki/Augusta,_Georgia), [Columbus](/wiki/Columbus,_Georgia), [Albany](/wiki/Albany,_Georgia), [Macon](/wiki/Macon,_Georgia), [Brunswick](/wiki/Brunswick,_Georgia), [Valdosta](/wiki/Valdosta,_Georgia), and [Athens](/wiki/Athens,_Georgia).

The [Georgia Ports Authority](/wiki/Georgia_Ports_Authority) manages two deepwater seaports, at Savannah and Brunswick, and two river ports, at Bainbridge and Columbus. The [Port of Savannah](/wiki/Port_of_Savannah) is a major U.S. seaport on the Atlantic coast.

The Metropolitan Atlanta Rapid Transit Authority ([MARTA](/wiki/Metropolitan_Atlanta_Rapid_Transit_Authority)) is the principal [rapid transit](/wiki/Rapid_transit) system in the Atlanta metropolitan area. Formed in 1971 as strictly a bus system, MARTA operates a network of [bus routes](/wiki/Bus_route) linked to a rapid transit system consisting of 48 miles (77 km) of [rail track](/wiki/Rail_track) with 38 [train stations](/wiki/Subway_station). MARTA operates almost exclusively in [Fulton](/wiki/Fulton_County,_Georgia) and [DeKalb](/wiki/DeKalb_County,_Georgia) counties, with bus service to two destinations in [Cobb](/wiki/Cobb_County,_Georgia) county and the [Cumberland](/wiki/Cumberland_(Atlanta)) Transfer Center next to the [Cumberland Mall](/wiki/Cumberland_Mall_(Georgia)), and a single rail station in [Clayton](/wiki/Clayton_County,_Georgia) County at Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport. MARTA also operates a separate [paratransit](/wiki/Paratransit) service for disabled customers. As of 2009, the average total daily ridership for the system (bus and rail) was 482,500 passengers.

### Healthcare

See also: [List of hospitals in Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/List_of_hospitals_in_Georgia_(U.S._state))

The state has 151 general hospitals, more than 15,000 doctors and almost 6,000 dentists. The state is ranked forty-first in the percentage of residents who engage in regular exercise.

## Notable people

Main article: [List of people from Georgia (U.S. state)](/wiki/List_of_people_from_Georgia_(U.S._state))

[Jimmy Carter](/wiki/Jimmy_Carter), from [Plains, Georgia](/wiki/Plains,_Georgia), was President of the United States from 1977 to 1981. [Martin Luther King Jr.](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.) was born in [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta) in 1929. He was a [civil rights movement](/wiki/Civil_rights_movement) leader who protested for equal rights and against racial discrimination. He won the [Nobel Peace Prize](/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize) in 1964. [Blake R. Van Leer](/wiki/Blake_R._Van_Leer) played an important role in the civil rights movement, Georgia's economy and was president of [Georgia Tech](/wiki/Georgia_Tech). [Mordecai Sheftall](/wiki/Mordecai_Sheftall), the highest ranking Jewish officer in the American Revolution, was born and lived his life in Georgia. [Naomi Chapman Woodruff](/wiki/Naomi_Chapman_Woodruff), originally from Idaho, was responsible for developing a peanut breeding program in Georgia which led to a harvest of nearly five times the typical amount.
