**Arch Colson Chipp Whitehead** (born November 6, 1969) is an American [novelist](/wiki/Novelist). He is the author of nine novels, including his [1999](/wiki/1999_in_literature) debut *[The Intuitionist](/wiki/The_Intuitionist)*; *[The Underground Railroad](/wiki/The_Underground_Railroad_(novel))* (2016), for which he won the 2016 [National Book Award for Fiction](/wiki/National_Book_Award_for_Fiction) and the 2017 [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction); and *[The Nickel Boys](/wiki/The_Nickel_Boys)*, for which he won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction again in 2020, making him one of only four writers ever to win the prize twice. He has also published two books of nonfiction. In 2002, he received a [MacArthur Fellowship](/wiki/MacArthur_Fellows_Program).

## Early life

Whitehead was born in [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City) on November 6, 1969, and grew up in [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan). He is one of four children of successful entrepreneur parents, his father Arch and mother, Mary Anne Whitehead who owned an executive recruiting firm. As a child in Manhattan, Whitehead went by his first name Arch. He later switched to Chipp, before switching to Colson. He attended [Trinity School](/wiki/Trinity_School_(New_York_City)) in Manhattan and graduated from [Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_University) in 1991. In college, he became friends with poet [Kevin Young](/wiki/Kevin_Young_(poet)).

## Career

After graduating from college, Whitehead wrote for *[The Village Voice](/wiki/The_Village_Voice)*. While working at the *Voice*, he began drafting his first novels.

Early in his career, Whitehead lived in [Fort Greene, Brooklyn](/wiki/Fort_Greene,_Brooklyn).

Whitehead has since produced 11 book-length works—nine novels and two nonfiction works, including a meditation on life in Manhattan in the style of [E. B. White](/wiki/E._B._White)'s famous 1949 essay *Here Is New York*. Whitehead's books are *[The Intuitionist](/wiki/The_Intuitionist)* (1999); *[John Henry Days](/wiki/John_Henry_Days)* (2001); *[The Colossus of New York](/wiki/The_Colossus_of_New_York_(book))* (2003); *[Apex Hides the Hurt](/wiki/Apex_Hides_the_Hurt)* (2006); *[Sag Harbor](/wiki/Sag_Harbor_(novel))* (2009); 2011's *[Zone One](/wiki/Zone_One)*, a [*New York Times* bestseller](/wiki/The_New_York_Times_Best_Seller_list); 2016's *The Underground Railroad*, which earned a [National Book Award for Fiction](/wiki/National_Book_Award_for_Fiction); *[The Nickel Boys](/wiki/The_Nickel_Boys)* (2019); *[Harlem Shuffle](/wiki/Harlem_Shuffle_(novel))* (2021); and *[Crook Manifesto](/wiki/Crook_Manifesto)* (2023). *[Esquire](/wiki/Esquire_(magazine))* magazine named *The Intuitionist* the best first novel of the year, and *[GQ](/wiki/GQ)* called it one of the "novels of the millennium". Novelist [John Updike](/wiki/John_Updike), reviewing *The Intuitionist* in *[The New Yorker](/wiki/The_New_Yorker)*, called Whitehead "ambitious", "scintillating", and "strikingly original", adding: "The young African-American writer to watch may well be a thirty-one-year-old Harvard graduate with the vivid name of Colson Whitehead."

*The Intuitionist* was nominated as the Common Novel at [Rochester Institute of Technology](/wiki/Rochester_Institute_of_Technology) (RIT). The Common Novel nomination was part of a longtime tradition at the Institute that included such authors as [Maya Angelou](/wiki/Maya_Angelou), [Andre Dubus III](/wiki/Andre_Dubus_III), [William Joseph Kennedy](/wiki/William_Joseph_Kennedy), and [Anthony Swofford](/wiki/Anthony_Swofford).

Whitehead's nonfiction, essays, and reviews have appeared in numerous publications, including *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*, *[The New Yorker](/wiki/The_New_Yorker)*, *[Granta](/wiki/Granta)*, and *[Harper's](/wiki/Harper%27s_Magazine)*.

Whitehead at the 2011 [Brooklyn Book Festival](/wiki/Brooklyn_Book_Festival)

His nonfiction account of the [2011 World Series of Poker](/wiki/2011_World_Series_of_Poker), *The Noble Hustle: Poker, Beef Jerky & Death*, was published by [Doubleday](/wiki/Doubleday_(publisher)) in 2014.

Whitehead has taught at [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University), [New York University](/wiki/New_York_University), the [University of Houston](/wiki/University_of_Houston), [Columbia University](/wiki/Columbia_University), [Brooklyn College](/wiki/Brooklyn_College), [Hunter College](/wiki/Hunter_College), and [Wesleyan University](/wiki/Wesleyan_University). He has been a writer-in-residence at [Vassar College](/wiki/Vassar_College), the [University of Richmond](/wiki/University_of_Richmond), and the [University of Wyoming](/wiki/University_of_Wyoming).

In 2015, he joined *[The New York Times Magazine](/wiki/The_New_York_Times_Magazine)* to write a column on language.

*[The Underground Railroad](/wiki/The_Underground_Railroad_(novel))* was a selection of [Oprah's Book Club 2.0](/wiki/Oprah%27s_Book_Club_2.0), and was chosen by President [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama) as one of five books on his summer vacation reading list. In 2017, the novel was awarded the Carnegie Medal for Excellence in Fiction at the American Library Association Mid-Winter Conference in [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta), Georgia. Colson was honored with the 2017 [Hurston/Wright Award](/wiki/Hurston/Wright_Legacy_Award) for fiction presented by the Zora Neale Hurston/Richard Wright Foundation. *The Underground Railroad* won the [2017](/wiki/2017_Pulitzer_Prize) [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction). Judges of the prize called the novel "a smart melding of realism and allegory that combines the violence of slavery and the drama of escape in a myth that speaks to contemporary America".

Whitehead's seventh novel, *[The Nickel Boys](/wiki/The_Nickel_Boys)*, was published in 2019. It was inspired by the story of the [Dozier School for Boys](/wiki/Dozier_School_for_Boys) in Florida, where children convicted of minor offenses suffered violent abuse. In conjunction with its publication, Whitehead was featured on the cover *[Time](/wiki/Time_(magazine))* magazine's July 8, 2019, edition, alongside the [strap-line](/wiki/Strap-line) "America's Storyteller". *The Nickel Boys* won the [2020](/wiki/2020_Pulitzer_Prize) [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction). Judges of the prize called the novel "a spare and devastating exploration of abuse at a reform school in Jim Crow-era Florida that is ultimately a powerful tale of human perseverance, dignity and redemption". It was Whitehead's second win, making him the fourth writer to win the prize twice. In 2022, it was announced that Whitehead will executive produce the upcoming [film adaptation of the same name](/wiki/The_Nickel_Boys_(film)).

Whitehead's eighth novel, *[Harlem Shuffle](/wiki/Harlem_Shuffle_(novel))*, was conceived and begun before he wrote *The Nickel Boys*. It is a work of crime fiction set in Harlem during the 1960s. Whitehead spent years writing it, and finished it in "bite-sized chunks" during the months he spent in quarantine in New York City during the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic). *Harlem Shuffle* was published by Doubleday on September 14, 2021. *[Crook Manifesto](/wiki/Crook_Manifesto)*, Whitehead's ninth novel and a follow-up to *Harlem Shuffle*, was published on July 18, 2023. *Cool Machine*, Whitehead's tenth novel and the conclusion to his "Harlem Trilogy," will be published on July 21, 2026.

## Personal life

Whitehead lives in Manhattan and also owns a home in [Sag Harbor](/wiki/Sag_Harbor) on [Long Island](/wiki/Long_Island). His wife, Julie Barer, is a literary agent. They have two children.
