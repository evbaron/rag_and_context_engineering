**Elizabeth II** (Elizabeth Alexandra Mary; 21 April 1926 – 8 September 2022) was [Queen of the United Kingdom](/wiki/Queen_of_the_United_Kingdom) and other [Commonwealth realms](/wiki/Commonwealth_realm) from 6 February 1952 until [her death](/wiki/Death_and_state_funeral_of_Elizabeth_II) in 2022. She was [queen regnant](/wiki/Queen_regnant) of [32 sovereign states](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_headed_by_Elizabeth_II) during her lifetime and was the monarch of 15 realms at her death. Her reign of 70 years and 214 days is the [longest of any British monarch](/wiki/List_of_monarchs_in_Britain_by_length_of_reign), the [second-longest of any sovereign state](/wiki/List_of_longest-reigning_monarchs), and the [longest of any queen regnant in history](/wiki/List_of_female_monarchs).

Elizabeth was born in [Mayfair](/wiki/Mayfair), London, during the reign of her paternal grandfather, [King George V](/wiki/King_George_V). She was the first child of the Duke and Duchess of York (later [King George VI](/wiki/King_George_VI) and [Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother](/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_the_Queen_Mother)). Her father acceded to the throne in 1936 upon [the abdication](/wiki/Abdication_of_Edward_VIII) of his brother [Edward VIII](/wiki/Edward_VIII), making the ten-year-old Princess Elizabeth the [heir presumptive](/wiki/Heir_presumptive). She was educated privately at home and began to undertake public duties during the [Second World War](/wiki/Second_World_War), serving in the [Auxiliary Territorial Service](/wiki/Auxiliary_Territorial_Service). In November 1947, [she married](/wiki/Wedding_of_Princess_Elizabeth_and_Philip_Mountbatten) [Philip Mountbatten](/wiki/Philip_Mountbatten), a former [prince of Greece and Denmark](/wiki/Prince_of_Greece_and_Denmark). Their marriage lasted 73 years until [his death in 2021](/wiki/Death_and_funeral_of_Prince_Philip,_Duke_of_Edinburgh). They had four children: [Charles](/wiki/Charles_III), [Anne](/wiki/Anne,_Princess_Royal), [Andrew](/wiki/Andrew_Mountbatten-Windsor), and [Edward](/wiki/Prince_Edward,_Duke_of_Edinburgh).

When [her father died](/wiki/Death_and_state_funeral_of_George_VI) in February 1952, Elizabeth, then 25 years old, became queen of seven independent Commonwealth countries: the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, [South Africa](/wiki/Union_of_South_Africa), [Pakistan](/wiki/Dominion_of_Pakistan), and [Ceylon](/wiki/Dominion_of_Ceylon), as well as [head of the Commonwealth](/wiki/Head_of_the_Commonwealth). Elizabeth reigned as a [constitutional monarch](/wiki/Constitutional_monarch) through times of significant political change such as [the Troubles](/wiki/The_Troubles) in Northern Ireland, [devolution in the United Kingdom](/wiki/Devolution_in_the_United_Kingdom), the [decolonisation of Africa](/wiki/Decolonisation_of_Africa), and the [United Kingdom's accession to the European Communities](/wiki/United_Kingdom%27s_accession_to_the_European_Communities) as well as its [subsequent withdrawal](/wiki/Brexit). The number of her realms varied over time as territories gained independence and as most realms [became republics](/wiki/Republics_in_the_Commonwealth_of_Nations). As queen, Elizabeth was advised by [more than 170 prime ministers](/wiki/List_of_prime_ministers_of_Elizabeth_II) across her realms. Her many historic visits and meetings included [state visits](/wiki/List_of_state_visits_made_by_Elizabeth_II) [to China](/wiki/State_visit_by_Elizabeth_II_to_China) in 1986, [to Russia](/wiki/State_visit_by_Elizabeth_II_to_Russia) in 1994, and [to the Republic of Ireland](/wiki/State_visit_by_Elizabeth_II_to_the_Republic_of_Ireland) in 2011; she met five popes and fourteen US presidents.

Significant events of her reign included [her coronation](/wiki/Coronation_of_Elizabeth_II) in 1953 and the celebrations of her [Silver](/wiki/Silver_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II), [Golden](/wiki/Golden_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II), [Diamond](/wiki/Diamond_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II), and [Platinum](/wiki/Platinum_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II) [jubilees](/wiki/Jubilee). Although there was occasional [republican](/wiki/Republicanism_in_the_United_Kingdom) sentiment and media criticism of her family – particularly after the breakdowns of her children's marriages, her *[annus horribilis](/wiki/Annus_horribilis#Elizabeth_II)* in 1992, and [the death](/wiki/Death_of_Diana,_Princess_of_Wales) of her former daughter-in-law [Diana](/wiki/Diana,_Princess_of_Wales) in 1997 – support for the monarchy and her popularity in the United Kingdom remained consistently high. Elizabeth died aged 96 at [Balmoral Castle](/wiki/Balmoral_Castle) in Scotland, and was succeeded by her eldest son as Charles III.

## Early life

Elizabeth was born at 2:40 am on 21 April 1926 by [Caesarean section](/wiki/Caesarean_section) at her maternal grandfather's London home, [17 Bruton Street](/wiki/17_Bruton_Street) in [Mayfair](/wiki/Mayfair), the first child of [Prince Albert, Duke of York](/wiki/Prince_Albert,_Duke_of_York) (later King George VI), and his wife, [Elizabeth, Duchess of York](/wiki/Elizabeth,_Duchess_of_York) (later Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother). Her father was the second son of [King George V](/wiki/King_George_V) and [Queen Mary](/wiki/Mary_of_Teck), and her mother was the youngest daughter of Scottish aristocrat [Claude Bowes-Lyon, 14th Earl of Strathmore and Kinghorne](/wiki/Claude_Bowes-Lyon,_14th_Earl_of_Strathmore_and_Kinghorne) and his wife [Cecilia](/wiki/Cecilia_Bowes-Lyon,_Countess_of_Strathmore_and_Kinghorne) ([née](/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names) Cavendish-Bentinck). She was [baptised](/wiki/Baptised) by the [Archbishop of York](/wiki/Archbishop_of_York), [Cosmo Gordon Lang](/wiki/Cosmo_Gordon_Lang) in the private chapel at [Buckingham Palace](/wiki/Buckingham_Palace) on 29 May, and she was named Elizabeth after her mother; Alexandra after [her paternal great-grandmother](/wiki/Alexandra_of_Denmark), who had [died five months earlier](/wiki/Death_and_funeral_of_Alexandra_of_Denmark); and Mary after her paternal grandmother. She was affectionately called "Lilibet" by her close family, based on what she called herself at first. She was cherished by her grandfather George V, whom she affectionately called "Grandpa England", and her regular visits during his serious illness in 1929 were credited in the popular press and by later biographers with raising his spirits and aiding his recovery.

On the [cover](/wiki/1929_Time_magazine_covers) of [*Time*](/wiki/Time_(magazine)), April 1929

Portrait by [Philip de László](/wiki/Philip_de_L%C3%A1szl%C3%B3), 1933

Elizabeth's sole sibling, [Princess Margaret](/wiki/Princess_Margaret), was born in 1930. The two princesses were cared for by their nanny, [Clara Knight](/wiki/Clara_Knight_(nanny)), and educated at home under the supervision of their mother and their [governess](/wiki/Governess), [Marion Crawford](/wiki/Marion_Crawford). Lessons concentrated on history, language, literature, and music. Crawford published a biography of Elizabeth and Margaret's childhood years titled *[The Little Princesses](/wiki/The_Little_Princesses)* in 1950, much to the dismay of the [royal family](/wiki/British_royal_family). The book describes Elizabeth's love of horses and dogs, her orderliness, and her attitude of responsibility. Others echoed such observations: [Winston Churchill](/wiki/Winston_Churchill) described Elizabeth when she was two as "a character. She has an air of authority and reflectiveness astonishing in an infant." Her cousin [Margaret Rhodes](/wiki/Margaret_Rhodes) described her as "a jolly little girl, but fundamentally sensible and well-behaved". Elizabeth's early life was spent primarily at the Yorks' residences at [145 Piccadilly](/wiki/145_Piccadilly) (their [town house](/wiki/Town_house) in London) and [Royal Lodge](/wiki/Royal_Lodge) in Windsor.

## Heir presumptive

During her grandfather's reign, Elizabeth was third in the [line of succession to the British throne](/wiki/Line_of_succession_to_the_British_throne), behind her uncle [Edward, Prince of Wales](/wiki/Edward_VIII), and her father. Although her birth generated public interest, she was not expected to become queen, as Edward was still young and expected to marry and have children of his own, who would precede Elizabeth in the line of succession. When [her grandfather died](/wiki/Death_and_state_funeral_of_George_V) in 1936 and her uncle succeeded as Edward VIII, she became second in line to the throne, after her father. Later that year, [Edward abdicated](/wiki/Abdication_of_Edward_VIII), after his proposed marriage to divorced American socialite [Wallis Simpson](/wiki/Wallis_Simpson) provoked a [constitutional crisis](/wiki/Constitutional_crisis). Consequently, Elizabeth's father became king, taking the [regnal name](/wiki/Regnal_name) George VI. Since Elizabeth had no brothers, she became [heir presumptive](/wiki/Heir_presumptive). If her parents had subsequently had a son, he would have been [heir apparent](/wiki/Heir_apparent) and before her in the line of succession, which was determined by the [male-preference primogeniture](/wiki/Male-preference_primogeniture) in effect at the time.

Elizabeth received private tuition in [constitutional history](/wiki/Constitutional_history) from [Henry Marten](/wiki/Henry_Marten_(educator)), [Vice-Provost](/wiki/List_of_provosts_of_Eton_College) of [Eton College](/wiki/Eton_College), and learned French from a succession of native-speaking governesses. A [Girl Guides](/wiki/Girl_Guides) company, the [1st Buckingham Palace Company](/wiki/1st_Buckingham_Palace_Company), was formed specifically so she could socialise with girls her age. Later, she was enrolled as a [Sea Ranger](/wiki/Ranger_(Girl_Guide)).

In 1939, Elizabeth's parents [toured Canada](/wiki/1939_royal_tour_of_Canada) and the United States. As in 1927, when they had [toured Australia](/wiki/Royal_tours_of_Australia) and [New Zealand](/wiki/Royal_visits_to_New_Zealand#1951–2000), Elizabeth remained in Britain since her father thought she was too young to undertake public tours. She "looked tearful" as her parents departed. They corresponded regularly, and she and her parents made the first royal [transatlantic telephone](/wiki/Transatlantic_telephone) call on 18 May.

### Second World War

In [Auxiliary Territorial Service](/wiki/Auxiliary_Territorial_Service) uniform, April 1945

In September 1939, [Britain entered the Second World War](/wiki/United_Kingdom_declaration_of_war_on_Germany_(1939)). [Lord Hailsham](/wiki/Douglas_Hogg,_1st_Viscount_Hailsham) suggested that Princesses Elizabeth and Margaret should be [evacuated](/wiki/Evacuations_of_civilians_in_Britain_during_World_War_II) to Canada to avoid the frequent [aerial bombings](/wiki/Aerial_bombing_of_cities) of London by the *[Luftwaffe](/wiki/Luftwaffe)*. This was rejected by their mother, who declared, "The children won't go without me. I won't leave without the King. And the King will never leave." The princesses stayed at [Balmoral Castle](/wiki/Balmoral_Castle), Scotland, until Christmas 1939, when they moved to [Sandringham House](/wiki/Sandringham_House), Norfolk. From February to May 1940, they lived at Royal Lodge, Windsor, until moving to [Windsor Castle](/wiki/Windsor_Castle), where they lived for most of the next five years. At Windsor, [the princesses staged pantomimes](/wiki/Princesses_Elizabeth_and_Margaret%27s_Christmas_pantomimes) at Christmas in aid of the Queen's Wool Fund, which bought [yarn](/wiki/Yarn) to knit into military garments. In 1940, the 14-year-old Elizabeth made her first radio broadcast during the [BBC](/wiki/BBC)'s *[Children's Hour](/wiki/Children%27s_Hour)*, addressing other children who had been evacuated from the cities. She stated: "We are trying to do all we can to help our gallant sailors, soldiers, and airmen, and we are trying, too, to bear our own share of the danger and sadness of war. We know, every one of us, that in the end all will be well."

In 1943, Elizabeth undertook her first solo public appearance on a visit to the [Grenadier Guards](/wiki/Grenadier_Guards), of which she had been appointed [colonel](/wiki/Colonel) the previous year. As she approached her 18th birthday, Parliament changed the law so that she could act as one of five [counsellors of state](/wiki/Counsellors_of_state) in the event of her father's incapacity or absence abroad, such as his visit to Italy in July 1944. In February 1945, she was appointed an honorary [second subaltern](/wiki/Auxiliary_Territorial_Service#Ranks) in the Auxiliary Territorial Service with the [service number](/wiki/Service_number) 230873. She trained as a driver and mechanic and was given the rank of honorary junior commander (female equivalent of [captain](/wiki/Captain_(British_Army_and_Royal_Marines)) at the time) five months later.

Elizabeth (far left) on the balcony of [Buckingham Palace](/wiki/Buckingham_Palace) with her family and Winston Churchill, [8 May 1945](/wiki/8_May_1945)

At the end of the war in Europe, on [Victory in Europe Day](/wiki/Victory_in_Europe_Day), Elizabeth and Margaret mingled incognito with the celebrating crowds in the streets of London. In 1985, Elizabeth recalled in a rare interview, "... we asked my parents if we could go out and see for ourselves. I remember we were terrified of being recognised ... I remember lines of unknown people linking arms and walking down [Whitehall](/wiki/Whitehall), all of us just swept along on a tide of happiness and relief."

During the war, plans were drawn to quell [Welsh nationalism](/wiki/Welsh_nationalism) by affiliating Elizabeth more closely with Wales. Proposals, such as appointing her Constable of [Caernarfon Castle](/wiki/Caernarfon_Castle) or a patron of [Urdd Gobaith Cymru](/wiki/Urdd_Gobaith_Cymru) (the Welsh League of Youth), were abandoned for several reasons, including fear of associating Elizabeth with [conscientious objectors](/wiki/Conscientious_objector) in the Urdd at a time when Britain was at war. Welsh politicians suggested she be made [Princess of Wales](/wiki/Princess_of_Wales) on her 18th birthday. Home Secretary [Herbert Morrison](/wiki/Herbert_Morrison) supported the idea, but the King rejected it because he felt such a title belonged solely to the wife of a [prince of Wales](/wiki/Prince_of_Wales) and the prince of Wales had always been the heir apparent. In 1946, she was inducted into [the Gorsedd of Bards](/wiki/The_Gorsedd_of_Bards) at the [National Eisteddfod of Wales](/wiki/National_Eisteddfod_of_Wales).

Elizabeth went on her first overseas tour in 1947, accompanying her parents through southern Africa. During the tour, in [a broadcast](/wiki/21st_birthday_speech_of_Princess_Elizabeth) to the [British Commonwealth](/wiki/British_Commonwealth) on her 21st birthday, she made the following pledge:

> I declare before you all that my whole life, whether it be long or short, shall be devoted to your service and the service of our great imperial family to which we all belong. But I shall not have strength to carry out this resolution alone unless you join in it with me, as I now invite you to do: I know that your support will be unfailingly given. God help me to make good my vow, and God bless all of you who are willing to share in it.

### Marriage

Main article: [Wedding of Princess Elizabeth and Philip Mountbatten](/wiki/Wedding_of_Princess_Elizabeth_and_Philip_Mountbatten)

Elizabeth met her future husband, [Prince Philip of Greece and Denmark](/wiki/Prince_Philip_of_Greece_and_Denmark), in 1934 and again in 1937. They were [second cousins once removed](/wiki/Cousin#Cousin_chart) through [King Christian IX of Denmark](/wiki/King_Christian_IX_of_Denmark) and third cousins through [Queen Victoria](/wiki/Queen_Victoria). After meeting for the third time at the [Royal Naval College](/wiki/Britannia_Royal_Naval_College) in [Dartmouth](/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon) in July 1939, Elizabeth – though only 13 years old – said she fell in love with Philip, who was 18, and they began to exchange letters. She was 21 when their engagement was officially announced on 9 July 1947.

The engagement attracted some controversy. Philip had no financial standing, was foreign-born (though a [British subject](/wiki/British_subject) who had served in the [Royal Navy](/wiki/Royal_Navy) throughout the Second World War), and his sisters had married German noblemen with [Nazi](/wiki/Nazi) links. Marion Crawford wrote, "Some of the King's advisors did not think him good enough for her. He was a prince without a home or kingdom. Some of the papers played long and loud tunes on the string of Philip's foreign origin." Later biographies reported that Elizabeth's mother had reservations about the union initially and teased Philip as "[the Hun](/wiki/Hun_(pejorative))". In later life, however, she told the biographer [Tim Heald](/wiki/Tim_Heald) that Philip was "an English gentleman".

At Buckingham Palace with Philip after their wedding, 1947

Before the marriage, Philip renounced his Greek and Danish titles, officially converted from [Greek Orthodoxy](/wiki/Greek_Orthodoxy) to [Anglicanism](/wiki/Anglicanism), and adopted the style *Lieutenant Philip Mountbatten*, taking [the surname of his mother's British family](/wiki/Mountbatten_family). Shortly before the wedding, he was created [Duke of Edinburgh](/wiki/Duke_of_Edinburgh) and granted the style *His Royal Highness*. Elizabeth and Philip were married on 20 November 1947 at [Westminster Abbey](/wiki/Westminster_Abbey). They received 2,500 wedding gifts from around the world. Elizabeth required [ration coupons](/wiki/Rationing_in_the_United_Kingdom) to buy the material for [her gown](/wiki/Wedding_dress_of_Princess_Elizabeth_of_the_United_Kingdom) (which was designed by [Norman Hartnell](/wiki/Norman_Hartnell)) because Britain had not yet completely recovered from the devastation of the war. In [post-war Britain](/wiki/Post-war_Britain_(1945%E2%80%931979)), it was not acceptable for Philip's German relations, including his three surviving sisters, to be invited to the wedding. Neither was an invitation extended to the Duke of Windsor, formerly King Edward VIII.

Elizabeth gave birth to her first child, [Prince Charles](/wiki/Prince_Charles), in November 1948. One month earlier, the King had issued [letters patent](/wiki/Letters_patent_(United_Kingdom)) allowing her children to use the style and title of a royal prince or princess, to which they otherwise would not have been entitled, as their father was no longer a royal prince. A second child, [Princess Anne](/wiki/Princess_Anne), was born in August 1950.

Following their wedding, the couple leased [Windlesham Moor](/wiki/Windlesham_Moor), near Windsor Castle, until July 1949, when they took up residence at [Clarence House](/wiki/Clarence_House) in London. At various times between 1949 and 1951, Philip was stationed in the British [Crown Colony of Malta](/wiki/Crown_Colony_of_Malta) as a serving Royal Navy officer. He and Elizabeth lived intermittently in Malta for several months at a time in the [hamlet](/wiki/Hamlet_(place)) of [Gwardamanġa](/wiki/Gwardaman%C4%A1a), at [Villa Guardamangia](/wiki/Villa_Guardamangia), the rented home of Philip's uncle [Lord Mountbatten](/wiki/Lord_Mountbatten). Their two children remained in Britain.

## Reign

### Accession and coronation

Main article: [Coronation of Elizabeth II](/wiki/Coronation_of_Elizabeth_II)

Coronation portrait by [Cecil Beaton](/wiki/Cecil_Beaton), 1953

As George VI's health declined during 1951, Elizabeth frequently stood in for him at public events. When she visited Canada and [Harry S. Truman](/wiki/Harry_S._Truman) in Washington, DC, in October 1951, her private secretary [Martin Charteris](/wiki/Martin_Charteris) carried a draft accession declaration in case the King died while she was on tour. In early 1952, Elizabeth and Philip set out for a tour of Australia and New Zealand by way of the British colony of [Kenya](/wiki/Kenya_Colony). On 6 February, they had just returned to their Kenyan home, [Sagana Lodge](/wiki/Sagana_Lodge), after a night spent at [Treetops Hotel](/wiki/Treetops_Hotel), when word arrived of [the death](/wiki/Death_and_state_funeral_of_George_VI) of Elizabeth's father. Philip broke the news to the new queen. She chose to retain Elizabeth as her regnal name, and was therefore called Elizabeth II. The numeral offended some Scots, as she was the first Elizabeth to rule in Scotland. She was [proclaimed queen](/wiki/Proclamation_of_accession_of_Elizabeth_II) throughout her realms, and the royal party hastily returned to the United Kingdom. Elizabeth and Philip moved into Buckingham Palace.

With Elizabeth's accession, it seemed possible that the [royal house](/wiki/Royal_house) would take her husband's name, in line with the custom for married women of the time. Lord Mountbatten advocated for *House of Mountbatten*, and Philip suggested *House of Edinburgh*, after his ducal title. The British prime minister, Winston Churchill, and Elizabeth's grandmother Queen Mary favoured the retention of the [House of Windsor](/wiki/House_of_Windsor). Elizabeth issued a declaration on 9 April 1952 that the royal house would continue to be *Windsor*. Philip complained, "I am the only man in the country not allowed to give his name to his own children." In 1960, the surname *[Mountbatten-Windsor](/wiki/Mountbatten-Windsor)* was adopted for Philip and Elizabeth's male-line descendants who do not carry royal titles.

Amid preparations for the coronation, Princess Margaret told her sister she wished to marry [Peter Townsend](/wiki/Peter_Townsend_(RAF_officer)), a divorcé 16 years Margaret's senior with two sons from his previous marriage. Elizabeth asked them to wait for a year; in the words of her [private secretary](/wiki/Private_Secretary_to_the_Sovereign), "the Queen was naturally sympathetic towards the Princess, but I think she thought – she hoped – given time, the affair would peter out." Senior politicians were against the match and the [Church of England](/wiki/Church_of_England) did not permit [remarriage](/wiki/Remarriage) after divorce. If Margaret had contracted a [civil marriage](/wiki/Civil_marriage), she would have been expected to renounce her [right of succession](/wiki/Right_of_succession). Margaret decided to abandon her plans with Townsend. In 1960, she married [Antony Armstrong-Jones](/wiki/Antony_Armstrong-Jones), who was created [Earl of Snowdon](/wiki/Earl_of_Snowdon) the following year. They divorced in 1978; Margaret did not remarry.

Despite [Queen Mary's death](/wiki/Death_and_funeral_of_Mary_of_Teck) on 24 March 1953, the coronation went ahead on 2 June, as originally scheduled, as Mary had requested. The coronation ceremony in Westminster Abbey was televised for the first time, with the exception of the [anointing](/wiki/Anointing) and [communion](/wiki/Eucharist). On Elizabeth's instruction, [her coronation gown](/wiki/Coronation_gown_of_Elizabeth_II) was embroidered with the [floral emblems](/wiki/Floral_emblem) of Commonwealth countries.

### Early reign

Dominions/realms

From Elizabeth's birth onwards, the [British Empire](/wiki/British_Empire) continued its transformation into the [Commonwealth of Nations](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations). By the time of her accession in 1952, her role as head of multiple independent states was already established. In 1953, Elizabeth and Philip embarked on a seven-month round-the-world tour, visiting 13 countries and covering more than 40,000 miles (64,000 km) by land, sea and air. She became the first reigning [monarch of Australia](/wiki/Monarch_of_Australia) and [New Zealand](/wiki/Monarchy_of_New_Zealand) to visit those nations. During the tour, crowds were immense; three-quarters of the population of Australia were estimated to have seen her. Throughout her reign, she made hundreds of [state visits](/wiki/List_of_state_visits_made_by_Elizabeth_II) to other countries and [tours of the Commonwealth](/wiki/List_of_Commonwealth_visits_made_by_Elizabeth_II); she was the most widely travelled [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state).

In 1956, the British and French prime ministers, [Sir Anthony Eden](/wiki/Sir_Anthony_Eden) and [Guy Mollet](/wiki/Guy_Mollet), discussed the possibility of France joining the Commonwealth. The proposal was never accepted, and the following year, France signed the [Treaty of Rome](/wiki/Treaty_of_Rome), which established the [European Economic Community](/wiki/European_Economic_Community), the precursor to the European Union. In November 1956, Britain and France [invaded Egypt](/wiki/Suez_Crisis) in an ultimately unsuccessful attempt to capture the [Suez Canal](/wiki/Suez_Canal). Lord Mountbatten said that Elizabeth was opposed to the invasion, though Eden denied it. Eden resigned two months later.

With Commonwealth leaders, at the 1960 [Commonwealth Conference](/wiki/Commonwealth_Prime_Ministers%27_Conference)

The governing [Conservative Party](/wiki/Conservative_Party_(UK)) had no formal mechanism for choosing a leader, meaning that it fell to Elizabeth to decide whom to [commission to form a government](/wiki/Kissing_hands) following Eden's resignation. Eden recommended she consult [Lord Salisbury](/wiki/Robert_Gascoyne-Cecil,_5th_Marquess_of_Salisbury), the [lord president of the council](/wiki/Lord_president_of_the_council). Lord Salisbury and [Lord Kilmuir](/wiki/Lord_Kilmuir), the [lord chancellor](/wiki/Lord_chancellor), consulted the [British Cabinet](/wiki/British_Cabinet), Churchill, and the chairman of the backbench [1922 Committee](/wiki/1922_Committee), resulting in Elizabeth appointing their recommended candidate: [Harold Macmillan](/wiki/Harold_Macmillan).

The Suez crisis and the choice of Eden's successor led, in 1957, to the first major personal criticism of Elizabeth. In a magazine, which he owned and edited, [Lord Altrincham](/wiki/John_Grigg) accused her of being "out of touch". Altrincham was denounced by public figures and slapped by a member of the public appalled by his comments. Six years later, in 1963, Macmillan resigned and advised Elizabeth to appoint [Alec Douglas-Home](/wiki/Alec_Douglas-Home) as the prime minister, advice she followed. Elizabeth again came under criticism for appointing the prime minister on the advice of a small number of ministers or a single minister. In 1965, the Conservatives adopted a formal mechanism for electing a leader, thus relieving the Queen of her involvement.

With Philip, seated on thrones at the [Canadian parliament](/wiki/Canadian_parliament), 1957

In 1957, Elizabeth made a state visit to the United States, where she addressed the [United Nations General Assembly](/wiki/United_Nations_General_Assembly) on behalf of the Commonwealth. On the same tour, she opened the [23rd Canadian Parliament](/wiki/23rd_Canadian_Parliament), becoming the first [monarch of Canada](/wiki/Monarch_of_Canada) to open a parliamentary session. Two years later, solely in her capacity as Queen of Canada, she revisited the United States and toured Canada. In 1961, she toured Cyprus, India, Pakistan, [Nepal](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Nepal), and [Iran](/wiki/Pahlavi_Iran). On a visit to Ghana the same year, she dismissed fears for her safety, even though her host, President [Kwame Nkrumah](/wiki/Kwame_Nkrumah), who had replaced her as head of state, was a target for assassins. Harold Macmillan wrote, "The Queen has been absolutely determined all through ... She is impatient of the attitude towards her to treat her as ... a film star ... She has indeed '[the heart and stomach of a man](/wiki/Speech_to_the_Troops_at_Tilbury)' ... She loves her duty and means to be a Queen." Before her tour through parts of [Quebec](/wiki/Quebec) in 1964, the press reported that extremists within the [Quebec separatist movement](/wiki/Quebec_separatist_movement) were plotting Elizabeth's assassination. No assassination attempt was made, but a riot did break out while she was in [Montreal](/wiki/Montreal); her "calmness and courage in the face of the violence" was noted.

Elizabeth gave birth to her third child, [Andrew](/wiki/Andrew_Mountbatten-Windsor), in February 1960; this was the first birth to a reigning British monarch since 1857. Her fourth child, [Prince Edward](/wiki/Prince_Edward,_Duke_of_Edinburgh), was born in March 1964. According to biographer [Gyles Brandreth](/wiki/Gyles_Brandreth), Elizabeth suffered a miscarriage at some point during her reign.

### Political reforms and crises

In [Queensland](/wiki/Queensland), Australia, 1970

With President [Josip Broz Tito](/wiki/Josip_Broz_Tito) of [Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia) in Belgrade, 1972

The 1960s and 1970s saw an acceleration in the [decolonisation of Africa](/wiki/Decolonisation_of_Africa) and the Caribbean. More than 20 countries gained independence from Britain as part of a planned transition to self-government. In 1965, however, the Rhodesian prime minister, [Ian Smith](/wiki/Ian_Smith), in opposition to moves towards [majority rule](/wiki/Majority_rule), [unilaterally declared independence](/wiki/Rhodesia%27s_Unilateral_Declaration_of_Independence) with Elizabeth as "[Queen of Rhodesia](/wiki/Queen_of_Rhodesia)". Although Elizabeth formally dismissed him, and the international community applied sanctions against Rhodesia, his regime survived for over a decade. As Britain's ties to its former empire weakened, the British government sought entry to the [European Community](/wiki/European_Community), a goal it [achieved in 1973](/wiki/Accession_of_the_United_Kingdom_to_the_European_Communities).

In 1966, the Queen was criticised for waiting eight days before visiting the village of [Aberfan](/wiki/Aberfan), where [a mining disaster](/wiki/Aberfan_disaster) killed 116 children and 28 adults. [Martin Charteris](/wiki/Martin_Charteris) said that the delay, made on his advice, was a mistake that she later regretted. Elizabeth toured [Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia) in October 1972, becoming the first British monarch to visit a [communist country](/wiki/Communist_country). She was received at the airport by President [Josip Broz Tito](/wiki/Josip_Broz_Tito), and a crowd of thousands greeted her in [Belgrade](/wiki/Belgrade).

In February 1974, British prime minister [Edward Heath](/wiki/Edward_Heath) advised Elizabeth to call [a general election](/wiki/February_1974_United_Kingdom_general_election) in the middle of her tour of the [Austronesian](/wiki/Austronesia) [Pacific Rim](/wiki/Pacific_Rim), requiring her to fly back to Britain. The election resulted in a [hung parliament](/wiki/Hung_parliament); Heath's Conservatives were not the largest party but could stay in office if they formed a coalition with the [Liberals](/wiki/Liberal_Party_(UK)). When discussions on forming a coalition foundered, Heath resigned, and Elizabeth asked the [Leader of the Opposition](/wiki/Leader_of_the_Opposition_(United_Kingdom)), [Labour](/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK))'s [Harold Wilson](/wiki/Harold_Wilson), to form a government.

A year later, at the height of the [1975 Australian constitutional crisis](/wiki/1975_Australian_constitutional_crisis), the Australian prime minister, [Gough Whitlam](/wiki/Gough_Whitlam), was dismissed from his post by Governor-General [Sir John Kerr](/wiki/Sir_John_Kerr), after the Opposition-controlled [Senate](/wiki/Australian_Senate) rejected Whitlam's budget proposals. As Whitlam had a majority in the [House of Representatives](/wiki/Australian_House_of_Representatives), Speaker [Gordon Scholes](/wiki/Gordon_Scholes) appealed to Elizabeth to reverse Kerr's decision. She declined, saying she would not interfere in decisions reserved by the [Constitution of Australia](/wiki/Constitution_of_Australia) for the [governor-general](/wiki/Governor-General_of_Australia). The crisis fuelled [Australian republicanism](/wiki/Australian_republicanism).

[Leaders of the G7 states](/wiki/3rd_G7_summit), members of the royal family and Elizabeth (centre), London, 1977

In 1977, Elizabeth marked the [Silver Jubilee](/wiki/Silver_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II) of her accession. Parties and events took place throughout the Commonwealth, many coinciding with [her associated national and Commonwealth tours](/wiki/List_of_events_during_the_Silver_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II). The celebrations re-affirmed Elizabeth's popularity, despite virtually coincident negative press coverage of Princess Margaret's separation from her husband, Lord Snowdon. In 1978, Elizabeth endured a state visit to the United Kingdom by [Romania](/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Romania)'s communist leader, [Nicolae Ceaușescu](/wiki/Nicolae_Ceau%C8%99escu), and his wife, [Elena](/wiki/Elena_Ceau%C8%99escu), though privately she thought they had "blood on their hands". The following year brought two blows: the unmasking of [Anthony Blunt](/wiki/Anthony_Blunt), former [Surveyor of the Queen's Pictures](/wiki/Surveyor_of_the_Queen%27s_Pictures), as a communist spy and the [assassination of Lord Mountbatten](/wiki/Assassination_of_Lord_Mountbatten) by the [Provisional Irish Republican Army](/wiki/Provisional_Irish_Republican_Army).

According to [Paul Martin Sr.](/wiki/Paul_Martin_Sr.), by the end of the 1970s, Elizabeth was worried [the Crown](/wiki/The_Crown) "had little meaning for" [Pierre Trudeau](/wiki/Pierre_Trudeau), the Canadian prime minister. [Tony Benn](/wiki/Tony_Benn) said Elizabeth found Trudeau "rather disappointing". Trudeau's supposed [republicanism](/wiki/Republicanism_in_Canada) seemed to be confirmed by his antics, such as sliding down banisters at Buckingham Palace and pirouetting behind Elizabeth's back in 1977, and the removal of various [Canadian royal symbols](/wiki/Canadian_royal_symbols) during his term of office. In 1980, Canadian politicians sent to London to discuss the [patriation](/wiki/Patriation) of the [Canadian constitution](/wiki/Canadian_constitution) found Elizabeth "better informed ... than any of the British politicians or bureaucrats". She was particularly interested after the failure of Bill C-60, which would have affected her role as head of state.

### Perils and dissent

Riding Burmese at the 1986 Trooping the Colour ceremony

During the 1981 [Trooping the Colour](/wiki/Trooping_the_Colour) ceremony, six weeks before the [wedding of Prince Charles and Lady Diana Spencer](/wiki/Wedding_of_Prince_Charles_and_Lady_Diana_Spencer), six shots were fired at Elizabeth from close range as she rode down [The Mall, London](/wiki/The_Mall,_London), on her horse, [Burmese](/wiki/Burmese_(horse)). Police later discovered the shots were blanks. The 17-year-old assailant, [Marcus Sarjeant](/wiki/Marcus_Sarjeant), was sentenced to five years in prison and released after three. Elizabeth's composure and skill in controlling her mount were widely praised. That October, Elizabeth was the subject of another attack while on a visit to [Dunedin](/wiki/Dunedin), New Zealand. [Christopher John Lewis](/wiki/Christopher_John_Lewis), who was 17 years old, fired a shot with a [.22 rifle](/wiki/.22_rifle) from the fifth floor of a building overlooking the parade but missed. Lewis was arrested, but instead of being charged with [attempted murder](/wiki/Attempted_murder) or [treason](/wiki/Treason) was sentenced to three years in jail for unlawful possession and discharge of a firearm. Two years into his sentence, he attempted to escape a [psychiatric hospital](/wiki/Psychiatric_hospital) with the intention of assassinating Charles, who was visiting the country with [Diana](/wiki/Diana,_Princess_of_Wales) and their son [Prince William](/wiki/Prince_William).

Riding at Windsor with President Reagan, June 1982

From April to September 1982, Elizabeth's son Andrew served with British forces in the [Falklands War](/wiki/Falklands_War), for which she reportedly felt anxiety and pride. On 9 July, she awoke in her bedroom at Buckingham Palace to find an intruder, [Michael Fagan](/wiki/Michael_Fagan), in the room with her. In a serious lapse of security, assistance only arrived after two calls to the Palace police switchboard. After hosting US president [Ronald Reagan](/wiki/Ronald_Reagan) at Windsor Castle in 1982 and visiting [his California ranch](/wiki/Rancho_del_Cielo) in 1983, Elizabeth was angered when [his administration](/wiki/Presidency_of_Ronald_Reagan) ordered the [invasion of Grenada](/wiki/Invasion_of_Grenada), one of her Caribbean realms, without informing her.

Intense media interest in the opinions and private lives of the royal family during the 1980s led to a series of sensational stories in the press, pioneered by *[The Sun](/wiki/The_Sun_(United_Kingdom))* tabloid. As [Kelvin MacKenzie](/wiki/Kelvin_MacKenzie), editor of *The Sun*, told his staff: "Give me a Sunday for Monday splash on the Royals. Don't worry if it's not true—so long as there's not too much of a fuss about it afterwards." Newspaper editor [Donald Trelford](/wiki/Donald_Trelford) wrote in *[The Observer](/wiki/The_Observer)* of 21 September 1986: "The royal soap opera has now reached such a pitch of public interest that the boundary between fact and fiction has been lost sight of ... it is not just that some papers don't check their facts or accept denials: they don't care if the stories are true or not." It was reported, most notably in *[The Sunday Times](/wiki/The_Sunday_Times)* of 20 July 1986, that Elizabeth was worried that [Margaret Thatcher](/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher)'s [economic policies](/wiki/Thatcherism#economicposition) fostered social divisions and was alarmed by high unemployment, [a series of riots](/wiki/1981_England_riots), the violence of [a miners' strike](/wiki/1984%E2%80%931985_United_Kingdom_miners%27_strike), and Thatcher's refusal to apply sanctions against the [apartheid](/wiki/Apartheid) regime in South Africa. The sources of the rumours included royal aide [Michael Shea](/wiki/Michael_Shea_(diplomat)) and Commonwealth secretary-general [Shridath Ramphal](/wiki/Shridath_Ramphal), but Shea claimed his remarks were taken [out of context](/wiki/Out_of_context) and embellished by speculation. Thatcher reputedly said Elizabeth would vote for the [Social Democratic Party](/wiki/Social_Democratic_Party_(UK)) – Thatcher's political opponents. Thatcher's biographer [John Campbell](/wiki/John_Campbell_(biographer)) claimed "the report was a piece of journalistic mischief-making". Reports of acrimony between them were exaggerated, and Elizabeth gave two honours in her personal gift – membership in the [Order of Merit](/wiki/Order_of_Merit) and the [Order of the Garter](/wiki/Order_of_the_Garter) – to Thatcher after her replacement as prime minister by [John Major](/wiki/John_Major). [Brian Mulroney](/wiki/Brian_Mulroney), Canadian prime minister between 1984 and 1993, said Elizabeth was a "behind the scenes force" in ending apartheid.

In 1986, Elizabeth paid a six-day [state visit to the People's Republic of China](/wiki/State_visit_by_Elizabeth_II_to_China), becoming the first British monarch to visit the country. The tour included the [Forbidden City](/wiki/Forbidden_City), the [Great Wall of China](/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China), and the [Terracotta Warriors](/wiki/Terracotta_Warriors). At a [state banquet](/wiki/State_banquet), Elizabeth joked about the first British emissary to China being lost at sea with [Queen Elizabeth I](/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_I)'s letter to the [Wanli Emperor](/wiki/Wanli_Emperor), and remarked, "fortunately postal services have improved since 1602". Elizabeth's visit also signified the acceptance of both countries that [sovereignty over Hong Kong would be transferred](/wiki/Handover_of_Hong_Kong) from the United Kingdom to China in 1997.

By the end of the 1980s, Elizabeth had become the target of satire. The involvement of younger members of the royal family in the charity game show *[It's a Royal Knockout](/wiki/It%27s_a_Royal_Knockout)* in 1987 was ridiculed. In Canada, Elizabeth publicly supported politically divisive [constitutional amendments](/wiki/Meech_Lake_Accord), prompting criticism from opponents of the proposed changes, including Pierre Trudeau. The same year, the elected Fijian government was deposed in [a military coup](/wiki/1987_Fijian_coups_d%27%C3%A9tat). As [monarch of Fiji](/wiki/Monarch_of_Fiji), Elizabeth supported the attempts of Governor-General [Ratu Sir Penaia Ganilau](/wiki/Ratu_Sir_Penaia_Ganilau) to assert executive power and negotiate a settlement. Coup leader [Sitiveni Rabuka](/wiki/Sitiveni_Rabuka) deposed Ganilau and declared Fiji a republic.

### Turbulent years

With Philip in the United States, May 1991

In the wake of coalition victory in the [Gulf War](/wiki/Gulf_War), Elizabeth became the first British monarch to address a [joint meeting](/wiki/Joint_session_of_the_United_States_Congress) of the [United States Congress](/wiki/United_States_Congress) in May 1991. In November 1992, in a speech to mark the [Ruby Jubilee](/wiki/Ruby_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II) of her accession, Elizabeth called 1992 her *[annus horribilis](/wiki/Annus_horribilis)* (a Latin phrase, meaning 'horrible year'). [Republican feeling in Britain](/wiki/Republican_feeling_in_Britain) had risen because of press estimates of Elizabeth's private wealth – contradicted by the Palace – and reports of affairs and strained marriages among her extended family. In March, her second son, Andrew, separated from his wife, [Sarah](/wiki/Sarah_Ferguson); her daughter, Princess Anne, divorced Captain [Mark Phillips](/wiki/Mark_Phillips) in April; angry demonstrators in [Dresden](/wiki/Dresden) threw eggs at Elizabeth during a state visit to Germany in October; and [a large fire broke out at Windsor Castle](/wiki/1992_Windsor_Castle_fire), one of her [official residences](/wiki/Official_residence), in November. The monarchy came under increased criticism and public scrutiny. In an unusually personal speech, Elizabeth said that any institution must expect criticism, but suggested it might be done with "a touch of humour, gentleness and understanding". Two days later, John Major announced plans to reform the royal finances, drawn up the previous year, including Elizabeth paying [income tax](/wiki/Income_tax) from 1993 onwards, and a reduction in the [civil list](/wiki/Civil_list). In December, Prince Charles and his wife, Diana, formally separated. At the end of the year, Elizabeth sued *The Sun* newspaper for breach of copyright when it published the text of her [annual Christmas message](/wiki/Royal_Christmas_Message) two days before it was broadcast. The newspaper was forced to pay her legal fees and donated £200,000 to charity. Elizabeth's solicitors had taken successful action against *The Sun* five years earlier for breach of copyright after it published a photograph of her daughter-in-law the Duchess of York and her granddaughter [Princess Beatrice](/wiki/Princess_Beatrice).

In January 1994, Elizabeth broke her left wrist when a horse she was riding at Sandringham tripped and fell. In October 1994, she became the first reigning British monarch to set foot on Russian soil. In October 1995, she was tricked into a [hoax call](/wiki/Hoax_call) by Montreal radio host [Pierre Brassard](/wiki/Pierre_Brassard) impersonating Canadian prime minister [Jean Chrétien](/wiki/Jean_Chr%C3%A9tien). Elizabeth, who believed that she was speaking to Chrétien, said she supported Canadian unity and would try to influence [Quebec's referendum](/wiki/1995_Quebec_referendum) on proposals to break away from Canada.

In the year that followed, public revelations on the state of Charles and Diana's marriage continued. In consultation with her husband and John Major, as well as the [Archbishop of Canterbury](/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury) ([George Carey](/wiki/George_Carey)) and her private secretary ([Robert Fellowes](/wiki/Robert_Fellowes,_Baron_Fellowes)), Elizabeth wrote to Charles and Diana at the end of December 1995, suggesting that a divorce would be advisable.

In August 1997, a year after the divorce, Diana [was killed](/wiki/Death_of_Diana,_Princess_of_Wales) in a car crash in Paris. Elizabeth was on holiday with her extended family at Balmoral. Diana's two sons, Princes William and [Harry](/wiki/Prince_Harry,_Duke_of_Sussex), wanted to attend church, so Elizabeth and Philip took them that morning. Afterwards, for five days, the royal couple shielded their grandsons from the intense press interest by keeping them at Balmoral where they could grieve in private, but the royal family's silence and seclusion, and the failure to fly a flag at [half-mast](/wiki/Half-mast) over Buckingham Palace, caused public dismay. Pressured by the hostile reaction, Elizabeth agreed to return to London and address the nation in a [live television broadcast](/wiki/Addresses_to_the_nation_by_Elizabeth_II) on 5 September, the day before [Diana's funeral](/wiki/Diana%27s_funeral). In the broadcast, she expressed admiration for Diana and her feelings "as a grandmother" for the two princes. As a result, much of the public hostility evaporated.

In October 1997, Elizabeth and Philip made a state visit to India, which included a controversial visit to the site of the [Jallianwala Bagh massacre](/wiki/Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre) to pay her respects. Protesters chanted "Killer Queen, go back", and there were demands for her to apologise for the action of British troops 78 years earlier. At the memorial in the park, she and Philip laid a [wreath](/wiki/Wreath) and stood for a 30‑second [moment of silence](/wiki/Moment_of_silence). As a result, much of the fury among the public softened, and the protests were called off. That November, the royal couple held a reception at [Banqueting House](/wiki/Banqueting_House) to mark their golden wedding anniversary. Elizabeth made a speech and praised Philip for his role as consort, referring to him as "my strength and stay".

In 1999, as part of the process of [devolution in the United Kingdom](/wiki/Devolution_in_the_United_Kingdom), Elizabeth formally opened newly established legislatures for Wales and Scotland: the [National Assembly for Wales](/wiki/National_Assembly_for_Wales) at [Cardiff](/wiki/Cardiff) in May, and the [Scottish Parliament](/wiki/Scottish_Parliament) at [Edinburgh](/wiki/Edinburgh) in July.

### Dawn of the new millennium

[Golden Jubilee](/wiki/Golden_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II) dinner with living British prime ministers, 2002: (left to right) [Tony Blair](/wiki/Tony_Blair), [Margaret Thatcher](/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher), [Edward Heath](/wiki/Edward_Heath), Elizabeth, [James Callaghan](/wiki/James_Callaghan), [John Major](/wiki/John_Major)

On the eve of the new millennium, Elizabeth and Philip boarded a vessel from [Southwark](/wiki/Southwark), bound for the [Millennium Dome](/wiki/Millennium_Dome). Before passing under [Tower Bridge](/wiki/Tower_Bridge), she lit the National Millennium Beacon in the [Pool of London](/wiki/Pool_of_London) using a laser torch. Shortly before midnight, she officially opened the Dome. During the singing of *[Auld Lang Syne](/wiki/Auld_Lang_Syne)*, Elizabeth held hands with Philip and British prime minister [Tony Blair](/wiki/Tony_Blair). Following the [9/11 attacks](/wiki/9/11_attacks) in the United States, Elizabeth, breaking with tradition, ordered the [American national anthem](/wiki/American_national_anthem) to be played during the [changing of the guard](/wiki/Changing_of_the_guard_(United_Kingdom)) at Buckingham Palace to express her solidarity with the country.

In 2002, Elizabeth marked her [Golden Jubilee](/wiki/Golden_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II), the 50th anniversary of her accession. Her sister died in February and [her mother in March](/wiki/Death_and_funeral_of_Queen_Elizabeth_the_Queen_Mother), and the media speculated on whether the Jubilee would be a success or a failure. Princess Margaret's death shook Elizabeth; her funeral was one of the rare occasions where Elizabeth openly cried. Elizabeth again undertook an extensive tour of her realms, beginning in Jamaica in February, where she called the farewell banquet "memorable" after a [power cut](/wiki/Power_cut) plunged [King's House](/wiki/King%27s_House,_Jamaica), the official residence of the [governor-general](/wiki/Governor-General_of_Jamaica), into darkness. As in 1977, there were street parties and commemorative events, and monuments were named to honour the occasion. One million people attended each day of the three-day main Jubilee celebration in London, and the enthusiasm shown for Elizabeth by the public was greater than many journalists had anticipated.

Greeting [NASA](/wiki/NASA) employees at the [Goddard Space Flight Center](/wiki/Goddard_Space_Flight_Center), Maryland, May 2007

In 2003, Elizabeth sued the *[Daily Mirror](/wiki/Daily_Mirror)* for [breach of confidence](/wiki/Breach_of_confidence_in_English_law) and obtained an [injunction](/wiki/Injunction) which prevented the outlet from publishing information gathered by a reporter who posed as a [footman](/wiki/Footman) at Buckingham Palace. The newspaper also paid £25,000 towards her legal costs. Though generally healthy throughout her life, in 2003 she had [keyhole surgery](/wiki/Keyhole_surgery) on both knees. In October 2006, she missed the opening of the new [Emirates Stadium](/wiki/Emirates_Stadium) because of a strained back muscle that had been troubling her since the summer.

In May 2007, citing unnamed sources, *[The Daily Telegraph](/wiki/The_Daily_Telegraph)* reported that Elizabeth was "exasperated and frustrated" by the policies of Tony Blair, that she was concerned the [British Armed Forces](/wiki/British_Armed_Forces) were overstretched in Iraq and [Afghanistan](/wiki/Islamic_Republic_of_Afghanistan), and that she had raised concerns over rural and countryside issues with Blair. She was, however, said to admire Blair's efforts to achieve [peace in Northern Ireland](/wiki/Peace_in_Northern_Ireland). She became the first British monarch to celebrate a diamond wedding anniversary in November 2007. On 20 March 2008, at the [Church of Ireland](/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) [St Patrick's Cathedral, Armagh](/wiki/St_Patrick%27s_Cathedral,_Armagh_(Church_of_Ireland)), Elizabeth attended the first [Maundy service](/wiki/Maundy_service) held outside England and Wales.

Elizabeth addressed the UN General Assembly for a second time in 2010, again in her capacity as Queen of all Commonwealth realms and [Head of the Commonwealth](/wiki/Head_of_the_Commonwealth). The [UN secretary-general](/wiki/UN_secretary-general), [Ban Ki-moon](/wiki/Ban_Ki-moon), introduced her as "an anchor for our age". During her visit to New York, which followed a tour of Canada, she officially opened a memorial garden for British [victims of the 9/11 attacks](/wiki/Victims_of_the_9/11_attacks). Elizabeth's 11-day visit to Australia in October 2011 was her 16th visit to the country since 1954. By invitation of the [Irish president](/wiki/Irish_president), [Mary McAleese](/wiki/Mary_McAleese), she made the first [state visit to the Republic of Ireland](/wiki/State_visit_by_Elizabeth_II_to_the_Republic_of_Ireland) by a British monarch in May 2011.

### Diamond Jubilee and milestones

Visiting [Birmingham](/wiki/Birmingham) in July 2012 as part of the Diamond Jubilee tour

The [2012 Diamond Jubilee](/wiki/2012_Diamond_Jubilee) marked 60 years since Elizabeth's accession, and celebrations were held throughout her realms, the wider Commonwealth, and beyond. She and Philip undertook an extensive tour of the United Kingdom, while their children and grandchildren embarked on royal tours of other Commonwealth states on her behalf. On 4 June, Jubilee beacons were lit around the world. On 18 December, the Queen became the first British [sovereign](/wiki/Sovereign) to attend a peacetime [Cabinet meeting](/wiki/Cabinet_of_the_United_Kingdom) since [George III](/wiki/George_III) in 1781.

Elizabeth, who opened the [Montreal Summer Olympics](/wiki/Montreal_Summer_Olympics) in 1976, also opened the [2012 Summer Olympics](/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics) and [Paralympics](/wiki/2012_Summer_Paralympics) in London, making her the first [head of state to open](/wiki/List_of_people_who_have_opened_the_Olympic_Games) two [Olympic Games](/wiki/Olympic_Games) in two countries. For the London Olympics, she portrayed herself in [a short film](/wiki/Happy_%26_Glorious) as part of [the opening ceremony](/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics_opening_ceremony), alongside [Daniel Craig](/wiki/Daniel_Craig) as [James Bond](/wiki/James_Bond). On 4 April 2013, she received an honorary [BAFTA](/wiki/BAFTA) award for her [patronage](/wiki/Patronage) of the film industry and was called "the most memorable [Bond girl](/wiki/Bond_girl) yet" at a special presentation at Windsor Castle.

Opening the [Borders Railway](/wiki/Borders_Railway) on the day she became the longest-reigning British monarch, 2015. In her speech, she said she had never aspired to achieve that milestone.

In March 2013, the Queen stayed overnight at [King Edward VII's Hospital](/wiki/King_Edward_VII%27s_Hospital) as a precaution after developing symptoms of [gastroenteritis](/wiki/Gastroenteritis). A week later, she signed the new [Charter of the Commonwealth](/wiki/Charter_of_the_Commonwealth). That year, because of her age and the need for her to limit travelling, she chose not to attend the biennial [Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting](/wiki/Commonwealth_Heads_of_Government_Meeting) for the first time in 40 years. She was represented at the [summit in Sri Lanka](/wiki/2013_Commonwealth_Heads_of_Government_Meeting) by Prince Charles. On 20 April 2018, the Commonwealth heads of government announced that Charles would succeed her as Head of the Commonwealth, which the Queen stated as her "sincere wish". She underwent [cataract surgery](/wiki/Cataract_surgery) in May 2018. In March 2019, she gave up driving on public roads, largely as a consequence of a car accident involving her husband two months earlier.

On 21 December 2007, Elizabeth surpassed her great-great-grandmother, Queen Victoria, to become the longest-lived British monarch, and she became the [longest-reigning British monarch](/wiki/List_of_monarchs_in_Britain_by_length_of_reign) and [longest-reigning queen regnant](/wiki/List_of_longest-reigning_monarchs) and female head of state in the world on 9 September 2015. She became the oldest living monarch after the death of [King Abdullah of Saudi Arabia](/wiki/King_Abdullah_of_Saudi_Arabia) on 23 January 2015. She later became the longest-reigning current monarch and the [longest-serving current head of state](/wiki/List_of_current_state_leaders_by_date_of_assumption_of_office) following the [death of King Bhumibol Adulyadej](/wiki/Death_of_King_Bhumibol_Adulyadej) of Thailand on 13 October 2016, and the [oldest current head of state](/wiki/Lists_of_state_leaders_by_age) on the [resignation of Robert Mugabe](/wiki/Resignation_of_Robert_Mugabe) of Zimbabwe on 21 November 2017. On 6 February 2017, she became the first British monarch to commemorate [a sapphire jubilee](/wiki/Sapphire_Jubilee_of_Elizabeth_II), and on 20 November that year, she was the first British monarch to celebrate a platinum wedding anniversary. Philip had retired from his official duties as the Queen's consort in August 2017.

### Pandemic and widowhood

[Virtual meeting](/wiki/Virtual_meeting) with [Cindy Kiro](/wiki/Cindy_Kiro) during the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic), October 2021

On 19 March 2020, as the [COVID-19 pandemic hit the United Kingdom](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_Kingdom), Elizabeth moved to Windsor Castle and sequestered there as a precaution. Public engagements were cancelled and Windsor Castle followed a strict sanitary protocol nicknamed "HMS Bubble". On 5 April, in a televised broadcast watched by an estimated 24 million viewers in the United Kingdom, Elizabeth asked people to "take comfort that while we may have more still to endure, better days will return: we will be with our friends again; we will be with our families again; we will meet again." On 8 May, the 75th anniversary of [VE Day](/wiki/VE_Day), in a television broadcast at 9 pm—the exact time at which her father had broadcast to the nation on the same day in 1945—she asked people to "never give up, never despair". In 2021, she received her first and second [COVID-19 vaccinations](/wiki/COVID-19_vaccinations) in January and April respectively.

[Prince Philip died](/wiki/Prince_Philip_died) on 9 April 2021, after 73 years of marriage, making Elizabeth the first British monarch to reign as a [widow](/wiki/Widow) or widower since Queen Victoria. She was reportedly at her husband's bedside when he died, and remarked in private that his death had "left a huge void". Due to the COVID-19 restrictions in place in England at the time, Elizabeth sat alone at Philip's funeral service, which evoked sympathy from people around the world. It was later reported in the press that Elizabeth had rejected a government offer to relax the rules. In her Christmas broadcast that year, which was ultimately her last, she paid a personal tribute to her "beloved Philip", saying, "That mischievous, inquiring twinkle was as bright at the end as when I first set eyes on him."

Despite the pandemic, Elizabeth attended the [2021 State Opening of Parliament](/wiki/2021_State_Opening_of_Parliament) in May, the [47th G7 summit](/wiki/47th_G7_summit) in June, and hosted US president [Joe Biden](/wiki/Joe_Biden) at Windsor Castle. Biden was the 14th US president that the Queen had met. In October 2021, Elizabeth cancelled a planned trip to Northern Ireland and stayed overnight at King Edward VII's Hospital for "preliminary investigations". On Christmas Day 2021, while she was staying at Windsor Castle, 19-year-old Jaswant Singh Chail broke into the gardens using a rope ladder and carrying a [crossbow](/wiki/Crossbow) with the aim of assassinating Elizabeth in revenge for the [Amritsar massacre](/wiki/Amritsar_massacre). Before he could enter any buildings, he was arrested and detained under the [Mental Health Act](/wiki/Mental_Health_Act_2007). In February 2023, Chail pleaded guilty to [attempting to injure or alarm the sovereign](/wiki/Treason_Act_1842#Section_2), and was sentenced in October to a nine-year custodial sentence plus an additional five years on extended licence. The sentencing judge also placed Chail under a hybrid order under section 45A of the [Mental Health Act 1983](/wiki/Mental_Health_Act_1983), ordering that he remain at [Broadmoor Hospital](/wiki/Broadmoor_Hospital) to be transferred into custody only after receiving psychiatric treatment.

### Platinum Jubilee and beyond

With the royal family on the balcony of Buckingham Palace following the [Platinum Jubilee Pageant](/wiki/Platinum_Jubilee_Pageant), June 2022

[Elizabeth's Platinum Jubilee celebrations](/wiki/Elizabeth%27s_Platinum_Jubilee_celebrations) began on 6 February 2022, marking 70 years since her accession. In her [accession day](/wiki/Accession_day) message, she renewed her commitment to a lifetime of public service, which she had originally made in 1947.

Later that month, Elizabeth fell ill with COVID-19 along with several family members, but she only exhibited "mild cold-like symptoms" and recovered by the end of the month. She was present at [the service of thanksgiving for her husband](/wiki/A_Service_of_Thanksgiving_for_the_life_of_The_Duke_of_Edinburgh) at Westminster Abbey on 29 March, but was unable to attend both the annual [Commonwealth Day](/wiki/Commonwealth_Day) service that month and the [Royal Maundy](/wiki/Royal_Maundy) service in April, because of "episodic mobility problems". In May, she missed the [State Opening of Parliament](/wiki/State_Opening_of_Parliament) for the first time in 59 years. (She did not attend the state openings in 1959 and 1963 as she was pregnant with Andrew and Edward, respectively.) Later that month she made a surprise visit to [Paddington Station](/wiki/Paddington_Station) and officially opened the [Elizabeth line](/wiki/Elizabeth_line), named in her honour.

The Queen was largely confined to balcony appearances during the public jubilee celebrations, and she missed the [National Service of Thanksgiving](/wiki/Platinum_Jubilee_National_Service_of_Thanksgiving) on 3 June. On 13 June, she became the second-longest reigning monarch in history (among those whose exact dates of reign are known), with 70 years and 127 days on the throne – surpassing [King Bhumibol Adulyadej](/wiki/King_Bhumibol_Adulyadej) of Thailand. On 6 September, she appointed her 15th British prime minister, [Liz Truss](/wiki/Liz_Truss), at Balmoral Castle in Scotland. This was the only occasion on which Elizabeth received a new prime minister at a location other than Buckingham Palace. No other British monarch appointed as many prime ministers. The Queen's last public message was issued on 7 September, in which she expressed her sympathy for those affected by the [Saskatchewan stabbings](/wiki/Saskatchewan_stabbings).

Elizabeth did not plan to [abdicate](/wiki/Abdicate), though she took on fewer public engagements in her later years and Prince Charles performed more of her duties. She told Canadian governor-general [Adrienne Clarkson](/wiki/Adrienne_Clarkson) in a meeting in 2002 that she would never abdicate, saying, "It is not our tradition. Although, I suppose if I became completely gaga, one would have to do something." In June 2022, Elizabeth met the Archbishop of Canterbury, [Justin Welby](/wiki/Justin_Welby), who "came away thinking there is someone who has no fear of death, has hope in the future, knows the rock on which she stands and that gives her strength."

## Death

Main article: [Death and state funeral of Elizabeth II](/wiki/Death_and_state_funeral_of_Elizabeth_II)

On 8 September 2022, Buckingham Palace stated, "Following further evaluation this morning, the Queen's doctors are concerned for Her Majesty's health and have recommended she remain under medical supervision. The Queen remains comfortable and at Balmoral." Her [immediate family](/wiki/Immediate_family) rushed to Balmoral. She died peacefully at 3:10 pm, aged 96. Her death was announced to the public at 6:30 pm, setting in motion [Operation London Bridge](/wiki/Operation_London_Bridge) and, because she died in Scotland, [Operation Unicorn](/wiki/Operation_London_Bridge#Operation_Unicorn). Elizabeth was the first monarch to die in Scotland since [James V](/wiki/James_V) in 1542. Her [death certificate](/wiki/Death_certificate) recorded her cause of death as "[old age](/wiki/Old_age)". According to former prime minister [Boris Johnson](/wiki/Boris_Johnson) and the biographer [Gyles Brandreth](/wiki/Gyles_Brandreth), she was suffering from a form of [bone marrow cancer](/wiki/Bone_marrow_cancer), which Brandreth wrote was [multiple myeloma](/wiki/Multiple_myeloma).

On 12 September, Elizabeth's coffin was carried up the [Royal Mile](/wiki/Royal_Mile) in a procession to [St Giles' Cathedral](/wiki/St_Giles%27_Cathedral), where the [Crown of Scotland](/wiki/Crown_of_Scotland) was placed on it. Her coffin lay at rest at the cathedral for 24 hours, guarded by the [Royal Company of Archers](/wiki/Royal_Company_of_Archers), during which around 33,000 people filed past it. On 13 September, the coffin was flown to [RAF Northolt](/wiki/RAF_Northolt) in west London, before continuing its journey by road to Buckingham Palace. On 14 September, her coffin was taken in a military procession to [Westminster Hall](/wiki/Westminster_Hall), where Elizabeth's body [lay in state](/wiki/Lay_in_state) for four days. The coffin was guarded by members of both the [Sovereign's Bodyguard](/wiki/Sovereign%27s_Bodyguard) and the [Household Division](/wiki/Household_Division). An estimated 250,000 members of the public [filed past the coffin](/wiki/Queue_for_the_lying-in-state_of_Elizabeth_II), as did politicians and other public figures. On 16 September, Elizabeth's children held [a vigil](/wiki/Vigil_of_the_Princes#Queen_Elizabeth_II) around her coffin, and the next day her eight grandchildren did the same.

Elizabeth's coffin on the [State Gun Carriage of the Royal Navy](/wiki/State_Gun_Carriage_of_the_Royal_Navy), during the procession to [Wellington Arch](/wiki/Wellington_Arch)

Elizabeth's [state funeral](/wiki/State_funerals_in_the_United_Kingdom) was held at Westminster Abbey on 19 September, marking the first time a monarch's funeral service had been held there since [George II](/wiki/George_II_of_Great_Britain) in 1760. More than a million people lined the streets of [central London](/wiki/Central_London), and the day was declared a holiday in several Commonwealth countries. In Windsor, a final procession involving 1,000 military personnel took place and was witnessed by 97,000 people. Elizabeth's [fell pony](/wiki/Fell_pony) and two [royal corgis](/wiki/Royal_corgis) stood at the side of the procession. After a committal service at [St George's Chapel, Windsor Castle](/wiki/St_George%27s_Chapel,_Windsor_Castle), Elizabeth's body was interred with her husband Philip's in the [King George VI Memorial Chapel](/wiki/King_George_VI_Memorial_Chapel) later the same day, in a private ceremony attended by her closest family members.

## Public image

Main article: [Personality and image of Elizabeth II](/wiki/Personality_and_image_of_Elizabeth_II)

### Beliefs, activities, and interests

Patting a dog in New Zealand, 1974

Elizabeth rarely gave interviews, and little was known of her political opinions, which she did not express explicitly in public. It is against convention to ask or reveal the monarch's views. When *Times* journalist [Paul Routledge](/wiki/Paul_Routledge) asked her about the [miners' strike of 1984–85](/wiki/Miners%27_strike_of_1984%E2%80%9385) during a royal tour of the newspaper's offices, she replied that it was "all about one man" (a reference to [Arthur Scargill](/wiki/Arthur_Scargill)), with which Routledge disagreed. Routledge was widely criticised in the media for asking the question and claimed that he was unaware of the protocols. After the [2014 Scottish independence referendum](/wiki/2014_Scottish_independence_referendum), Prime Minister [David Cameron](/wiki/David_Cameron) was overheard saying that Elizabeth was pleased with the outcome. She had arguably issued a public coded statement about the referendum by telling one woman outside Balmoral Kirk that she hoped people would think "very carefully" about the outcome. It emerged later that Cameron had specifically requested that she register her concern.

Elizabeth had a deep sense of religious and [civic duty](/wiki/Civic_duty), and took her [Coronation Oath](/wiki/Coronation_Oath) seriously. Aside from her [official religious role](/wiki/Monarchy_of_the_United_Kingdom#Religious_role) as [supreme governor](/wiki/Supreme_governor) of the [established](/wiki/State_religion) Church of England, she worshipped with that church and with the national [Church of Scotland](/wiki/Church_of_Scotland). She demonstrated support for [inter-faith](/wiki/Inter-faith) relations and met with leaders of other churches and religions, including five popes: [Pius XII](/wiki/Pius_XII), [John XXIII](/wiki/John_XXIII), [John Paul II](/wiki/John_Paul_II), [Benedict XVI](/wiki/Benedict_XVI) and [Francis](/wiki/Pope_Francis). A personal note about her faith often featured in her annual [Christmas Message](/wiki/Christmas_Message) broadcast to the Commonwealth. In 2000, she said:

> To many of us, our beliefs are of fundamental importance. For me the teachings of Christ and my own personal accountability before God provide a framework in which I try to lead my life. I, like so many of you, have drawn great comfort in difficult times from Christ's words and example.

Elizabeth was [patron](/wiki/Patron) of more than 600 organisations and charities. The [Charities Aid Foundation](/wiki/Charities_Aid_Foundation) estimated that Elizabeth helped raise over £1.4 billion for her patronages during her reign. Her main leisure interests included [equestrianism](/wiki/Horses_of_Elizabeth_II) and dogs, especially her [Pembroke Welsh Corgis](/wiki/Pembroke_Welsh_Corgi). Her lifelong love of corgis began in 1933 with [Dookie](/wiki/Dookie_(dog)), the first of many royal corgis. Scenes of a relaxed, informal home life were occasionally witnessed; she and her family, from time to time, prepared a meal together and washed the dishes afterwards.

### Media depiction and public opinion

In the 1950s, as a young woman at the start of her reign, Elizabeth was depicted as a glamorous "fairytale Queen". After the trauma of the Second World War, it was a time of hope, a period of progress and achievement heralding a "new [Elizabethan age](/wiki/Elizabethan_age)". Lord Altrincham's accusation in 1957 that her speeches sounded like those of a "[priggish](/wiki/Prig) schoolgirl" was an extremely rare criticism. In the late 1960s, attempts to portray a more modern image of the monarchy were made in the television documentary *[Royal Family](/wiki/Royal_Family_(film))* and by televising [Prince Charles's investiture as Prince of Wales](/wiki/Prince_Charles%27s_investiture_as_Prince_of_Wales). Elizabeth also instituted other new practices; her first royal walkabout, meeting ordinary members of the public, took place during a tour of Australia and New Zealand in 1970. Her wardrobe developed a recognisable, signature style driven more by function than fashion. In public, she took to wearing mostly solid-colour [overcoats](/wiki/Overcoat) and decorative hats, allowing her to be seen easily in a crowd. By the end of her reign, nearly one third of Britons had seen or met Elizabeth in person.

At Elizabeth's Silver Jubilee in 1977, the crowds and celebrations were genuinely enthusiastic; but, in the 1980s, public criticism of the royal family increased, as the personal and working lives of Elizabeth's children came under media scrutiny. Her popularity sank to a low point in the 1990s. Under pressure from public opinion, she began to pay income tax for the first time, and Buckingham Palace was opened to the public. Although support for [republicanism in Britain](/wiki/Republicanism_in_Britain) seemed higher than at any time in living memory, republican ideology was still a minority viewpoint, and Elizabeth herself had high approval ratings. Criticism was focused on the institution of the monarchy itself, and the conduct of Elizabeth's wider family, rather than her own behaviour and actions. Discontent with the monarchy reached its peak on the death of Diana, Princess of Wales, although Elizabeth's personal popularity – as well as general support for the monarchy – rebounded after her live television broadcast to the world five days after Diana's death.

In [Brisbane](/wiki/Brisbane), Australia, 1982

In November 1999, [a referendum in Australia](/wiki/1999_Australian_republic_referendum) on the future of the [Australian monarchy](/wiki/Australian_monarchy) favoured its retention in preference to an indirectly elected head of state. Many republicans credited Elizabeth's personal popularity with the survival of the monarchy in Australia. In 2010, Prime Minister [Julia Gillard](/wiki/Julia_Gillard) noted that there was a "deep affection" for Elizabeth in Australia and that another referendum on the monarchy should wait until after her reign. Gillard's successor, [Malcolm Turnbull](/wiki/Malcolm_Turnbull), who led the republican campaign in 1999, similarly believed that Australians would not vote to become a republic in her lifetime. "She's been an extraordinary head of state", Turnbull said in 2021, "and I think frankly, in Australia, there are more Elizabethans than there are monarchists." Similarly, referendums in both [Tuvalu in 2008](/wiki/2008_Tuvaluan_constitutional_referendum) and [Saint Vincent and the Grenadines in 2009](/wiki/2009_Vincentian_constitutional_referendum) saw voters reject proposals to become republics.

Polls in Britain in 2006 and 2007 revealed strong support for the monarchy, and in 2012, Elizabeth's Diamond Jubilee year, her approval ratings hit 90 per cent. Her family came under scrutiny again in the last few years of her life due to her son Andrew's association with convicted sex offenders [Jeffrey Epstein](/wiki/Jeffrey_Epstein) and [Ghislaine Maxwell](/wiki/Ghislaine_Maxwell), [his lawsuit](/wiki/Giuffre_v._Prince_Andrew) with [Virginia Giuffre](/wiki/Virginia_Giuffre) amidst accusations of sexual impropriety, and her grandson Harry and his wife [Meghan](/wiki/Meghan,_Duchess_of_Sussex)'s [exit from the working royal family](/wiki/Megxit) and subsequent move to the United States. Polling in Great Britain during the Platinum Jubilee, however, showed support for maintaining the monarchy and Elizabeth's personal popularity remained strong. As of 2021 she remained the third [most admired woman](/wiki/Most_admired_woman) in the world according to the annual [Gallup poll](/wiki/Gallup_poll), her 52 appearances on the list meaning she had been in the top ten more than any other woman in the poll's history.

Elizabeth was portrayed in a variety of media by many notable artists, including painters [Pietro Annigoni](/wiki/Pietro_Annigoni), [Peter Blake](/wiki/Peter_Blake_(artist)), [Chinwe Chukwuogo-Roy](/wiki/Chinwe_Chukwuogo-Roy), [Terence Cuneo](/wiki/Terence_Cuneo), [Lucian Freud](/wiki/Lucian_Freud), [Rolf Harris](/wiki/Rolf_Harris), [Damien Hirst](/wiki/Damien_Hirst), [Juliet Pannett](/wiki/Juliet_Pannett) and [Tai-Shan Schierenberg](/wiki/Tai-Shan_Schierenberg). Notable photographers of Elizabeth included [Cecil Beaton](/wiki/Cecil_Beaton), [Yousuf Karsh](/wiki/Yousuf_Karsh), [Anwar Hussein](/wiki/Anwar_Hussein_(photographer)), [Annie Leibovitz](/wiki/Annie_Leibovitz), [Lord Lichfield](/wiki/Patrick_Anson,_5th_Earl_of_Lichfield), [Terry O'Neill](/wiki/Terry_O%27Neill_(photographer)), [John Swannell](/wiki/John_Swannell_(photographer)) and [Dorothy Wilding](/wiki/Dorothy_Wilding). The first official [portrait photograph](/wiki/Portrait_photograph) of Elizabeth was taken by [Marcus Adams](/wiki/Marcus_Adams_(photographer)) in 1926.

Personal flag of Elizabeth II

### Titles and styles

Elizabeth held many titles and honorary military positions throughout [the Commonwealth](/wiki/The_Commonwealth), was sovereign of many orders in her own countries and received honours and awards from around the world. In each of her realms, she had a distinct title that follows a similar formula: *Queen of Saint Lucia and of Her other Realms and Territories* in [Saint Lucia](/wiki/Monarchy_of_Saint_Lucia), *Queen of Australia and Her other Realms and Territories* in Australia, etc. She was also styled *[Defender of the Faith](/wiki/Defender_of_the_Faith)*.

### Arms

See also: [Flags of Elizabeth II](/wiki/Flags_of_Elizabeth_II)

From 21 April 1944 until her accession, Elizabeth's arms consisted of a [lozenge](/wiki/Lozenge_(heraldry)) bearing the [royal coat of arms of the United Kingdom](/wiki/Royal_coat_of_arms_of_the_United_Kingdom) differenced with a [label](/wiki/Label_(heraldry)) of three points [argent](/wiki/Argent), the centre point bearing a [Tudor rose](/wiki/Tudor_rose) and the first and third a [cross of Saint George](/wiki/Cross_of_Saint_George). Upon her accession, she inherited the various arms her father held as sovereign, with a subsequently modified representation of the crown. Elizabeth also possessed [royal standards](/wiki/Heraldic_banner) and personal flags for use [in the United Kingdom](/wiki/Royal_Standard_of_the_United_Kingdom), [Canada](/wiki/Royal_standards_of_Canada), [Australia](/wiki/Queen%27s_Personal_Australian_Flag), [New Zealand](/wiki/Queen%27s_Personal_New_Zealand_Flag), [Jamaica](/wiki/Queen%27s_Personal_Jamaican_Flag), and elsewhere. Elizabeth approved her modified British arms on 26 May 1954.
