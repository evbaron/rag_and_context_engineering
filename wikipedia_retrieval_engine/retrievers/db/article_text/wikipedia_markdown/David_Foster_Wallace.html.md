**David Foster Wallace** (February 21, 1962 – September 12, 2008) was an American writer and professor who published novels, short stories, and essays. He is best known for his 1996 novel *[Infinite Jest](/wiki/Infinite_Jest)*, which *[Time](/wiki/Time_(magazine))* magazine named one of the 100 best English-language novels published from 1923 to 2005. In 2008, David Ulin wrote for the *[Los Angeles Times](/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)* that Wallace was "one of the most influential and innovative writers of the last twenty years".

Wallace grew up in Illinois. He graduated from [Amherst College](/wiki/Amherst_College) and the [University of Arizona](/wiki/University_of_Arizona). His honors thesis at Amherst was adapted into his debut novel *[The Broom of the System](/wiki/The_Broom_of_the_System)* (1987). In his writing, Wallace intentionally avoided [tropes](/wiki/Trope_(literature)) of [postmodern art](/wiki/Postmodern_art) such as [irony](/wiki/Irony) or forms of [metafiction](/wiki/Metafiction), saying in 1990 that they were "agents of a great despair and stasis" in contemporary American culture. *Infinite Jest*, his second novel, is known for its unconventional [narrative structure](/wiki/Narrative_structure) and extensive use of [endnotes](/wiki/Endnotes).

Wallace published three short story collections: *[Girl with Curious Hair](/wiki/Girl_with_Curious_Hair)* (1989), *[Brief Interviews with Hideous Men](/wiki/Brief_Interviews_with_Hideous_Men)* (1999) and *[Oblivion: Stories](/wiki/Oblivion:_Stories)* (2004). His short stories and essays were published in outlets like *[The New Yorker](/wiki/The_New_Yorker)* and *[Rolling Stone](/wiki/Rolling_Stone)* magazines, and three collections of his essays were published as books: *[A Supposedly Fun Thing I'll Never Do Again](/wiki/A_Supposedly_Fun_Thing_I%27ll_Never_Do_Again)* (1997), *[Consider the Lobster](/wiki/Consider_the_Lobster)* (2005) and *[Both Flesh and Not](/wiki/Both_Flesh_and_Not)* (2012). Wallace also taught English and [creative writing](/wiki/Creative_writing) at [Emerson College](/wiki/Emerson_College), [Illinois State University](/wiki/Illinois_State_University), and [Pomona College](/wiki/Pomona_College).

In 2008, after struggling with depression for many years, Wallace died by suicide at age 46. His unfinished novel *[The Pale King](/wiki/The_Pale_King)* was published in 2011 and was a finalist for the 2012 [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction).

## Early life and education

David Foster Wallace was born in [Ithaca, New York](/wiki/Ithaca,_New_York), to Sally Jean Wallace ([née](/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names) Foster) and [James Donald Wallace](/wiki/James_D._Wallace). The family moved to [Champaign–Urbana, Illinois](/wiki/Champaign%E2%80%93Urbana), where he was raised along with his younger sister, Amy Wallace-Havens. His father was a philosophy professor at the [University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign](/wiki/University_of_Illinois_Urbana-Champaign). His mother was an English professor at [Parkland College](/wiki/Parkland_College_(United_States)), a community college in Champaign, which recognized her work with a "Professor of the Year" award in 1996. From fourth grade, Wallace lived with his family in [Urbana](/wiki/Urbana,_Illinois), where he attended Yankee Ridge Elementary School, Brookens Junior High School and [Urbana High School](/wiki/Urbana_High_School_(Illinois)).

As an adolescent, Wallace was a regionally ranked junior [tennis](/wiki/Tennis) player. He wrote about this period in the essay "Derivative Sport in Tornado Alley", originally published in *[Harper's Magazine](/wiki/Harper%27s_Magazine)* as "Tennis, Trigonometry, Tornadoes". Although his parents were [atheists](/wiki/Atheism), Wallace twice attempted to join the [Catholic Church](/wiki/Catholic_Church), but "flunk[ed] the period of inquiry". He later attended a [Mennonite](/wiki/Mennonites) church.

Wallace attended [Amherst College](/wiki/Amherst_College), his father's alma mater, where he majored in English and philosophy and graduated *[summa cum laude](/wiki/Latin_honors#Distinctions)* in 1985. Among other extracurricular activities, he participated in [glee club](/wiki/Glee_club); his sister recalls that he "had a lovely singing voice". In studying philosophy, Wallace pursued [modal logic](/wiki/Modal_logic) and mathematics, and presented in 1985 a senior thesis in philosophy and modal logic that was awarded the Gail Kennedy Memorial Prize and posthumously published as *Fate, Time, and Language: An Essay on Free Will* (2010).

Wallace adapted his honors thesis in English as the manuscript of his first novel, *[The Broom of the System](/wiki/The_Broom_of_the_System)* (1987), and committed to being a writer. He told [David Lipsky](/wiki/David_Lipsky_(author)): "Writing *The Broom of the System*, I felt like I was using 97 percent of me, whereas philosophy was using 50 percent." Wallace completed a [Master of Fine Arts](/wiki/Master_of_Fine_Arts) degree in creative writing at the [University of Arizona](/wiki/University_of_Arizona) in 1987. He moved back to Massachusetts to attend graduate school in philosophy at [Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_University), but soon left the program.

## Work

### Career

*[The Broom of the System](/wiki/The_Broom_of_the_System)* (1987) garnered national attention and critical praise. In *The New York Times*, [Caryn James](/wiki/Caryn_James) called it a "manic, human, flawed extravaganza ... emerging straight from the excessive tradition of [Stanley Elkin](/wiki/Stanley_Elkin)'s *The Franchiser*, [Thomas Pynchon](/wiki/Thomas_Pynchon)'s *[V.](/wiki/V.)*, [and] [John Irving](/wiki/John_Irving)'s *[World According to Garp](/wiki/The_World_According_to_Garp)*".

Autographed title page of *[Infinite Jest](/wiki/Infinite_Jest)*

In 1991, Wallace began teaching literature as an adjunct professor at [Emerson College](/wiki/Emerson_College) in Boston. The next year, at the suggestion of colleague and supporter [Steven Moore](/wiki/Steven_Moore_(author)), Wallace obtained a position in the [English](/wiki/English_studies) department at [Illinois State University](/wiki/Illinois_State_University). He had begun work on his second novel, *[Infinite Jest](/wiki/Infinite_Jest)*, in 1991, and submitted a draft to his editor in December 1993. After the publication of excerpts throughout 1995, the book was published in 1996.

In 1997, Wallace received a [MacArthur Fellowship](/wiki/MacArthur_Fellows_Program). He also received the [Aga Khan Prize for Fiction](/wiki/Aga_Khan_Prize_for_Fiction), awarded by editors of *[The Paris Review](/wiki/The_Paris_Review)* for one of the stories in *[Brief Interviews with Hideous Men](/wiki/Brief_Interviews_with_Hideous_Men)*, which had been published in the magazine.

In 2002, Wallace moved to [Claremont, California](/wiki/Claremont,_California), to become the first [Roy E. Disney](/wiki/Roy_E._Disney)-endowed Professor of Creative Writing and Professor of English at [Pomona College](/wiki/Pomona_College). He taught one or two undergraduate courses per semester and focused on writing.

Wallace delivered the commencement address to the 2005 graduating class at [Kenyon College](/wiki/Kenyon_College). The speech was published as a book, *[This Is Water](/wiki/This_Is_Water)*, in 2009. In May 2013, parts of the speech were used in a popular online video, also titled "This Is Water".

Bonnie Nadell was Wallace's literary agent during his entire career. Michael Pietsch was his editor on *Infinite Jest*.

Wallace died in 2008. In March 2009, [Little, Brown and Company](/wiki/Little,_Brown_and_Company) announced that it would publish the manuscript of an unfinished novel, *[The Pale King](/wiki/The_Pale_King)*, that Wallace had been working on before his death. Pietsch pieced the novel together from pages and notes Wallace left behind. [Several excerpts](/wiki/The_Pale_King#Published_excerpts) were published in *[The New Yorker](/wiki/The_New_Yorker)* and other magazines. *The Pale King* was published on April 15, 2011, and received generally positive reviews. Michiko Kakutani of *The New York Times* wrote that *The Pale King* "showcases [Wallace's] embrace of discontinuity; his fascination with both the meta and the microscopic, postmodern pyrotechnics and old-fashioned storytelling; and his ongoing interest in contemporary America's obsession with self-gratification and entertainment." The book was nominated for the Pulitzer Prize.

Throughout his career, Wallace published short fiction in periodicals such as *The New Yorker*, *[GQ](/wiki/GQ)*, *[Harper's Magazine](/wiki/Harper%27s_Magazine)*, *[Playboy](/wiki/Playboy)*, *The Paris Review*, *[Mid-American Review](/wiki/Mid-American_Review)*, *[Conjunctions](/wiki/Conjunctions_(journal))*, *[Esquire](/wiki/Esquire_(magazine))*, *[Open City](/wiki/Open_City_(magazine))*, *[Puerto del Sol](/wiki/Puerto_del_Sol)*, and *[Timothy McSweeney's Quarterly Concern](/wiki/Timothy_McSweeney%27s_Quarterly_Concern)*.

### Themes and styles

Wallace wanted to progress beyond the [irony](/wiki/Irony) and [metafiction](/wiki/Metafiction) associated with [postmodernism](/wiki/Postmodernism) and explore a [post-postmodern](/wiki/Post-postmodernism) or [metamodern](/wiki/Metamodernism) style. In the essay "E Unibus Pluram: Television and U.S. Fiction" (written 1990, published 1993), he proposed that television has an ironic influence on fiction, and urged literary authors to eschew TV's shallow rebelliousness:

> I want to convince you that irony, poker-faced silence, and fear of ridicule are distinctive of those features of contemporary U.S. culture (of which cutting-edge fiction is a part) that enjoy any significant relation to the television whose weird, pretty hand has my generation by the throat. I'm going to argue that irony and ridicule are entertaining and effective, and that, at the same time, they are agents of a great despair and stasis in U.S. culture, and that, for aspiring fictionists, they pose terrifically vexing problems.

Wallace used many forms of irony, but tended to focus on individual persons' continued longing for earnest, unself-conscious experience and communication in a media-saturated society.

Wallace's fiction combines narrative modes and authorial voices that incorporate jargon and invented vocabulary, such as self-generated abbreviations and acronyms, long, multi-[clause](/wiki/Clause) sentences, and an extensive use of explanatory [endnotes](/wiki/Note_(typography)) and footnotes, as in *Infinite Jest* and the story "Octet" (collected in *[Brief Interviews with Hideous Men](/wiki/Brief_Interviews_with_Hideous_Men)*), and most of his non-fiction after 1996. In a 1997 interview on *[Charlie Rose](/wiki/Charlie_Rose_(talk_show))*, Wallace said that the notes were to disrupt the linear narrative, to reflect his perception of reality without jumbling the narrative structure, and that he could have jumbled the sentences "but then no one would read it". Much of Wallace's writing contains [philosophical](/wiki/Philosophical_fiction) and [mathematical](/wiki/Mathematical_fiction) ideas and references.

D. T. Max has described Wallace's work as an "unusual mixture of the cerebral and the hot-blooded", often featuring multiple protagonists and spanning different locations in a single work. His writing comments on the fragmentation of thought, the relationship between happiness and boredom, and the psychological tension between the beauty and hideousness of the human body. According to Wallace, "fiction's about what it is to be a fucking human being", and he said he wanted to write "morally passionate, passionately moral fiction" that could help the reader "become less alone inside". In his Kenyon College commencement address (later published as *[This Is Water](/wiki/This_Is_Water)*), Wallace described the human condition as daily crises and chronic disillusionment and warned against succumbing to [solipsism](/wiki/Solipsism), invoking the existential values of compassion and mindfulness:

> The really important kind of freedom involves attention, and awareness, and discipline, and effort, and being able truly to care about other people and to sacrifice for them, over and over, in myriad petty little unsexy ways, every day. ... The only thing that's capital-T True is that you get to decide how you're going to try to see it. You get to consciously decide what has meaning and what doesn't. ... The trick is keeping the truth up-front in daily consciousness.

### Nonfiction

Wallace covered Senator [John McCain](/wiki/John_McCain)'s [2000 presidential campaign](/wiki/John_McCain_2000_presidential_campaign) and the [September 11 attacks](/wiki/September_11_attacks) for *[Rolling Stone](/wiki/Rolling_Stone)*; cruise ships (in what became the title essay of his first nonfiction book), [state fairs](/wiki/State_fair), and [tornadoes](/wiki/Tornado) for *[Harper's Magazine](/wiki/Harper%27s_Magazine)*; the [US Open](/wiki/US_Open_(tennis)) tournament for *[Tennis](/wiki/Tennis_(magazine))* magazine; [Roger Federer](/wiki/Roger_Federer) for *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*; the director [David Lynch](/wiki/David_Lynch) and the [pornography industry](/wiki/Sex_industry) for *[Première](/wiki/Premi%C3%A8re_(magazine))* magazine; the [tennis](/wiki/Tennis) player [Michael Joyce](/wiki/Michael_Joyce_(tennis)) for *[Esquire](/wiki/Esquire_(magazine))*; the movie-special-effects industry for *[Waterstone's](/wiki/Waterstones)* magazine; conservative talk radio host [John Ziegler](/wiki/John_Ziegler_(talk_show_host)) for *[The Atlantic](/wiki/The_Atlantic)*; and a [Maine](/wiki/Maine) lobster festival for *[Gourmet](/wiki/Gourmet_(magazine))* magazine. He also reviewed books in several genres for the *Los Angeles Times*, *[The Washington Post](/wiki/The_Washington_Post)*, *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*, and *[The Philadelphia Inquirer](/wiki/The_Philadelphia_Inquirer)*. In the November 2007 issue of *The Atlantic*, which commemorated the magazine's 150th anniversary, Wallace was among the authors, artists, politicians and others who wrote short pieces on "the future of the American idea".

These and other essays appear in three collections, *[A Supposedly Fun Thing I'll Never Do Again](/wiki/A_Supposedly_Fun_Thing_I%27ll_Never_Do_Again)*, *[Consider the Lobster](/wiki/Consider_the_Lobster)* and the posthumous *[Both Flesh and Not](/wiki/Both_Flesh_and_Not)*, the last of which contains some of Wallace's earliest work, including his first published essay, "Fictional Futures and the Conspicuously Young". Wallace's tennis writing was compiled into a volume titled *String Theory: David Foster Wallace on Tennis*, published in 2016.

Some writers have found parts of Wallace's nonfiction implausible. [Jonathan Franzen](/wiki/Jonathan_Franzen) has said that he believes Wallace made up dialogue and incidents: "those things didn't actually happen". Of the essays "Shipping Out" and "Ticket to the Fair", John Cook has remarked that in Wallace's nonfiction:

> Wallace encounters pitch-perfect characters who speak comedically crystalline lines and place him in hilariously absurd situations...I used both stories [when teaching journalism] as examples of the inescapable temptation to shave, embellish, and invent narratives.

## Personal life

Wallace struggled with depression, alcoholism, drug addiction, and [suicidal ideation](/wiki/Suicidal_ideation), and was repeatedly hospitalized for [psychiatric care](/wiki/Psychiatric_care). In 2008, his father said that Wallace had suffered from [major depressive disorder](/wiki/Major_depressive_disorder) for more than 20 years, and that [antidepressant](/wiki/Antidepressant) medication had allowed him to be productive. In 1989, Wallace spent four weeks at [McLean Hospital](/wiki/McLean_Hospital), a [psychiatric institute](/wiki/Psychiatric_institution) in [Belmont, Massachusetts](/wiki/Belmont,_Massachusetts), affiliated with [Harvard Medical School](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School), where he completed a drug and alcohol [detoxification program](/wiki/Drug_detoxification); he later said his time there changed his life.

In the early 1990s, Wallace dated writer [Mary Karr](/wiki/Mary_Karr). In 2012, Karr described his earlier behavior toward her as obsessive and volatile, alleging that he once threw a coffee table at her and once physically forced her out of a car, leaving her to walk home. In 2018, Karr said that the account of Wallace's alleged abuse in D.T. Max's biography of him was "about 2% of what happened"; she claimed that Wallace had kicked her, climbed up the side of her house at night, followed her five-year-old son home from school, and attempted to buy a gun to kill her ex-husband. In 2015, Karr said that Wallace was violent toward other women he dated, and in 2018, she said that several women, including former students of Wallace, contacted her to share their alleged physical and emotional abuse by Wallace.

In 2002, Wallace met painter [Karen L. Green](/wiki/Karen_Green_(artist)). They married on December 27, 2004. At the time of Wallace's death, they lived in a house in [Claremont, California](/wiki/Claremont,_California).

Dogs were important to Wallace, and he spoke of opening a shelter for stray canines. According to his friend [Jonathan Franzen](/wiki/Jonathan_Franzen), Wallace "had a predilection for dogs who'd been abused, and [were] unlikely to find other owners who were going to be patient enough for them".

In June 2007, Wallace suffered a physical illness involving severe stomach pains. His doctors believed it was caused by an [interaction](/wiki/Drug_interaction) of one of his medications with food he had eaten at a restaurant. On his doctor's advice, and out of concern that the drug might be interfering with his writing, Wallace stopped taking [Nardil](/wiki/Nardil) ([phenelzine](/wiki/Phenelzine)), his primary antidepressant. His depression recurred, and he tried other treatments, including [electroconvulsive therapy](/wiki/Electroconvulsive_therapy). He eventually went back on phenelzine, but found it ineffective, leaving him with major depressive symptoms.

## Death

On September 12, 2008, at age 46, Wallace wrote a [suicide note](/wiki/Suicide_note) to his wife, arranged part of the manuscript for *[The Pale King](/wiki/The_Pale_King)*, and hanged himself on the back porch of his house. Memorial gatherings were held at Pomona College, Amherst College, the University of Arizona, Illinois State University, and, on October 23, 2008, at New York University (NYU). The [eulogists](/wiki/Eulogy) at NYU included his sister, Amy Wallace-Havens; his literary agent, Bonnie Nadell; [Gerry Howard](/wiki/Gerry_Howard), editor of his first two books; [Colin Harrison](/wiki/Colin_Harrison_(writer)), an editor at *Harper's Magazine*; Michael Pietsch, editor of *Infinite Jest* and later works; Deborah Treisman, fiction editor at *The New Yorker* magazine; and the writers [Don DeLillo](/wiki/Don_DeLillo), [Zadie Smith](/wiki/Zadie_Smith), [George Saunders](/wiki/George_Saunders), Mark Costello, [Donald Antrim](/wiki/Donald_Antrim), and [Jonathan Franzen](/wiki/Jonathan_Franzen).

## Legacy

In March 2010, it was announced that Wallace's personal papers and archives—drafts of books, stories, essays, poems, letters, and research, including the handwritten notes for *[Infinite Jest](/wiki/Infinite_Jest)*—had been purchased by the [University of Texas at Austin](/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin). They are held at that university's [Harry Ransom Center](/wiki/Harry_Ransom_Center).

Since 2011, [Loyola University New Orleans](/wiki/Loyola_University_New_Orleans) has offered English seminar courses on Wallace. Similar courses have also been taught at [Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_University). The first David Foster Wallace Conference was hosted by the [Illinois State University](/wiki/Illinois_State_University) Department of English in May 2014; the second was held in May 2015.

In January 2017, the International David Foster Wallace Society and the *Journal of David Foster Wallace Studies* were launched.

Among the writers who have cited Wallace as an influence are [Dave Eggers](/wiki/Dave_Eggers), [Jonathan Franzen](/wiki/Jonathan_Franzen), [Rivka Galchen](/wiki/Rivka_Galchen), [Matthew Gallaway](/wiki/Matthew_Gallaway), [David Gordon](/wiki/David_Gordon_(novelist)), [John Green](/wiki/John_Green), [Porochista Khakpour](/wiki/Porochista_Khakpour), [George Saunders](/wiki/George_Saunders), [Michael Schur](/wiki/Michael_Schur), [Zadie Smith](/wiki/Zadie_Smith), [Darin Strauss](/wiki/Darin_Strauss), [Deb Olin Unferth](/wiki/Deb_Olin_Unferth), [Elizabeth Wurtzel](/wiki/Elizabeth_Wurtzel), and [Charles Yu](/wiki/Charles_Yu).

### Adaptations

#### Film and television

A feature-length film adaptation of *[Brief Interviews with Hideous Men](/wiki/Brief_Interviews_with_Hideous_Men_(film))*, directed by [John Krasinski](/wiki/John_Krasinski) with an ensemble cast, was released in 2009 and premiered at the [Sundance Film Festival](/wiki/Sundance_Film_Festival).

The 19th episode of the 23rd season of *[The Simpsons](/wiki/The_Simpsons)*, "[A Totally Fun Thing Bart Will Never Do Again](/wiki/A_Totally_Fun_Thing_Bart_Will_Never_Do_Again)" (2012), is loosely based on Wallace's essay "Shipping Out" from his 1997 collection, *[A Supposedly Fun Thing I'll Never Do Again](/wiki/A_Supposedly_Fun_Thing_I%27ll_Never_Do_Again)*. The Simpson family takes a cruise, and Wallace appears in the background of a scene, wearing a tuxedo T-shirt while eating in the ship's dining room.

The 2015 film *[The End of the Tour](/wiki/The_End_of_the_Tour)* is based on conversations [David Lipsky](/wiki/David_Lipsky_(author)) had with Wallace, transcribed in *[Although of Course You End Up Becoming Yourself](/wiki/Although_of_Course_You_End_Up_Becoming_Yourself)* (2010). [Jason Segel](/wiki/Jason_Segel) played Wallace, and [Jesse Eisenberg](/wiki/Jesse_Eisenberg) played Lipsky. The film won an Audience Award for Best Narrative Feature at the [Sarasota Film Festival](/wiki/Sarasota_Film_Festival), and Segel was nominated for the [Independent Spirit Award for Best Male Lead](/wiki/Independent_Spirit_Award_for_Best_Male_Lead).

"Partridge", a Season 5 episode of [NBC](/wiki/NBC)'s *[Parks and Recreation](/wiki/Parks_and_Recreation)*, repeatedly references *Infinite Jest*, of which the show's co-creator, [Michael Schur](/wiki/Michael_Schur), is a noted fan. Schur also directed the music video for [The Decemberists](/wiki/The_Decemberists)' "Calamity Song", which depicts the Eschaton game from *Infinite Jest.*

#### Stage and music adaptations

Twelve of the interviews from *Brief Interviews with Hideous Men* were adapted as a stage play in 2000 by Dylan McCullough. This was the first theatrical adaptation of Wallace's work. The play, *Hideous Men*, was also directed by McCullough, and premiered at the [New York International Fringe Festival](/wiki/New_York_International_Fringe_Festival) in August 2000.

*Brief Interviews* was also adapted by director Marc Caellas as a play, *Brief Interviews with Hideous Writers*, which premiered at Fundación Tomás Eloy Martinez in [Buenos Aires](/wiki/Buenos_Aires) on November 4, 2011. In 2012 it was adapted as a play by artist [Andy Holden](/wiki/Andy_Holden_(artist)) for a two-night run at the [ICA](/wiki/Institute_of_Contemporary_Arts) in London.

The short story "Tri-Stan: I Sold Sissee Nar to Ecko", from *Brief Interviews with Hideous Men*, was adapted by composer [Eric Moe](/wiki/Eric_Moe_(composer)) into a 50-minute operatic piece, to be performed with accompanying video projections. The piece was described as having "subversively inscribed classical music into pop culture".

*Infinite Jest* was performed once as a stage play by Germany's experimental theater [Hebbel am Ufer](/wiki/Hebbel_am_Ufer). The play was staged in various locations throughout [Berlin](/wiki/Berlin), and the action took place over a 24-hour period.

"[Good Old Neon](/wiki/Good_Old_Neon)", from *[Oblivion: Stories](/wiki/Oblivion:_Stories)*, was adapted and performed by Ian Forester at the 2011 [Hollywood Fringe Festival](/wiki/Hollywood_Fringe_Festival), produced by the Los Angeles independent theater company Needtheater.

The song "Surrounded by Heads and Bodies", from the album *[A Brief Inquiry into Online Relationships](/wiki/A_Brief_Inquiry_into_Online_Relationships)* by [The 1975](/wiki/The_1975), borrows its title from the opening line of *Infinite Jest*. [Matty Healy](/wiki/Matty_Healy), The 1975's lead singer, said in an interview with *[Pitchfork](/wiki/Pitchfork_(website))* that he was inspired by the novel after reading it during a stint in rehabilitation:

> I was reading [*Infinite Jest*] when I was in rehab. There was no one there. It was me and my nurses, who'd come in and check on me, and then Angela [the protagonist of the song], miles away. I was surrounded by no one, and the book was just open on the front page, as most copies of *Infinite Jest* are ... nobody reads [*Infinite Jest*] all the way! Everyone our age has got a battered, quarter-read copy of *Infinite Jest*.
