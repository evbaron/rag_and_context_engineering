**Thailand**, officially the **Kingdom of Thailand**, and formerly known as **Siam** until 1939, is a country located in [Mainland Southeast Asia](/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia). It shares land borders with [Myanmar](/wiki/Myanmar) to the west and northwest, [Laos](/wiki/Laos) to the east and northeast, [Cambodia](/wiki/Cambodia) to the southeast, and [Malaysia](/wiki/Malaysia) to the south. Its maritime boundaries include the [Gulf of Thailand](/wiki/Gulf_of_Thailand) and the [Andaman Sea](/wiki/Andaman_Sea), as well as maritime borders with [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam), [Indonesia](/wiki/Indonesia), and [India](/wiki/India). Thailand has a population of nearly 66 million people and covers an area of approximately 513,115 km2 (198,115 sq mi). The country's [capital](/wiki/Capital_city) and [largest city](/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_Thailand#Largest_cities_by_urban_population) is [Bangkok](/wiki/Bangkok).

Archaeological evidence indicates that humans have inhabited the area of present-day Thailand for at least 40,000 years. Indigenous ethnic groups include the [Mon](/wiki/Mon_people), [Khmer](/wiki/Khmer_people), and [Malay](/wiki/Malay_people) people. The [Tai people](/wiki/Tai_people) are believed to have originated from the [Điện Biên Phủ](/wiki/%C4%90i%E1%BB%87n_Bi%C3%AAn_Ph%E1%BB%A7) region since the 5th century and began migrating into the territory of modern Thailand between the 8th and 10th centuries. During the classical historical period, major kingdoms such as [Sukhothai](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom), [Lan Na](/wiki/Lan_Na), and [Ayutthaya](/wiki/Ayutthaya_Kingdom) were established. The Sukhothai Kingdom is regarded as the beginning of [Thai history](/wiki/Thai_history), while the Ayutthaya Kingdom, founded in 1350 CE, became a [regional power](/wiki/Regional_power) replacing the [Khmer Empire](/wiki/Khmer_Empire). European contact began in 1511 CE when [Portuguese](/wiki/Portuguese_Empire) envoys arrived in Ayutthaya. The Ayutthaya Kingdom flourished until its complete destruction during the [1765–1767 Burmese–Siamese War](/wiki/Burmese%E2%80%93Siamese_War_(1765%E2%80%931767)) by the [Burmese forces](/wiki/Royal_Burmese_Armed_Forces) under the [Konbaung dynasty](/wiki/Konbaung_dynasty) in 1767.

After the fall of Ayutthaya, [King Taksin](/wiki/King_Taksin) reunified the kingdom and established the [Thonburi Kingdom](/wiki/Thonburi_Kingdom), which lasted only 15 years before he was overthrown by [Phutthayotfa Chulalok](/wiki/Rama_I) (Rama I), the founder of the [Chakri dynasty](/wiki/Chakri_dynasty). King Rama I established the [Rattanakosin Kingdom](/wiki/Rattanakosin_Kingdom) and moved the capital to Bangkok in 1782. During the era of [Western imperialism](/wiki/Western_imperialism_in_Asia), Siam remained the only country in Southeast Asia to avoid colonisation by European powers, although it ceded territory, trade rights, and legal privileges through several [unequal treaties](/wiki/Unequal_treaties). The governance system evolved into an [absolute monarchy](/wiki/Absolute_monarchy) centralised under the rule of [King Chulalongkorn](/wiki/King_Chulalongkorn) (Rama V). Siam [adapted to international relations](/wiki/Chulalongkorn%27s_first_visit_to_Europe) during the imperialist era and joined [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I) on the side of the [Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I), a political decision aimed at revising the effects of unequal treaties and enhancing Siam's international status.

Following the [Siamese revolution of 1932](/wiki/Siamese_revolution_of_1932) by the [Khana Ratsadon](/wiki/Khana_Ratsadon) (lit. 'People's Party'), Siam transitioned from an absolute monarchy to a [parliamentary constitutional monarchy](/wiki/Parliamentary_constitutional_monarchy) and was officially renamed Thailand. During [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II_in_Thailand), the country was under the [military dictatorship](/wiki/Military_dictatorship) of [Plaek Phibunsongkhram](/wiki/Plaek_Phibunsongkhram) and allied with the [Empire of Japan](/wiki/Empire_of_Japan) as part of the [Axis powers](/wiki/Axis_powers), but Thailand did not become a defeated nation due to the underground [Free Thai Movement](/wiki/Free_Thai_Movement)'s "Declaration of Peace", which was recognised by the [Allies](/wiki/Allies_in_World_War_II). During the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War), Thailand became a key [major non-NATO ally](/wiki/Major_non-NATO_ally) of the [United States](/wiki/United_States) and played a major role in [countering communism](/wiki/Anti-communism) in the [Korean War](/wiki/Korean_War), the [Vietnam War](/wiki/Vietnam_War), and other [proxy wars](/wiki/Proxy_wars). Thailand also joined the [Southeast Asia Treaty Organization](/wiki/Southeast_Asia_Treaty_Organization) (SEATO). Despite brief periods of [liberal democracy](/wiki/Liberal_democracy) in the 1970s and 1990s, Thailand alternated between [liberal democracy](/wiki/Liberal_democracy) and military dictatorship.

Since the 2000s, Thailand has experienced political conflict between supporters and opponents of Prime Minister [Thaksin Shinawatra](/wiki/Thaksin_Shinawatra) (in office [2001–2006](/wiki/Premiership_of_Thaksin_Shinawatra)), leading to the [2006](/wiki/2006_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat) and [2014](/wiki/2014_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat) coup d'états. Today, Thailand operates under the [2017 Constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Thailand) and a [coalition government](/wiki/Coalition_government) following the [2019 Thai general election](/wiki/2019_Thai_general_election), as well as [political demonstrations demanding democracy and monarchy reform](/wiki/2020%E2%80%932021_Thai_protests). The current constitutional structure still allows *de facto* political influence by the [Royal Thai Armed Forces](/wiki/Royal_Thai_Armed_Forces). Thailand has also faced border tensions from the [2008–2011 Cambodian–Thai border crisis](/wiki/2008%E2%80%932011_Cambodian%E2%80%93Thai_border_crisis) extending to the [2025 Cambodia–Thailand border crisis](/wiki/2025_Cambodia%E2%80%93Thailand_border_crisis) involving territorial disputes and military clashes.

Currently, Thailand is recognised as a [developing country](/wiki/Developing_country) and holds significant geopolitical importance in Southeast Asia. It is a [unitary state](/wiki/Unitary_state) governed under a [parliamentary system](/wiki/Parliamentary_system) with a [bicameral legislature](/wiki/National_Assembly_of_Thailand) comprising the elected [House of Representatives](/wiki/House_of_Representatives_of_Thailand) and the appointed [Senate](/wiki/Senate_of_Thailand). Thailand is a member of the [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations), a major non-[NATO](/wiki/NATO) ally of the United States, and a founding member of the [Association of Southeast Asian Nations](/wiki/Association_of_Southeast_Asian_Nations) (ASEAN), as well as a [regional power](/wiki/Regional_power). The Royal Thai Armed Forces are among the largest military forces in Southeast Asia. Thailand's economy is the second-largest in the region and the 23rd globally by [purchasing power parity](/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity), ranking 29th by [gross domestic product](/wiki/Gross_domestic_product). Thailand is classified as a [newly industrialised country](/wiki/Newly_industrialized_country), with manufacturing, agriculture, and [tourism](/wiki/Tourism_in_Thailand) being the main economic sectors.

## Etymology

Thailand was known by outsiders before 1939 as *Siam*. According to [George Cœdès](/wiki/George_C%C5%93d%C3%A8s), the word *Thai* ([ไทย](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%B9%84%E0%B8%97%E0%B8%A2)) means 'free man' in the Thai language, "differentiating the Thai from the natives encompassed in Thai society as serfs". According to [Chit Phumisak](/wiki/Chit_Phumisak), Thai (ไท) simply means 'people' or 'human being'; his investigation shows that some rural areas used the word "Thai" instead of the usual Thai word *khon* ([คน](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%B8%84%E0%B8%99)) for people. According to [Michel Ferlus](/wiki/Michel_Ferlus), the ethnonyms Thai-Tai (or Thay-Tay) would have evolved from the etymon *\*k(ə)ri:* 'human being'.

Thais often refer to their country using the polite form *prathet Thai* ([Thai](/wiki/Thai_language): ประเทศไทย). They also use the more colloquial term *mueang Thai* ([Thai](/wiki/Thai_language): เมืองไทย) or simply *Thai;* the word *[mueang](/wiki/Mueang)*, archaically referring to a [city-state](/wiki/Mandala_(political_model)), is commonly used to refer to a city or town as the centre of a region. *Ratcha Anachak Thai* ([Thai](/wiki/Thai_language): ราชอาณาจักรไทย) means 'kingdom of Thailand' or 'kingdom of Thai'.

Etymologically, its components are: *ratcha* ([Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit_language): राजन्, *[rājan](/wiki/R%C4%81jan)*, 'king, royal, realm'), *ana-* ([Pali](/wiki/Pali) *āṇā* 'authority, command, power', itself from the Sanskrit आज्ञा, *ājñā*, of the same meaning), and *-chak* (from Sanskrit [चक्र](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%9A%E0%A4%95%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0) *cakra-* 'wheel', a symbol of power and rule).

The [Thai National Anthem](/wiki/Thai_National_Anthem) ([Thai](/wiki/Thai_language): เพลงชาติ), written by [Luang Saranupraphan](/wiki/Luang_Saranupraphan) during the patriotic 1930s, refers to the Thai nation as *prathet Thai* ([Thai](/wiki/Thai_language): ประเทศไทย). The first line of the national anthem is: *prathet thai ruam lueat nuea chat chuea thai* ([Thai](/wiki/Thai_language): ประเทศไทยรวมเลือดเนื้อชาติเชื้อไทย), 'Thailand is founded on blood and flesh'.

The former name *Siam* may have originated from [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit) श्याम (*śyāma*, 'dark') or [Mon](/wiki/Mon_language) ရာမည (*rhmañña*, 'stranger'), probably the same root as *[Shan](/wiki/Shan_people)* and *[Assam](/wiki/Assam)*. The word *Śyâma* is possibly not the true origin, but a pre-designed deviation from its proper, original meaning. Another theory is the name derives from the Chinese calling this region 'Xian'.

The ancient Khmers used the word *Siam* to refer to people settled in the west [Chao Phraya River](/wiki/Chao_Phraya_River) valley surrounding the ancient city of [Nakhon Pathom](/wiki/Nakhon_Pathom) in the present-day [central Thailand](/wiki/Central_Thailand); it may probably originate from the name of Lord [Krishna](/wiki/Krishna), which also called *Shyam*, as in the [Wat Sri Chum Inscription](/wiki/Wat_Sri_Chum_Inscription), dated 13th century CE, mentions Phra Maha Thera Sri Sattha came to restore [Phra Pathommachedi](/wiki/Phra_Pathommachedi) at the city of Lord Krishna (Nakhon Pathom) in the early era of the [Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom).

*SPPM Mongkut Rex Siamensium*, [King Mongkut](/wiki/King_Mongkut)'s signature

The signature of King [Mongkut](/wiki/Mongkut) (r. 1851–1868) reads *SPPM* (*Somdet Phra Poramenthra Maha*) *Mongkut Rex Siamensium* (Mongkut, King of the Siamese). This usage of the name in [the country's first international treaty](/wiki/Bowring_Treaty) gave the name *Siam* official status, until 24 June 1939 when it was changed to *Thailand*.

## History

Main article: [History of Thailand](/wiki/History_of_Thailand)

### Prehistory and origins

Main article: [Prehistoric Thailand](/wiki/Prehistoric_Thailand)

See also: [History of Thailand](/wiki/History_of_Thailand) and [Tai peoples](/wiki/Tai_peoples)

There is evidence of continuous human habitation in present-day Thailand from 20,000 years ago to the present day. The earliest evidence of rice growing is dated at 2,000 BCE. Areas comprising what is now Thailand participated in the Maritime Jade Road, as ascertained by archeological research. The trading network existed for 3,000 years, between 2000 BCE to 1000 CE. Bronze appeared c. 1,250–1,000 BCE. The site of [Ban Chiang](/wiki/Ban_Chiang) in northeast Thailand currently ranks as the earliest known centre of copper and bronze production in Southeast Asia. Iron appeared around 500 BCE.

The [Kingdom of Funan](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Funan) was the first and most powerful Southeast Asian kingdom at the time (2nd century BCE). The [Mon people](/wiki/Mon_people) established the principalities of [Dvaravati](/wiki/Dvaravati) and Kingdom of [Hariphunchai](/wiki/Hariphunchai) in the 6th century. The [Khmer people](/wiki/Khmer_people) established the [Khmer empire](/wiki/Khmer_empire), centred in [Angkor](/wiki/Angkor), in the 9th century. [Tambralinga](/wiki/Tambralinga), a Malay state controlling trade through the [Malacca Strait](/wiki/Strait_of_Malacca), rose in the 10th century. The Indochina peninsula was heavily influenced by the [culture and religions of India](/wiki/Indianized_kingdom) from the time of the Kingdom of Funan to that of the Khmer Empire.

The [Thai people](/wiki/Thai_people) are of the [Tai ethnic group](/wiki/Tai_peoples), characterised by common linguistic roots. Chinese chronicles first mention the Tai peoples in the 6th century BCE. While there are many assumptions regarding the origin of Tai peoples, [David K. Wyatt](/wiki/David_K._Wyatt), a historian of Thailand, argued that their ancestors who at present inhabit Laos, Thailand, Myanmar, India, and China came from the [Điện Biên Phủ](/wiki/%C4%90i%E1%BB%87n_Bi%C3%AAn_Ph%E1%BB%A7) area between the 5th and the 8th century.

Thai people began migrating into present-day Thailand gradually from the 6th to 11th century, which Mon and Khmer people occupied at the time. Thus Thai culture was influenced by Indian, Mon, and Khmer cultures. Tai people intermixed with various ethnic and cultural groups in the region, resulting in many groups of present-day Thai people. Genetic evidences suggested that ethnolinguistics could not accurately predict the origins of the Thais. [Sujit Wongthes](/wiki/Sujit_Wongthes) argued that Thai is not a race or ethnicity but a culture group.

According to French historian [George Cœdès](/wiki/George_C%C5%93d%C3%A8s), "The Thai first enter history of [Farther India](/wiki/Farther_India) in the eleventh century with the mention of *Syam* slaves or prisoners of war in [Champa](/wiki/Champa) epigraphy", and "in the twelfth century, the [bas-reliefs](/wiki/Bas-relief) of [Angkor Wat](/wiki/Angkor_Wat)" where "a group of warriors" are described as *Syam*, though Cham accounts do not indicate the origins of *Syam* or what ethnic group they belonged to. The origins and ethnicity of the *Syam* remain unclear, with some literature suggesting that *Syam* refers to the [Shan people](/wiki/Shan_people), the [Bru people](/wiki/Bru_people), or the [Brau people](/wiki/Brau_people). However, [Mainland Southeast Asian](/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia) sources from before the fourteenth century primarily used the word *Syam* as an [ethnonym](/wiki/Ethnonym), referring to those who belonged to a separate cultural category different from the Khmer, Cham, Bagan, or Mon. This contrasts with the Chinese sources, where *Xian* was used as a [toponym](/wiki/Toponym).

### Early Tai confederate cities (691 BCE – 13th century CE)

See also: [Initial states of Thailand](/wiki/Initial_states_of_Thailand) and [Lan Na](/wiki/Lan_Na)

Map showing geographic distribution of [Kra–Dai linguistic family](/wiki/Kra%E2%80%93Dai_languages). Arrows represent general pattern of the migration of [Tai-speaking tribes](/wiki/Tai_peoples) along the rivers and over the lower passes.

[Lan Na](/wiki/Lan_Na) kingdom during the reign of [Tilokaraj](/wiki/Tilokaraj) (r. 1441–1487)

Theoretically, [Tai-Kadai](/wiki/Tai-Kadai)-speaking people formed as early as the 12th century BCE in the middle of the [Yangtze basin](/wiki/Yangtze_basin). Some groups later migrated south to [Guangxi](/wiki/Guangxi). However, after several bloody centuries against [Chinese](/wiki/China) influence in [Guangxi](/wiki/Guangxi) between 333 BCE and the 11th century, hundreds of thousands of Tais were killed, thus, [Tai people](/wiki/Tai_people) began to move southwestward along the rivers and over the lower passes into the mountain north of [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia) and river valleys in present-day [Assam](/wiki/Assam) of [India](/wiki/India). Some evidence indicates that the ancestors of Tai people migrated en masse southwestwards out of Yunnan only after the 1253 [Mongol invasion of Dali](/wiki/Uriyangkhadai#Invasion_of_Yunnan_and_Tibet); however, it is not generally accepted.

Tais defeated [indigenous tribes](/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) and emerged as the new power in the new region. As a result, several Tai city-states were established, scattered from [Điện Biên Phủ](/wiki/%C4%90i%E1%BB%87n_Bi%C3%AAn_Ph%E1%BB%A7) in present-day northwestern [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam) and highland [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia) to [northeastern India](/wiki/Northeastern_India).

According to the *Simhanavati legend* given in several chronicles, the first Tai city-state in northern Thailand, [Singhanavati](/wiki/Singhanavati), was founded around the 7th century. However, several modern [geology](/wiki/Geology) and [archaeology](/wiki/Archaeology) studies found that its centre, Yonok Nahaphan, dates from 691 BCE–545 CE; this roughly coincides with the establishment of [Shan States](/wiki/Shan_people#History), another Tai's federated principalities in the present-day northeast [Myanmar](/wiki/Myanmar). as well as [Muang Sua](/wiki/Muang_Sua) ([Luang Prabang](/wiki/Luang_Prabang)) in the east.

After Singhanavati was submerged below [Chiang Saen Lake](/wiki/Chiang_Saen_Lake) due to an [earthquake](/wiki/Earthquake) in 545, the survivors then founded a new seat at Wieng–Prueksha. The kingdom lasted for another 93 years.

In addition to [Singhanavati](/wiki/Singhanavati), another northern principality probably related to the Tai people, [Ngoenyang](/wiki/Ngoenyang), was established as the successor of Singhanavati in 638 by Lavachakkaraj, also centred in Wieng–Prueksha (present-day [Mae Sai District](/wiki/Mae_Sai_District), [Chiang Rai](/wiki/Chiang_Rai_province)). Its seat was moved to [Chiang Mai](/wiki/Chiang_Mai) in 1262 by King [Mangrai](/wiki/Mangrai), which considered the foundation of the [Lan Na](/wiki/Lan_Na) kingdom. Mangrai unified the surrounding area and also created a network of states through political alliances to the east and north of the [Mekong](/wiki/Mekong). His dynasty would rule the kingdom continuously for the next two centuries. [Lan Na](/wiki/Lan_Na) expanded its territory southward and annexed the [Mon](/wiki/Mon_people) [Hariphunchai](/wiki/Hariphunchai) of [Dvaravati](/wiki/Dvaravati) in 1292.

In the late tenth century, Tai people began to migrate further south to the present-day upper [central Thailand](/wiki/Central_Thailand). Around the 1100s period, several cities in this area, such as Songkwae, Sawankhalok, and Chakangrao, were ruled by the Tai people, eventually launching several battles against the pre-existing [Mon](/wiki/Mon_people) of [Lavo](/wiki/Lavo_Kingdom), who had been falling under [Chenla](/wiki/Chenla) and [Khmer](/wiki/Khmer_Empire) influences since the 7th century, thus bringing the establishment of the Tai people's independent state, [Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom), in the upper [Chao Phraya River](/wiki/Chao_Phraya_River) valley in 1238.

The earliest conflict between [Tai people](/wiki/Tai_people) and the preexisting ethnics was recorded in the mid-4th century when the ruler of [Singhanavati](/wiki/Singhanavati), Pangkharat, forcibly lost the seat at Yonok to King Khom from Umongasela (present-day [Fang](/wiki/Fang_district)). He then fled to Vieng Si Tuang (เวียงศรีทวง; present-day Wiang Phang Kham, [Mae Sai district](/wiki/Mae_Sai_district)) but had to send tributes to Yonok annually until his son, [Phrom](/wiki/Phrom), took back Yonok and expelled King Khom from Umongasela. [Phrom](/wiki/Phrom) also marched the troops south to occupy [Chakangrao](/wiki/Kamphaeng_Phet_province) from the enemy as well as founding the city of [Songkwae](/wiki/Phitsanulok). Some historians suggest that [Lavo](/wiki/Lavo_Kingdom)'s capital, [Lopburi](/wiki/Lopburi), was once seized by [Phrom](/wiki/Phrom). In contrast, [Tai people](/wiki/Tai_people) instead established relationships with Mon via [royal intermarriages](/wiki/Royal_intermarriage).

### Mon and Lavo Kingdoms (5th century CE – 1388 CE)

See also: [Mon people](/wiki/Mon_people), [Thai people](/wiki/Thai_people), [Mon kingdoms](/wiki/Mon_kingdoms), and [Lavo Kingdom](/wiki/Lavo_Kingdom)

Political entities of the Mon people around the 6th–7th centuries

Map of settlements of Dvaravati culture from the 6th to 9th centuries

The [Chao Phraya River](/wiki/Chao_Phraya_River) in what is now [central Thailand](/wiki/Central_Thailand) had once been the home of the [Mon](/wiki/Mon_people) [Dvaravati](/wiki/Dvaravati) culture, which prevailed from the seventh century to the tenth century. Samuel Beal discovered the polity among the Chinese writings on Southeast Asia as "Duoluobodi". During the early 20th century, archaeological excavations led by [George Coedès](/wiki/George_Coed%C3%A8s) found [Nakhon Pathom Province](/wiki/Nakhon_Pathom_Province) to be a centre of Dvaravati culture. The two most important sites were Nakorn Pathom and U Thong (in modern [Suphan Buri Province](/wiki/Suphan_Buri_Province)). The inscriptions of Dvaravati were in [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit) and [Mon](/wiki/Mon_language) using the script derived from the [Pallava alphabet](/wiki/Pallava_alphabet) of the [South Indian](/wiki/South_India) Pallava dynasty.

It is believed that the Dvaravati borrowed [Theravada Buddhism](/wiki/Theravada) through its contacts with Sri Lanka, while the ruling class participated in [Hindu](/wiki/Hindu) rites. [Dvaravati art](/wiki/Dvaravati_art), including the Buddha sculptures and [stupas](/wiki/Stupa), showed strong similarities to those of the [Gupta Empire](/wiki/Gupta_Empire) of India. The eastern parts of the Chao Phraya valley were subjected to a more Khmer and Hindu influence as the inscriptions are found in Khmer and Sanskrit.

Dvaravati was a network of city-states paying tribute to more powerful ones according to the [mandala political model](/wiki/Mandala_(Southeast_Asian_political_model)). Dvaravati culture expanded into [Isan](/wiki/Isan) as well as south as far as the [Kra Isthmus](/wiki/Kra_Isthmus). Dvaravati culture lost its influence around the tenth century when they submitted to the more unified Lavo-Khmer polity.

Around the tenth century, the city-states of Dvaravati merged into the mandalas of: [Lavo](/wiki/Lavo) (modern [Lopburi](/wiki/Lopburi)) and [Suvarnabhumi](/wiki/Suvarnabhumi) (modern [Suphan Buri](/wiki/Suphan_Buri)). According to a legend in the Northern Chronicles, in 903, a king of [Tambralinga](/wiki/Tambralinga) invaded and took Lavo and installed a Malay prince on the Lavo throne. The Malay prince was married to a Khmer princess who had fled an Angkorian dynastic bloodbath. The son of the couple contested the Khmer throne and became [Suryavarman I](/wiki/Suryavarman_I) (1006–1050), thus bringing Lavo under Khmer domination through marital union. Suryavarman I also expanded into the [Khorat Plateau](/wiki/Khorat_Plateau) (later styled "Isan"), constructing many temples.

Wat [Phra Prang Sam Yod](/wiki/Phra_Prang_Sam_Yod), a 13th-century Angkorian temple in [Lopburi](/wiki/Lopburi).

After the [Angkor](/wiki/Angkor) lost Lavo to [Suphannabhum](/wiki/Suphannabhum) princes in 1052, the younger prince [Chandrachota](/wiki/Chandrachota) claimed the throne. His son, [Narai I](/wiki/Narai_I), moved Lavo's seat to [Ayodhya](/wiki/Phra_Nakhon_Si_Ayutthaya_(city)) in the 1080s. This resulted in the throne of Lavo's Lavaburi being vacant from 1087 to 1106 (or ruled by unknown kings), [Si Satchanalai](/wiki/Si_Satchanalai) king, [Kesariraja](/wiki/Kesariraja) who is of [Mon](/wiki/Mon_people)'s [Chaliang](/wiki/Mueang_Chaliang) and [Tai](/wiki/Tai_people)'s [Chiang Saen](/wiki/Chiang_Saen) lineages, took over the seat. An attempt to re-expand influence to Lavo by the Angkor occurred in 1181 when [Jayavarman VII](/wiki/Jayavarman_VII) appointed his lineage Narupatidnavarman to govern Lavapura. Due to this political pressure, a Tai ruler Sri Thammasokkarat
(ศรีธรรมโศกราช), who was also from Si Satchanalai, fled to [Nakhon Si Thammarat](/wiki/Nakhon_Si_Thammarat_Kingdom). Sri Thammasokkarat married to princess of Dhanyapura (Dong Mae Nang Mueang in the present-day [Nakhon Sawan province](/wiki/Nakhon_Sawan_province)), and their descendants ruled [Tambralinga](/wiki/Tambralinga) until the late 13th century. [The Customs of Cambodia](/wiki/The_Customs_of_Cambodia) of [Zhou Daguan](/wiki/Zhou_Daguan), as an official delegation sent by the [Yuan dynasty](/wiki/Yuan_dynasty) to [Angkor](/wiki/Angkor) from 1296 to 1297, says the Siamese people exerted significant influence over Lavo's [Lavapura](/wiki/Lopburi) and appeared in huge numbers in the Angkorian capital of [Yaśodharapura](/wiki/Ya%C5%9Bodharapura).

[Prang Sam Yot](/wiki/Phra_Prang_Sam_Yot) was built during this period. However, Lavapura was probably taken back by a Tai royal from Phraek Si Racha (แพรกศรีราชา; present-day [Sankhaburi](/wiki/Sankhaburi_district)) in the 13th century. During this era, Angkor's power waned due to the weak rule and feuding began in the Angkor.

All of the turmoil, as mentioned earlier, also led to the independence declaration of the [Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom) in 1238.

#### The earliest evidence mentioning the Siamese

The earliest evidence to mention the Siam people are stone inscriptions found in [Angkor Borei](/wiki/Angkor_Borei) of [Funan](/wiki/Funan) (K.557 and K.600), dated 661 CE, the slave's name is mentioned as "Ku Sayam" meaning "Sayam female slaves" (Ku is a prefix used to refer to female slaves in the pre-Angkorian era), and the Takéo inscriptions (K.79) written in 682 during the reign of [Bhavavarman II](/wiki/Bhavavarman_II) of [Chenla](/wiki/Chenla) also mention Siam Nobel: *Sāraṇnoya Poña Sayam*, which was transcribed into English as: *the rice field that was given to the poña (noble rank) who was called Sayam (Siam)*.

The [Song Huiyao Jigao](/wiki/Song_Huiyao_Jigao) (960–1279) indicate Siamese people settled in the west [central Thailand](/wiki/Central_Thailand) and their state was called *Xiān guó* ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 暹國), while the eastern plain belonged to the Mon of [Lavo](/wiki/Lavo_Kingdom) ([Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language): 羅渦國), who later fell under the [Chenla](/wiki/Chenla) and [Khmer](/wiki/Khmer_Empire) hegemony around the 7th–9th centuries. Those Mon political entities, which also included [Haripuñjaya](/wiki/Haripu%C3%B1jaya) in the north and several city-states in the [northeast](/wiki/Northeastern_Thailand), are collectively called [Dvaravati](/wiki/Dvaravati).

However, the states of Siamese and Lavo were later merged via the [royal intermarriage](/wiki/Royal_intermarriage) and became [Ayutthaya Kingdom](/wiki/Ayutthaya_Kingdom) in the mid-14th century, while the southwestern [Isan](/wiki/Isan) principalities, centred in [Phanom Rung](/wiki/Phanom_Rung_Historical_Park) and [Phimai](/wiki/Phimai), later pledged allegiance to Siamese's Ayutthaya during the reign of [Borommarachathirat II](/wiki/Borommarachathirat_II) (r. 1424–1448). The remaining principal city-states in [Isan](/wiki/Isan) region became [Lan Xang](/wiki/Lan_Xang) around 1353 after the twin cities of [Muang Sua](/wiki/Muang_Sua) ([Luang Prabang](/wiki/Luang_Prabang)) and *Vieng Chan Vieng Kham* ([Vientiane](/wiki/Vientiane)) became independent following the death of the [Sukhothai](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom) king [Ram Khamhaeng](/wiki/Ram_Khamhaeng).

According to the Wat Kud Tae inscription (K.1105), dated c. 7th century, during the period that the eastern Mon entity, [Lavo](/wiki/Lavo_Kingdom), was strongly influenced by the [Chenla](/wiki/Chenla), the Siamese in the west also established a [royal intermarriage](/wiki/Royal_intermarriage) with [Chenla](/wiki/Chenla) as Sri Chakatham, prince of Sambhuka (ศามภูกะ, in the present-day [Ratchaburi province](/wiki/Ratchaburi_province)), married to a princess of [Isanavarman I](/wiki/Isanavarman_I), and two [mandalas](/wiki/Mandala_(political_model)) then became an ally. After [Chenla](/wiki/Chenla) sieged [Funan](/wiki/Funan) and moved the centre to [Angkor](/wiki/Angkor), both Siamese and the Angkorian eventually marched the troops to attack [Vijaya](/wiki/Vijaya_(Champa)) of [Champa](/wiki/Champa) in 1201 during the reign of [Jayavarman VII](/wiki/Jayavarman_VII), as recorded in the Cho-Dinh inscription (C.3).

### Sukhothai Kingdom (1238–1438)

Main article: [Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom)

[Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom)

Sukhothai and neighbours, end of 13th century CE

Phra Achana, Wat Si Chum, Sukhothai Historical Park

The ruins of Wat Mahathat, [Sukhothai Historical Park](/wiki/Sukhothai_Historical_Park)

After the decline of the Khmer Empire and [Kingdom of Pagan](/wiki/Pagan_Kingdom) in the early 13th century, various states thrived in their place. The domains of Tai people existed from the northeast of present-day India to the north of present-day Laos and to the [Malay Peninsula](/wiki/Malay_Peninsula). During the 13th century, Tai people had already settled in the core land of [Dvaravati](/wiki/Dvaravati) and [Lavo Kingdom](/wiki/Lavo_Kingdom) to [Nakhon Si Thammarat](/wiki/Nakhon_Si_Thammarat_Kingdom) in the south. There are, however, no records detailing the arrival of the Tais.

Around 1240, [Pho Khun Bang Klang Hao](/wiki/Si_Inthrathit), a local Tai ruler, rallied the people to rebel against the Khmer. He later crowned himself the first king of [Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom) in 1238. Mainstream Thai historians count Sukhothai as the first kingdom of Thai people. Sukhothai expanded furthest during the reign of [Ram Khamhaeng](/wiki/Ram_Khamhaeng) (r. 1279–1298). However, it was mostly a network of local lords who swore fealty to Sukhothai, not directly controlled by it. He is believed have invented [Thai script](/wiki/Thai_script) and Thai ceramics were an important export in his era. Sukhothai embraced [Theravada](/wiki/Theravada) [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism) in the reign of [Maha Thammaracha I](/wiki/Maha_Thammaracha_I) (1347–1368).

### Ayutthaya Kingdom (1351–1767)

Main articles: [Ayutthaya Kingdom](/wiki/Ayutthaya_Kingdom), [Galeote Pereira](/wiki/Galeote_Pereira), [Burmese–Siamese War (1547–1549)](/wiki/Burmese%E2%80%93Siamese_War_(1547%E2%80%931549)), and [Burmese–Siamese War (1584–1593)](/wiki/Burmese%E2%80%93Siamese_War_(1584%E2%80%931593))

[Ayutthaya Kingdom](/wiki/Ayutthaya_Kingdom)

Ayutthaya and neighbors, c. 1390 CE

Ayutthaya and neighbors, c. 1540 CE

Painting of Ayutthaya city c. 1665, by [Johannes Vingboons](/wiki/Johannes_Vingboons)

According to the most widely accepted version of its origin, the Ayutthaya Kingdom rose from the earlier, nearby [Lavo Kingdom](/wiki/Lavo_Kingdom) and Suvarnabhumi with [Uthong](/wiki/Uthong) as its first king. Ayutthaya was a patchwork of self-governing principalities and tributary provinces owing allegiance to the King of Ayutthaya under the [mandala system](/wiki/Mandala_(political_model)). Its initial expansion was through conquest and political marriage.

Before the end of the 15th century, Ayutthaya invaded the Khmer Empire three times and sacked its capital [Angkor](/wiki/Angkor). Ayutthaya then became a regional power in place of the Khmer. Constant interference of Sukhothai effectively made it a vassal state of Ayutthaya and it was finally incorporated into the kingdom. [Borommatrailokkanat](/wiki/Borommatrailokkanat) brought about bureaucratic reforms which lasted into the 20th century and created a system of social hierarchy called *[sakdina](/wiki/Sakdina)*, where male commoners were conscripted as [corvée](/wiki/Corv%C3%A9e) labourers for six months a year. Ayutthaya was interested in the [Malay Peninsula](/wiki/Malay_Peninsula) but failed to conquer the [Malacca Sultanate](/wiki/Malacca_Sultanate), which was supported by the Chinese [Ming dynasty](/wiki/Ming_dynasty).

European contact and trade started in the early-16th century, with the [envoy](/wiki/Duarte_Fernandes) of [Portuguese](/wiki/Portuguese_people) duke [Afonso de Albuquerque](/wiki/Afonso_de_Albuquerque) in 1511. Portugal became an ally and ceded some soldiers to King Rama Thibodi II. The Portuguese were followed in the 17th century by the French, Dutch, and English. Rivalry for supremacy over Chiang Mai and the Mon people pitted Ayutthaya against the Burmese Kingdom. Several wars with its ruling [Taungoo dynasty](/wiki/Taungoo_dynasty) starting in the 1540s in the reign of [Tabinshwehti](/wiki/Tabinshwehti) and [Bayinnaung](/wiki/Bayinnaung) were ultimately ended with the [capture of the capital in 1570](/wiki/Burmese%E2%80%93Siamese_War_(1568%E2%80%9370)). Then a brief period of vassalage to Burma until [Naresuan](/wiki/Naresuan) proclaimed independence in 1584 followed.

Ayutthaya sought to improve relations with European powers for many successive reigns. The kingdom especially prospered during cosmopolitan [Narai](/wiki/Narai)'s reign (1656–1688), when some European travellers regarded Ayutthaya as an Asian great power, alongside China and India. However, growing French influence later in his reign was met with nationalist sentiment and eventually led to the [Siamese revolution of 1688](/wiki/Siamese_revolution_of_1688). Despite the revolution, overall relations remained stable, with French missionaries still actively preaching Christianity.

After a bloody period of dynastic struggle, Ayutthaya entered what has been called the Siamese "[golden age](/wiki/Golden_age)", a relatively peaceful episode in the second quarter of the 18th century where [art](/wiki/Thai_art), [literature](/wiki/Thai_literature), and learning flourished. There were seldom foreign wars, apart from conflict with the [Nguyễn lords](/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_lords) for control of [Cambodia](/wiki/Cambodia) starting around 1715. The last fifty years of the kingdom witnessed bloody succession crises, where there were purges of court officials and able generals for many consecutive reigns. In 1765, a combined 40,000-strong force of Burmese armies [invaded it](/wiki/Burmese%E2%80%93Siamese_War_(1759%E2%80%9360)) from the north and west. The Burmese under the new [Alaungpaya](/wiki/Alaungpaya) dynasty quickly rose to become a new local power by 1759. After a 14-month siege, the capital city's walls fell and the city was burned in April 1767.

### Thonburi Kingdom (1767–1782)

Main article: [Thonburi Kingdom](/wiki/Thonburi_Kingdom)

The capital and many of its territories lay in chaos after the war. The former capital was occupied by the [Burmese](/wiki/Burma) garrison army and five local leaders declared themselves overlords, including the lords of Sakwangburi, [Phitsanulok](/wiki/Phitsanulok), [Pimai](/wiki/Pimai), [Chanthaburi](/wiki/Chanthaburi), and [Nakhon Si Thammarat](/wiki/Nakhon_Si_Thammarat). [Chao Tak](/wiki/Taksin), a capable military leader, proceeded to make himself a lord by [right of conquest](/wiki/Right_of_conquest), beginning with the legendary sack of [Chanthaburi](/wiki/Chanthaburi). Based at Chanthaburi, Chao Tak raised troops and resources, and sent a fleet up the [Chao Phraya](/wiki/Chao_Phraya) to take the fort of [Thonburi](/wiki/Thonburi). In the same year, Chao Tak was able to retake Ayutthaya from the Burmese only seven months after the fall of the city.

Chao Tak then crowned himself as [Taksin](/wiki/Taksin) and proclaimed [Thonburi](/wiki/Thonburi) as temporary capital in the same year. He also quickly subdued the other warlords. His forces engaged in wars with Burma, Laos, and Cambodia, which successfully [drove the Burmese out of Lan Na](/wiki/Burmese%E2%80%93Siamese_War_(1774%E2%80%931775)) in 1775, [captured Vientiane](/wiki/Lao%E2%80%93Siamese_War_(1778%E2%80%931779)) in 1778 and [tried to install a pro-Thai king in Cambodia](/wiki/Siamese%E2%80%93Vietnamese_War_(1769%E2%80%931773)) in the 1770s. In his final years there was a coup, supposedly caused by his "insanity", and eventually Taksin and his sons were executed by his longtime companion General [Chao Phraya Chakri](/wiki/Rama_I) (the future Rama I). He was the first king of the ruling [Chakri dynasty](/wiki/Chakri_dynasty) and founder of the [Rattanakosin Kingdom](/wiki/Rattanakosin_Kingdom) on 6 April 1782.

### Rattanakosin Kingdom and modernisation (1782–1932)

Main article: [Rattanakosin Kingdom](/wiki/Rattanakosin_Kingdom_(1782%E2%80%931932))

[Rattanakosin Kingdom](/wiki/Rattanakosin_Kingdom)

Detailed map of Siam's provinces, vassals, and monthons in 1900

[Emerald Buddha](/wiki/Emerald_Buddha) in [Wat Phra Kaew](/wiki/Wat_Phra_Kaew). Considered the sacred palladium of Thailand.

[Chulalongkorn](/wiki/Chulalongkorn) with [Nicholas II](/wiki/Nicholas_II) in Saint Petersburg, 1897

Siamese territory and dominion at its largest in 1805, resulting from the [Burmese–Siamese War (1802–1805)](/wiki/Burmese%E2%80%93Siamese_War_(1802%E2%80%931805))

Under [Rama I](/wiki/Rama_I) (1782–1809), Rattanakosin successfully defended against Burmese attacks and put an end to Burmese incursions. He also created suzerainty over large portions of Laos and Cambodia. In 1821, Briton [John Crawfurd](/wiki/John_Crawfurd) was sent to negotiate a new trade agreement with Siam – the first sign of an issue which was to dominate 19th century Siamese politics. Bangkok signed the [Burney Treaty](/wiki/Burney_Treaty) in 1826, after the British victory in the [First Anglo-Burmese War](/wiki/First_Anglo-Burmese_War).

[Anouvong](/wiki/Anouvong) of Vientiane, who mistakenly held the belief that Britain was about to launch an invasion of Bangkok, started the [Lao rebellion](/wiki/Lao_rebellion_(1826%E2%80%9328)) in 1826, which was suppressed. Vientiane was destroyed and a large number of [Lao people](/wiki/Lao_people) were relocated to [Khorat Plateau](/wiki/Khorat_Plateau) as a result. Bangkok also waged [several wars](/wiki/Siamese%E2%80%93Vietnamese_wars) with [Vietnam](/wiki/T%C3%A2y_S%C6%A1n_dynasty), where Siam successfully regained hegemony over Cambodia.

From the late 19th century, Siam tried to rule the ethnic groups in the realm as colonies. In the reign of [Mongkut](/wiki/Mongkut) (1851–1868), who recognised the potential threat Western powers posed to Siam, his court contacted the [British government](/wiki/Government_of_the_United_Kingdom) directly to defuse tensions. A British mission led by Sir [John Bowring](/wiki/John_Bowring), Governor of [Hong Kong](/wiki/British_Hong_Kong), led to the signing of the [Bowring Treaty](/wiki/Bowring_Treaty), the first of many [unequal treaties](/wiki/Unequal_treaty) with Western countries. This, however, brought trade and economic development to Siam. The unexpected death of Mongkut from [malaria](/wiki/Malaria) led to the reign of underage King [Chulalongkorn](/wiki/Chulalongkorn), with [Somdet Chaophraya Sri Suriwongse](/wiki/Somdet_Chaophraya_Sri_Suriwongse) (Chuang Bunnag) acting as regent.

Chulalongkorn (r. 1868–1910) initiated centralisation, set up a privy council, and abolished slavery and the [corvée](/wiki/Corv%C3%A9e) system. The [Front Palace crisis](/wiki/Front_Palace_crisis) of 1874 stalled attempts at further reforms. In the 1870s and 1880s, he incorporated the protectorates up north into the kingdom proper, which later expanded to the protectorates in the northeast and the south. He established twelve *krom* in 1888, which were equivalent to present-day ministries. The [crisis of 1893](/wiki/Franco-Siamese_War) erupted, caused by French demands for Laotian territory east of Mekong.

Thailand is the only Southeast Asian state never to have been colonised by a Western power, in part because Britain and France agreed in 1896 to make the [Chao Phraya](/wiki/Chao_Phraya) valley a [buffer state](/wiki/Buffer_state). Not until the 20th century could Siam renegotiate every unequal treaty dating from the Bowring Treaty, including [extraterritoriality](/wiki/Extraterritoriality). The advent of the *monthon* system marked the creation of the modern Thai nation-state. In 1905, there were unsuccessful rebellions in the ancient [Patani](/wiki/Patani_(historical_region)) area, Ubon Ratchathani, and Phrae in opposition to an attempt to blunt the power of local lords.

The [Palace Revolt of 1912](/wiki/Palace_Revolt_of_1912) was a failed attempt by Western-educated military officers to overthrow the Siamese monarchy. [Vajiravudh](/wiki/Vajiravudh) (r. 1910–1925) responded by propaganda for the entirety of his reign, which promoted the [idea of the Thai nation](/wiki/Thaification). In 1917, Siam [joined the First World War](/wiki/Siam_in_World_War_I) on the side of [the Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I). In the aftermath, Siam had a seat at the [Paris Peace Conference](/wiki/Paris_Peace_Conference,_1919) and gained freedom of taxation and the revocation of extraterritoriality.

### Military dictatorship era (1932–1973)

Main articles: [Thailand in World War II](/wiki/Thailand_in_World_War_II) and [History of Thailand (1932–1973)](/wiki/History_of_Thailand_(1932%E2%80%931973))

Field Marshal Plaek Phibunsongkhram

A period of wasteful spending by [King Vajiravudh](/wiki/Vajiravudh) followed by the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression) brought economic hardships on Thailand, with sharply falling rice prices, a significant reduction in public spending, eroded confidence in the monarchy, and discontent among aristocratic supporters all contributing to the destabilisation of the [Chakri dynasty](/wiki/Chakri_dynasty). These conditions spurred on the bloodless [Siamese revolution of 1932](/wiki/Siamese_revolution_of_1932), in which the [Khana Ratsadon](/wiki/Khana_Ratsadon) movement forced [King Prajadhipok](/wiki/Prajadhipok) to sign the country's first constitution, thereby ending centuries of feudal and [absolute monarchy](/wiki/Absolute_monarchy). Power was partially ceded to a [democratic](/wiki/Democracy) government, but it would not last a year before the rise of significant military influence in Thai political affairs.

In 1933, [Prime Minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Thailand) [Phraya Mano](/wiki/Phraya_Manopakorn_Nitithada) led a [counter-revolutionary rebellion](/wiki/Boworadet_rebellion) to reinstate an absolute monarchy under Prajadhipok. This was soon followed by a military coup that installed [Phraya Phahon](/wiki/Phraya_Phahonphonphayuhasena) – a leader of the 1932 revolution – as Prime Minister. Prajadhipok's conflict with the government eventually led to his abdication, and the new military-led government selected [Switzerland](/wiki/Switzerland)-raised [Ananda Mahidol](/wiki/Ananda_Mahidol) to be the new king.

The armed wing of Khana Ratsadon gradually came to dominate Siamese politics. Military strongman [Plaek Phibunsongkhram](/wiki/Plaek_Phibunsongkhram), often known as "Phibun", became premier in 1938 and launched a [campaign of political repression](/wiki/Songsuradet_rebellion) that saw the execution of both Monarchists and pro-democracy [Liberals](/wiki/Liberalism). His government adopted [Thai nationalist](/wiki/Thai_nationalism), pro-[Westernisation](/wiki/Westernisation), anti-royalist, [Irredentist](/wiki/Irredentism), [anti-Chinese](/wiki/Sinophobia) and anti-French policies.

In 1939, Phibun changed the country's name from "Siam" to "Thailand". This coincided with the rise of [fascist ideology](/wiki/Fascism) in Thailand, with the name change reflecting policies that prioritised the needs of [ethnic Thais](/wiki/Tai_peoples) over those of minorities like the [Thai Chinese](/wiki/Thai_Chinese). Phibun was an admirer of [Benito Mussolini](/wiki/Benito_Mussolini), and he imposed twelve [Thai cultural mandates](/wiki/Thai_cultural_mandates) requiring citizens to obey nationalist norms such as saluting the flag, knowing the national anthem, speaking only Thai, and wearing Western clothing.

In October 1940, months after [fall of France](/wiki/Battle_of_France) to [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany), Phibun launched an opportunistic [invasion](/wiki/Franco-Thai_War) of [French Indochinese](/wiki/French_Indochina) territories [lost](/wiki/Territorial_losses_of_Thailand) to the [French Empire](/wiki/France) during the reign of [King Chulalongkorn](/wiki/Chulalongkorn). The invasion was a success, with Thailand additionally gaining some [Lao](/wiki/Laos) and [Cambodian](/wiki/Cambodia) territories.Japan, which had also been invading French Indochinese territories, brokered a ceasefire that forced France to give up several territories to Thailand.

On 8 December 1941, [the Japanese Empire launched an invasion of Thailand](/wiki/Japanese_invasion_of_Thailand), and fierce fighting broke out in Southern Thailand before Phibun ordered an [armistice](/wiki/Armistice) and joined the [Axis powers](/wiki/Axis_powers) just five hours later. Japan was granted free passage through Thai territory, and on 21 December Thailand and Japan signed a military alliance, with a secret protocol wherein the Japanese government agreed to help Thailand gain further territories throughout southeast Asia.

The Thai government then declared war on the United States and the United Kingdom. The United Kingdom, whose colony [Malaya](/wiki/British_Malaya) was under immediate threat from Thai forces, responded in kind, but the United States refused to declare war and ignored Thailand's declaration. The [Free Thai Movement](/wiki/Free_Thai_Movement) was launched both in Thailand and abroad to oppose the Thai government and Japanese occupation. After the war ended in 1945, Phibun was ousted and Thailand signed formal agreements to end the state of war with [the Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II). In June 1946, King Ananda was found dead under mysterious circumstances, and his younger brother [Bhumibol Adulyadej](/wiki/Bhumibol_Adulyadej) ascended to the throne.

King [Bhumibol Adulyadej](/wiki/Bhumibol_Adulyadej) addresses a [joint session of the United States Congress](/wiki/Joint_session_of_the_United_States_Congress), 1960.

This period of Liberal rule was brief, with Phibun launching another military coup to take power in 1947. In his second term as Prime Minister, Phibun pursued an alliance with the United States by undertaking a campaign of domestic [anti-communism](/wiki/Anti-communism), aiding the [US invasion of Korea](/wiki/Korean_War), and helping to establish the US-aligned [Southeast Asia Treaty Organization](/wiki/Southeast_Asia_Treaty_Organization) (SEATO) in 1954. Following a tour of Europe and the US in 1955 where he was impressed by democratic practices like [freedom of speech](/wiki/Freedom_of_speech_in_the_United_States), Phibun began to democratise Thai politics by allowing new political parties and trade unions to form, opening public forums, granting amnesty to former political opponents, and preparing Thailand for its first [free elections](/wiki/Election). However, these practices likely aroused opposition to Phibun's dictatorial legacy, and the US supported his final ouster in 1957.

Field Marshal [Sarit Thanarat](/wiki/Sarit_Thanarat) took power by coup in 1957, and he proceeded to reverse Phibun's democratic reforms and ban the Khana Ratsadon party from politics. His rule from 1959 to 1963 was autocratic; he built his legitimacy around the god-like status of the monarch and by channelling the government and public's loyalty to the king. Though Phibun had been criticised for his close relationship to the United States, Sarit also aligned himself with them and militarily supported the [United States invasion of Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam_War) in 1961, leading a simultaneous campaign of [anti-communist mass killings](/wiki/Anti-communist_mass_killings#Thailand) in Thailand that continued under his successor [Thanom Kittikachorn](/wiki/Thanom_Kittikachorn).

The Sarit and Thanom periods coincided with significant [modernisation](/wiki/Modernisation) and [Westernisation](/wiki/Westernisation) of Thai society. Rapid [urbanisation](/wiki/Urbanisation) occurred as the rural populace sought work in growing cities like [Bangkok](/wiki/Bangkok). Economic development and public education enabled the rise of an urban middle class. The growth of this economy based on [wage labour](/wiki/Wage_labour) rather than [subsistence agriculture](/wiki/Subsistence_agriculture) resulted in increased [class consciousness](/wiki/Class_consciousness) and sympathies to the [Communist Party of Thailand](/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Thailand), while university-educated students formed movements against the military dictatorship.

### Transition toward democracy (1973–2001)

Main article: [History of Thailand (1973–2001)](/wiki/History_of_Thailand_(1973%E2%80%932001))

In October 1973, a [popular democratic uprising](/wiki/1973_Thai_popular_uprising) against the Thanom government began to bring about an end to Thailand's military regime. In response to mass killings of protesters, King Bhumibol installed [Sanya Dharmasakti](/wiki/Sanya_Dharmasakti) as Prime Minister, marking the first time that the king had intervened in Thai politics directly since 1932. The aftermath of the event marked a short-lived parliamentary democracy, often called the "era when democracy blossomed" (ยุคประชาธิปไตยเบ่งบาน).

With new democratic institutions limited in their capability to solidify power and pass reforms, the three years after the uprising were marked by political and economic instability. Right-wing fears of a Communist takeover of Thailand grew following the captures of South Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos by Communist insurgents. These factors culminated in the [Thammasat University massacre](/wiki/6_October_1976_massacre) in October 1976, in which police and right-wing mobs massacred students protesting the return of Thanom Kittikachorn to Thai politics. A military coup d'état on that day brought Thailand a new ultra-[right-wing](/wiki/Right-wing_politics) military government which cracked down on media outlets, government officials, and intellectuals, engendering a [Communist insurgency](/wiki/Communist_insurgency_in_Thailand). Another military coup the following year installed a more moderate military government, which offered amnesty to some Communist fighters in 1978.

Military figure [Prem Tinsulanonda](/wiki/Prem_Tinsulanonda) was appointed Prime Minister from in 1980. He oversaw the beginning of a period of significant economic growth and democratisation throughout the 1980s. Prem's premiership was often referred to as "premocracy" because it involved [semi-democratic](/wiki/Semi-democracy) practices such as a Parliament composed of an all-elected House and an all-appointed Senate. Prem's [constitutionalist](/wiki/Constitutionalism) political approach aided with a transition away from military rule and a negotiated end to the Communist insurgency by 1983. He maintained political stability via a close relationship with [King Bhumibol](/wiki/Bhumibol_Adulyadej), who helped to foil two military coups in attempts against Prem in [1981](/wiki/1981_Thai_military_rebellion) and in [1985](/wiki/1985_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat_attempt).

In 1988, Thailand saw its first freely-elected prime minister since the 1976 coup. [Chatichai Choonhavan](/wiki/Chatichai_Choonhavan) ruled for 3 years before being overthrown in a coup that ushered in two years of military rule under the [National Peace Keeping Council](/wiki/National_Peace_Keeping_Council). One of the coup leaders, [Suchinda Kraprayoon](/wiki/Suchinda_Kraprayoon), was nominated as prime minister for the majority coalition government after the [March 1992 general election](/wiki/March_1992_Thai_general_election). This caused a popular demonstration in Bangkok, which ended with a bloody military crackdown known as [Black May](/wiki/Black_May_(1992)). The King intervened in the event, granting amnesty to the belligerents and forcing the resignation of Suchinda.

Free elections were once again held in September 1992, bringing the [neoliberal](/wiki/Neoliberalism) [Democrat Party](/wiki/Democrat_Party_(Thailand)) to power. Power passed between various neoliberal parties throughout the decade, who all had a hand in drafting the [1997 "People's Constitution."](/wiki/1997_constitution_of_Thailand) Spurred on by the events of Black May, the constitution explicitly acknowledged several [human rights](/wiki/Human_rights) and established several government and electoral accountability measures, including the creation of a fully-elected [bicameral legislature](/wiki/Bicameral_legislature).

### Shinawatra era (2001–present)

Main article: [History of Thailand (2001–present)](/wiki/History_of_Thailand_(2001%E2%80%93present))

The neoliberal government's attempt to [peg](/wiki/Currency_peg) the [Thai baht](/wiki/Thai_baht) to the [United States dollar](/wiki/United_States_dollar) induced the [1997 Asian financial crisis](/wiki/1997_Asian_financial_crisis), crashing regional economies and ending nearly 40 years of uninterrupted economic growth in Thailand. To cover a shortage of foreign currency and investments, which had come primarily from Japan, [Chavalit Yongchaiyudh](/wiki/Chavalit_Yongchaiyudh)'s government took out a $17 billion [International Monetary Fund](/wiki/International_Monetary_Fund) loan in US dollars. To satisfy the conditions of the loan, Thailand underwent a series of [austerity measures](/wiki/Austerity) until the loan was repaid in 2003.

The crisis brought on the ascendancy of the populist [Thai Rak Thai party](/wiki/Thai_Rak_Thai_party), whose base consisted primarily of indebted rural workers. Its prime minister [Thaksin Shinawatra](/wiki/Thaksin_Shinawatra) governed from 2001 until 2006. His policies were successful in reducing rural poverty and providing [universal healthcare](/wiki/Universal_healthcare). However, Thaksin was viewed by opponents among the Thai upper and upper middle classes as a corrupt populist who was destroying the middle class in order to favour himself and the rural poor. Additionally, his rural poverty policies directly conflicted with King Bhumibol's recommendations, drawing the ire of royalists, a powerful faction in Thailand. Thaksin also faced crises in the form of the [South Thailand insurgency](/wiki/Southern_Thailand_insurgency), which escalated starting from 2004, and the [2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami](/wiki/2004_Indian_Ocean_earthquake_and_tsunami) which claimed around 8,000 lives in Thailand.

Massive protests against Thaksin led by the [reactionary](/wiki/Reactionary) [People's Alliance for Democracy](/wiki/People%27s_Alliance_for_Democracy) (PAD) started in his second term as prime minister, and the monarchy and the military eventually agreed to oust him. The [crisis](/wiki/2005%E2%80%932006_Thai_political_crisis) peaked in 2005 with a series of controversies surrounding Buddhist and monarchic institutions, and the army dissolved Thaksin's party with [a coup d'état in 2006](/wiki/2006_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat) and banned over a hundred of its executives from politics. After the coup, a military government was installed for one year.

[United Front for Democracy Against Dictatorship](/wiki/United_Front_for_Democracy_Against_Dictatorship), Red Shirts, [protest](/wiki/2010_Thai_political_protests) in 2010

The military government faced significant resistance, with protesters frequently storming government buildings and elements within the military threatening yet another coup. In 2007, a civilian government led by the Thaksin-aligned [People's Power Party](/wiki/People%27s_Power_Party_(Thailand)) (PPP) was [elected](/wiki/2007_Thai_general_election). [Another protest led by PAD](/wiki/2008_Thai_political_crisis) ended with the dissolution of PPP, and the [Democrat Party](/wiki/Democrat_Party_(Thailand)) led a coalition government in its place. The pro-Thaksin [United Front for Democracy Against Dictatorship](/wiki/United_Front_for_Democracy_Against_Dictatorship) (UDD) protested both [in 2009](/wiki/2009_Thai_political_unrest) and [in 2010](/wiki/2010_Thai_political_protests), the latter of which ended with [a violent military crackdown](/wiki/2010_Thai_military_crackdown) causing more than 70 civilian deaths.

After [the general election of 2011](/wiki/2011_Thai_general_election), the [populist](/wiki/Populism) [Pheu Thai Party](/wiki/Pheu_Thai_Party) won a majority. [Yingluck Shinawatra](/wiki/Yingluck_Shinawatra), Thaksin's younger sister, became prime minister. The [People's Democratic Reform Committee](/wiki/People%27s_Democratic_Reform_Committee) organised another anti-Shinawatra protest after the ruling party proposed an amnesty bill which would benefit Thaksin. Yingluck dissolved parliament and [a general election](/wiki/2014_Thai_general_election) was scheduled, but her decision was invalidated by the [Constitutional Court](/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Thailand). The [crisis](/wiki/2013%E2%80%932014_Thai_political_crisis) ended with [another coup d'état in 2014](/wiki/2014_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat).

The ensuing [National Council for Peace and Order](/wiki/National_Council_for_Peace_and_Order), a military junta led by General [Prayut Chan-o-cha](/wiki/Prayut_Chan-o-cha), led the country until 2019. Civil and political rights were restricted, and the country saw a surge in *[lèse-majesté](/wiki/L%C3%A8se-majest%C3%A9_in_Thailand)* cases. Political opponents and dissenters were sent to "attitude adjustment" camps; this was described by academics as indicating the return of fascism to Thailand. Bhumibol, the longest-reigning Thai king, [died in 2016](/wiki/Death_and_funeral_of_Bhumibol_Adulyadej), and his son [Vajiralongkorn](/wiki/Vajiralongkorn) ascended to the throne. The referendum and adoption of Thailand's current constitution happened under the junta's rule. The junta bound future governments to a 20-year national strategy 'road map' it laid down, effectively locking the country into [military-guided democracy](/wiki/Guided_democracy).

In 2019, the junta agreed to schedule [a general election](/wiki/2019_Thai_general_election). General Prayut continued his premiership with the support of an elected [Palang Pracharath Party](/wiki/Palang_Pracharath_Party) coalition in the House and junta-appointed Senate, despite allegations of election fraud. An extended two-year period of [pro-democracy protests](/wiki/2020%E2%80%932021_Thai_protests) were subsequently triggered by distrust in the election as well as increasing [royal prerogative](/wiki/Royal_prerogative), democratic and economic regression under monarchy-supported military rule, retaliatory dissolution of the pro-democracy [Future Forward Party](/wiki/Future_Forward_Party), regressive policies on political issues like women's and labour rights, forced disappearances and deaths of political activists like [Wanchalearm Satsaksit](/wiki/Wanchalearm_Satsaksit), and various political corruption scandals including [1MDB](/wiki/1Malaysia_Development_Berhad_scandal). The protests brought forward unprecedented demands to reform the monarchy and the highest sense of [republicanism](/wiki/Republicanism) in the country's history.

In May 2023, Thailand's reformist opposition – the progressive [Move Forward Party](/wiki/Move_Forward_Party) (MFP) and Thaksin's populist Pheu Thai Party – won the [general election](/wiki/2023_Thai_general_election), ending 9 years of royalist-military rule under Prayut. On 22 August 2023, [Srettha Thavisin](/wiki/Srettha_Thavisin) of the Pheu Thai party became Thailand's new prime minister, while the ex-PM and party figurehead Thaksin Shinawatra returned to Thailand after years in self-imposed exile from the military regime. Thavisin was later dismissed from his prime ministerial role on 14 August 2024 by the [Constitutional Court](/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Thailand) for "gross ethics violations". He was succeeded by Thaksin's daughter [Paetongtarn Shinawatra](/wiki/Paetongtarn_Shinawatra), who spent a year in office before the Constitutional Court ordered her removal over a controversial phone call with Cambodia's president. The Court then ordered the arrest of Thaksin in September 2025.

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Thailand](/wiki/Geography_of_Thailand)

[Ko Tarutao](/wiki/Ko_Tarutao), [Satun](/wiki/Satun_Province)

Totalling 513,120 square kilometres (198,120 sq mi), Thailand is the [50th-largest country](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area) by total area. Thailand comprises several distinct geographic regions, partly corresponding to the provincial groups. The north of the country is the mountainous area of the [Thai highlands](/wiki/Thai_highlands), with the highest point being [Doi Inthanon](/wiki/Doi_Inthanon) in the [Thanon Thong Chai Range](/wiki/Thanon_Thong_Chai_Range) at 2,565 metres (8,415 ft) above sea level. The northeast, [Isan](/wiki/Isan), consists of the [Khorat Plateau](/wiki/Khorat_Plateau), bordered to the east by the [Mekong River](/wiki/Mekong_River). The centre of the country is dominated by the predominantly flat [Chao Phraya](/wiki/Chao_Phraya) river valley, which runs into the [Gulf of Thailand](/wiki/Gulf_of_Thailand). Southern Thailand consists of the narrow [Kra Isthmus](/wiki/Kra_Isthmus) that widens into the [Malay Peninsula](/wiki/Malay_Peninsula).

The Chao Phraya and the Mekong River are the indispensable water courses of rural Thailand. Industrial scale production of crops use both rivers and their tributaries. The Gulf of Thailand covers 320,000 square kilometres (124,000 sq mi) and is fed by the Chao Phraya, [Mae Klong](/wiki/Mae_Klong_River), [Bang Pakong](/wiki/Bang_Pakong_River), and [Tapi](/wiki/Tapi_River,_Thailand) Rivers. It contributes to the tourism sector owing to its clear shallow waters along the coasts in the southern region and the Kra Isthmus. The eastern shore of the Gulf of Thailand has the kingdom's premier deepwater port in [Sattahip](/wiki/Sattahip_District) and its busiest commercial port, [Laem Chabang](/wiki/Laem_Chabang). [Phuket](/wiki/Phuket_Province), [Krabi](/wiki/Krabi_Province), [Ranong](/wiki/Ranong), [Phang Nga](/wiki/Phang_Nga) and [Trang](/wiki/Trang_Province), and their islands, all lay along the coasts of the [Andaman Sea](/wiki/Andaman_Sea).

### Climate

Further information: [Climate change in Thailand](/wiki/Climate_change_in_Thailand)

Thailand map of Köppen climate classification

Thailand's climate is influenced by monsoon winds that have a seasonal character (the southwest and northeast monsoon). Most of the country is classified as [Köppen](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification)'s [tropical savanna climate](/wiki/Tropical_savanna_climate). The majority of the south, as well as the eastern tip, have a [tropical monsoon climate](/wiki/Tropical_monsoon_climate). Parts of the south also have a [tropical rainforest climate](/wiki/Tropical_rainforest_climate).

A year in Thailand is divided into three seasons. The first is the rainy or southwest monsoon season (mid–May to mid–October), which is caused by southwestern wind from the [Indian Ocean](/wiki/Indian_Ocean).

Rainfall is also contributed by [Intertropical Convergence Zone](/wiki/Intertropical_Convergence_Zone) (ITCZ) and tropical [cyclones](/wiki/Cyclones), with August and September being the wettest period of the year. The country receives a mean annual rainfall of 1,200 to 1,600 mm (47 to 63 in).

Winter or the northeast monsoon occurs from mid-October until mid-February. Most of Thailand experiences dry weather with mild temperatures. Summer or the pre-monsoon season runs from mid-February until mid-May.

Due to their inland position and latitude, the north, northeast, central and eastern parts of Thailand experience a long period of warm weather, where temperatures can reach up to 40 °C (104 °F) during March to May, in contrast to close to or below 0 °C (32 °F) in some areas in winter.

Southern Thailand is characterised by mild weather year-round with less diurnal and seasonal variations in temperatures due to maritime influences. It receives abundant rainfall, particularly during October to November. Thailand is among the world's ten countries that are most exposed to climate change. In particular, it is highly vulnerable to [rising sea levels](/wiki/Sea_level_rise) and extreme weather events.

### Biodiversity and conservation

See also: [List of protected areas of Thailand](/wiki/List_of_protected_areas_of_Thailand), [List of forest parks of Thailand](/wiki/List_of_forest_parks_of_Thailand), [List of species native to Thailand](/wiki/List_of_species_native_to_Thailand), and [Environmental issues in Thailand](/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_Thailand)

The population of [Asian elephants](/wiki/Asian_elephants) in Thailand's wild has dropped to an estimated 2,000–3,000.

[National parks](/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_Thailand) in Thailand are defined as *an area that contains natural resources of ecological importance or unique beauty, or flora and fauna of special importance*. Thailand's protected areas include 156 national parks, 58 wildlife sanctuaries, 67 non-hunting areas, and 120 forest parks. They cover almost 31 per cent of the kingdom's territory. The parks are administered by the [National Parks, Wildlife and Plant Conservation Department](/wiki/Department_of_National_Parks,_Wildlife_and_Plant_Conservation) (DNP) of the [Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment](/wiki/Ministry_of_Natural_Resources_and_Environment_(Thailand)) (MNRE). The [Khao Kheow Open Zoo](/wiki/Khao_Kheow_Open_Zoo) in [Chonburi](/wiki/Chonburi) is the second largest zoo in Asia. The largest national park of Thailand is [Kaeng Krachan National Park](/wiki/Kaeng_Krachan_National_Park).

Thailand has a mediocre but improving performance in the global [Environmental Performance Index](/wiki/Environmental_Performance_Index) (EPI), with an overall ranking of 91 out of 180 countries in 2016. The environmental areas where Thailand performs worst (i.e., highest-ranking) are [air quality](/wiki/Air_quality) (167), environmental effects of the agricultural industry (106), and the [climate and energy](/wiki/Climate_and_energy) sector (93), the later mainly because of a high [CO2 emission](/wiki/CO2_emission) per kWh produced. Thailand performs best (i.e., lowest-ranking) in [water resource management](/wiki/Water_resource_management) (66), with some major improvements expected for the future, and [sanitation](/wiki/Sanitation) (68). The country had a 2019 [Forest Landscape Integrity Index](/wiki/Forest_Landscape_Integrity_Index) mean score of 6.00/10, ranking it 88th globally out of 172 countries.

The population of elephants, the country's [national symbol](/wiki/List_of_national_animals), has fallen from 100,000 in 1850 to an estimated 2,000. Poachers have long hunted elephants for [ivory](/wiki/Ivory) and hides, and now increasingly for [meat](/wiki/Elephant_meat). Young elephants are often captured for use in tourist attractions or as work animals, where there have been claims of mistreatment. In 1989, the government banned the use of elephants for [logging](/wiki/Logging), leading many elephant owners to move their domesticated animals to the tourism industry.

Poaching of protected species remains a major problem. [Tigers](/wiki/Tigers), [leopards](/wiki/Leopards), and other large cats are hunted for their pelts. Many are farmed or hunted for their meat, which supposedly has medicinal properties. Although such trade is illegal, the well-known Bangkok market [Chatuchak](/wiki/Chatuchak_Weekend_Market) is still known for the sale of endangered species. The practice of keeping wild animals as pets affects species such as [Asiatic black bear](/wiki/Asiatic_black_bear), [Malayan sun bear](/wiki/Malayan_sun_bear), [white-handed lar](/wiki/Lar_gibbon), [pileated gibbon](/wiki/Pileated_gibbon), and [binturong](/wiki/Binturong).

## Politics and government

Main articles: [Politics of Thailand](/wiki/Politics_of_Thailand) and [Government of Thailand](/wiki/Government_of_Thailand)

Further information: [Constitutions of Thailand](/wiki/Constitutions_of_Thailand) and [Elections in Thailand](/wiki/Elections_in_Thailand)

[Vajiralongkorn](/wiki/Vajiralongkorn)
[King of Thailand](/wiki/King_of_Thailand)
since 13 October 2016

[Anutin Charnvirakul](/wiki/Anutin_Charnvirakul)
[Prime Minister of Thailand](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Thailand)
since 7 September 2025

Prior to 1932, Thai kings were feudal or [absolute monarchs](/wiki/Absolute_monarchy). During [Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom), the king was seen as a *Dharmaraja* or 'king who rules in accordance with [Dharma](/wiki/Dharma)'. The system of government was a network of tributaries ruled by local lords. Modern absolute monarchy and statehood was established by Chulalongkorn when he transformed the decentralised protectorate system into a unitary state. On 24 June 1932, [Khana Ratsadon](/wiki/Khana_Ratsadon) (People's Party) carried out a bloodless revolution which marked the beginning of [constitutional monarchy](/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy).

Thailand has had 20 constitutions and charters since 1932, including the latest and current 2017 Constitution. All constitutions state that the politics is conducted within the framework of a constitutional monarchy, but the *de facto* form of government has ranged from military dictatorship to electoral democracy. Thailand's current form of government is part democracy and part dictatorship; many terms are used to describe it. Thailand has had the fourth-most [coups](/wiki/Coup_d%27%C3%A9tat) in the world. "Uniformed or ex-military men have led Thailand for 55 of the 83 years" between 1932 and 2009. Most recently, the [military junta](/wiki/Military_junta) self-titled as the [National Council for Peace and Order](/wiki/National_Council_for_Peace_and_Order) ruled the country between 2014 and 2019.

[Sappaya-Sapasathan](/wiki/Sappaya-Sapasathan) is the current Parliament House of Thailand

Government is [separated](/wiki/Separation_of_powers) into three branches:

- The legislative branch: the [National Assembly](/wiki/National_Assembly_of_Thailand) is composed of the [Senate](/wiki/Senate_of_Thailand), the 200-member indirectly elected [upper house](/wiki/Upper_house) and [House of Representatives](/wiki/House_of_Representatives_of_Thailand), the elected 500-member [lower house](/wiki/Lower_house). Its most recent election is [the 2023 general election](/wiki/2023_Thai_general_election). The coalition led by Pheu Thai Party currently holds the majority. The [2024 Thai Senate election](/wiki/2024_Thai_Senate_election) was the first senate election held under the current constitution in the process criticised as "the most complicated election in the world". The senate is allegedly dominated by [Bhumjaithai Party](/wiki/Bhumjaithai_Party)-affiliated senators.
- The executive branch consisting of the [Prime Minister of Thailand](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Thailand), the [head of government](/wiki/Head_of_government), and other cabinet members of up to 35 people. The Prime Minister was elected by the House of Representatives. The current constitution mandates that prime ministers are to be considered from candidates nominated by political parties before the election. The current prime minister is [Anutin Charnvirakul](/wiki/Anutin_Charnvirakul), a member of the Bhumjaithai Party.
- The [judiciary](/wiki/Judiciary) is supposed to be independent of the executive and the legislative branches, although judicial rulings are suspected of being based on political considerations rather than on existing law.

Military and bureaucratic aristocrats fully controlled political parties between 1946 and the 1980s. Most parties in Thailand are short-lived. Between 1992 and 2006, Thailand had a [two-party system](/wiki/Two-party_system). Later constitutions created a multi-party system where a single party cannot gain a majority in the house.

A [hereditary monarch](/wiki/Hereditary_monarchy) serves as Thailand's [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state). The current King of Thailand is [Vajiralongkorn](/wiki/Vajiralongkorn) (Rama X), who has reigned since October 2016. The powers of the king are limited by the constitution and he is primarily a symbolic figurehead. However, the monarch still occasionally intervenes in Thai politics, as all constitutions pave the way for customary royal rulings. Some academics outside Thailand, including [Duncan McCargo](/wiki/Duncan_McCargo) and Federico Ferrara, noted extraconstitutional role of the monarch through a "[network monarchy](/wiki/Network_monarchy)" behind the political scenes. The monarchy is protected by the severe [*lèse majesté* law](/wiki/L%C3%A8se_majest%C3%A9_in_Thailand), even though the people's attitude towards the institution varies from one reign to another.

Timeline of Prime Ministers

The kings are protected by *lèse-majesté* laws which allow critics to be jailed for three to fifteen years. After the [coup d'état in 2014](/wiki/2014_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat), Thailand had the highest [number of *lèse-majesté* prisoners](/wiki/Category:People_accused_of_l%C3%A8se_majest%C3%A9_in_Thailand) in the nation's history. [Human rights in Thailand](/wiki/Human_rights_in_Thailand) has been rated *[not free](/wiki/Freedom_in_the_World_(report))* on the [Freedom House](/wiki/Freedom_House) Index since 2014. On August 7, 2024, Thailand's [Constitutional Court](/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Thailand) banned the victors of the [2023 parliamentary elections](/wiki/2023_Thai_general_election), the [Move Forward Party](/wiki/Move_Forward_Party) and all of its leaders from politics for its proposal to reform the lèse-majesté law, arguing it posed a threat to the constitutional order. *[The Economist](/wiki/The_Economist)* criticised the move as an example of "[lawfare](/wiki/Lawfare)" and pointed to the dissolution of its predecessor party, [Future Forward](/wiki/Future_Forward_Party) in 2020, as the latest example of how an "alliance of conservative forces in Thailand—including monarchists, the army and a handful of business tycoons—has sought to suppress opposition".

On the [Freedom in the World 2024 Report](/wiki/Freedom_House) for Thailand, their status improved from *[not free](/wiki/Freedom_in_the_World)* to *[partly free](/wiki/Freedom_in_the_world)* due to competitive parliamentary elections and the formation of a new governing coalition by what had been a major opposition party, though unelected senators ensured that the party with the most votes was excluded.

### Administrative divisions

Main articles: [Organization of the government of Thailand](/wiki/Organization_of_the_government_of_Thailand), [Provinces of Thailand](/wiki/Provinces_of_Thailand), and [Administrative Divisions of Thailand](/wiki/Administrative_Divisions_of_Thailand)

See also: [Regions of Thailand](/wiki/Regions_of_Thailand)

Thailand is a [unitary state](/wiki/Unitary_state); the administrative services of the executive branch are divided into three levels by *National Government Organisation Act, BE 2534* (1991): central, provincial and local. Thailand is composed of 76 [provinces](/wiki/Provinces_of_Thailand) (จังหวัด, *changwat*), which are first-level [administrative divisions](/wiki/Administrative_division). There are also two specially governed districts: the capital [Bangkok](/wiki/Bangkok) and [Pattaya](/wiki/Pattaya). Bangkok is at provincial level and thus often counted as a province. Each province is divided into [districts](/wiki/Districts_of_Thailand) (อำเภอ, *amphoe*) and the districts are further divided into sub-districts (ตำบล, *tambons*). The name of each province's capital city (เมือง, mueang) is the same as that of the province. For example, the capital of [Chiang Mai Province](/wiki/Chiang_Mai_Province) (*Changwat Chiang Mai*) is *Mueang Chiang Mai* or *Chiang Mai*. All provincial governors and district chiefs, which are administrators of provinces and districts respectively, are appointed by the central government. Thailand's provinces are sometimes grouped into four to six regions, depending on the source.

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of Thailand](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Thailand)

King [Bhumibol Adulyadej](/wiki/Bhumibol_Adulyadej) in a meeting with U.S. President [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama), 18 November 2012

Siam's and Thailand's way of conducting foreign relations has long been described as "bamboo bending with the wind", of policies that are "always solidly rooted, but flexible enough to bend whichever way the wind blows in order to survive", or adaptable and pragmatic. In order to secure independence, it sought to pit one great power against the others so that it would be dominated by none.

During the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War), Thailand sought to prevent the spread of communism so it joined the United States, including participating in [SEATO](/wiki/SEATO) alliance, sending expeditions to Korea and Vietnam, and offering the US to use its base. Thailand is one of the five founding members of [Association of Southeast Asian Nations](/wiki/Association_of_Southeast_Asian_Nations) (ASEAN), initially to safeguard against communism. The end of [Vietnam War](/wiki/Vietnam_War) was a turning point in Thai foreign policy and afterwards it sought to improve relations with Communist China and its now-Communist neighbours. Thailand remains an active member of ASEAN and seek to project its influence in it. Thailand has developed increasingly close ties with other members, with progressing regional co-operation in economic, trade, banking, political, and cultural matters.

In the 2000s, Thailand had taken an active role on the international stage and participated fully in international and regional organisations. It is a [major non-NATO ally](/wiki/Major_non-NATO_ally) and Priority Watch List [Special 301 Report](/wiki/Special_301_Report) of the United States. When [East Timor](/wiki/East_Timor) gained independence from Indonesia, Thailand contributed troops to the international peacekeeping effort. As part of its effort to increase international ties, Thailand had reached out to such regional organisations as the [Organization of American States](/wiki/Organization_of_American_States) (OAS) and the [Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe](/wiki/Organization_for_Security_and_Co-operation_in_Europe) (OSCE).

During [Thaksin Shinawatra](/wiki/Thaksin_Shinawatra)'s premiership, negotiations for several [free trade agreements](/wiki/Free_trade_agreement) with China, Australia, Bahrain, India, and the US were initiated. Thaksin sought to position Thailand as a regional leader, initiating various development projects in poorer neighbouring countries. More controversially, he established close, friendly ties with the Burmese dictatorship. Thailand joined the US-led [invasion of Iraq](/wiki/2003_invasion_of_Iraq), sending a [humanitarian contingent](/wiki/Thai_Humanitarian_Assistance_Task_Force_976_Thai-Iraq) until September 2004. Thailand also had contributed troops to reconstruction efforts in [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan).

In April 2009, the [2008–2013 Cambodian–Thai border crisis](/wiki/2008%E2%80%932013_Cambodian%E2%80%93Thai_border_crisis) brought troops on territory immediately adjacent to the 900-year-old ruins of Cambodia's [Preah Vihear](/wiki/Preah_Vihear) Hindu temple near the border.

After the 2014 coup, Thailand leaned more towards China. Growing Chinese influence and capital inflow caused some members of parliament to raise the concern about "economic colony" under China after many concessions.

During the [Israel-Gaza war](/wiki/Gaza_war) in 2023, at first Thailand's prime minister stated that his government strongly condemns the attack against Israel and extends its deepest condolences to the government and the people of Israel but the government later changed its position and announced that Thailand adopted a neutral stance in this conflict. 28 Thai nationals were killed in this conflict.

### Armed forces

Main article: [Royal Thai Armed Forces](/wiki/Royal_Thai_Armed_Forces)

The [HTMS *Chakri Naruebet*](/wiki/HTMS_Chakri_Naruebet), an [aircraft carrier](/wiki/Aircraft_carrier) of the [Royal Thai Navy](/wiki/Royal_Thai_Navy)

The [Royal Thai Armed Forces](/wiki/Royal_Thai_Armed_Forces) (กองทัพไทย; [RTGS](/wiki/Royal_Thai_General_System_of_Transcription): *Kong Thap Thai*) constitute the military of the Kingdom of Thailand. It consists of the [Royal Thai Army](/wiki/Royal_Thai_Army) (กองทัพบกไทย), the [Royal Thai Navy](/wiki/Royal_Thai_Navy) (กองทัพเรือไทย), and the [Royal Thai Air Force](/wiki/Royal_Thai_Air_Force) (กองทัพอากาศไทย). It also incorporates various [paramilitary](/wiki/Paramilitary) forces.

The [head of the Thai Armed Forces](/wiki/Monarchy_of_Thailand) (จอมทัพไทย, *Chom Thap Thai*) is the king, although this position is only nominal. The armed forces are managed by the [Ministry of Defence of Thailand](/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_(Thailand)), which is headed by the Minister of Defence (a member of the [cabinet of Thailand](/wiki/Cabinet_of_Thailand)) and commanded by the [Royal Thai Armed Forces Headquarters](/wiki/Royal_Thai_Armed_Forces_Headquarters), which in turn is headed by the [Chief of Defence Forces of Thailand](/wiki/List_of_Commanders_of_the_Royal_Thai_Armed_Forces_Headquarters). Thai annual defence budget almost tripled from US$1.98 billion in 2005 to US$5.88 billion in 2016, accounting for approximately 1.4% of GDP. Thailand ranked 16th worldwide in the Military Strength Index based on the [Credit Suisse](/wiki/Credit_Suisse) report in September 2015.

A Royal Thai Air Force [JAS 39 Gripen](/wiki/JAS_39_Gripen)

The military is also tasked with humanitarian missions, such as escorting [Rohingya](/wiki/Rohingya) to Malaysia or Indonesia, ensuring security and welfare for refugees during [Indochina refugee crisis](/wiki/Indochina_refugee_crisis).

According to the constitution, serving in the armed forces is a duty of all Thai citizens. Thailand still use active draft system for males over the age of 21. They are subjected to varying lengths of active service depending on the duration of reserve training as [Territorial Defence Student](/wiki/Territorial_Defence_Student) and their level of education. Those who have completed three years or more of reserve training will be exempted entirely. The practice has long been criticised, as some media question its efficacy and value. It is alleged that conscripts end up as servants to senior officers or clerks in military cooperative shops. In a report issued in March 2020, [Amnesty International](/wiki/Amnesty_International) charged that Thai military conscripts face institutionalised abuse systematically hushed up by military authorities.

Critics observed that Thai military's main objective is to deal with internal rather than external threats. [Internal Security Operations Command](/wiki/Internal_Security_Operations_Command) is called the political arm of the Thai military, which has overlapping social and political functions with civilian bureaucracy. It also has anti-democracy mission. The military is also notorious for numerous corruption incidents, such as accusation of [human trafficking](/wiki/Human_trafficking_in_Thailand), and [nepotism](/wiki/Nepotism) in promotion of high-ranking officers. The military is deeply entrenched in politics. Most recently, the appointed senators include more than 100 active and retired military.

Thailand is the 75th most peaceful country in the world, according to the 2024 [Global Peace Index](/wiki/Global_Peace_Index).

[Sathorn](/wiki/Sathorn) in Bangkok is a skyscraper-studded business district that is home to major hotels and embassies.

The economy of Thailand is heavily export-dependent, with exports accounting for more than two-thirds of [gross domestic product](/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) (GDP). Thailand exports over US$105 billion worth of goods and services annually. Major exports include cars, computers, electrical appliances, [rice](/wiki/Rice_production_in_Thailand), textiles and footwear, fishery products, rubber, and jewellery.

Thailand is an [emerging economy](/wiki/Emerging_markets) and is considered a [newly industrialised country](/wiki/Newly_industrialised_country). Thailand had a 2017 GDP of US$1.236 trillion (on a [purchasing power parity](/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity) basis). Thailand is the second largest economy in Southeast Asia after Indonesia. Thailand ranks midway in the wealth spread in Southeast Asia as it is the fourth richest nation according to GDP per capita, after Singapore, Brunei, and Malaysia.

Thailand functions as an [anchor economy](/wiki/Anchor_economy) for the neighbouring developing economies of Laos, Myanmar, and Cambodia. In the third quarter of 2014, the unemployment rate in Thailand stood at 0.84% according to Thailand's National Economic and Social Development Board (NESDB).

In 2017, the Thai economy grew an inflation-adjusted 3.9%, up from 3.3% in 2016, marking its fastest expansion since 2012. High public spending, especially during the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Thailand), prompted the authorities to raise Thailand's public debt ceiling from 60% to 70% of GDP.

As of 2024, Thailand struggles with low productivity, poor education, high household debt, low private investment and slow economic growth, with an economic research group forecasted an annual GDP growth of below 2% in the next decades without structural reforms.

As of 2025, The Bank of Thailand's economic outlook page projects Thai GDP growth of around 2.2% in 2025 and 1.6% in 2026, noting that growth in the first half of 2025 was supported by front‑loaded exports to the U.S. ahead of tariff hikes, while the second half is expected to slow as higher U.S. tariffs bite.

### Income and wealth disparities

Development of real GDP per capita, 1890 to 2022

Thais have median wealth per one adult person of $1,469 in 2016, increasing from $605 in 2010. In 2016, Thailand was [ranked 87th in Human Development Index](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_Human_Development_Index), and [70th in the inequality-adjusted HDI](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_inequality-adjusted_HDI).

In 2017, Thailand's median household income was ฿26,946 per month. Top quintile households had a 45.0% share of all income, while bottom quintile households had 7.1%. There were 26.9 million persons who had the bottom 40% of income earning less than ฿5,344 per person per month. During the [2013–2014 Thai political crisis](/wiki/2013%E2%80%932014_Thai_political_crisis), a survey found that anti-government [PDRC](/wiki/People%27s_Democratic_Reform_Committee) mostly (32%) had a monthly income of more than ฿50,000, while pro-government [UDD](/wiki/United_Front_for_Democracy_Against_Dictatorship) mostly (27%) had between ฿10,000 and ฿20,000.

In 2014, [Credit Suisse](/wiki/Credit_Suisse) reported that Thailand was the world's third most unequal country, behind Russia and India. The top 10% richest held 79% of the country's assets. The top 1% held 58% of the assets. The 50 richest Thai families had a total net worth accounting to 30% of GDP. [Bank of Thailand](/wiki/Bank_of_Thailand) reported that during 2006–16, Thailand's top 5% largest companies had 85% of all corporate revenue in the nation, and only 6% of the country's companies were in export industries, which made up 60% of the country's GDP.

In 2016, 5.81 million people lived in poverty, or 11.6 million people (17.2% of population) if "near poor" is included. The proportion of the poor relative to total population in each region was 12.96% in the Northeast, 12.35% in the South, and 9.83% in the North. In 2017, there were 14 million people who applied for social welfare (yearly income of less than ฿100,000 was required). In the first quarter of 2023, Thai household debts totalled 14.6 trillion baht or 89.2% of GDP; the average debt per household was approximately 500,000 baht. In 2016, there were estimated 30,000 homeless persons in the country.

### Exports and manufacturing

The economy of Thailand is heavily export-dependent, with exports accounting for more than two-thirds of gross domestic products (GDPs). Major exports include cars, computers, electrical appliances, [rice](/wiki/Rice_production_in_Thailand), textiles and footwear, fishery products, rubber, and jewellery. In 2022, Thailand's export of goods is worth roughly US$290 billion while its import worth roughly US$305 billion.

Substantial industries include electric appliances, components, computer components, and vehicles. Thailand's recovery from the 1997–1998 Asian financial crisis depended mainly on exports, among various other factors. As of 2012, the [Thai automotive industry](/wiki/Automotive_industry_in_Thailand) was the largest in [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia) and the [9th largest in the world](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_motor_vehicle_production). The Thailand industry has an annual output of near 1.5 million vehicles, mostly commercial vehicles.

Most of the vehicles built in Thailand are developed and licensed by foreign producers, mainly Japanese and American. The Thai car industry takes advantage of the [ASEAN Free Trade Area](/wiki/ASEAN_Free_Trade_Area) (AFTA) to find a market for many of its products. Eight manufacturers, five Japanese, two US, and [Tata](/wiki/Tata_Group) of India, produce pick-up trucks in Thailand. As of 2012, due to its favourable taxation for 2-door pick-ups at only 3–12% against 17–50% for passenger cars, Thailand was the second largest consumer of pick-up trucks in the world, after the US. In 2014, pick-ups accounted for 42% of all new vehicle sales in Thailand.

### Tourism

Further information: [Tourism in Thailand](/wiki/Tourism_in_Thailand)

[Wat Arun](/wiki/Wat_Arun), [Bangkok](/wiki/Bangkok), is among the best-known Thailand's landmarks.

Tourism makes up about 6% of the country's economy. Prior to the pandemic, Thailand was the world's eighth most visited country according to the [World Tourism rankings](/wiki/World_Tourism_rankings) compiled by the [United Nations World Tourism Organisation](/wiki/United_Nations_World_Tourism_Organization). In 2019, Thailand received 39.8 million international tourists, ahead of [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom) and [Germany](/wiki/Germany) and was the fourth highest in international tourism earning 60.5 billion [US dollars](/wiki/US_dollars).

Thailand was the most visited country in Southeast Asia in 2013, according to the World Tourism Organisation. Estimates of tourism receipts directly contributing to the Thai GDP of 12 trillion baht range from 9 per cent (1 trillion baht) (2013) to 16 per cent. When including the indirect effects of tourism, it is said to account for 20.2 per cent (2.4 trillion baht) of Thailand's GDP.

Asian tourists primarily visit Thailand for Bangkok and the historical, natural, and cultural sights in its vicinity. Western tourists not only visit Bangkok and surrounding areas; many travel to the southern beaches and islands. The north is the chief destination for trekking and [adventure travel](/wiki/Adventure_travel) with its diverse ethnic minority groups and forested mountains. The region hosting the fewest tourists is [Isan](/wiki/Isan). To accommodate foreign visitors, a separate tourism police with offices were set up in the major tourist areas and an emergency telephone number.

Thailand ranks as the worlds fifth largest [medical tourism](/wiki/Medical_tourism) destination in spending, according to the [World Travel and Tourism Council](/wiki/World_Travel_and_Tourism_Council), attracting over 2.5 million visitors in 2018, and is number one in Asia. The country is popular for the growing practice of [sex reassignment surgery](/wiki/Sex_reassignment_surgery) (SRS) and cosmetic surgery. In 2010–2012, more than 90% of medical tourists travelled to Thailand for SRS. [Prostitution in Thailand](/wiki/Prostitution_in_Thailand) and sex tourism also form a *de facto* part of the economy. Campaigns promote Thailand as exotic to attract tourists. One estimate published in 2003 placed the trade at US$4.3 billion per year or about 3% of the Thai economy. It is believed that at least 10% of tourist dollars are spent on the sex trade.

### Agriculture and natural resources

Further information: [Agriculture in Thailand](/wiki/Agriculture_in_Thailand)

Thailand has long been [one of the largest rice exporters in the world](/wiki/Rice_production_in_Thailand). Forty-nine per cent of Thailand's labour force is employed in agriculture.

Forty-nine per cent of Thailand's labour force is employed in [agriculture](/wiki/Agriculture_in_Thailand). This is down from 70% in 1980. Rice is the most important crop in the country and Thailand had long been the world's leading exporter of rice, until recently falling behind both India and Vietnam. Thailand has the highest percentage of arable land, 27.25%, of any state in the [Greater Mekong Subregion](/wiki/Greater_Mekong_Subregion). About 55% of the arable land area is used for rice production.

Agriculture has been experiencing a transition from labour-intensive and transitional methods to a more industrialised and competitive sector. Between 1962 and 1983, the agricultural sector grew by 4.1% per year on average and continued to grow at 2.2% between 1983 and 2007. The relative contribution of agriculture to GDP has declined while exports of goods and services have increased.

Furthermore, access to [biocapacity](/wiki/Biocapacity) in Thailand is lower than world average. In 2016, Thailand had 1.2 global hectares of biocapacity per person within its territory, a little less than world average of 1.6 global hectares per person. In contrast, in 2016, they used 2.5 global hectares of biocapacity—their [ecological footprint](/wiki/Ecological_footprint) of consumption. This means they use about twice as much biocapacity as Thailand contains, resulting in a deficit.

### Informal economy

[The Train Night Market](/wiki/Talat_Rotfai) in Bangkok

In 2012, it was estimated that informal workers comprised 62.6% of the Thai workforce. The [Ministry of Labour](/wiki/Ministry_of_Labour_(Thailand)) defines informal workers to be individuals who work in informal economies and do not have employee status under a given country's Labour Protection Act (LPA). The informal sector in Thailand has grown significantly over the past 60 years over the course of Thailand's gradual transition from an agriculture-based economy to becoming more industrialised and service-oriented. Between 1993 and 1995, ten per cent of the Thai labour force moved from the agricultural sector to urban and industrial jobs, especially in the manufacturing sector. It is estimated that between 1988 and 1995, the number of factory workers in the country doubled from two to four million, as Thailand's GDP tripled.

While the [Asian financial crisis](/wiki/1997_Asian_financial_crisis) that followed in 1997 hit the Thai economy hard, the industrial sector continued to expand under widespread deregulation, as Thailand was mandated to adopt a range of structural adjustment reforms upon receiving funding from the IMF and World Bank. These reforms implemented an agenda of increased privatisation and trade liberalisation in the country, and decreased federal subsidisation of public goods and utilities, agricultural price supports, and regulations on fair wages and labour conditions. Many migrant farmers took jobs in sweatshops and factories with few labour regulations and often exploitative conditions. Those that could not find formal factory work, including illegal migrants and the families of rural Thai migrants, are under the regulation imposed by the structural adjustment programmes. Scholars argue that the economic consequences and social costs of Thailand's labour reforms in the wake of the 1997 Asian financial crisis fell on individuals and families rather than the state.

[Songkran](/wiki/Songkran_(Thailand)) Festival, [Chiang Mai](/wiki/Chiang_Mai)'s ancient city wall

Informal labour in entertainment, nightlife, and the sex industry face additional vulnerabilities, including recruitment into circles of sexual exploitation and human trafficking. A 2012 study found that 64% of informal workers had not completed education beyond primary school. Many informal workers are also migrants, only some of which have legal status in the country. The informal labour sector is also not recognised under the Labour Protection Act (LPA). Thai social security policies fail to protect against workplace accidents and unemployment and retirement insurance. Many informal workers are not legally contracted for their employment, and many do not make a living wage. Tens of thousands of migrants from neighbouring countries face exploitation in a few industries, especially in fishing where slave-like conditions have been reported.

### Science and technology

Thailand ranked 45th in the [Global Innovation Index](/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2025. The [Ministry of Higher Education, Science, Research and Innovation](/wiki/Ministry_of_Higher_Education,_Science,_Research_and_Innovation) and [its agencies](/wiki/Ministry_of_Higher_Education,_Science,_Research_and_Innovation#Organization) oversees the development of science, technology, and research in Thailand. According to the National Research Council of Thailand, the country devoted 1.1% of its GDP to the research and development of science in 2019, with over 166,788 research and development personnel in full-time equivalent that year.

## Infrastructure

### Transportation

Main articles: [Transport in Thailand](/wiki/Transport_in_Thailand) and [List of airports in Thailand](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_Thailand)

The [BTS Skytrain](/wiki/BTS_Skytrain) is an elevated rapid transit system in Bangkok.

The [State Railway of Thailand](/wiki/State_Railway_of_Thailand) (SRT) operates all of Thailand's national rail lines. [Krung Thep Aphiwat Central Terminal](/wiki/Krung_Thep_Aphiwat_Central_Terminal) and [Bangkok (Hua Lamphong)](/wiki/Bangkok_(Hua_Lamphong)_railway_station) are the main termini of intercity routes. Phahonyothin and ICD [Lat Krabang](/wiki/Lat_Krabang) are the main freight terminals. As of 2025 SRT had 4,507 km (2,801 mi) of track, all of it [metre gauge](/wiki/Meter_gauge). Nearly all is single-track (2702.1 km), although some important sections around Bangkok are double (1,234.9 km or 767.3 mi) or triple-tracked (107 km or 66 mi), and there are plans to extend this.

[Rail transport in Bangkok](/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Bangkok) includes long-distance services. There are four rapid transit rail systems in the capital: the [BTS Skytrain](/wiki/BTS_Skytrain), [MRT](/wiki/MRT_(Bangkok)), [SRT Red Lines](/wiki/SRT_Red_Lines), and the [Airport Rail Link](/wiki/Airport_Rail_Link_(Bangkok)). In Bangkok, there were two failed rapid rail projects [Lavalin Skytrain](/wiki/Lavalin_Skytrain) and [Bangkok Elevated Road and Train System](/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System), before [Mass Rapid Transit Master Plan in Bangkok Metropolitan Region](/wiki/Mass_Rapid_Transit_Master_Plan_in_Bangkok_Metropolitan_Region) was endorsed by the cabinet on 27 September 1994 and implemented from 1995 to the present.

Thailand has 390,000 kilometres (240,000 miles) of highways. As of 2017, Thailand has over 462,133 roads and 37 million registered vehicles, 20 million of them motorbikes. A number of undivided two-lane highways have been converted into divided four-lane highways. Within the Bangkok Metropolitan Region, there are a number of [controlled-access highways](/wiki/Controlled-access_highways_in_Thailand). There are 4,125 public vans operating on 114 routes from Bangkok alone. Other forms of road transport includes [tuk-tuks](/wiki/Auto_rickshaw), taxis—with over 80,647 registered taxis nationwide as of 2018, vans ([minibus](/wiki/Minibus)), motorbike taxis, and [songthaews](/wiki/Songthaew).

As of 2012, Thailand has 103 airports with 63 paved runways, in addition to 6 heliports. The busiest airport in the country is Bangkok's [Suvarnabhumi Airport](/wiki/Suvarnabhumi_Airport).

### Energy

Further information: [Energy in Thailand](/wiki/Energy_in_Thailand)

75% of Thailand's electrical generation is powered by [natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas) in 2014. Coal-fired power plants produce an additional 20% of electricity, with the remainder coming from biomass, hydro, and biogas. Compared to other ASEAN's countries, Thailand is the largest importer of gas in weight. In 2022, Thailand's oil and gas production dropped by 19% and 17%, respectively.

The government, in 2018, has developed an Alternative Energy Development Plan 2018–2037 (AEDP 2018). The plan defines goals for the increase of renewable energy to almost 30,000 MW by 2037.

## Demographics

Thailand had a population of 65.95 million on 8 Jan 2025. There were 64,953,661 Thai nationals and 997,549 non-Thai residents. Thailand's first census in 1909 found the population to be 8.2 million. Thailand's population is largely rural, concentrated in the rice-growing areas of the central, northeastern, and northern regions. About 44.2% of Thailand's population lived in urban areas as of 2010, slowly increasing from 29.4% in the 1990 census and 31.1% in the 2000 census.

Thailand's government-sponsored family planning programme resulted in a dramatic decline in population growth from 3.1% in 1960 to around 0.4% today. In 1970, an average of 5.7 people lived in a Thai household; in 2022, the average Thai household size was 3 people. Now, more than 20% of its population is aged over 60 and has a low birth rate, posing economic challenges. The [sex ratio](/wiki/Sex_ratio) between male and female is 1.05, with Thailand having slightly more males. Thailand's [total fertility rate](/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) declined to 1.0 in 2024. This ranks Thailand among countries with [ultra-low fertility](/wiki/Sub-replacement_fertility) rates.

### Ethnic groups

Further information: [Ethnic groups in Thailand](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Thailand)

Hill tribes girls in the Northeast of Thailand

As of 2010, Thai people make up the majority of Thailand's population (95.9%). The remaining 4.1% of the population are Burmese (2.0%), others (1.3%), and unspecified (0.9%).

According to genetic research, the present-day Thai people were divided into three groups: the northern group (Khon Mueang) are closely related to the [Tai](/wiki/Tai_people) ethnic groups in [southern China](/wiki/Southern_China), the northeastern group ([Isan people](/wiki/Isan_people)) are mixed Tai and several [Austroasiatic](/wiki/Austroasiatic)-speaking ethnic groups, while the central and southern groups (formerly called Siamese) strongly share genetic profiles with the [Mon people](/wiki/Mon_people).

According to the Royal Thai Government's 2011 Country Report to the UN Committee responsible for the [International Convention for the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination](/wiki/International_Convention_on_the_Elimination_of_All_Forms_of_Racial_Discrimination), available from the Department of Rights and Liberties Promotion of the Thai Ministry of Justice, 62 ethnic communities are officially recognised in Thailand. Twenty million Central Thai (together with approximately 650,000 [Khorat Thai](/wiki/Khorat_Thai)) made up approximately 20,650,000 (34.1 per cent) of the state's population of 60,544,937 at the time of completion of the Mahidol University *Ethnolinguistic Maps of Thailand* data (1997).

The 2011 Thailand Country Report provides population numbers for mountain peoples ('hill tribes') and ethnic communities in the Northeast and is explicit about its main reliance on the Mahidol University Ethnolinguistic Maps of Thailand data. Thus, though over 3.288 million people in the Northeast alone could not be categorised, the population and percentages of other ethnic communities c. 1997 are known for all of Thailand and constitute minimum populations. In descending order, the largest (equal to or greater than 400,000) are a) 15,080,000 Lao (24.9 per cent) consisting of the Thai Lao (14 million) and other smaller Lao groups, namely the Thai Loei (400–500,000), Lao Lom (350,000), Lao Wiang/Klang (200,000), Lao Khrang (90,000), Lao Ngaew (30,000), and Lao Ti (10,000); b) six million Khon Muang (9.9 per cent, also called Northern Thais); c) 4.5 million Pak Tai (7.5 per cent, also called Southern Thais); d) 1.4 million Khmer Leu (2.3 per cent, also called Northern Khmer); e) 900,000 Malay (1.5%); f) 500,000 Nyaw (0.8 per cent); g) 470,000 Phu Thai (0.8 per cent); h) 400,000 Kuy/Kuay (also known as Suay) (0.7 per cent), and i) 350,000 Karen (0.6 per cent). [Thai Chinese](/wiki/Thai_Chinese), including those of significant Chinese origins, are 14% of the population, while Thais with partial Chinese ancestry comprise up to 40% of the population. [Thai Malays](/wiki/Thai_Malays) also represent 3% of the Thai population's heritage, with the remainder consisting of [Mons](/wiki/Mon_people), [Khmers](/wiki/Khmers), and various "[hill tribes](/wiki/Hill_tribe_(Thailand))".

Increasing numbers of migrants from neighbouring Myanmar, Laos, and Cambodia, as well as from Nepal and India, have pushed the total number of non-national residents to around 3.5 million as of 2009, up from an estimated 2 million in 2008. Some 41,000 [Britons](/wiki/British_people) and 20,000 [Australians](/wiki/Australians) live in Thailand.

### Language

Main article: [Languages of Thailand](/wiki/Languages_of_Thailand)

An ethnolinguistic map of Thailand

The Silajaruek of [Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom) are hundreds of stone inscriptions that form a historical record of the period.

[Thai](/wiki/Thai_language) is the official language. It is a [Kra–Dai language](/wiki/Kra%E2%80%93Dai_language) closely related to [Lao](/wiki/Lao_language), [Shan](/wiki/Shan_language) in Myanmar, and numerous smaller languages spoken in an arc from [Hainan](/wiki/Hainan) and [Yunnan](/wiki/Yunnan) south to the Chinese border. It is the principal language of education and government and spoken throughout the country. The standard is based on the dialect of the central Thai people, and it is written in the [Thai alphabet](/wiki/Thai_alphabet), an [abugida](/wiki/Abugida) script that evolved from the [Khmer alphabet](/wiki/Khmer_alphabet).

Sixty-two languages are recognised by the Royal Thai Government. For the purposes of the national census, four dialects of Thai exist; these partly coincide with regional designations, such as [Southern Thai](/wiki/Southern_Thai_language) and [Northern Thai](/wiki/Northern_Thai_language).

The largest of Thailand's minority languages is the [Lao](/wiki/Lao_language) dialect of [Isan](/wiki/Isan_language) spoken in the northeastern provinces. In the far south, [Kelantan-Pattani Malay](/wiki/Kelantan-Pattani_Malay) is the primary language of Malay Muslims. Varieties of Chinese are also spoken by the large [Thai Chinese](/wiki/Thai_Chinese) population, with the [Teochew dialect](/wiki/Teochew_dialect) best-represented. Numerous tribal languages are also spoken, including many [Austroasiatic languages](/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages) such as [Mon](/wiki/Mon_language), [Khmer](/wiki/Khmer_language), [Lawa](/wiki/Lawa_language) and [Mlabri](/wiki/Mlabri_language); [Austronesian languages](/wiki/Austronesian_languages) such as [Cham](/wiki/Cham_language), [Moken](/wiki/Moken_language) and [Urak Lawoi'](/wiki/Urak_Lawoi%27_language); [Sino-Tibetan languages](/wiki/Sino-Tibetan_languages) like [Akha](/wiki/Akha_language) and [Karen](/wiki/Karen_languages); and other [Tai languages](/wiki/Tai_languages) such as [Phu Thai](/wiki/Phu_Thai_language), and [Saek](/wiki/Saek_language). [Hmong](/wiki/Hmong_language) is a member of the [Hmong–Mien languages](/wiki/Hmong%E2%80%93Mien_languages), which is now regarded as a language family of its own.

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in Thailand](/wiki/Religion_in_Thailand)

Religion in Thailand (2018)

1. [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism_in_Thailand) (93.5%)
2. [Islam](/wiki/Islam_in_Thailand) (5.37%)
3. [Christianity](/wiki/Christianity_in_Thailand) (1.13%)
4. [Other](/wiki/Religion_in_Thailand) (0.04%)

The country's most prevalent religion is [Theravada](/wiki/Theravada) Buddhism, which is an integral part of Thai identity and culture. Active participation in Buddhism is among the highest in the world. Thailand has the second-largest number of [Buddhists](/wiki/Buddhist) in the world after China. According to the 2018 [National Statistical Office](/wiki/National_Statistical_Office_(Thailand)) data, 93.46% of the country's population self-identified as Buddhists.

*[Samanera](/wiki/Samanera)* of [Theravada](/wiki/Theravada) Buddhism, the most practised religion in Thailand

[Muslims](/wiki/Islam_in_Thailand) constitute the second largest religious group in Thailand, comprising 5.37% of the population in 2018. Islam is concentrated mostly in the country's southernmost provinces: [Pattani](/wiki/Pattani_Province), [Yala](/wiki/Yala_Province), [Satun](/wiki/Satun_Province), [Narathiwat](/wiki/Narathiwat_Province), and part of [Songkhla](/wiki/Songkhla_Province), which are predominantly [Malay](/wiki/Malays_(ethnic_group)), most of whom are [Sunni Muslims](/wiki/Sunni_Muslim). Christians represented 1.13% of the population in 2018, with the remaining population consisting of [Hindus](/wiki/Hinduism_in_Thailand) and [Sikhs](/wiki/Sikhism_in_Thailand), who live mostly in the country's cities. There is also a small [Jewish community in Thailand](/wiki/Jews_and_Judaism_in_Thailand) dating back to the 17th century.

The constitution does not name an official state religion, and provides for freedom of religion. There have been no widespread reports of societal abuses or discrimination based on religious belief or practice. Thai law officially recognises five religious groups: Buddhists, Muslims, Brahmin-Hindus, Sikhs, and Christians. However, some laws are inspired from Buddhist practices, such as banning alcohol sales on religious holidays.

### Education

Main article: [Education in Thailand](/wiki/Education_in_Thailand)

[Chulalongkorn University](/wiki/Chulalongkorn_University), established in 1917, is the oldest university in Thailand.

In 1995, as minister of education, [Sukavich Rangsitpollaid](/wiki/Sukavich_Rangsitpol) let out his plans for educational reform in Thailand. The reform was considered a landmark movement after nearly 100 years of education under the previous system. Thailand's youth literacy rate was 98.1% in 2015. Education is provided by a school system of kindergartens, primary, lower secondary and upper secondary schools, numerous vocational colleges, and universities. Education is compulsory up to and including age 14, while the government is mandated to provide free education through to age 17. Issues concerning university entrance have been in constant upheaval for a number of years. The country is also one of the few that still mandates uniform up to the university years, which is still a subject of ongoing debate.

In 2013, the [Ministry of Information and Communication Technology](/wiki/Ministry_of_Information_and_Communication_Technology_(Thailand)) announced that 27,231 schools would receive classroom-level access to [high-speed internet](/wiki/Internet). However, the country's educational infrastructure was still underprepared for online teaching, as smaller and more remote schools were particularly hindered by COVID-19 restrictions.

The number of higher education institutions in Thailand has grown over the past decades to 156 officially. The two top-ranking universities in Thailand are [Chulalongkorn University](/wiki/Chulalongkorn_University) and [Mahidol University](/wiki/Mahidol_University). Thai universities' research output is still relatively low, even though the country's journal publications increased by 20% between 2011 and 2016. Thailand has the second highest number of English-medium private international schools in [Southeast Asian Nations](/wiki/ASEAN). [Cram schools](/wiki/Cram_school) are especially popular for university entrance exams.

Students in ethnic minority areas score consistently lower in standardised national and international tests. This is likely due to unequal allocation of educational resources, weak teacher training, poverty, and low Thai language skill, the language of the tests. As of 2020, Thailand was ranked 89th out of 100 countries globally for English proficiency. Thailand is the third most popular study destination in ASEAN. The number of international degree students in Thailand increased by 9.7 times between 1999 and 2012, from 1,882 to 20,309 students. Most of international students come from neighbour countries like China, Myanmar, Cambodia and Vietnam.

### Health

Main articles: [Health in Thailand](/wiki/Health_in_Thailand), [Healthcare in Thailand](/wiki/Healthcare_in_Thailand), and [Hospitals in Thailand](/wiki/Hospitals_in_Thailand)

[Siriraj Hospital](/wiki/Siriraj_Hospital) in Bangkok, the oldest and largest hospital in Thailand

Thailand ranks world's sixth, and Asia's first in the 2019 [Global Health Security Index](/wiki/Global_Health_Security_Index) of global health security capabilities in 195 countries, making it the only [developing country](/wiki/Developing_country) on the world's top ten. Thailand had 62 hospitals accredited by [Joint Commission International](/wiki/Joint_Commission). In 2002, [Bumrungrad](/wiki/Bumrungrad_International_Hospital) became the first hospital in Asia to meet the standard.

Health and medical care is overseen by the [Ministry of Public Health](/wiki/Ministry_of_Public_Health_(Thailand)) (MOPH), with total national expenditures on health amounting to 4.3 per cent of GDP in 2009. [Non-communicable diseases](/wiki/Non-communicable_disease) form the major burden of morbidity and mortality, while infectious diseases including malaria and tuberculosis, as well as traffic accidents, are also important public health issues.

In December 2018, the interim parliament voted to legalise the use of [cannabis](/wiki/Cannabis) for medical reasons, making Thailand the first Southeast Asian country to allow the use of [medical cannabis](/wiki/Medical_cannabis).

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Thailand](/wiki/Culture_of_Thailand)

Thai women wearing [sabai](/wiki/Sabai), [Jim Thompson House](/wiki/Jim_Thompson_House)

Thai culture and traditions incorporate influences from India, China, Cambodia, and the rest of Southeast Asia. Thailand's national religion, [Theravada Buddhism](/wiki/Theravada), is central to modern [Thai identity](/wiki/Thai_identity). [Thai Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism_in_Thailand) has evolved over millennia from the [Mon kingdom of Dvaravati](/wiki/Dvaravati) in [Central Thailand](/wiki/Central_Thailand) (6th–11th century). Nowadays it includes many regional beliefs originating from [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism), [animism](/wiki/Animism), as well as ancestor worship. The [official calendar](/wiki/Thai_solar_calendar) in Thailand is based on the Eastern version of the [Buddhist Era](/wiki/Buddhist_calendar) (BE). The ancient Thai identity originates from the [Tai-speaking groups](/wiki/Tai_peoples) who migrated from southern China to Southeast Asia in 6th to 11th century. The Tai brought the [Thai language](/wiki/Thai_language) and established city-states. They mixed with the existing Austro-Asiatic groups like the [Mon](/wiki/Mon_people) and [Khmer](/wiki/Khmer_people) while absorbing their cultures and beliefs in the [Chao Phraya Basin](/wiki/Chao_Phraya_River). [King Ramkhamhaeng](/wiki/Ramkhamhaeng)'s reign (1279-1298) marked the inception of a unified Thai identity and [Sukhothai](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom) as a pivotal kingdom in the region. The prevalent culture of [Central Thailand](/wiki/Central_Thailand) formed the basis of the modern Thai identity. This was [spread nationwide](/wiki/Thaification) by the [Phibun](/wiki/Plaek_Phibunsongkhram) regime to assimilate the ethnic groups in the 1940s.

Several ethnic groups mediated change between their traditional local culture, national Thai, and global cultural influences. [Overseas Chinese](/wiki/Overseas_Chinese) also form a significant part of Thai society, particularly in and around Bangkok. Thai Chinese businesses prosper as part of the larger [bamboo network](/wiki/Bamboo_network).

People floating krathong rafts during the [Loi Krathong](/wiki/Loi_Krathong) festival in Chiang Mai, Thailand

Respect for elderly and superiors (by age, position, monks, or certain professions) is Thai [mores](/wiki/Mores), reflecting in many classes of [honorifics](/wiki/Thai_honorifics). *[Wai](/wiki/Thai_greeting)* is a familiar Thai greeting, and is generally offered first by a person who is younger or lower in social status and position. Older siblings have duties to younger ones.

[Taboos](/wiki/Taboo) in Thai culture include touching someone's head or pointing with the feet, as the head is considered the most sacred and the foot the lowest part of the body.

### Art

Main article: [Thai art](/wiki/Thai_art)

Scene from the [Ramakien](/wiki/Ramakien) depicted on a mural at [Wat Phra Kaew](/wiki/Wat_Phra_Kaew)

The origins of Thai art were influenced by [Buddhist art](/wiki/Buddhist_art) and by scenes from the Indian epics. Traditional Thai [sculpture](/wiki/Sculpture) almost exclusively depicts [images of the Buddha](/wiki/Buddha_image), being very similar with the other styles from [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia). Traditional Thai [paintings](/wiki/Painting) usually consist of book illustrations, and painted ornamentation of buildings such as [palaces](/wiki/Palace) and [temples](/wiki/Temple).

Thai art was influenced by indigenous civilisations of the [Mon](/wiki/Dvaravati) and other civilisations. By the Sukothai and Ayutthaya periods, Thai had developed into its own unique style and was later further influenced by the other Asian styles, mostly by [Sri Lankan](/wiki/Indian_art) and [Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_art). Thai sculpture and painting, and the royal courts provided patronage, erecting temples and other religious shrines as acts of merit or to commemorate important events.

Traditional Thai paintings showed subjects in two [dimensions](/wiki/Dimension) without [perspective](/wiki/Perspective_(graphical)). The size of each element in the picture reflected its degree of importance. The primary technique of [composition](/wiki/Composition_(visual_arts)) is that of apportioning areas: the main elements are isolated from each other by space transformers. This eliminated the intermediate ground, which would otherwise imply perspective. Perspective was introduced only as a result of [Western](/wiki/Western_world) influence in the mid-19th century. Monk artist [Khrua In Khong](/wiki/Khrua_In_Khong) is well known as the first artist to introduce [linear perspective](/wiki/Linear_perspective) to Thai traditional art.

The most frequent narrative subjects for paintings were or are: the [Jataka](/wiki/Jataka) stories, episodes from the life of the [Buddha](/wiki/Gautama_Buddha), the Buddhist [heavens](/wiki/Heaven) and [hells](/wiki/Hell), themes derived from the Thai versions of the *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata*, and scenes of daily life. Some of the scenes are influenced by [Thai folklore](/wiki/Thai_folklore) instead of following strict Buddhist [iconography](/wiki/Iconography).

### Architecture

Main article: [Architecture of Thailand](/wiki/Architecture_of_Thailand)

Two sculptures in front of the eastern gate to the main chapel of [Wat Arun](/wiki/Wat_Arun)

The [Ayutthaya Kingdom](/wiki/Ayutthaya_Kingdom) movement is designed to display might and riches. The temples in Ayutthaya seldom built eaves stretching from the masterhead. [Buddhist temples in Thailand](/wiki/Buddhist_temples_in_Thailand) are known as "[wats](/wiki/Wat)", from the [Pāḷi](/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B8%B7i) *vāṭa*, meaning an enclosure: a temple has an enclosing wall that divides it from the secular world. Wat [architecture](/wiki/Architecture) demonstrates many differences in layout and style, but they all adhere to the same principles.

### Literature

Main article: [Thai literature](/wiki/Thai_literature)

Thai literature has had a long history. Even before the establishment of the Sukhothai Kingdom there existed oral and written works.

During the [Sukhothai Kingdom](/wiki/Sukhothai_Kingdom), most literary works were written in simple prose with certain alliteration schemes. Major works include King [Ram Khamhaeng Inscription](/wiki/Ram_Khamhaeng_Inscription) describing life at the time, which is considered the first literary work in Thai script, but some historians questioned its authenticity. *Trai Phum [Phra Ruang](/wiki/Phra_Ruang)*, written in 1345 by King [Maha Thammaracha I](/wiki/Maha_Thammaracha_I), expounds Buddhist philosophy based on an extensive study with reference to over 30 sacred texts and could be considered the nation's first piece of research [dissertation](/wiki/Dissertation).

Sculptures of [Phra Aphai Mani](/wiki/Phra_Aphai_Mani) and the Mermaid from the [epic poem](/wiki/Epic_poem) *[Phra Aphai Mani](/wiki/Phra_Aphai_Mani)*, a work of [Sunthorn Phu](/wiki/Sunthorn_Phu)

During the [Ayutthaya Kingdom](/wiki/Ayutthaya_Kingdom), new [poetic](/wiki/Thai_poetry) forms were created, with different rhyme schemes and metres. It is common to find a combination of different poetic forms in one poetic work. *Lilit [Yuan Phai](/wiki/Yuan_Phai)* is a narrative poem describing [the war](/wiki/Ayutthaya%E2%80%93Lan_Na_War_(1441%E2%80%931474)) between King [Borommatrailokkanat](/wiki/Borommatrailokkanat) of Ayutthaya and Prince [Tilokaraj](/wiki/Tilokaraj) of [Lan Na](/wiki/Lan_Na). One literary work is *Kap He Ruea*, composed by Prince [Thammathibet](/wiki/Thammathibet) in the *[nirat](/wiki/Nirat)* tradition. Traditionally, the verse is sung during the [royal barge](/wiki/Royal_barge) procession and has been the model for subsequent poets to emulate. The same prince also composed the greatly admired *Kap Ho Khlong* on the Visit to Than Thongdaeng and *Kap Ho Khlong Nirat Phrabat*. The [Thonburi](/wiki/Thonburi_Kingdom) period produced *[Ramakien](/wiki/Ramakien)*, a verse drama contributed by [King Taksin the Great](/wiki/King_Taksin_the_Great).

During the 18th century [Rattanakosin](/wiki/Rattanakosin_Kingdom) period, which still fought with the Burmese, many of the early Rattanakosin works dealt with war and military strategy. Some examples are *Nirat Rop Phama Thi Tha Din Daeng*, *Phleng Yao Rop Phama Thi Nakhon Si Thammarat*. There were also verse recitals with musical accompaniment, such as [Mahori](/wiki/Mahori) telling the story of *[Kaki](/wiki/Kaki_Klon_Suphap)* and [Sepha](/wiki/Sepha), relating the story of *[Khun Chang Khun Phaen](/wiki/Khun_Chang_Khun_Phaen)*. Other recitals include [Sri Thanonchai](/wiki/Sri_Thanonchai). The Thai poet [Sunthorn Phu](/wiki/Sunthorn_Phu) is known as "the bard of Rattanakosin" ([Thai](/wiki/Thai_language): กวีเอกแห่งกรุงรัตนโกสินทร์). Sunthorn Phu is best known for his epic poem *[Phra Aphai Mani](/wiki/Phra_Aphai_Mani)*, a versified fantasy-adventure novel, a genre of Siamese literature known as *nithan kham klon* ([Thai](/wiki/Thai_language): นิทานคำกลอน).

Some of the most well-known modern Thai writers include [Kukrit Pramoj](/wiki/Kukrit_Pramoj), [Kulap Saipradit](/wiki/Kulap_Saipradit), (penname [Siburapha](/wiki/Siburapha)), Suweeriya Sirisingh (penname Botan), [Chart Korbjitti](/wiki/Chart_Korbjitti), [Prabda Yoon](/wiki/Prabda_Yoon), and [Duanwad Pimwana](/wiki/Duanwad_Pimwana).

### Music and dance

Main articles: [Music of Thailand](/wiki/Music_of_Thailand) and [Dance in Thailand](/wiki/Dance_in_Thailand)

[Khon](/wiki/Khon) show

Aside from folk and regional dances (southern Thailand's [Menora](/wiki/Menora_(dance)) and [Ramwong](/wiki/Ramwong), for example), the two major forms of Thai classical dance drama are [Khon](/wiki/Khon) and [Lakhon nai](/wiki/Lakhon_nai). In the beginning, both were exclusively court entertainments, and it was not until much later that a popular style of dance theatre, [likay](/wiki/Likay), evolved as a diversion for common folk. Folk dance forms include dance theatre forms like [likay](/wiki/Likay), numerous regional dances (*ram*), the ritual dance [ram muay](/wiki/Ram_muay), and homage to the teacher, [wai khru](/wiki/Wai_khru_ram_muay). Both ram muay and wai khru take place before all traditional [muay Thai](/wiki/Muay_Thai) matches.

The three primary classical ensembles are the [Piphat](/wiki/Piphat), [Khrueang sai](/wiki/Khrueang_sai), and [Mahori](/wiki/Mahori). Mahori employ small ching hand cymbals.

### Media

[Jeff Satur](/wiki/Jeff_Satur) and [Lalisa Manobal](/wiki/Lalisa_Manobal)

Main articles: [Mass media in Thailand](/wiki/Mass_media_in_Thailand), [Censorship in Thailand](/wiki/Censorship_in_Thailand), [Cinema of Thailand](/wiki/Cinema_of_Thailand), and [Thai television soap opera](/wiki/Thai_television_soap_opera)

Thai films are exported and exhibited in [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia). Thai cinema has developed its own unique identity.

The Thai heist thriller film *[Bad Genius](/wiki/Bad_Genius_(2017_film))* (2017) was one of the most internationally successful Thai films; it broke Thai film earning records in several Asian countries, Bad Genius won in 12 categories at the 27th [Suphannahong National Film Awards](/wiki/Suphannahong_National_Film_Awards), and also won the Jury Award at the 16th [New York Asian Film Festival](/wiki/New_York_Asian_Film_Festival) with a worldwide collection of more than $42 million.

*[Shutter](/wiki/Shutter_(2004_film))* (2004) was one of the best-known Thai horror movies and was recognised worldwide. Films such as *[Ong-Bak: Muay Thai Warrior](/wiki/Ong-Bak:_Muay_Thai_Warrior)* (2003) and *[Tom-Yum-Goong](/wiki/Tom-Yum-Goong)* (2005), starring [Tony Jaa](/wiki/Tony_Jaa), feature distinctive aspects of Thai martial arts "[Muay Thai](/wiki/Muay_Thai)". Thailand television dramas, known as [Lakorn](/wiki/Thai_television_soap_opera), have become popular in Thailand and regionally.

The entertainment industries are estimated to have directly contributed $2.1 billion in GDP to the Thai economy in 2011. They also directly supported 86,600 jobs. Several Thai [dance-pop](/wiki/Dance-pop) artists are internationally successful like [Lisa](/wiki/Lisa_(rapper)), [Violette Wautier](/wiki/Violette_Wautier), [Jeff Satur](/wiki/Jeff_Satur), and [Tata Young](/wiki/Tata_Young).

### Cuisine

Further information: [Thai cuisine](/wiki/Thai_cuisine)

**Clockwise from top left**: [Chicken massaman curry](/wiki/Massaman_curry); [mango sticky rice](/wiki/Mango_sticky_rice); [pad Thai](/wiki/Pad_Thai); and [tom yum](/wiki/Tom_yum)

Thai cuisine is one of the most popular in the world. Common ingredients include [garlic](/wiki/Garlic), [lemongrass](/wiki/Lemongrass), [kaffir lime](/wiki/Kaffir_lime), [galangal](/wiki/Galangal), [turmeric](/wiki/Turmeric), [coriander](/wiki/Coriander), and [coconut milk](/wiki/Coconut_milk). Each region of Thailand has its specialities: *kaeng khiao wan* ([green curry](/wiki/Green_curry)) in the central region, *som tam* ([green papaya salad](/wiki/Green_papaya_salad)) in the northeast, *[khao soi](/wiki/Khao_soi)* in the north, and [*massaman* curry](/wiki/Massaman_curry) in the south.

In 2017, seven Thai dishes appeared on a list of the "World's 50 Best Foods"—an online worldwide poll by [CNN Travel](/wiki/CNN_Travel). Thailand had more dishes on the list than any other country. They were: *[tom yam](/wiki/Tom_yam) goong* (4th), *[pad Thai](/wiki/Pad_Thai)* (5th), *som tam* (6th), *massaman* curry (10th), [green curry](/wiki/Green_curry) (19th), [Thai fried rice](/wiki/Thai_fried_rice) (24th) and *[nam tok mu](/wiki/Nam_tok_mu)* (36th). Two desserts were also listed in CNN's 50 Best Desserts Around The World: [mango sticky rice](/wiki/Mango_sticky_rice) and [tub tim krob](/wiki/Tub_tim_krob).

The staple food in Thailand is rice, particularly [jasmine rice](/wiki/Jasmine_rice), which forms part of almost every meal. Thailand is a leading exporter of rice, and Thais consume over 100 kg of milled rice per person per year. Thailand is also the world leader in edible insect industry and well known for its street food; Bangkok is sometimes called the street food capital of the world.

### Units of measurement

Further information: [Thai units of measurement](/wiki/Thai_units_of_measurement)

Thailand generally uses the [metric system](/wiki/Metric_system), but [traditional units of measurement](/wiki/Thai_units_of_measurement) for land area are used, and [imperial units](/wiki/Imperial_units) of measurement are occasionally used for building materials. Years are numbered as B.E. ([Buddhist Era](/wiki/Thai_solar_calendar)) in educational settings, civil service, government, contracts, and newspaper datelines. However, in banking, and increasingly in industry and commerce, standard Western year (Christian or [Common Era](/wiki/Common_Era)) counting is the standard practice.

### Sports

Main article: [Sport in Thailand](/wiki/Sport_in_Thailand)

See also: [Thailand at the Olympics](/wiki/Thailand_at_the_Olympics), [Rugby union in Thailand](/wiki/Rugby_union_in_Thailand), [Golf in Thailand](/wiki/Golf_in_Thailand), [Football in Thailand](/wiki/Football_in_Thailand), and [List of sporting events held in Thailand](/wiki/List_of_sporting_events_held_in_Thailand)

[Muay Thai](/wiki/Muay_Thai), Thailand's signature sport

[Muay Thai](/wiki/Muay_Thai) (lit. 'Thai boxing') is a [combat sport](/wiki/Combat_sport) that uses stand-up striking along with various [clinching](/wiki/Clinch_fighting) techniques. Muay Thai became widespread internationally in the late-20th to 21st centuries. Famous practitioners include [Buakaw Banchamek](/wiki/Buakaw_Banchamek), [Samart Payakaroon](/wiki/Samart_Payakaroon), and [Apidej Sit-Hirun](/wiki/Apidej_Sit-Hirun). [Association football](/wiki/Association_football) has overtaken Muay Thai as the most widely followed sport in Thailand. The [Thailand national football team](/wiki/Thailand_national_football_team) has played the [AFC Asian Cup](/wiki/AFC_Asian_Cup) six times and reached the semifinals in 1972. The country has hosted the Asian Cup twice, in [1972](/wiki/1972_AFC_Asian_Cup) and in [2007](/wiki/2007_AFC_Asian_Cup) (along with Indonesia, Malaysia, and Vietnam for the 2007).

[Volleyball](/wiki/Volleyball) is rapidly growing as one of the most popular sports. The [women's team](/wiki/Thailand_women%27s_national_volleyball_team) has often participated in the [World Championship](/wiki/FIVB_Volleyball_Women%27s_World_Championship), [World Cup](/wiki/FIVB_Volleyball_Women%27s_World_Cup), and [World Grand Prix](/wiki/FIVB_Volleyball_World_Grand_Prix) [Asian Championship](/wiki/Asian_Championship). They have won the [Asian Championship](/wiki/Asian_Women%27s_Volleyball_Championship) twice and the [Asian Cup](/wiki/AVC_Cup_for_Women) once. [Takraw](/wiki/Takraw) is a sport native to Thailand in which the players hit a rattan ball and are only allowed to use their feet, knees, chest, and head to touch the ball. [Sepak takraw](/wiki/Sepak_takraw) is a form of this sport which is similar to volleyball. A rather similar game but played only with the feet is [buka ball](/wiki/Buka_ball).

[Rugby](/wiki/Rugby_football) is also a growing sport in Thailand with the [Thailand national rugby union team](/wiki/Thailand_national_rugby_union_team) rising to be ranked 61st in the world. Thailand became the first country in the world to host an international 80 welterweight rugby tournament in 2005. Thailand has also attracts golfers from Japan, Korea, and Western countries. There are more than 200 world-class golf courses nationwide. For basketball, the Chang Thailand Slammers won the 2011 [ASEAN Basketball League](/wiki/ASEAN_Basketball_League) Championship. The [Thailand national basketball team](/wiki/Thailand_national_basketball_team) had its most successful year at the [1966 Asian Games](/wiki/Basketball_at_the_1966_Asian_Games) where it won the silver medal.

[Lumpinee Boxing Stadium](/wiki/Lumpinee_Boxing_Stadium)

The [Lumpinee Boxing Stadium](/wiki/Lumpinee_Boxing_Stadium) originally sited at [Rama IV Road](/wiki/Rama_IV_Road) near [Lumphini Park](/wiki/Lumphini_Park) hosted its final Muay Thai boxing matches on 8 February 2014 after the venue first opened in December 1956. On 11 February 2014, the stadium was relocated to Ram Intra Road due to the new venue's capacity. [Thammasat Stadium](/wiki/Thammasat_Stadium) in Bangkok was built for the [1998 Asian Games](/wiki/1998_Asian_Games). [Rajamangala National Stadium](/wiki/Rajamangala_National_Stadium) is the biggest sporting arena in Thailand, with a capacity of around 50,000.
