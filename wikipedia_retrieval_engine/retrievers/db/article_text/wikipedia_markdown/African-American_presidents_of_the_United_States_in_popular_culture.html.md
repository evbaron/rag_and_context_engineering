# African-American presidents of the United States in popular culture

Before and after the election of [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama) as the first [African American](/wiki/African_American) [president of the United States](/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) in 2008, the idea of a Black president has been explored by various writers in novels (including [science fiction](/wiki/Science_fiction)), films and television, as well as other media. Numerous actors have portrayed a Black president. Such portrayals have occurred in both serious works and comedies.

## Effect of media depictions

As writers and directors cast Black actors as president in several memorable portrayals, depictions of fictional Black presidents may have accustomed Americans to accept a Black man as president. Actor [Dennis Haysbert](/wiki/Dennis_Haysbert), who played a Black president on the hit show *[24](/wiki/24_(TV_series))*, said the portrayal "may have opened the eyes, the minds and the hearts of people because the character was so well liked." The show also raised the issue of whether television series "like political trial ballons, can ready the populace for change."

After [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama)'s election, the television show the *[Cosby Show](/wiki/Cosby_Show)* was cited for what has been termed the "Huxtable effect" for the influence of its "warmhearted" portrayal, "free of street conflicts and ghetto stereotypes - that broke ground for its depiction of an upwardly mobile Black family." The show has even been cited by some observers as a factor in Obama's victory.

## Novels

See also: [Lists of fictional presidents of the United States](/wiki/Lists_of_fictional_presidents_of_the_United_States)

In 1926, Brazilian writer [Monteiro Lobato](/wiki/Monteiro_Lobato) published *[O Presidente Negro](/wiki/O_Presidente_Negro)* ("The Black President"), set in a dystopian future U.S. in 2228. It features Jim Roy, a fictional black president.

In 1964 [Irving Wallace](/wiki/Irving_Wallace) published *[The Man](/wiki/The_Man_(1964_novel))*, a popular novel addressing the idea of a black president, named Douglass Dilman in the book. Recently a critic described it as a window into "[Kennedy](/wiki/John_Fitzgerald_Kennedy)-era racial pathologies", despite the author's liberal attitude. It included the portrayal of attractive multiracial or "[mulatto](/wiki/Mulatto)" women who could [pass](/wiki/Passing_(racial_identity)) for white, as does the hero Dilman's own light-skinned daughter. *The Man*—which was made into a [1972 movie](/wiki/The_Man_(1972_film)) starring [James Earl Jones](/wiki/James_Earl_Jones) as Dilman—noted factors against a black president being elected in America, and Dilman's coming to power through an unlikely series of circumstances of succession.

Other novels featuring a first black president include [Philip K. Dick](/wiki/Philip_K._Dick)'s *[The Crack in Space](/wiki/The_Crack_in_Space)* (1966), T. Ernesto Bethancourt's young adult novel *[The Tomorrow Connection](/wiki/The_Tomorrow_People_(novel))* (1984).

## Stand-up comedy

After civil rights and voting rights legislation was passed in 1964 and 1965, the move of blacks into full political participation began. Portrayals of blacks as president began to appear in comedians' routines. In the early years of his career in the 1960s, comedian [Bill Cosby](/wiki/Bill_Cosby) frequently told jokes along racial lines, including one about an imaginary first black president. He stopped when he decided to reach a wider audience.

In 1983 at age 22, [Eddie Murphy](/wiki/Eddie_Murphy) (who was born the same year as Obama) enacted a parody of a black president in one of his stand-up routines, *[Eddie Murphy Delirious](/wiki/Eddie_Murphy_Delirious)*, filmed in [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.)

## Movies and television

Writers and directors have featured a black man as president in several memorable portrayals. There have been film and television proposals based on the idea, as well.
The first movie portrayal of a black American president was probably that of [Sammy Davis Jr.](/wiki/Sammy_Davis_Jr.) in the [1933](/wiki/1933_in_film) film *[Rufus Jones for President](/wiki/Rufus_Jones_for_President)*. In this short musical comedy, the 7-year-old Davis is told by his mother, portrayed by [Ethel Waters](/wiki/Ethel_Waters), that "anyone can become president, and later dreams of his own inauguration". Outside the dreams, the film reflects contemporary racist attitudes.

The 1941 musical movie *[Babes on Broadway](/wiki/Babes_on_Broadway)* included [Judy Garland](/wiki/Judy_Garland) in black male [drag](/wiki/Drag_(clothing)) singing a song "Franklin Delano Jones", about the first black president of the United States.

"When Rod Serling adapted [Irving Wallace](/wiki/Irving_Wallace)'s "[The Man](/wiki/The_Man_(Wallace_novel))" to the screen in 1972—it was a joint production of [Paramount Pictures](/wiki/Paramount_Pictures) and [ABC Circle Films](/wiki/ABC_Circle_Films), originally intended to air on [ABC](/wiki/American_Broadcasting_Company)'s made-for-television *Movie of the Week* series, but the network chose not to air it, prompting Paramount to release the film to theaters instead—the political climate had changed sufficiently that he could promote Douglass Dilman from survivor to competitor—a genuine leader who, after standing up to his white rivals, vows to win the presidency through 'legitimate' electoral means." With [James Earl Jones](/wiki/James_Earl_Jones) starring in 1972, the film version had a heroic black man as president, who ended the film in a position of [moral authority](/wiki/Moral_authority).

In 1977 comedian [Richard Pryor](/wiki/Richard_Pryor) portrayed the first black president of the United States in a skit on *[The Richard Pryor Show](/wiki/The_Richard_Pryor_Show)*, his short-lived foray on [NBC](/wiki/NBC) television.

[Lizzie Borden](/wiki/Lizzie_Borden_(director))'s 1983 science fiction film *[Born in Flames](/wiki/Born_in_Flames)* about a radical feminist insurgency, set in an alternative United States Socialist Democracy, features a black president.

The 1987 animated series *[Spiral Zone](/wiki/Spiral_Zone)* is the first television show in history to show a serious depiction of an African-American president of the United States in the episode [The "Imposter"](https://web.archive.org/web/20090417133344/http://spiral-zone.com/tonka/12.htm).

In the 1993 science-fiction series *[SeaQuest DSV](/wiki/SeaQuest_DSV)*, the unnamed president of the United States featured in the first season episode "Better Than Martians" is portrayed by African-American actor [Steven Williams](/wiki/Steven_Williams).

In the 1997 science-fiction film *[The Fifth Element](/wiki/The_Fifth_Element)*, character actor [Tom Lister, Jr.](/wiki/Tom_Lister,_Jr.) played President Lindberg, the commander-in-chief of not just the United States of America but the planet [Earth](/wiki/Earth). His competence to lead is not questioned due to his race. In fact, his skin color is never mentioned.

A generation after *The Man*, the 1998 science fiction film *[Deep Impact](/wiki/Deep_Impact_(film))* featured black actor [Morgan Freeman](/wiki/Morgan_Freeman) as president Tom Beck. Critic Louis Bayard noticed that Dennis Haysbert seemed to adopt Freeman's cadences for his own role as president.

[Ernie Hudson](/wiki/Ernie_Hudson) played President Westwood in the 1999 direct-to-video movie *Stealth Fighter*.

In the hit show *[24](/wiki/24_(TV_series))*, a television precedent was set when [Dennis Haysbert](/wiki/Dennis_Haysbert) portrayed a lead character, [David Palmer](/wiki/David_Palmer_(24_character)), and successful president who fought terrorism. Critic Charles Taylor described him as showing "the determination of magnetism, brains, resolve, compassion and willingness to make tough calls we dream of in a president." After the show portrayed the assassination of Palmer, his brother Wayne, played by [D.B. Woodside](/wiki/D.B._Woodside), was also elected president. [The *Jerusalem Post*](/wiki/The_Jerusalem_Post) speculated in June 2008 that television ratings "may have predicted Obama's primary victory over Hillary Clinton, as the [most recent female television president](/wiki/Commander_in_Chief_(TV_series)) appears to have been less popular than the black leaders of 24."

In 2000, [Chris Tucker](/wiki/Chris_Tucker) planned on writing, directing, producing and starring in a movie about the first black president of the United States.

[Chris Rock](/wiki/Chris_Rock) wrote, directed, and starred as presidential candidate Mays Gilliam in the 2003 comedy *[Head of State](/wiki/Head_of_State_(2003_film))*, described as "undernourished." The movie's tagline was "The only thing white is the house". Another critic described Rock as in way over his head, and found it "depressing to see Rock pander to the most reactionary elements of the black audience." He also was surprised at some of the settings. "Rock doesn't seem to know much about contemporary America; when his character travels to [Memphis](/wiki/Memphis,_Tennessee) (a majority-black city with a black mayor) we see only white people."

In 2004, a sketch on [Chappelle's Show](/wiki/Chappelle%27s_Show) called "Black Bush" featured [Dave Chappelle](/wiki/Dave_Chappelle) as an African-American "interpretation" of then President [George W. Bush](/wiki/George_W._Bush) and [his administration](/wiki/George_W._Bush_administration). It was controversial due to its set-up segment (which had Chappelle mocking fellow comedian [Dennis Miller](/wiki/Dennis_Miller) over the comedian's infamous "free pass" comment regarding not saying anything bad about [George W. Bush](/wiki/George_W._Bush)) and its overall theme that if Bush and his top aides were black, that the public would be more willing to be critical of the president and his decisions. The sketch also features [cameo appearances](/wiki/Cameo_appearance) by actor [Jamie Foxx](/wiki/Jamie_Foxx), who appears as "Black [Tony Blair](/wiki/Tony_Blair)" and [Mos Def](/wiki/Mos_Def) as "Black [Head of the CIA](/wiki/Director_of_the_Central_Intelligence_Agency)" holding "[Yellowcake](/wiki/Yellowcake) from Africa" ([Anthony Berry's](/wiki/Anthony_Berry_(actor)) character warns the other not to "drop that shit", though it is clearly just yellow [cake](/wiki/Cake)).

In CBS's 2004 TV series *[Century City](/wiki/Century_City_(TV_series))'*s fictional timeline, [Oprah Winfrey](/wiki/Oprah_Winfrey) is the US president.

[Louis Gossett Jr.](/wiki/Louis_Gossett_Jr.) played the president in two different movies in 2005 — in the Christian movie [Left Behind: World at War](/wiki/Left_Behind:_World_at_War) he played President Gerald Fitzhugh and in the direct-to-DVD [Solar Attack](/wiki/Solar_Attack) he played President Ryan Gordon.

[Mike Judge](/wiki/Mike_Judge)'s 2006 *[Idiocracy](/wiki/Idiocracy)* featured President Dwayne Elizondo Mountain Dew Herbert Camacho as a former porn star and champion wrestler played by erstwhile NFL defensive end [Terry Alan Crews](/wiki/Terry_Alan_Crews). Critic Bayard thought it odd that the lead character seemed so little advanced from earlier 20th century caricatures. The "joke is essentially unchanged from the days of Rufus Jones: These are the last guys in the world -- or any world -- you'd want to vote for."

A 2006 [BBC Four](/wiki/BBC_Four) adaptation of John Wyndham's short story *[Random Quest](/wiki/Random_Quest)* depicts the main character being sucked into an alternate reality in which [Condoleezza Rice](/wiki/Condoleezza_Rice) is president of the United States.

In ABC's 2008 series *[Life on Mars](/wiki/Life_on_Mars_(American_TV_series))* (a remake of [BBC](/wiki/BBC)'s [series of the same name](/wiki/Life_on_Mars_(UK_TV_series))), it is hinted that [Malia Obama](/wiki/Malia_Obama), the daughter of then-candidate [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama), is the president in 2035.

The comic book *[Final Crisis](/wiki/Final_Crisis)* #7 introduces an African-American version of [Superman](/wiki/Superman) who is the President of the United States in his secret identity, [Calvin Ellis](/wiki/Calvin_Ellis). Writer [Grant Morrison](/wiki/Grant_Morrison) has confirmed that this Superman is a homage to President [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama).

### After Obama's election

Actor [Danny Glover](/wiki/Danny_Glover) played President Thomas Wilson in the 2009 movie *[2012](/wiki/2012_(film)).*

[NBC](/wiki/NBC)'s 2010 series "[The Event](/wiki/The_Event_(TV_series))" features [Blair Underwood](/wiki/Blair_Underwood) in a starring role as Elias Martinez, an [Afro-Cuban](/wiki/Afro-Cuban) US president.

Actor [Jamie Foxx](/wiki/Jamie_Foxx) played President James William Sawyer in the 2013 movie *[White House Down](/wiki/White_House_Down)*.

Actor [Samuel L. Jackson](/wiki/Samuel_L._Jackson) played William Alan Moore, the US president in the 2014 movie *[Big Game](/wiki/Big_Game_(2014_film))*.

In the 2014-2015 NBC TV series *[State of Affairs](/wiki/State_of_Affairs_(TV_series))*, [Alfre Woodard](/wiki/Alfre_Woodard) plays Constance Payton, the first black female president of the United States.

In 2015, [Keith David](/wiki/Keith_David) first voiced [President Curtis](/wiki/The_President_(Rick_and_Morty)) in *[Rick and Morty](/wiki/Rick_and_Morty)* in the episode titled "Get Schwifty" (Season 2, Episode 5). The President has also appeared in episodes: "The Rickchurian Mortydate" (Season 3, Episode 10), "Rickdependence Spray" (Season 5, Episode 4), "Rick and Morty's Thanksploitation Spectacular" (Season 5, Episode 6), "Juricksic Mort" (Season 6, Episode 6), "Ricktional Mortpoon's Rickmas Mortcation" (Season 6, Episode 10), and "Air Force Wong" (Season 7, Episode 3).

In 2016, actor Danny Johnson played President Todd in season 2 of the television show *[Quantico](/wiki/Quantico_(TV_series))*.

In 2019, the [flashforward](/wiki/Flashforward) to 2045 in the final episode of *[Veep](/wiki/Veep)* revealed that President [Kemi Talbot](/wiki/List_of_Veep_characters) ([Toks Olagundoye](/wiki/Toks_Olagundoye)) served two terms sometime between 2025 and 2041. President [Richard Splett](/wiki/List_of_Veep_characters) ([Sam Richardson](/wiki/Sam_Richardson_(actor))) was the incumbent president of 2045, having won a landslide reelection in 2044.

In 2019, [Morgan Freeman](/wiki/Morgan_Freeman) played President Allan Trumbull in *[Angel Has Fallen](/wiki/Angel_Has_Fallen)*, the third movie in the *[Fallen](/wiki/Fallen_(film_series))* film series. He previously played Allan Trumbull as [Speaker of the House](/wiki/Speaker_of_the_United_States_House_of_Representatives) in *[Olympus Has Fallen](/wiki/Olympus_Has_Fallen)* and as [Vice President](/wiki/Vice_President_of_the_United_States) in *[London Has Fallen](/wiki/London_Has_Fallen)*. Freeman previously played President Tom Beck in the 1998 film *[Deep Impact](/wiki/Deep_Impact_(film))*.

In 2025, [Viola Davis](/wiki/Viola_Davis) played President Danielle Sutton in the film [*G20*](/wiki/G20_(film)).

In 2025, [Angela Bassett](/wiki/Angela_Bassett) appears as President Mitchell in the Netflix show *[Zero Day](/wiki/Zero_Day_(American_TV_series))* and she also appeared as President Erika Sloane in *[Mission: Impossible - The Final Reckoning](/wiki/Mission:_Impossible_-_The_Final_Reckoning)*.

In 2025, [Idris Elba](/wiki/Idris_Elba) portrayed the President of the United States in the movie [*A House of Dynamite*](/wiki/A_House_of_Dynamite).

## Music

[Parliament](/wiki/Parliament_(band))'s 1975 [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.)-themed song *[Chocolate City](/wiki/Chocolate_City_(song))* had [Muhammad Ali](/wiki/Muhammad_Ali) as President of the United States and [James Brown](/wiki/James_Brown) as vice president, among others.

In 1983, R&B artist [Blowfly](/wiki/Blowfly_(musician)) released a track entitled "The first black president", a conversation between *President Blowfly* and his assistant over hip hop music.

The music video accompanying [N.W.A](/wiki/N.W.A)'s ["Express Yourself"](/wiki/Express_Yourself_(N.W.A_song)) featured a shot of the [White House](/wiki/White_House) with the caption, "Live from the Black House", followed by one of [Dr. Dre](/wiki/Dr._Dre) swiveling a chair in what is apparently meant to represent the [Oval Office](/wiki/Oval_Office). Several more shots in the video continue the same scene.

Rap artist [Nas](/wiki/Nas) was inspired by the Obama campaign to write a song entitled "[Black President](/wiki/Untitled_Nas_album)", which includes quotations from Obama. The track [samples](/wiki/Sampling_(music)) [Tupac Shakur](/wiki/Tupac_Shakur) with a modified lyric saying, "And although it seems heaven sent, we ain't ready to have a black president."

Rap artist [Young Jeezy](/wiki/Young_Jeezy), also inspired by the Obama campaign, wrote a song entitled "[My President](/wiki/My_President)", which also featured Nas, and featured the chorus "My President Is Black ..."

When he appeared in speaking roles on [Snoop Dogg](/wiki/Snoop_Dogg)'s album *[No Limit Top Dogg](/wiki/No_Limit_Top_Dogg)*, actor [Rudy Ray Moore](/wiki/Rudy_Ray_Moore) joked that he would run for president with two priorities - painting the [White House](/wiki/White_House) black and legalizing just about everything.

## Effect of Obama's presidency on television

The Obama presidency had the potential to affect television shows, but people had differing reactions to that. The comedian and actor [Bill Cosby](/wiki/Bill_Cosby) said he was "not all that optimistic that Obama's presidency will make a major difference in terms of onscreen diversity," saying "they would die before putting another show on about a black family and black pride."

Pastor [T.D. Jakes](/wiki/T.D._Jakes) noted the portrayal on television of "middle-class African-Americans who are articulate, intelligent and thoughtful." He hoped the new president would make a difference in encouraging those types of depictions. "The Obama effect might even go beyond bolstering the presence of blacks on television and actually bring about a tonal change in programming," according to Brok Akil. She wrote a script based on a book called *Making Friends With Black People*, a buddy comedy that focuses on the state of race relations in the U.S. She added that, "In our pitch to NBC, we referenced Obama." She also said, "We talked about how he has gotten us to the table to talk about race in a meaningful way and it's time to continue the discussion. So our new president has already had an impact."
