**China**, officially the **People's Republic of China** (**PRC**), is a country in [East Asia](/wiki/East_Asia). It is the [second-most populous country](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_population_(United_Nations)) after India, with [a population](/wiki/Population_of_China) exceeding 1.4 billion, representing 17% of the world's population. China [borders fourteen countries by land](/wiki/Borders_of_China) across an area of 9.6 million square kilometers (3,700,000 sq mi), making it the [third-largest country by area](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area). The country is divided into 33 [province-level divisions](/wiki/Province-level_divisions_of_China): 22 [provinces](/wiki/Provinces_of_China), 5 [autonomous regions](/wiki/Autonomous_regions_of_China), 4 [municipalities](/wiki/Direct-administered_municipalities_of_China), and 2 semi-autonomous [special administrative regions](/wiki/Special_administrative_regions). [Beijing](/wiki/Beijing) is the capital, while [Shanghai](/wiki/Shanghai) is the [most populous city by urban area](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_China_by_population) and largest [financial center](/wiki/Financial_center).

The first humans in China arrived during the [Paleolithic](/wiki/Paleolithic) era. By the 2nd millennium BCE [dynastic states](/wiki/Dynasties_in_Chinese_history) had emerged in the [Yellow River](/wiki/Yellow_River) basin. The 8th–3rd centuries BCE saw a breakdown in the authority of the [Zhou dynasty](/wiki/Zhou_dynasty), accompanied by the emergence of administrative and military techniques, [literature](/wiki/Chinese_classics) and [philosophy](/wiki/Chinese_philosophy). In 221 BCE, China was unified under [an emperor](/wiki/Emperor_of_China), ushering in two millennia of imperial rule. Chinese achievements include the invention of [gunpowder](/wiki/Invention_of_gunpowder), [paper](/wiki/History_of_paper#Paper_in_China), [printing](/wiki/Printing) and the [compass](/wiki/Compass), the establishment of the [Silk Road](/wiki/Silk_Road), and the building of the [Great Wall](/wiki/Great_Wall). [Chinese culture](/wiki/Chinese_culture) has flourished and has had a great influence on the [region and beyond](/wiki/Sinosphere). China began to cede [parts of the country](/wiki/Foreign_concessions_in_China) in the 19th century, to European powers through a series of [unequal treaties](/wiki/Unequal_treaties). The [1911 Revolution](/wiki/1911_Revolution) overthrew the [Qing dynasty](/wiki/Qing_dynasty) and the [Republic of China](/wiki/Republic_of_China_(1912%E2%80%931949)) was established the following year. The country was unstable and fragmented during the [Warlord Era](/wiki/Warlord_Era); this came to an end with the [Northern Expedition](/wiki/Northern_Expedition) conducted by the [Kuomintang](/wiki/Kuomintang) to reunify the country.

The [Chinese Civil War](/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War) began in 1927, when Kuomintang forces [purged](/wiki/Shanghai_massacre) members of the [Chinese Communist Party](/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party) (CCP). China was invaded by the [Empire of Japan](/wiki/Empire_of_Japan) in 1937, leading the CCP and Kuomintang to form the [Second United Front](/wiki/Second_United_Front) to fight the Japanese. The [Second Sino-Japanese War](/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War) ended in a Chinese victory; however, the CCP and the Kuomintang resumed their civil war. In 1949, the CCP [proclaimed the People's Republic of China](/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China) and forced the [Kuomintang-led government to retreat](/wiki/Retreat_of_the_government_of_the_Republic_of_China_to_Taiwan) to the [island of Taiwan](/wiki/Island_of_Taiwan). The country was split, with [both sides](/wiki/Two_Chinas) claiming to be the [legitimate government](/wiki/One_China). Following the implementation of [land reforms](/wiki/Land_Reform_Movement), attempts by the CCP to realize [communism](/wiki/Communism) failed: the [Great Leap Forward](/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward) was responsible for the [Great Chinese Famine](/wiki/Great_Chinese_Famine) which resulted in millions of deaths, and the [Cultural Revolution](/wiki/Cultural_Revolution) was a period of turmoil and persecution. The [reform and opening up](/wiki/Reform_and_opening_up) that began in 1978 moved the country away from a [planned economy](/wiki/Planned_economy) towards a [market-based economy](/wiki/Socialist_market_economy), spurring an economic boom. A movement for political liberalization stalled after the [1989 Tiananmen Square protests and massacre](/wiki/1989_Tiananmen_Square_protests_and_massacre).

Since 1949, China has been a [unitary](/wiki/Unitary_state) communist state with the CCP as its [sole ruling party](/wiki/One-party_state). It is one of the five [permanent members](/wiki/Permanent_members_of_the_United_Nations_Security_Council) of the [UN Security Council](/wiki/UN_Security_Council) and a member of [numerous multilateral and regional organizations](/wiki/International_organization_membership_of_China). Making up around one-fifth of the world's economy, China is the [second-wealthiest country](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_total_wealth#Total_wealth_by_country) in the world, with the [Chinese economy](/wiki/Chinese_economy) being the largest when adjusted for [PPP](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(PPP)). However, China [ranks poorly](/wiki/International_rankings_of_China) in measures of [democracy](/wiki/Democracy_in_China) and [human rights](/wiki/Human_rights_in_China). China has been one of the [fastest-growing](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_real_GDP_growth_rate) modern economies and is the world's [largest manufacturer](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_manufacturing_output) and [exporter](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_exports), as well as the [second-largest importer](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_imports). China has the world's [largest standing army](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_military_and_paramilitary_personnel), [second-largest defense budget](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_military_expenditures), and [third-largest nuclear weapons stockpile](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_China). Since the 2020s, it has been described as a [superpower](/wiki/Superpower) due to its influence in the fields of geopolitics, [science and technology](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_China), [manufacturing](/wiki/Industry_in_China), economics and culture. China is known for its [cuisine](/wiki/Chinese_cuisine) and [culture](/wiki/Chinese_culture). It is a [megadiverse country](/wiki/Megadiverse_country), and has [60 UNESCO World Heritage Sites](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_China).

## Etymology

Main article: [Names of China](/wiki/Names_of_China)

The word "China" has been used in English since the 16th century; however, it was not used by the Chinese themselves during this period. Its origin has been traced through [Portuguese](/wiki/Portuguese_language), [Malay](/wiki/Malay_language), and [Persian](/wiki/Persian_language) back to the [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit) word चिन *Cīna*, used in [ancient India](/wiki/Ancient_India). "China" appears in [Richard Eden](/wiki/Richard_Eden_(translator))'s 1555 translation of the 1516 journal of the Portuguese explorer [Duarte Barbosa](/wiki/Duarte_Barbosa). Barbosa's usage was derived from Persian *Chīn* ([چین](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%DA%86%DB%8C%D9%86)), which in turn derived from Sanskrit *[Cīna](/wiki/Chinas)* ([चीन](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%9A%E0%A5%80%E0%A4%A8)). The origin of the Sanskrit word is a matter of debate. *Cīna* was first used in early [Hindu](/wiki/Hindu) scripture, including the *[Mahabharata](/wiki/Mahabharata)* (3rd century BCE–4th century CE) and the *[Laws of Manu](/wiki/Manusmriti)* (2nd century BCE–2nd century CE). In 1655, [Martino Martini](/wiki/Martino_Martini) suggested that the word China is derived ultimately from the name of the [Qin dynasty](/wiki/Qin_dynasty) (221–206 BCE) or the prior state of [Qin](/wiki/Qin_(state)). Although use in Indian sources precedes this dynasty, though not the state, this derivation is still given in various sources. Alternative suggestions include the names for [Yelang](/wiki/Yelang) and the [Jing](/wiki/Chu_(state)) or Chu state.

The official name of the modern state is the "People's Republic of China" ([simplified Chinese](/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters): [中华人民共和国](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E5%8D%8E%E4%BA%BA%E6%B0%91%E5%85%B1%E5%92%8C%E5%9B%BD); [traditional Chinese](/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters): [中華人民共和國](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E8%8F%AF%E4%BA%BA%E6%B0%91%E5%85%B1%E5%92%8C%E5%9C%8B); [pinyin](/wiki/Pinyin): *Zhōnghuá rénmín gònghéguó*). The shorter form is "China" ([中国](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E5%9B%BD); [中國](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E5%9C%8B); *Zhōngguó*), from *zhōng* ('central') and *guó* ('state'), a term which developed under the [Western Zhou](/wiki/Western_Zhou) dynasty in reference to its [royal demesne](/wiki/Demesne). It was used in official documents as a synonym for the state under the [Qing](/wiki/Qing_dynasty). The name *Zhongguo* is also translated as 'Middle Kingdom' in English. China is sometimes referred to as [mainland China](/wiki/Mainland_China) or "the Mainland" when distinguishing it from the [Republic of China](/wiki/Republic_of_China) or the [PRC's Special Administrative Regions](/wiki/Special_administrative_regions_of_China).

## History

Main article: [History of China](/wiki/History_of_China)

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of Chinese history](/wiki/Timeline_of_Chinese_history).

### Prehistory

Pottery vessel, Xianren Cave culture (18,000–7,000 BCE). Among the earliest known pottery in the world. [National Museum of China](/wiki/National_Museum_of_China), Beijing.

[Archaeological evidence](/wiki/Archaeological_excavation) suggests that early [hominids](/wiki/Hominidae) inhabited China 2.25 million years ago. The hominid fossils of [Peking Man](/wiki/Peking_Man), a *[Homo erectus](/wiki/Homo_erectus)* who [used fire](/wiki/Control_of_fire_by_early_humans), have been dated to between 680,000 and 780,000 [years ago](/wiki/Before_Present). The fossilized teeth of *Homo sapiens* (dated to 125,000–80,000 years ago) have been discovered in [Fuyan Cave](/wiki/Fuyan_Cave). Chinese [proto-writing](/wiki/Proto-writing) existed in [Jiahu](/wiki/Jiahu) around 6600 BCE, at [Damaidi](/wiki/Damaidi) around 6000 BCE, [Dadiwan](/wiki/Dadiwan_culture) from 5800 to 5400 BCE, and [Banpo](/wiki/Banpo) dating from the 5th millennium BCE. Some scholars have suggested that the [Jiahu symbols](/wiki/Jiahu_symbols) (7th millennium BCE) constituted the earliest Chinese writing system.

### Early dynastic rules

Further information: [Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors](/wiki/Three_Sovereigns_and_Five_Emperors), [Xia dynasty](/wiki/Xia_dynasty), [Shang dynasty](/wiki/Shang_dynasty), [Zhou dynasty](/wiki/Zhou_dynasty), [Spring and Autumn period](/wiki/Spring_and_Autumn_period), and [Warring States period](/wiki/Warring_States_period)

Rectangular cauldron (*fangding*, 方鼎), Shang dynasty, ca. 13th–11th century BCE. Bronze. [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](/wiki/Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art), New York.

According to traditional [Chinese historiography](/wiki/Chinese_historiography), the [Xia dynasty](/wiki/Xia_dynasty) was established during the late 3rd millennium BCE, marking the beginning of the dynastic cycle that was understood to underpin China's entire political history. In the modern era, the Xia's historicity came under increasing scrutiny, in part due to the earliest known attestation of the Xia being written millennia after the date given for their collapse. In 1958, archaeologists discovered sites belonging to the [Erlitou culture](/wiki/Erlitou_culture) that existed during the early [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age); they have since been characterized as the remains of the historical Xia, but this conception is often rejected. The [Shang dynasty](/wiki/Shang_dynasty) that traditionally succeeded the Xia is the earliest for which there are both contemporary written records and undisputed archaeological evidence. The Shang ruled much of the [Yellow River](/wiki/Yellow_River) valley until the 11th century BCE, with the earliest hard evidence dated c. 1300 BCE. The [oracle bone script](/wiki/Oracle_bone_script), attested from c. 1250 BCE but generally assumed to be considerably older, represents the oldest known form of [written Chinese](/wiki/Written_Chinese), and is the direct ancestor of modern [Chinese characters](/wiki/Chinese_characters).

The Shang were overthrown by the [Zhou](/wiki/Zhou_dynasty), who ruled between the 11th and 5th centuries BCE, though the centralized authority of [Son of Heaven](/wiki/Son_of_Heaven) was slowly eroded by *[fengjian](/wiki/Fengjian)* lords. Some principalities eventually emerged from the weakened Zhou and continually waged war with each other during the 300-year [Spring and Autumn period](/wiki/Spring_and_Autumn_period). By the time of the [Warring States period](/wiki/Warring_States_period) of the 5th–3rd centuries BCE, there were seven major powerful states left.

### Imperial China

Further information: [Chinese Empire](/wiki/Chinese_Empire) and [History of China § Imperial China](/wiki/History_of_China#Imperial_China)

#### Qin and Han

The [southward expansion of the Han dynasty](/wiki/Southward_expansion_of_the_Han_dynasty) during the 2nd century BCE

The [Warring States period](/wiki/Warring_States_period) ended in 221 BCE after the [state of Qin](/wiki/Qin_(state)) conquered the other six states, reunited China and established the dominant order of [autocracy](/wiki/Autocracy). [Qin Shi Huang](/wiki/Qin_Shi_Huang) proclaimed himself the Emperor of the [Qin dynasty](/wiki/Qin_dynasty), becoming the first emperor of a unified China. He enacted [legalist](/wiki/Legalism_(Chinese_philosophy)) reforms, notably the standardization of Chinese characters, [measurements](/wiki/Chinese_units_of_measurement), road widths, and [currency](/wiki/History_of_Chinese_currency). His dynasty also [conquered the Yue tribes](/wiki/Qin_campaign_against_the_Baiyue) in [Guangxi](/wiki/Guangxi), [Guangdong](/wiki/Guangdong), and [Northern Vietnam](/wiki/Northern_Vietnam). The Qin dynasty lasted only fifteen years, falling soon after the Qin Shi Huang's death.

Following [widespread revolts](/wiki/Chu%E2%80%93Han_Contention) during which the imperial library [was burned](/wiki/List_of_destroyed_libraries#Human_action), the [Han dynasty](/wiki/Han_dynasty) emerged to rule China between 206 BCE and 220 CE, creating a cultural identity among its populace still remembered in the ethnonym of the modern [Han Chinese](/wiki/Han_Chinese). The Han [expanded the empire's territory considerably](/wiki/History_of_the_Han_dynasty), with military campaigns reaching [Central Asia, Mongolia](/wiki/Han%E2%80%93Xiongnu_Wars), [Korea](/wiki/Han_conquest_of_Gojoseon), and [Yunnan](/wiki/Han_conquest_of_Dian), and the [recovery of Guangdong and northern Vietnam](/wiki/Southward_expansion_of_the_Han_dynasty) from [Nanyue](/wiki/Nanyue). Han involvement in Central Asia and [Sogdia](/wiki/Sogdia) helped establish the land route of the [Silk Road](/wiki/Silk_Road), replacing the earlier path over the [Himalayas](/wiki/Himalayas) to India. Han China gradually became the largest economy of the ancient world. Despite the Han's initial decentralization and the official abandonment of the Qin philosophy of Legalism in favor of [Confucianism](/wiki/Confucianism), Qin's legalist institutions and policies continued to be employed by the Han government and its successors.

#### Three Kingdoms, Jin, Northern and Southern dynasties

Cave 20, [Yungang Grottoes](/wiki/Yungang_Grottoes), Datong, [Shanxi](/wiki/Shanxi). Northern Wei dynasty, c. 460–465 CE.

After the [end of the Han dynasty](/wiki/End_of_the_Han_dynasty), a period of strife known as [Three Kingdoms](/wiki/Three_Kingdoms) followed, at the end of which [Wei](/wiki/Cao_Wei) was swiftly overthrown by the [Jin dynasty](/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(266%E2%80%93420)). The Jin fell to [civil war](/wiki/War_of_the_Eight_Princes) upon the ascension of the developmentally disabled [Emperor Hui](/wiki/Emperor_Hui_of_Jin); the [Five Barbarians](/wiki/Five_Barbarians) then [rebelled](/wiki/Upheaval_of_the_Five_Barbarians) and ruled northern China as the [Sixteen States](/wiki/Sixteen_Kingdoms). The [Xianbei](/wiki/Xianbei) unified them as the [Northern Wei](/wiki/Northern_Wei), whose [Emperor Xiaowen](/wiki/Emperor_Xiaowen_of_Northern_Wei) reversed his predecessors' apartheid policies and [enforced a drastic sinification on his subjects](/wiki/Northern_dynasties). In the south, the general [Liu Yu](/wiki/Emperor_Wu_of_Song) secured the abdication of the Jin in favor of the [Liu Song](/wiki/Liu_Song_dynasty). The various successors of these states became known as the [Northern and Southern dynasties](/wiki/Northern_and_Southern_dynasties), with the two areas finally reunited by the [Sui](/wiki/Sui_dynasty) in 589.

#### Sui, Tang and Song

The Sui restored the Han to power through China, reformed its agriculture, economy and [imperial examination](/wiki/Imperial_examination) system, constructed the [Grand Canal](/wiki/Grand_Canal_of_China), and patronized [Buddhism](/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism). However, they fell quickly when their conscription for public works and a [failed war](/wiki/Goguryeo%E2%80%93Sui_War) in [northern Korea](/wiki/Goguryeo) provoked widespread unrest.
Under the succeeding [Tang](/wiki/Tang_dynasty) and [Song dynasties](/wiki/Song_dynasty), Chinese economy, technology, and culture entered a golden age. The Tang dynasty retained control of the [Western Regions](/wiki/Western_Regions) and the Silk Road, which brought traders to as far as [Mesopotamia](/wiki/Mesopotamia) and the [Horn of Africa](/wiki/Horn_of_Africa), and made the capital [Chang'an](/wiki/Chang%27an) a cosmopolitan urban center. However, it was devastated and weakened by the [An Lushan rebellion](/wiki/An_Lushan_rebellion) in the 8th century.

[Court Ladies Preparing Newly Woven Silk](/wiki/Court_Ladies_Preparing_Newly_Woven_Silk), originally painted by [Zhang Xuan](/wiki/Zhang_Xuan) (713–755) during the Tang dynasty; this 12th-century Song dynasty copy is the earliest surviving version. Silk painting. Museum of Fine Arts, Boston.

[Wang Ximeng](/wiki/Wang_Ximeng), A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains (千里江山圖), detail. Northern Song dynasty, c. 1113. Handscroll, ink and mineral pigments on silk. [Palace Museum](/wiki/Palace_Museum), Beijing.

The Tang and Song dynasties represented a period of transformation across multiple domains of civilization. In technology, the Song dynasty witnessed the first systematic military application of gunpowder, including fire arrows and proto-firearms such as the fire lance, as well as the adoption of the magnetic compass for maritime navigation. Movable type printing, invented by Bi Sheng around 1040, preceded Gutenberg's press by over four centuries and dramatically accelerated the diffusion of knowledge across East Asia. Economically, the Song period has been characterized by some historians as a proto-industrial revolution: iron production reached levels comparable to early eighteenth-century England, the rigid Tang-era ward system (*fang*) gave way to open commercial streets and night markets, and cities such as Bianjing ([Kaifeng](/wiki/Kaifeng)) and Lin'an ([Hangzhou](/wiki/Hangzhou)) each surpassed one million inhabitants. The maritime Silk Road expanded significantly, with the ports of Quanzhou and Guangzhou becoming among the busiest in the contemporary world, connecting China to Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean, and the Persian Gulf. In intellectual life, the Neo-Confucian synthesis associated with the [Cheng brothers](/wiki/Cheng_brothers) and [Zhu Xi](/wiki/Zhu_Xi) reinterpreted classical thought in response to Buddhist metaphysics, producing a philosophical framework that became the dominant state ideology across East Asia, including Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, for several centuries. The Tang dynasty, meanwhile, produced what many scholars regard as the apogee of classical Chinese poetry, with works that remained canonical across the [Sinosphere](/wiki/Sinosphere), while the cosmopolitan character of Chang'an, home to Sogdian merchants, Zoroastrian temples, Nestorian Christian communities, and Japanese diplomatic missions, reflected the Tang empire's integration into a broader Eurasian world.

[Emperor Huizong of Song](/wiki/Emperor_Huizong_of_Song), *Auspicious Cranes* (瑞鶴圖), detail, 1112. Handscroll, ink and color on silk. [Liaoning Provincial Museum](/wiki/Liaoning_Provincial_Museum), Shenyang.

In 907, the Tang disintegrated when the local military governors became ungovernable. The Song dynasty ended the [separatist situation](/wiki/Five_Dynasties_and_Ten_Kingdoms_period) in 960, leading to a balance of power between the Song and the [Liao dynasty](/wiki/Liao_dynasty). The Song was the first government in world history to issue paper money and the first Chinese polity to establish a permanent navy which was supported by the developed shipbuilding industry along with the sea trade.

Between the 10th and 11th century CE, the population of China doubled to around 100 million people, mostly because of the expansion of rice cultivation in central and southern China, and the production of abundant food surpluses. The Song dynasty also saw a [revival of Confucianism](/wiki/Neo-Confucianism), in response to the growth of Buddhism during the Tang, and a flourishing of philosophy and the arts, as [landscape art](/wiki/Landscape_art) and [porcelain](/wiki/Porcelain) were brought to new levels of complexity. However, the military weakness of the Song army was observed by the [Jin dynasty](/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234)). In 1127, [Emperor Emeritus Huizong](/wiki/Emperor_Huizong_of_Song), [Emperor Qinzong](/wiki/Emperor_Qinzong) and the capital [Bianjing](/wiki/Kaifeng) were captured during the [Jin–Song wars](/wiki/Jin%E2%80%93Song_wars). The remnants of the Song retreated to [southern China](/wiki/Northern_and_southern_China) and reestablished [the Song](/wiki/Southern_Song_Dynasty) at [Jiankang](/wiki/Nanjing).

#### Yuan, Ming and Qing

The [Great Wall of China](/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China), photographed by [Thomas Child](/wiki/Thomas_Child_(photographer)) in the 1870s. Most extant sections of the Wall date to the [Ming dynasty](/wiki/Ming_dynasty).

The [Mongol conquest of China](/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_China) began in 1205 with the [campaigns](/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_Western_Xia) against [Western Xia](/wiki/Western_Xia) by [Genghis Khan](/wiki/Genghis_Khan), who also [invaded Jin territories](/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_the_Jin_dynasty). In 1271, the Mongol leader [Kublai Khan](/wiki/Kublai_Khan) established the [Yuan dynasty](/wiki/Yuan_dynasty), which [conquered the last remnant of the Song dynasty](/wiki/Mongol_conquest_of_the_Song_dynasty) in 1279. Before the Mongol invasion, the population of Song China was 120 million citizens; this was reduced to 60 million by the time of the census in 1300. A peasant named Zhu Yuanzhang [overthrew the Yuan](/wiki/Red_Turban_Rebellions) in 1368 and founded the [Ming dynasty](/wiki/Ming_dynasty) as the [Hongwu Emperor](/wiki/Hongwu_Emperor). Under the Ming dynasty, China enjoyed another golden age, developing one of the strongest navies in the world and a rich and prosperous economy amid a flourishing of art and culture. It was during this period that admiral [Zheng He](/wiki/Zheng_He) led the [Ming treasure voyages](/wiki/Ming_treasure_voyages) throughout the Indian Ocean, reaching as far as East Africa.

In the early Ming dynasty, China's capital was moved from [Nanjing](/wiki/Nanjing) to Beijing. With the budding of capitalism, philosophers such as [Wang Yangming](/wiki/Wang_Yangming) critiqued and expanded Neo-Confucianism with concepts of [individualism](/wiki/Individualism) and equality of [four occupations](/wiki/Four_occupations). The [scholar-official](/wiki/Scholar-official) stratum became a supporting force of industry and commerce in the tax boycott movements, which, together with the famines and defense against [Japanese invasions of Korea (1592–1598)](/wiki/Japanese_invasions_of_Korea_(1592%E2%80%931598)) and [Later Jin](/wiki/Later_Jin_(1616%E2%80%931636)) incursions led to an exhausted treasury. In 1644, Beijing was captured by a coalition of [peasant rebel](/wiki/Late_Ming_peasant_rebellions) forces led by [Li Zicheng](/wiki/Li_Zicheng). The [Chongzhen Emperor](/wiki/Chongzhen_Emperor) committed suicide when the city fell. The Manchu [Qing dynasty](/wiki/Qing_dynasty), then allied with Ming dynasty general [Wu Sangui](/wiki/Wu_Sangui), overthrew Li's short-lived [Shun dynasty](/wiki/Shun_dynasty) and subsequently seized control of Beijing, which became the new capital of the Qing dynasty.

The [Qing conquest of the Ming](/wiki/Qing_conquest_of_the_Ming) and expansion of the empire

The Qing dynasty, which lasted from 1644 until 1912, was the last imperial dynasty of China. The [transition from Ming to Qing](/wiki/Transition_from_Ming_to_Qing) (1618–1683) cost 25 million lives, but the Qing appeared to have restored China's imperial power and inaugurated another flowering of the arts. After the [Southern Ming](/wiki/Southern_Ming) ended, the further conquest of the [Dzungar Khanate](/wiki/Dzungar_Khanate) added Mongolia, Tibet and Xinjiang to the empire. Meanwhile, China's population growth resumed and shortly began to accelerate. It is commonly agreed that pre-modern China's population experienced two growth spurts, one during the [Northern Song](/wiki/Northern_Song) period (960–1127), and other during the Qing period (around 1700–1830). By the [High Qing era](/wiki/High_Qing_era) China was possibly the most commercialized country in the world, and imperial China experienced a second commercial revolution by the end of the 18th century. On the other hand, the centralized autocracy was strengthened in part to suppress [anti-Qing sentiment](/wiki/Anti-Qing_sentiment) with the policy of valuing agriculture and restraining commerce, like the *[Haijin](/wiki/Haijin)* during the early Qing period and ideological control as represented by the [literary inquisition](/wiki/Literary_inquisition), causing some social and technological stagnation.

The [Eight-Nation Alliance](/wiki/Eight-Nation_Alliance) invaded China to defeat the anti-foreign [Boxers](/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion) and their Qing backers. The image shows a celebration ceremony inside the Chinese imperial palace, the [Forbidden City](/wiki/Forbidden_City) after the signing of the [Boxer Protocol](/wiki/Boxer_Protocol) in 1901.

In the mid-19th century, the [Opium Wars](/wiki/Opium_Wars) with Britain and [France](/wiki/France) forced China to pay compensation, open treaty ports, allow [extraterritoriality](/wiki/Extraterritoriality) for foreign nationals, and cede [Hong Kong](/wiki/British_Hong_Kong) to the British under the 1842 [Treaty of Nanking](/wiki/Treaty_of_Nanking), the first of what have been termed the [unequal treaties](/wiki/Unequal_treaty). The [First Sino-Japanese War](/wiki/First_Sino-Japanese_War) (1894–1895) resulted in Qing China's loss of influence in the [Korean Peninsula](/wiki/Korean_Peninsula), as well as the [cession of Taiwan](/wiki/Treaty_of_Shimonoseki) to [Japan](/wiki/Empire_of_Japan). The Qing dynasty also began experiencing [internal unrest](/wiki/Timeline_of_late_anti-Qing_rebellions) in which tens of millions of people died, especially in the [White Lotus Rebellion](/wiki/White_Lotus_Rebellion), the failed [Taiping Rebellion](/wiki/Taiping_Rebellion) that ravaged southern China in the 1850s and 1860s and the [Dungan Revolt (1862–1877)](/wiki/Dungan_Revolt_(1862%E2%80%931877)) in the northwest. The initial success of the [Self-Strengthening Movement](/wiki/Self-Strengthening_Movement) of the 1860s was frustrated by a series of military defeats in the 1880s and 1890s. In the 19th century, the great [Chinese diaspora](/wiki/Chinese_emigration) began. Losses due to emigration were added to by conflicts and catastrophes such as the [Northern Chinese Famine of 1876–1879](/wiki/Northern_Chinese_Famine_of_1876%E2%80%931879), in which between 9 and 13 million people died. The [Guangxu Emperor](/wiki/Guangxu_Emperor) drafted a [reform plan](/wiki/Hundred_Days%27_Reform) in 1898 to establish a modern [constitutional monarchy](/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy), but these plans were thwarted by the [Empress Dowager Cixi](/wiki/Empress_Dowager_Cixi). The ill-fated anti-foreign [Boxer Rebellion](/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion) of 1899–1901 further weakened the dynasty. Although Cixi sponsored a program of reforms known as the [late Qing reforms](/wiki/Late_Qing_reforms), the [Xinhai Revolution](/wiki/1911_Revolution) of 1911 ended the Qing dynasty. [Puyi](/wiki/Puyi), the last Emperor, [abdicated in 1912](/wiki/Imperial_Edict_of_the_Abdication_of_the_Qing_Emperor).

### Republic of China

Main article: [Republic of China (1912–1949)](/wiki/Republic_of_China_(1912%E2%80%931949))

Further information: [1911 Revolution](/wiki/1911_Revolution), [Second Sino-Japanese War](/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War), [Chinese Civil War](/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War), and [Chinese Communist Revolution](/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Revolution)

Calendar poster (*yuefenpai*, 月份牌), Republic of China, year 1 (1912).

On 1 January 1912, the [Republic of China](/wiki/Republic_of_China_(1912%E2%80%931949)) (ROC) was established, and [Sun Yat-sen](/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen) of the [Kuomintang](/wiki/Kuomintang) was proclaimed provisional president. In 1912, the presidency was given to [Yuan Shikai](/wiki/Yuan_Shikai), a former Qing general who in 1915 proclaimed himself [Emperor of China](/wiki/Empire_of_China_(1915%E2%80%931916)). In the face of popular condemnation and opposition from his own [Beiyang Army](/wiki/Beiyang_Army), he was forced to re-establish the republic in 1916. After Yuan's death in 1916, China was politically fragmented. Its Beijing-based government was internationally recognized but virtually powerless; regional warlords controlled most of its territory. During this [period](/wiki/Warlord_Era), China [participated in](/wiki/China_during_World_War_I) [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I) and saw a far-reaching popular uprising called the [May Fourth Movement](/wiki/May_Fourth_Movement). In the late 1920s, the Kuomintang under [Chiang Kai-shek](/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek) reunified most the country through the [Northern Expedition](/wiki/Northern_Expedition). The Kuomintang moved the nation's capital to [Nanjing](/wiki/Nanjing) and implemented "political tutelage", an intermediate stage of political development outlined in Sun Yat-sen's [Three Principles of the People](/wiki/Three_Principles_of_the_People) program for transforming China into a modern democratic state. The Kuomintang [briefly allied](/wiki/First_United_Front) with the CCP during the Northern Expedition, though the alliance broke down in 1927 after Chiang [violently suppressed](/wiki/Shanghai_massacre) the CCP and other leftists in Shanghai, marking the beginning of the [Chinese Civil War](/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War). [CCP forces in Jiangxi were defeated by the Nationalist government in 1934](/wiki/Fifth_encirclement_campaign_against_the_Jiangxi_Soviet), leading the CCP to initiate the [Long March](/wiki/Long_March) and relocate to [Yan'an](/wiki/Yan%27an) in [Shaanxi](/wiki/Shaanxi). It would be the base of the communists before major combat in the Chinese Civil War ended in 1949.

[Chiang Kai-shek](/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek) and [Mao Zedong](/wiki/Mao_Zedong) toasting together in 1945 following the [end of World War II](/wiki/End_of_World_War_II_in_Asia)

In 1931, Japan [invaded and occupied Manchuria](/wiki/Japanese_invasion_of_Manchuria). Additionally, Japan invaded other parts of China in 1937, precipitating the [Second Sino-Japanese War](/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War) (1937–1945), a [theater](/wiki/Theater_(warfare)) of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II). The war forced an [uneasy alliance](/wiki/Second_United_Front) between the Kuomintang and the CCP. Japanese forces committed numerous [war atrocities](/wiki/Japanese_war_crimes) against the civilian population; as many as 20 million Chinese civilians died. An estimated 40,000 to over 300,000 Chinese were [massacred in Nanjing](/wiki/Nanjing_Massacre) alone during the Japanese occupation. The Republic of China, along with the UK, the United States, and the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union), were recognized as the Allied "[Big Four](/wiki/Four_Policemen)" in the [Declaration by United Nations](/wiki/Declaration_by_United_Nations). Along with the other three great powers, China was one of the four major [Allies of World War II](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II), and was later considered one of the primary victors in the war. After the [surrender of Japan](/wiki/Surrender_of_Japan) in 1945, China emerged victorious but war-ravaged and financially drained. Taiwan, along with the [Penghu](/wiki/Penghu), were [handed over to ROC control](/wiki/Retrocession_of_Taiwan); however, the validity of this handover is controversial. In 1946, fighting between CCP and KMT forces broke out and the country resumed a state of civil war that lasted more than three years. Constitutional rule was established in 1947, but because of the ongoing war, many provisions of the [ROC constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Republic_of_China) were never implemented in mainland China.

### People's Republic of China

Main article: [History of the People's Republic of China](/wiki/History_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China)

On 1 October 1949, [Mao Zedong](/wiki/Mao_Zedong) announced the [founding of the People's Republic of China](/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China)

By 1949, the CCP took control of most of mainland China, and the [ROC government retreated to Taiwan](/wiki/Retreat_of_the_government_of_the_Republic_of_China_to_Taiwan). On 1 October 1949, [CCP Chairman](/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party) [Mao Zedong](/wiki/Mao_Zedong) formally [proclaimed the People's Republic of China](/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China). In 1950, the PRC [captured Hainan](/wiki/Battle_of_Hainan_Island) from the ROC and [annexed Tibet](/wiki/Annexation_of_Tibet_by_China). The CCP consolidated its popularity among the peasants through the [Land Reform Movement](/wiki/Land_Reform_Movement), which included the state-tolerated executions of between 1 and 2 million landlords by peasants and former tenants. Though the PRC initially allied closely with the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union), the relations between the two [communist](/wiki/Communism) nations [gradually deteriorated](/wiki/Sino-Soviet_split), leading China to develop an independent industrial system and [its own nuclear weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_China). The Chinese population increased from 550 million in 1950 to 900 million in 1974. However, the [Great Leap Forward](/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward), a massive [industrialization](/wiki/Industrialisation) project, resulted in [an estimated 15 to 55 million deaths](/wiki/Great_Chinese_Famine) between 1959 and 1961, mostly from starvation. In 1964, China [detonated its first atomic bomb](/wiki/Project_596). In 1966, Mao and his allies launched the [Cultural Revolution](/wiki/Cultural_Revolution), sparking a decade of political recrimination and social upheaval that lasted until Mao's [death](/wiki/Death_and_state_funeral_of_Mao_Zedong) in 1976.

The [1989 Tiananmen Square protests](/wiki/1989_Tiananmen_Square_protests) were ended by a military-led massacre.

After Mao's death, the [Gang of Four](/wiki/Gang_of_Four) were [arrested](/wiki/Smashing_the_Gang_of_Four) by [Hua Guofeng](/wiki/Hua_Guofeng). The Cultural Revolution was rebuked, with millions rehabilitated. [Deng Xiaoping](/wiki/Deng_Xiaoping) took power in 1978, and started the [reform and opening up](/wiki/Reform_and_opening_up), instituting large-scale [political](/wiki/Boluan_Fanzheng) and economic reforms to transition away from planned economy. China adopted its current [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_China) on 4 December 1982. In 1989, there were protests such [those in Tiananmen Square](/wiki/1989_Tiananmen_Square_protests_and_massacre), and then throughout the entire nation. [Jiang Zemin](/wiki/Jiang_Zemin) was elevated to become the CCP general secretary, becoming the paramount leader. China's economy grew sevenfold during Jiang's tenure. [British Hong Kong](/wiki/British_Hong_Kong) and [Portuguese Macau](/wiki/Portuguese_Macau) returned to China in [1997](/wiki/Handover_of_Hong_Kong) and [1999](/wiki/Handover_of_Macau), respectively, as [special administrative regions](/wiki/Special_administrative_regions_of_China) under the principle of [one country, two systems](/wiki/One_country,_two_systems). In 2002, [Hu Jintao](/wiki/Hu_Jintao) succeeded Jiang as the general secretary, though it was accompanied by large-scale restructuring of [state-owned enterprises](/wiki/State-owned_enterprises_of_China), resulting in widespread layoffs and urban unemployment known as *[xiagang](/wiki/Xiagang)*. Under Hu, China maintained its high rate of economic growth, overtaking the United Kingdom, France, Germany and Japan to become the world's second-largest economy. However, the growth also severely impacted the country's resources and environment, and caused major social displacement. [Xi Jinping](/wiki/Xi_Jinping) succeeded Hu as paramount leader in 2012. Shortly after his ascension to power, Xi launched [a vast anti-corruption crackdown](/wiki/Anti-corruption_campaign_under_Xi_Jinping), that prosecuted more than 2 million officials by 2022. During [his tenure](/wiki/China_under_Xi_Jinping), Xi has consolidated power unseen since the initiation of economic and political reforms.

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of China](/wiki/Geography_of_China)

A topographic map of China

China's landscape is vast and diverse, ranging from the [Gobi](/wiki/Gobi_Desert) and [Taklamakan Deserts](/wiki/Taklamakan_Desert) in the arid north to the [subtropical](/wiki/Subtropical) forests in the wetter south. The [Himalaya](/wiki/Himalaya), [Karakoram](/wiki/Karakoram), [Pamir](/wiki/Pamir_Mountains) and [Tian Shan](/wiki/Tian_Shan) mountain ranges separate China from much of [South](/wiki/South_Asia) and [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia). The [Yangtze](/wiki/Yangtze_River) and Yellow Rivers, the third- and sixth-longest in the world, respectively, run from the [Tibetan Plateau](/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau) to the densely populated eastern seaboard. China's coastline along the [Pacific Ocean](/wiki/Pacific_Ocean) is 14,500 km (9,000 mi) long and is bounded by the [Bohai](/wiki/Bohai_Sea), [Yellow](/wiki/Yellow_Sea), [East China](/wiki/East_China_Sea) and [South China](/wiki/South_China_Sea) seas. China connects through the Kazakh border to the [Eurasian Steppe](/wiki/Eurasian_Steppe).

[Huangshan](/wiki/Huangshan) in [Anhui](/wiki/Anhui)

Biluthu Yinderitu lake amid the dunes of the [Badain Jaran Desert](/wiki/Badain_Jaran_Desert), [Inner Mongolia](/wiki/Inner_Mongolia).

The territory of China lies between [latitudes](/wiki/Latitude) [18°](/wiki/18th_parallel_north) and [54° N](/wiki/54th_parallel_north), and [longitudes](/wiki/Longitude) [73°](/wiki/73rd_meridian_east) and [135° E](/wiki/135th_meridian_east). The [geographical center](/wiki/Geographical_center) of China is marked by the Center of the Country Monument at [35°50′40.9″N 103°27′7.5″E / 35.844694°N 103.452083°E / 35.844694; 103.452083 (Geographical center of China)](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=China&params=35_50_40.9_N_103_27_7.5_E_region:CN-62_type:landmark&title=Geographical+center+of+China). China's landscapes vary significantly across its vast territory. In the east, along the shores of the Yellow Sea and the East China Sea, there are extensive and densely populated [alluvial plains](/wiki/Alluvial_plain), while on the edges of the Inner Mongolian plateau in the north, broad [grasslands](/wiki/Grassland) predominate. Southern China is dominated by hills and low mountain ranges, while the central-east hosts the [deltas](/wiki/River_delta) of China's two major rivers, the Yellow River and the Yangtze River. Other major rivers include the [Xi](/wiki/Xi_River), [Mekong](/wiki/Mekong), [Brahmaputra](/wiki/Brahmaputra_River) and [Amur](/wiki/Amur_River). To the west sit major mountain ranges, most notably the Himalayas. High [plateaus](/wiki/Plateau) feature among the more arid landscapes of the north, such as the Taklamakan and the Gobi Desert. The world's highest point, [Mount Everest](/wiki/Mount_Everest) (8,848 m), lies on the Sino-Nepalese border. The country's lowest point, and the world's third-lowest, is the dried lake bed of [Ayding Lake](/wiki/Ayding_Lake) (−154 m) in the [Turpan Depression](/wiki/Turpan_Depression).
Despite spanning the equivalent of five geographical time zones (from UTC+5 to UTC+9), China uses a [single national time zone](/wiki/Time_in_China), China Standard Time (UTC+8). This uniform time policy was adopted in 1949.

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of China](/wiki/Climate_of_China)

Further information: [Great Green Wall (China)](/wiki/Great_Green_Wall_(China))

China's climate is mainly dominated by [dry seasons](/wiki/Dry_season) and wet [monsoons](/wiki/Monsoons), which lead to pronounced temperature differences between winter and summer. In the winter, northern winds coming from high-latitude areas are cold and dry; in summer, southern winds from coastal areas at lower latitudes are warm and moist.

[Köppen-Geiger climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) map for mainland China

A major environmental issue in China is the continued [expansion of its deserts](/wiki/Desertification), particularly the Gobi Desert. Although barrier tree lines planted since the 1970s have reduced the frequency of [sandstorms](/wiki/Sandstorms), prolonged drought and poor agricultural practices have resulted in [dust storms](/wiki/Asian_dust) plaguing northern China each spring, which then spread to other parts of East Asia, including Japan and Korea. Water quality, [erosion](/wiki/Erosion), and [pollution control](/wiki/Pollution_in_China) have become important issues in China's relations with other countries. Melting [glaciers](/wiki/Glaciers) in the Himalayas could potentially lead to [water shortages](/wiki/Water_shortage) for hundreds of millions of people. According to academics, in order to limit [climate change in China](/wiki/Climate_change_in_China) to 1.5 °C (2.7 °F) electricity generation from [coal in China](/wiki/Coal_in_China) without [carbon capture](/wiki/Carbon_capture_and_storage) must be phased out by 2045.

Official government statistics about Chinese agricultural productivity are considered unreliable, due to exaggeration of production at subsidiary government levels. Much of China has a climate very suitable for agriculture and the country has been the world's largest producer of rice, wheat, tomatoes, eggplant, grapes, watermelon, spinach, and many other crops. In 2021, 12 percent of global permanent meadows and pastures belonged to China, as well as 8% of global cropland.

### Biodiversity

Main article: [Wildlife of China](/wiki/Wildlife_of_China)

A [giant panda](/wiki/Giant_panda), one of China's most famous symbols, at the [Chengdu Panda Base](/wiki/Chengdu_Research_Base_of_Giant_Panda_Breeding) in [Sichuan](/wiki/Sichuan)

China is one of 17 [megadiverse countries](/wiki/Megadiverse_countries), lying in two of the world's major [biogeographic realms](/wiki/Biogeographic_realm): the [Palearctic](/wiki/Palearctic_realm) and the [Indomalayan](/wiki/Indomalayan_realm). By one measure, China has over 34,687 species of animals and vascular plants, making it the third-most biodiverse country in the world, after [Brazil](/wiki/Brazil) and [Colombia](/wiki/Colombia). The country is a party to the [Convention on Biological Diversity](/wiki/Convention_on_Biological_Diversity); its [National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan](/wiki/Biodiversity_action_plan) was received by the convention in 2010.

China is home to at least 551 species of [mammals](/wiki/List_of_mammals_of_China) (the third-highest in the world), 1,221 species of birds (eighth), 424 species of reptiles (seventh) and 333 species of amphibians (seventh). Wildlife in China shares habitat with, and bears acute pressure from, one of the world's largest population of humans. At least 840 [animal species are threatened, vulnerable or in danger of local extinction](/wiki/List_of_endangered_and_protected_species_of_China), due mainly to human activity such as habitat destruction, pollution and poaching for food, fur and [traditional Chinese medicine](/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_medicine). Endangered wildlife is protected by law, and as of 2019, the country has over 2,750 [nature reserves](/wiki/Protected_areas_of_China), covering a total area of 1,470,000 km2 (570,000 sq mi), 15 percent of China's total land area. Most wild animals have been eliminated from the core agricultural regions of east and central China, but they have fared better in the mountainous south and west. The [Baiji](/wiki/Baiji) was confirmed extinct on 12 December 2006.

China has over 32,000 species of vascular plants, and is home to a variety of forest types. China lost 19 000 ha of primary forest between 1990 and 2000 but gained of 100 000 ha per year between 2015 and 2025. Cold [coniferous](/wiki/Coniferous) forests predominate in the north of the country, supporting animal species such as [moose](/wiki/Moose) and [Asian black bear](/wiki/Asian_black_bear), along with over 120 bird species. The [understory](/wiki/Understory) of moist [conifer](/wiki/Conifer) forests may contain thickets of [bamboo](/wiki/Bamboo). In higher [montane](/wiki/Montane_ecosystems) stands of [juniper](/wiki/Juniper) and [yew](/wiki/Taxus), the bamboo is replaced by [rhododendrons](/wiki/Rhododendron). [Subtropical](/wiki/Subtropical) forests, which are predominate in central and southern China, support a high density of plant species including numerous rare endemics. Tropical and seasonal [rainforests](/wiki/Rainforest), though confined to [Yunnan](/wiki/Yunnan) and [Hainan](/wiki/Hainan), contain a quarter of all the animal and plant species found in China. China has over 10,000 recorded species of [fungi](/wiki/Fungi).

### Environment

Main articles: [Environment of China](/wiki/Environment_of_China) and [Environmental issues in China](/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_China)

See also: [Renewable energy in China](/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_China), [Water resources of China](/wiki/Water_resources_of_China), [Energy policy of China](/wiki/Energy_policy_of_China), and [Climate change in China](/wiki/Climate_change_in_China)

The [Three Gorges Dam](/wiki/Three_Gorges_Dam) is the largest hydroelectric dam in the world.

The China Energy Engineering Corporation 50 MW Hami power tower has 8 hours of molten-salt storage

In the early 2000s, China has suffered from [environmental deterioration and pollution](/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_China) due to its rapid pace of industrialization. Environmental regulations fairly stringent, though they are poorly enforced, frequently disregarded in favor of rapid economic development. China has the second-highest death toll because of air pollution, after [India](/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_India), with approximately 1 million deaths. Although China ranks as the highest [CO2 emitting](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_carbon_dioxide_emissions) country, it only emits 8 tons of [CO2 per capita](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_carbon_dioxide_emissions_per_capita), significantly lower than developed countries such as the United States (16.1), Australia (16.8) and South Korea (13.6). [Greenhouse gas emissions by China](/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions_by_China) are the [world's largest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_greenhouse_gas_emissions). 90.4% of China's national surface water was graded suitable for human consumption by the [Ministry of Ecology and Environment](/wiki/Ministry_of_Ecology_and_Environment) in 2024.

China has prioritized clamping down on pollution, bringing a significant decrease in air pollution in the 2010s. In 2020, the Chinese government announced its aims for the country to reach its peak emissions levels before 2030, and achieve carbon neutrality by 2060 in line with the [Paris Agreement](/wiki/Paris_Agreement), which, according to [Climate Action Tracker](/wiki/Climate_Action_Tracker), would lower the expected rise in global temperature by 0.2–0.3 degrees – "the biggest single reduction ever estimated by the Climate Action Tracker". According to China's government, the forest coverage of the country grew from 10% of the overall territory in 1949 to 25% in 2024.

China is the world's leading investor in [renewable energy](/wiki/Renewable_energy) and [its commercialization](/wiki/Renewable_energy_commercialization), with [$](/wiki/US$)546 billion invested in 2022; it the world's leading manufacturer and innovator of renewable energy technologies and invests heavily in local-scale renewable energy projects. Long heavily relying on non-renewable energy sources such as coal, China's adaptation of [renewable energy](/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_China) has increased significantly in recent years. In 2025, 54.4% of China's electricity came from [coal](/wiki/Coal_in_China) (largest producer in the world), 13.2% from [hydroelectric power](/wiki/Hydroelectric_power_in_Himachal_Pradesh) (largest), 11.1% from [solar energy](/wiki/Solar_power) (largest), 10.7% from [wind](/wiki/Wind_power_in_China) (largest), 4.6% from [nuclear energy](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_China) (second-largest), 3.2% from [natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas_in_China) (fifth-largest), and 2% from [bioenergy](/wiki/Bioenergy) (largest); in total, 42% of China's energy came from clean energy sources. Despite its emphasis on renewables, China remains deeply connected to global oil markets and next to India, has been the largest importer of Russian [crude oil](/wiki/Petroleum) in 2022.

### Political geography

Main articles: [Borders of China](/wiki/Borders_of_China), [Coastline of China](/wiki/Coastline_of_China), and [Territorial changes of the People's Republic of China](/wiki/Territorial_changes_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China)

Map depicting territorial disputes between the PRC and neighboring states.

China is the [second-largest](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area) country by land area after [Russia](/wiki/Russia), and the third- or fourth-largest country by total area. China's total area is generally stated as being approximately 9,600,000 km2 (3,700,000 sq mi). Specific area figures range from 9,572,900 km2 (3,696,100 sq mi) according to the *[Encyclopædia Britannica](/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica)*, to 9,596,961 km2 (3,705,407 sq mi) according to the *[UN Demographic Yearbook](/wiki/Yearbook_of_the_United_Nations)*, and *[The World Factbook](/wiki/The_World_Factbook)*.

China has the [longest combined land border in the world](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_territories_by_number_of_land_borders), measuring 22,117 km (13,743 mi) and its [coastline](/wiki/Coastline_of_China) covers approximately 14,500 km (9,000 mi) from the mouth of the [Yalu River](/wiki/Yalu_River) (Amnok River) to the [Gulf of Tonkin](/wiki/Gulf_of_Tonkin). China [borders 14 nations](/wiki/Borders_of_China) and covers the bulk of East Asia, bordering [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam), [Laos](/wiki/Laos), and [Myanmar](/wiki/Myanmar) in Southeast Asia; [India](/wiki/India), [Bhutan](/wiki/Bhutan), [Nepal](/wiki/Nepal), [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan) and [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan) in South Asia; [Tajikistan](/wiki/Tajikistan), [Kyrgyzstan](/wiki/Kyrgyzstan) and [Kazakhstan](/wiki/Kazakhstan) in Central Asia; and [Russia](/wiki/Russia), [Mongolia](/wiki/Mongolia), and [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea) in [Inner Asia](/wiki/Inner_Asia) and [Northeast Asia](/wiki/Northeast_Asia). It is narrowly separated from [Bangladesh](/wiki/Bangladesh) and [Thailand](/wiki/Thailand) to the southwest and south, and has several maritime neighbors such as [Japan](/wiki/Japan), [Philippines](/wiki/Philippines), [Malaysia](/wiki/Malaysia), and [Indonesia](/wiki/Indonesia).

China has resolved its land borders with 12 out of 14 neighboring countries, having pursued substantial compromises in most of them. China currently has a disputed land border with [India](/wiki/Sino-Indian_border_dispute) and [Bhutan](/wiki/Bhutan%E2%80%93China_border). China is additionally involved in maritime disputes with multiple countries over territory in the [East](/wiki/East_China_Sea_EEZ_disputes) and [South China Seas](/wiki/Territorial_disputes_in_the_South_China_Sea), such as the [Senkaku Islands](/wiki/Senkaku_Islands_dispute), which it refers as the Diaoyu Islands, and the entirety of [South China Sea Islands](/wiki/South_China_Sea_Islands).

## Government and politics

Main article: [Politics of China](/wiki/Politics_of_China)

See also: [Party and state leaders](/wiki/Party_and_state_leaders) and [List of current Chinese provincial leaders](/wiki/List_of_current_Chinese_provincial_leaders)

The [Great Hall of the People](/wiki/Great_Hall_of_the_People)
where the [National People's Congress](/wiki/National_People%27s_Congress) convenes

The [Zhongnanhai](/wiki/Zhongnanhai), headquarters of the [Chinese government](/wiki/State_Council_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China) and [Chinese Communist Party](/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party)

The People's Republic of China is a [communist state](/wiki/Communist_state) under the [absolute leadership](/wiki/The_Party_leads_everything) of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). The CCP is officially [guided](/wiki/Ideology_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party) by [socialism with Chinese characteristics](/wiki/Socialism_with_Chinese_characteristics), which it describes as [Marxism adapted to Chinese circumstances](/wiki/Chinese_Marxist_philosophy). The [Chinese constitution](/wiki/Chinese_constitution) states that the PRC "is a [socialist state](/wiki/Socialist_state_(communism)) governed by a [people's democratic dictatorship](/wiki/People%27s_democratic_dictatorship) that is led by the working class and based on an alliance of workers and peasants", and that "the defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics is the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party."

The PRC officially characterizes itself as [a democracy](/wiki/Democracy_in_China)—more specifically, a [whole-process people's democracy](/wiki/Whole-process_people%27s_democracy)—that is organized around the [Leninist](/wiki/Leninist) principle of [democratic centralism](/wiki/Democratic_centralism). However, the country is commonly described as an [authoritarian](/wiki/Authoritarianism) one-party state and a [dictatorship](/wiki/Dictatorship), with some of the world's heaviest restrictions in many civil areas, most notably against [freedom of the press](/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press_in_China), [freedom of assembly](/wiki/Freedom_of_assembly), [free formation of social organizations](/wiki/Non-governmental_organization), [freedom of religion](/wiki/Freedom_of_religion_in_China) and [freedom of the Internet](/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_China). China has consistently been ranked amongst the lowest as an "authoritarian regime" by the [Economist Intelligence Unit](/wiki/Economist_Intelligence_Unit)'s [Democracy Index](/wiki/The_Economist_Democracy_Index), ranking at 141st out of 167 countries in 2025. In the [V-Dem Democracy Indices](/wiki/V-Dem_Democracy_Indices), China ranks amongst the lowest as a "closed autocracy", ranking at 175th and 172nd out of 179 countries in electoral democracy and liberal democracy indices respectively. Other sources suggest that terming China as "authoritarian" does not sufficiently account for the multiple consultation mechanisms that exist in the Chinese governmental system.

### Chinese Communist Party

Main article: [Chinese Communist Party](/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party)

The [Chinese Communist Party](/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party) is the founding and governing political party of the People's Republic of China.

The CCP is the founding and sole ruling party of the PRC. According to the [CCP constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party), the Party's highest body is the [National Congress](/wiki/National_Congress_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party) held every five years. The National Congress elects the [Central Committee](/wiki/Central_Committee_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party), which is the CCP's highest organ between congresses and convenes at least once a year. The Central Committee then elects the party's [Politburo](/wiki/Politburo_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party), [Politburo Standing Committee](/wiki/Politburo_Standing_Committee_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party) and the [general secretary](/wiki/General_Secretary_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party) ([party leader](/wiki/Leader_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party)), the top leadership of the country. The Politburo usually gathers once a month, while the smaller Politburo Standing Committee is thought to meet weekly. The general secretary holds ultimate power and authority over party and state and serves as the [paramount leader](/wiki/Paramount_leader) of China. The current general secretary is [Xi Jinping](/wiki/Xi_Jinping), who took office on 15 November 2012. The National Congress also elects the [Central Commission for Discipline Inspection](/wiki/Central_Commission_for_Discipline_Inspection), the CCP's supreme disciplinary organ.

The government in China is under the sole control of the CCP. The CCP controls appointments in government bodies, with most senior government officials being CCP members. The CCP maintains committees in the each level of government. At the local level, the CCP committee of a subdivision and its [secretary](/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party_Committee_Secretary) outranks the local government and its head; CCP committee of a provincial division and its secretary outranks the provincial people's government and the governor while the CCP committee of a city and its secretary outranks the municipal people's government the mayor.

The [National People's Congress](/wiki/National_People%27s_Congress) (NPC), with nearly 3,000-members, as the [supreme organ of state power](/wiki/Supreme_state_organ_of_power) holds the [unified powers of the state](/wiki/Unified_state_power), creating a system where all state organs including the [presidency](/wiki/President_of_China), the [State Council](/wiki/State_Council_of_China), the [State Central Military Commission](/wiki/Central_Military_Commission_(China)), the [Supreme People's Court](/wiki/Supreme_People%27s_Court), the [Supreme People's Procuratorate](/wiki/Supreme_People%27s_Procuratorate), and the [National Supervisory Commission](/wiki/National_Supervisory_Commission) are subject to its oversight through democratic centralism. However, observers often describe it as a "[rubber stamp](/wiki/Rubber_stamp_(politics))" body. The NPC meets annually, while the [NPC Standing Committee](/wiki/Standing_Committee_of_the_National_People%27s_Congress), around 175 members elected from NPC delegates, meets every couple of months. Its [elections](/wiki/Elections_in_China) are [indirect](/wiki/Indirect_election) and not [pluralistic](/wiki/Pluralism_(political_philosophy)), with nominations at all levels being controlled by the CCP by law. The NPC is dominated by the CCP, with another [eight minor parties](/wiki/Democratic_parties_(China)) having nominal representation under the condition of upholding CCP leadership.

The NPC elects the president, who is the ceremonial state representative. The incumbent president is [Xi Jinping](/wiki/Xi_Jinping), who is also the general secretary of the CCP and the [chairman of the Central Military Commission](/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Central_Military_Commission_(China)), making him China's paramount leader and [supreme commander](/wiki/Supreme_command_of_the_armed_forces_in_China) of the armed forces. The [premier](/wiki/Premier_of_China) is the [head of government](/wiki/Head_of_government), with [Li Qiang](/wiki/Li_Qiang) being the incumbent. The premier is officially nominated by the president and then elected by the NPC, and has generally been either the second- or third-ranking member of the Politburo Standing Committee (PSC). The premier presides over the State Council, China's cabinet, composed of four vice premiers, [state councillors](/wiki/State_councillor), and the heads of ministries and commissions. The [Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference](/wiki/Chinese_People%27s_Political_Consultative_Conference) (CPPCC) is a political advisory body that formally leads China's "[united front](/wiki/United_front_(China))" system, which aims to gather non-CCP voices to support the CCP. Similar to the people's congresses, CPPCCs have subdivisions; the [National Committee of the CPPCC](/wiki/National_Committee_of_the_Chinese_People%27s_Political_Consultative_Conference) is chaired by [Wang Huning](/wiki/Wang_Huning), the fourth-ranking member of the PSC.

The governance of China is characterized by a high degree of political centralization but significant economic decentralization. Policy instruments or processes are often tested locally before being applied more widely, resulting in a policy that involves experimentation and feedback. Generally, central government leadership refrains from drafting specific policies, instead using the informal networks and site visits to affirm or suggest changes to the direction of local policy experiments or pilot programs. The typical approach is that central government leadership begins drafting formal policies, law, or regulations after policy has been developed at local levels.

### Administrative divisions

Main articles: [Administrative divisions of China](/wiki/Administrative_divisions_of_China), [Districts of Hong Kong](/wiki/Districts_of_Hong_Kong), and [Municipalities and parishes of Macau](/wiki/Municipalities_and_parishes_of_Macau)

The PRC is constitutionally a [unitary state](/wiki/Unitary_state) divided into 23 [provinces](/wiki/Provinces_of_China), five [autonomous regions](/wiki/Autonomous_regions_of_China) (each with a designated minority group), four [direct-administered municipalities](/wiki/Direct-administered_municipality)—collectively referred to as "mainland China"—as well as the [special administrative regions](/wiki/Special_administrative_regions_of_China) (SARs) of Hong Kong and Macau. The PRC regards the [island of Taiwan](/wiki/Geography_of_Taiwan) as its [Taiwan Province](/wiki/Taiwan_Province,_People%27s_Republic_of_China), [Kinmen](/wiki/Kinmen) and [Matsu](/wiki/Matsu_Islands) as a part of [Fujian Province](/wiki/Fujian), and islands the ROC controls in the South China Sea as a part of [Hainan Province](/wiki/Hainan) and [Guangdong Province](/wiki/Guangdong), though all these territories are governed by the [Republic of China](/wiki/Taiwan) (ROC). Geographically, all 31 provincial divisions of mainland China can be grouped into six regions: [North China](/wiki/North_China), [East China](/wiki/East_China), [Southwestern China](/wiki/Southwestern_China), [South Central China](/wiki/South_Central_China), [Northwestern China](/wiki/Northwestern_China), and [Northeast China](/wiki/Northeast_China).

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of China](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_China)

The PRC has diplomatic relations with 179 [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) member-states and maintains [embassies in 174](/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_of_China). As of 2024, China has the largest diplomatic networks of any country in the world. China is of the five permanent members of the [United Nations Security Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council). It is a member of intergovernmental organizations including the [G20](/wiki/G20), the [Shanghai Cooperation Organisation](/wiki/Shanghai_Cooperation_Organisation), the [BRICS](/wiki/BRICS), the [East Asia Summit](/wiki/East_Asia_Summit), and the [APEC](/wiki/Asia-Pacific_Economic_Cooperation). China is also a former member and leader of the [Non-Aligned Movement](/wiki/Non-Aligned_Movement) and still considers itself an advocate for [developing countries](/wiki/Developing_countries). China is widely [described as either a potential or established superpower](/wiki/China_as_a_potential_superpower), due to its influence in the fields of geopolitics, technology, manufacturing, economics and culture.

Much of current [Chinese foreign policy](/wiki/Foreign_policy_of_China) is officially based on Premier [Zhou Enlai](/wiki/Zhou_Enlai)'s [Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence](/wiki/Five_Principles_of_Peaceful_Coexistence), as well as by the concept of "harmony without uniformity", which encourages diplomatic relations between states despite ideological differences. Per its policy of non-alignment, China maintains no military alliances except its [defense treaty](/wiki/Treaty_on_Friendship,_Cooperation_and_Mutual_Assistance_between_China_and_the_DPRK) with [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea). Most countries have switched recognition from the ROC to the PRC since the latter replaced the former in the UN in 1971. The PRC officially maintains the [one China principle](/wiki/One_China_principle): the view that there is only one sovereign state with the name "China"—represented by the PRC—and that Taiwan is an integral part of China. The unique status of Taiwan has led to countries formally recognizing the PRC to maintain unique "[one China](/wiki/One_China) policies" that differ from each other; some countries [explicitly recognize](/wiki/International_recognition_of_Taiwan) the PRC's claim over Taiwan, while others, including the U.S. and Japan, only *acknowledge* the claim.

Chinese President [Xi Jinping](/wiki/Xi_Jinping) at the [16th BRICS summit](/wiki/16th_BRICS_summit) in [Kazan](/wiki/Kazan), Russia, 23 October 2024

China has a [close political, economic and military relationship](/wiki/China%E2%80%93Russia_relations) with Russia, and the two states often vote in unison in the UN Security Council. China has provided Russia with economic, technological, and diplomatic support during the [Russo-Ukrainian war](/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_(2022%E2%80%93present)). [China's relationship with the United States](/wiki/China%E2%80%93United_States_relations) is complex, and includes deep trade ties but significant political differences. Since the early 2000s, China has followed a policy of [engaging with African nations](/wiki/Sino-African_relations) for trade and bilateral co-operation. It maintains extensive and highly diversified trade links with the European Union, and became its largest trading partner for goods. China is increasing its influence in [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia) and South Pacific. The country has strong trade ties with [ASEAN](/wiki/ASEAN) countries and major South American economies, and is the largest trading partner of Brazil, Chile, Peru, Uruguay, Argentina, and several others.

In 2013, China initiated the [Belt and Road Initiative](/wiki/Belt_and_Road_Initiative) (BRI), a large global infrastructure building initiative with funding on the order of $50–100 billion per year. BRI could be one of the largest development plans in modern history. It expanded significantly over the next six years and, as of April 2020, included 138 countries and 30 international organizations. In addition to intensifying foreign policy relations, the focus is particularly on building efficient transport routes, especially the [maritime Silk Road](/wiki/21st_Century_Maritime_Silk_Road) with its connections to East Africa and Europe. However many loans made under the program are unsustainable and China has faced a number of calls for [debt relief](/wiki/Debt_relief) from debtor nations.

### Military

Main articles: [Military budget of China](/wiki/Military_budget_of_China), [People's Liberation Army](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army), and [Paramilitary forces of China](/wiki/Paramilitary_forces_of_China)

[Chengdu J-20](/wiki/Chengdu_J-20) [5th generation](/wiki/Fifth-generation_fighter) stealth fighter

The [People's Liberation Army](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army) (PLA) is considered one of the world's most powerful militaries and has [rapidly modernized](/wiki/Modernization_of_the_People%27s_Liberation_Army) in the recent decades. Per the principle of "[the Party commands the gun](/wiki/The_Party_commands_the_gun)," the CCP maintains absolute control of the PLA. It consists of four services: the [Ground Force](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Ground_Force) (PLAGF), the [Navy](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Navy) (PLAN), the [Air Force](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Air_Force) (PLAAF) and the [Rocket Force](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Rocket_Force) (PLARF). It also has four independent arms: the [Aerospace Force](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Aerospace_Force), the [Cyberspace Force](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Cyberspace_Force), the [Information Support Force](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Information_Support_Force), and the [Joint Logistics Support Force](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Joint_Logistics_Support_Force), the first three of which were split from the disbanded [Strategic Support Force](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army_Strategic_Support_Force) (PLASSF). Its 2 million active duty personnel is the [largest in the world](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_military_and_paramilitary_personnel). The PLA holds the world's [third-largest stockpile of nuclear weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_China), and the world's second-largest navy by tonnage.

China's official [military budget](/wiki/Military_budget_of_China) for 2024 totalled US$246 billion (1.78 trillion Yuan), the [second-largest in the world](/wiki/List_of_countries_with_highest_military_expenditures), though the [Stockholm International Peace Research Institute](/wiki/Stockholm_International_Peace_Research_Institute) estimates that its real expenditure that year was US$336 billion, making up 12% of global military spending and accounting for 1.7% of the country's GDP. On a [Purchasing Power Parity](/wiki/Purchasing_Power_Parity) basis, China's 2024 military budget was the equivalent of US$567 billion. The PLA is commanded by the [Central Military Commission](/wiki/Central_Military_Commission_(China)) (CMC) of the party and the state; though officially two separate organizations, the two CMCs have identical membership except during leadership transition periods and effectively function as one organization. The [chairman of the CMC](/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Central_Military_Commission_(China)) is the [commander-in-chief](/wiki/Supreme_command_of_the_armed_forces_in_China) of the PLA, who maintains absolute control over the military per the [chairman responsibility system](/wiki/Chairman_responsibility_system).

### Sociopolitical issues and human rights

Main articles: [Human rights in China](/wiki/Human_rights_in_China) and [Censorship in China](/wiki/Censorship_in_China)

The situation of [human rights in China](/wiki/Human_rights_in_China) has attracted significant criticism from foreign governments, foreign press agencies, and non-governmental organizations, alleging widespread civil rights violations such as detention without trial, forced confessions, torture, restrictions of fundamental rights, and [excessive use of the death penalty](/wiki/Capital_punishment_in_China). Since its inception, [Freedom House](/wiki/Freedom_House) has ranked China as "not free" in its *[Freedom in the World](/wiki/Freedom_in_the_World)* survey, while [Amnesty International](/wiki/Amnesty_International) has documented significant human rights abuses. The [Chinese constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_China) nevertheless states that the "[fundamental rights](/wiki/Fundamental_rights)" of citizens include [freedom of speech](/wiki/Freedom_of_speech), [freedom of the press](/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press), the [right to a fair trial](/wiki/Right_to_a_fair_trial), [freedom of religion](/wiki/Freedom_of_religion), [universal suffrage](/wiki/Universal_suffrage), and [property rights](/wiki/Property). However, in practice, these provisions do not afford significant protection against criminal prosecution by the state. Although some criticisms of government policies and the ruling CCP are tolerated, [censorship](/wiki/Censorship) of political speech and information is amongst the harshest in the world and is routinely used to prevent collective action. The government suppresses popular protests and demonstrations that it considers a potential threat to "social stability". China additionally uses a massive surveillance network of cameras, facial recognition software, sensors, and surveillance of personal technology as a means of social control of persons living in the country.

In [Xinjiang](/wiki/Xinjiang), China has been accused of detaining more than one million [Uyghurs](/wiki/Uyghurs) and other ethnic minorities in camps.



[2019–20 Hong Kong protests](/wiki/2019%E2%80%9320_Hong_Kong_protests)

China is regularly accused of [large-scale repression](/wiki/Cultural_repression_in_Tibet) and human rights abuses in [Tibet](/wiki/Human_rights_in_Tibet) and [Xinjiang](/wiki/Persecution_of_Uyghurs_in_China), where significant numbers of ethnic minorities reside, including violent police crackdowns and [religious suppression](/wiki/Religious_suppression). Since 2017, the Chinese government has been engaged in a harsh crackdown in Xinjiang, with around one million [Uyghurs](/wiki/Uyghurs) and other ethnic and religion minorities being detained in [internment camps](/wiki/Xinjiang_internment_camps) aimed at changing the political thinking of detainees, their identities, and their religious beliefs, in what some described as a genocide or [crimes against humanity](/wiki/Crimes_against_humanity). According to reports, political indoctrination, torture, [physical](/wiki/Physical_abuse) and [psychological](/wiki/Psychological_abuse) abuse, [forced sterilization](/wiki/Compulsory_sterilization), [sexual abuse](/wiki/Sexual_abuse), and [forced labor](/wiki/Forced_labour) are common in these facilities. The Chinese authorities have also cracked down on dissent in [Hong Kong](/wiki/Hong_Kong), especially after the passage of a [national security law](/wiki/2020_Hong_Kong_national_security_law) in 2020.

In 2017 and 2020, the [Pew Research Center](/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) ranked the severity of Chinese government restrictions on religion as being among the world's highest, despite ranking religious-related social hostilities in China as low in severity. China has limited protections regarding [LGBT rights](/wiki/LGBT_rights_in_China). The [Global Slavery Index](/wiki/Global_Slavery_Index) estimated that in 2016 more than 3.8 million people (0.25% of the population) were living in "conditions of modern [slavery](/wiki/Slavery_in_China)", including victims of human trafficking, forced labor, forced marriage, child labor, and state-imposed forced labor. The state-imposed [re-education through labor](/wiki/Re-education_through_labor) (*laojiao*) system was formally abolished in 2013, but it is not clear to what extent its practices have stopped. The much larger [reform through labor](/wiki/Laogai) (*laogai*) system includes labor prison factories, detention centers, and re-education camps; the [Laogai Research Foundation](/wiki/Laogai_Research_Foundation) has estimated in June 2008 that there were nearly 1,422 of these facilities, though it cautioned that this number was likely an underestimate.

### Public views of government

Further information: [Politics of China § Citizen surveys](/wiki/Politics_of_China#Citizen_surveys)

Political concerns in China include the growing gap between rich and poor and government corruption. Nonetheless, international surveys show the Chinese public have a high level of satisfaction with their government. These views are generally attributed to the material comforts and security available to large segments of the Chinese populace as well as the government's attentiveness and responsiveness.  According to the [World Values Survey](/wiki/World_Values_Survey) (2022), 91% of Chinese respondents have significant confidence in their government. A Harvard University survey published in July 2020 found that citizen satisfaction with the government had increased since 2003, also rating China's government as more effective and capable than ever in the survey's history. A 2020 study by [University of Southern California](/wiki/University_of_Southern_California) researchers affiliated with the [Hoover Institution](/wiki/Hoover_Institution) found that more anonymous surveys show 50 to 70 percent think the government works for the people, lower than what direct surveys show support at above 90 percent.

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of China](/wiki/Economy_of_China)

Blue hour view of the Bund from the [Shanghai World Financial Center](/wiki/Shanghai_World_Financial_Center)

China has the world's [second-largest economy](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)) in terms of [nominal GDP](/wiki/Nominal_GDP), and the world's [largest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(PPP)) in terms of [purchasing power parity](/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity) (PPP). As of 2022, China accounts for around 18% of the [global economy](/wiki/World_economy) by nominal GDP. China is one of the world's [fastest-growing](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_real_GDP_growth_rate) major economies, with its economic growth having been almost consistently above 5 percent since the introduction of the [reform and opening up](/wiki/Reform_and_opening_up) policy in 1978. According to the World Bank, China's GDP grew from $150 billion in 1978 to $18.74 trillion by 2024. It ranks [75th by nominal GDP per capita](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)_per_capita), making it an upper-middle income country. Of the world's [500 largest companies](/wiki/Fortune_Global_500), 135 are headquartered in China.

China [was one of the world's foremost economic powers](/wiki/Economic_history_of_China_before_1912) throughout the arc of [East Asian](/wiki/Economy_of_East_Asia#China) and [global history](/wiki/Economic_history_of_China_before_1912). The country [had one of the largest economies](/wiki/List_of_regions_by_past_GDP_(PPP)) in the world for most of the [past two millennia](/wiki/Pax_Sinica), during which it has seen cycles of prosperity and decline. Since economic reforms began in 1978, China has developed into a highly diversified economy and one of the most consequential players in international trade. China officially calls its economic system as the [socialist market economy](/wiki/Socialist_market_economy), in which the market plays a decisive role in resource allocation under the macro-control of the socialist state. Modern-day China is often described as an example of [state capitalism](/wiki/State_capitalism) or [party-state capitalism](/wiki/Party-state_capitalism), with both market forces and the state playing a major role in the economy. China has numerous [state-owned enterprises](/wiki/State-owned_enterprises_of_China); the state dominates in strategic "pillar" sectors such as infrastructure, telecommunications, finance, energy production and [heavy industries](/wiki/Heavy_industry). The Chinese government issues [five-year plans](/wiki/Five-year_plans_of_China) to guide the direction of the economy. Private enterprise has expanded enormously, with around 57 million private businesses recorded in 2025. According to official statistics, privately owned companies constitute more than 60% of China's GDP.

### Wealth

See also: [Income inequality in China](/wiki/Income_inequality_in_China)

GDP per capita in China, from 1850 to 2022

China [accounted for 18.6%](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_total_wealth) of the world's total wealth in 2022, second highest in the world after the U.S. China brought more people out of extreme [poverty](/wiki/Poverty_in_China) than any other country in history—between 1978 and 2018, China reduced extreme poverty by 800 million, with the average standard of living multiplying by a factor of twenty-six. From 1990 to 2018, the proportion of the Chinese population living with an income of less than $1.90 per day (2011 [PPP](/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity)) decreased from 66.3% to 0.3%, the share living with an income of less than $3.20 per day from 90.0% to 2.9%, and the share living with an income of less than $5.50 per day decreased from 98.3% to 17.0%. Per capita incomes have risen significantly – when the PRC was founded in 1949, per capita income in China was one-fifth of the world average; per capita incomes now equal the world average itself.

China's development is highly uneven; its major cities and coastal areas are far more prosperous than its rural and interior regions. It has a high level of economic inequality, which has increased quickly since the economic reforms. [Income inequality](/wiki/Income_inequality) decreased in the 2010s, and China's [Gini coefficient](/wiki/Gini_coefficient) was 0.37 in 2023. In March 2025, *[Forbes](/wiki/Forbes)* estimated China ranked second in the world, after the U.S., in [total number of billionaires](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_billionaires) and [total number of millionaires](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_millionaires), with 516 Chinese billionaires, while *Hurun Global Rich List* estimated it ranked first, with 1,021 billionaires. China also has 6.3 million millionaires as of 2025, second highest after the U.S. In 2019, China overtook the U.S. as the home to the highest number of people who have a net personal wealth of at least $110,000, according to the global wealth report by [Credit Suisse](/wiki/Credit_Suisse). China had 85 female billionaires as of January 2021, two-thirds of the global total. China has had the world's largest middle-class population since 2015; the middle-class grew to 500 million by 2024.

### Industry and services

China is the world's leading manufacturing power, accounting for 30% of global manufacturing. It has been the world's [largest manufacturing nation](/wiki/Manufacturing#List_of_countries_by_manufacturing_output) since 2010, after overtaking the U.S., which had been the largest for the previous hundred years. China is the world's leading producer of [steel](/wiki/Steel_industry_in_China) and [rare earths](/wiki/Rare-earth_industry_in_China), the world's leading [electronics industry manufacturer](/wiki/Electronics_industry_in_China), and the world's dominant shipbuilding manufacturer. China has the world's largest market for automobiles, having surpassed the United States in both auto sales and [production](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_motor_vehicle_production). The country is the world's largest exporter of cars by number as of 2024. The [Chinese automotive industry](/wiki/Automotive_industry_in_China) is regarded as one of the most competitive and innovative in the world. China is the world's leader in electric vehicle consumption, production and innovation, as well as the leading producer of batteries for electric vehicles.

China is the second-largest retail market after the United States. China leads the world in e-commerce, accounting for over 37% of the global market share in 2021. China has three out of the ten [largest stock exchanges](/wiki/List_of_major_stock_exchanges) in the world—[Shanghai](/wiki/Shanghai_Stock_Exchange), [Hong Kong](/wiki/Hong_Kong_Stock_Exchange) and [Shenzhen](/wiki/Shenzhen_Stock_Exchange)—that together have a market capitalization of over $15.9 trillion, as of October 2020. China has three out of the world's ten most competitive financial centers according to the 2026 [Global Financial Centres Index](/wiki/Global_Financial_Centres_Index)—[Shanghai](/wiki/Shanghai), [Hong Kong](/wiki/Hong_Kong), and [Shenzhen](/wiki/Shenzhen). As of at least 2024, China has the world's second-largest equity markets and futures markets, as well as the third-largest bond market. China has the world's largest [banking sector](/wiki/Banking_in_China). Its finance sector is dominated by state-owned institutions.

### China in the global economy

China has been a member of the [World Trade Organization](/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) since 2001 and is the world's [largest trading power](/wiki/List_of_largest_trading_countries). By 2016, China was the largest trading partner of 124 countries. China became the world's largest trading nation in 2013 by the sum of imports and exports, as well as the world's largest commodity importer, accounting for roughly 45% of maritime's [dry-bulk market](/wiki/Shipping_markets). [China's foreign exchange reserves](/wiki/Foreign-exchange_reserves_of_China) reached US$3.246 trillion as of March 2024, making its reserves by far the world's largest. In 2022, China was amongst the world's largest recipient of inward [foreign direct investment](/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment) (FDI), attracting $180 billion, though most of these were speculated to be from Hong Kong. China also invests abroad, with a total outward FDI of $147.9 billion in 2023.

The Chinese government has promoted the [internationalization of the renminbi](/wiki/Internationalization_of_the_renminbi) in order to wean itself off its dependence on the U.S. dollar. The renminbi is the world's fourth-most traded currency as of 2023. However, partly due to capital controls that make the renminbi fall short of being a fully convertible currency, it remains far behind the Euro and the U.S. Dollar in international trade volumes. The Chinese government has also been cited for failing to crack down on the manufacturing and export of [counterfeit](/wiki/Counterfeit) goods. In 2023, [Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_University)'s [Economic Complexity Index](/wiki/Economic_Complexity_Index) ranked complexity of China's exports 16th in the world, up from 24th in 2010.

The [Forbidden City](/wiki/Forbidden_City) is one of the most popular tourist attractions in the world.

Chongqing Skyline At Night

It also experiences an enormous volume of [domestic tourism](/wiki/Domestic_tourism); Chinese tourists made an estimated 6.5 billion travels within the country in 2025. ### Science and technology

Main articles: [Science and technology in China](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_China), [List of Chinese discoveries](/wiki/List_of_Chinese_discoveries), and [List of Chinese inventions](/wiki/List_of_Chinese_inventions)

#### Historical

Main article: [History of science and technology in China](/wiki/History_of_science_and_technology_in_China)

Earliest known written [formula for gunpowder](/wiki/History_of_gunpowder), from the *[Wujing Zongyao](/wiki/Wujing_Zongyao)* of 1044 CE

China was a world leader in science and technology until the [Ming dynasty](/wiki/Ming_dynasty). Ancient and medieval [Chinese discoveries](/wiki/List_of_Chinese_discoveries) and [inventions](/wiki/List_of_Chinese_inventions), such as [papermaking](/wiki/Papermaking), [printing](/wiki/History_of_typography_in_East_Asia), the [compass](/wiki/Compass), and [gunpowder](/wiki/Gunpowder) (the [Four Great Inventions](/wiki/Four_Great_Inventions)), became widespread across East Asia, the Middle East and later Europe. Chinese mathematicians were the first to use [negative numbers](/wiki/Negative_numbers#History). By the 17th century, the Western World surpassed China in scientific and technological advancement. The causes of this early modern [Great Divergence](/wiki/Great_Divergence) continue to be debated by scholars.

After [repeated military defeats](/wiki/Century_of_humiliation) by the [European colonial powers](/wiki/Eight-Nation_Alliance) and [Imperial Japan](/wiki/First_Sino-Japanese_War) in the 19th century, Chinese reformers began promoting modern science and technology as part of the [Self-Strengthening Movement](/wiki/Self-Strengthening_Movement). After the Communists came to power in 1949, efforts were made to organize science and technology based on the model of the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union), in which scientific research was part of central planning. After Mao's death in 1976, science and technology were promoted as one of the [Four Modernizations](/wiki/Four_Modernizations), and the Soviet-inspired academic system was gradually reformed.

#### Modern era

Since the end of the [Cultural Revolution](/wiki/Cultural_Revolution), China has made significant investments in scientific research and is quickly catching up with the U.S. [in R&D spending](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_by_research_and_development_spending). China officially spent around 2.7% of its GDP on R&D in 2024, totaling to around $496 billion. China was ranked 10th in the [Global Innovation Index](/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2025, a considerable improvement from its rank of 35th in 2013. [Chinese supercomputers](/wiki/Supercomputing_in_China) are ranked among the [fastest in the world](/wiki/TOP500). China is the world's largest [industrial robotics](/wiki/Industrial_robot) producer and user in the world, accounting for 54% of the world's 2024 total demand, and 43% of global production. Its efforts to develop the most advanced semiconductors and jet engines have seen delays and setbacks. China is also considered a world leader in [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_industry_in_China).

China is developing [its education system](/wiki/Education_in_China) with an emphasis on [science, technology, engineering, and mathematics](/wiki/Science,_technology,_engineering,_and_mathematics) (STEM). [Its academic publication apparatus](/wiki/Academic_publishing_in_China) became the world's [largest publisher of scientific papers](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_scientific_and_technical_journal_articles) in 2016. According to the [World Intellectual Property Indicators](/wiki/World_Intellectual_Property_Indicators), China received more applications than the U.S. did in 2018 and 2019 and ranked first globally in patents, utility models, trademarks, industrial designs, and creative goods exports in 2021. In 2022, China overtook the US in the [Nature Index](/wiki/Nature_Index), which measures the share of published articles in leading scientific journals.

##### Space program

Main articles: [Chinese space program](/wiki/Chinese_space_program) and [China National Space Administration](/wiki/China_National_Space_Administration)

Launch of [Shenzhou 13](/wiki/Shenzhou_13) by a [Long March 2F](/wiki/Long_March_2F) rocket. China is one of the only three countries with independent [human spaceflight](/wiki/Human_spaceflight) capability.

The Chinese space program started in 1958 with some technology transfers from the Soviet Union. However, it did not launch the nation's first satellite until 1970 with the [Dong Fang Hong I](/wiki/Dong_Fang_Hong_I), which made China the fifth country to do so independently.

In 2003, China became the third country in the world to independently send humans into space with [Yang Liwei](/wiki/Yang_Liwei)'s spaceflight aboard [Shenzhou 5](/wiki/Shenzhou_5). As of 2023, [eighteen Chinese nationals](/wiki/List_of_Chinese_astronauts) have journeyed into space, including two women. In 2011, China launched its first space station testbed, [Tiangong-1](/wiki/Tiangong-1). In 2013, a Chinese robotic rover *[Yutu](/wiki/Yutu_(rover))* successfully touched down on the lunar surface as part of the [Chang'e 3](/wiki/Chang%27e_3) mission. In 2019, China became the first country to land a probe—[Chang'e 4](/wiki/Chang%27e_4)—on the [far side of the Moon](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon). In 2020, [Chang'e 5](/wiki/Chang%27e_5) successfully returned Moon samples to the Earth, making China the third country to do so independently. In 2021, China became the third country to land a spacecraft on Mars and the second one to deploy a [rover (*Zhurong*)](/wiki/Zhurong_(rover)) on Mars. China completed its own modular [space station](/wiki/Space_station), the [Tiangong](/wiki/Tiangong_space_station), in [low Earth orbit](/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit) on 3 November 2022. On 29 November 2022, China performed its first in-orbit crew handover aboard the *Tiangong*.

In May 2023, China announced a plan to [land humans on the Moon](/wiki/Moon_landing) by 2030. To that end, China has been developing a lunar-capable super-heavy launcher, the [Long March 10](/wiki/Long_March_10), a new [crewed spacecraft](/wiki/Next-generation_crewed_spacecraft), and a [crewed lunar lander](/wiki/Chinese_crewed_lunar_lander). China sent [Chang'e 6](/wiki/Chang%27e_6) on 3 May 2024, which conducted the first lunar sample return from [Apollo Basin](/wiki/Apollo_(crater)) on the [far side of the Moon](/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon). This is China's second lunar sample return mission, the first was achieved by [Chang'e 5](/wiki/Chang%27e_5) from the lunar near side 4 years ago. It also carried a Chinese rover called *Jinchan* to conduct [infrared spectroscopy](/wiki/Absorption_spectroscopy) of lunar surface and imaged Chang'e 6 lander on lunar surface.

## Infrastructure

After a decades-long infrastructural boom, China has produced numerous world-leading infrastructural projects: it has the [largest high-speed rail network](/wiki/List_of_high-speed_railway_lines_in_China), the [most supertall skyscrapers](/wiki/List_of_supertall_skyscrapers), the largest power plant (the [Three Gorges Dam](/wiki/Three_Gorges_Dam)), the most extensive [ultra-high-voltage transmission network](/wiki/Ultra-high-voltage_electricity_transmission_in_China) and innovation infrastructure, and [a global satellite navigation system](/wiki/BeiDou) with the largest number of satellites.

### Telecommunications

Main article: [Telecommunications in China](/wiki/Telecommunications_in_China)

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/2020.4_5G_signal_to_cover_summit_of_Mt._Qomolangma.webm/250px--2020.4_5G_signal_to_cover_summit_of_Mt._Qomolangma.webm.jpg)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/8/8a/2020.4_5G_signal_to_cover_summit_of_Mt._Qomolangma.webm/2020.4_5G_signal_to_cover_summit_of_Mt._Qomolangma.webm.480p.vp9.webm)

[China Mobile](/wiki/China_Mobile) built a 5G station to cover summit of [Mount Everest](/wiki/Mount_Everest) in 2020

China is the largest telecom market in the world and currently has the [largest number of active cellphones of any country](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_mobile_numbers_in_use), with over 1.7 billion subscribers, as of February 2023. It has the largest number of [internet](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_Internet_users) and [broadband users](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_by_number_of_broadband_Internet_subscriptions), with over 1.125 billion Internet users as of December 2025—equivalent to around 80.1% of its population. By 2018, China had more than 1 billion 4G users, accounting for 40% of world's total. As of December 2023, China had over 810 million [5G](/wiki/5G) users and 3.38 million base stations installed. [China Mobile](/wiki/China_Mobile), [China Unicom](/wiki/China_Unicom) and [China Telecom](/wiki/China_Telecom), are the three large providers of mobile and internet in China. Combined, the three operators had over 3.4 million 4G base-stations in China. China has developed its own [satellite navigation](/wiki/Satellite_navigation) system, dubbed [BeiDou](/wiki/BeiDou), which began offering commercial navigation services across Asia in 2012 as well as global services by the end of 2018. BeiDou followed [GPS](/wiki/Global_Positioning_System) and [GLONASS](/wiki/GLONASS) as the third completed global navigation satellite.

### Transport

Main article: [Transport in China](/wiki/Transport_in_China)

[Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge](/wiki/Hong_Kong%E2%80%93Zhuhai%E2%80%93Macau_Bridge), completed 2018. At 55 kilometres, it is the world's longest sea crossing.

Since the late 1990s, China's national road network has been significantly expanded through the creation of a network of [national highways](/wiki/National_highways_of_China) and [expressways](/wiki/Expressways_of_China). In 2022, China's highways had reached a total length of 177,000 km (110,000 mi), making it the [longest highway system](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_road_network_size) in the world. In urban areas, bicycles remain a common mode of transport, despite the increasing prevalence of automobiles – as of 2023, there are approximately 200 million bicycles in China.

A [Fuxing](/wiki/Fuxing_(train)) high-speed train running near the [Beijing CBD](/wiki/Beijing_central_business_district)

[China's railways](/wiki/Rail_transport_in_China), which are operated by the state-owned [China Railway](/wiki/China_Railway), are among [the busiest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_rail_usage) in the world. As of 2025, the country had 165,000 km (102,526 mi) of railways, the [second-longest network](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_rail_transport_network_size) in the world. The railways strain to meet enormous demand particularly during the [Chinese New Year](/wiki/Chinese_New_Year) holiday, when the world's largest annual human migration called [Chunyun](/wiki/Chunyun) takes place. China's [high-speed rail (HSR) system](/wiki/High-speed_rail_in_China) started construction in the early 2000s. By the end of 2025, high speed rail in China had reached 50,400 kilometers (31,317 miles) of dedicated lines alone, making it the [longest HSR network](/wiki/List_of_high-speed_railway_lines) in the world. Services on the [Beijing–Shanghai](/wiki/Beijing%E2%80%93Shanghai_high-speed_railway), [Beijing–Tianjin](/wiki/Beijing%E2%80%93Tianjin_intercity_railway), and [Chengdu–Chongqing](/wiki/Chengdu%E2%80%93Chongqing_intercity_railway) lines reach up to 350 km/h (217 mph), making them the fastest conventional high speed railway services in the world. With an annual ridership of over 3.3 billion passengers in 2024, it is the world's busiest. The network includes the [Beijing–Guangzhou high-speed railway](/wiki/Beijing%E2%80%93Guangzhou_high-speed_railway), the single longest HSR line in the world, and the [Beijing–Shanghai high-speed railway](/wiki/Beijing%E2%80%93Shanghai_high-speed_railway), which has [three of longest railroad bridges in the world](/wiki/List_of_longest_bridges). The [Shanghai maglev train](/wiki/Shanghai_maglev_train), which reaches 431 km/h (268 mph), is the fastest commercial train service in the world. Since 2000, the growth of rapid transit systems in Chinese cities has accelerated. As of December 2023, 55 Chinese cities have [urban mass transit systems](/wiki/Urban_rail_transit_in_China) in operation. As of 2020, China boasts the five longest [metro systems](/wiki/List_of_metro_systems) in the world with the networks in [Shanghai](/wiki/Shanghai_Metro), [Beijing](/wiki/Beijing_Subway), [Guangzhou](/wiki/Guangzhou_Metro), [Chengdu](/wiki/Chengdu_Metro) and [Shenzhen](/wiki/Shenzhen_Metro) being the largest.

[Air China](/wiki/Air_China), the [flag carrier](/wiki/Flag_carrier) of People's Republic of China

The [civil aviation industry in China](/wiki/Civil_aviation_in_China) is mostly state-dominated, with the Chinese government retaining a majority stake in the majority of Chinese airlines. The top three airlines in China are [Air China](/wiki/Air_China), [China Southern Airlines](/wiki/China_Southern_Airlines), and [China Eastern Airlines](/wiki/China_Eastern_Airlines), which collectively made up 71% of the market in 2018, are all state-owned. Air travel has expanded rapidly in the last decades, with the number of passengers increasing from 16.6 million in 1990 to 551.2 million in 2017. China had [approximately 259 airports in 2024](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_China). China has [over 2,000 river and seaports](/wiki/List_of_ports_in_China), about 130 of which are open to foreign shipping. Of the [fifty busiest container ports](/wiki/List_of_busiest_container_ports), 18 are located in China, of which the busiest is the [Port of Shanghai](/wiki/Port_of_Shanghai), also the busiest port in the world. The country's inland waterways are the world's [sixth-longest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_waterways_length), and total 27,700 km (17,212 mi).

### Water supply and sanitation

Main article: [Water supply and sanitation in China](/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_China)

Water supply and sanitation infrastructure in China is facing challenges such as rapid urbanization, as well as [water scarcity, contamination, and pollution](/wiki/Water_resources_of_China). According to the [Joint Monitoring Program for Water Supply and Sanitation](/wiki/Joint_Monitoring_Programme_for_Water_Supply_and_Sanitation), 93% of rural households had access to [basic sanitation](/wiki/Basic_sanitation) in 2022 (up from 77% in 2015). The ongoing [South–North Water Transfer Project](/wiki/South%E2%80%93North_Water_Transfer_Project) intends to abate water shortage in the north.

## Demographics

Main article: [Demographics of China](/wiki/Demographics_of_China)

Map of China's population density across third-level administrative divisions. Based on the results of the 2020 census.

The [2020 Chinese census](/wiki/2020_Chinese_census) recorded the population as approximately 1,411,778,724. About 17.95% were 14 years old or younger, 63.35% were between 15 and 59 years old, and 18.7% were over 60 years old. Between 2010 and 2020, the average population growth rate was 0.53%. Since 2022, deaths have outpaced births in the country. In 2023, the [total fertility rate](/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) was reported to be 1.09, ranking [among the lowest in the world](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_total_fertility_rate). In 2023, [National Bureau of Statistics](/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Statistics_of_China) estimated that the population fell 850,000 from 2021 to 2022, the first decline since 1961. In 2025, China's population officially stood at 1.404 billion with births at 7.92 million, the lowest recorded birthrate since at least 1949.

### Population policies

Main article: [Family planning policies of China](/wiki/Family_planning_policies_of_China)

Given concerns about population growth, China implemented a two-child limit during the 1970s, and, in 1979, began to advocate for an even stricter limit of one child per family. Beginning in the mid-1980s, however, given the unpopularity of the strict limits, China began to allow some major exemptions, particularly in rural areas, resulting in what was actually a "1.5"-child policy from the mid-1980s to 2015; ethnic minorities were also exempt from one-child limits. The next major loosening of the policy was enacted in December 2013, allowing families to have two children if one parent is an only child. In 2016, the [one-child policy](/wiki/One-child_policy) was replaced in favor of a [two-child policy](/wiki/Two-child_policy#China). A [three-child policy](/wiki/Three-child_policy) was announced in May 2021, due to [population aging](/wiki/Aging_of_China), and in July 2021, all family size limits as well as penalties for exceeding them were removed.

According to one group of scholars, one-child limits had little effect on population growth or total population size. However, these scholars have been challenged. The policy, along with traditional preference for boys, may have contributed to an imbalance in the [sex ratio](/wiki/Human_sex_ratio) at birth. The 2020 census found that males accounted for 51.2% of the total population. However, China's sex ratio is more balanced than it was in 1953, when males accounted for 51.8% of the population. China maintains a restrictive [immigration policy](/wiki/Immigration_to_China), with permanent residence granted to only around 12,000 foreigners as of 2023.

### Urbanization

Main article: [Urbanization in China](/wiki/Urbanization_in_China)

See also: [List of cities in China](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_China), [List of cities in China by population](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_China_by_population), and [Megalopolises in China](/wiki/Megalopolises_in_China)

Map of the ten [largest cities](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_China_by_population) in China (2010)

China [has urbanized](/wiki/Urbanization_in_China) significantly in recent decades. The percent of the country's population living in urban areas increased from 20% in 1980 to over 68% in 2025, according to Chinese government figures. According to the United Nations, the country's urbanization rate is 83.7 percent, having peaked in absolute numbers in 2021. China has over 160 cities with a population of over one million, including the 18 [megacities](/wiki/Megacity) as of 2024 (cities with a population of over 10 million) of [Chongqing](/wiki/Chongqing), [Shanghai](/wiki/Shanghai), [Beijing](/wiki/Beijing), [Chengdu](/wiki/Chengdu), [Guangzhou](/wiki/Guangzhou), [Shenzhen](/wiki/Shenzhen), [Tianjin](/wiki/Tianjin), [Xi'an](/wiki/Xi%27an), [Suzhou](/wiki/Suzhou), [Zhengzhou](/wiki/Zhengzhou), [Wuhan](/wiki/Wuhan), [Hangzhou](/wiki/Hangzhou), [Linyi](/wiki/Linyi), [Shijiazhuang](/wiki/Shijiazhuang), [Dongguan](/wiki/Dongguan), [Qingdao](/wiki/Qingdao), [Changsha](/wiki/Changsha) and [Hefei](/wiki/Hefei). The total permanent population of Chongqing, Shanghai, Beijing and Chengdu is above 20 million. Shanghai is China's [most populous urban area](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_China_by_population) while Chongqing is its [largest city proper](/wiki/List_of_largest_cities), the only city in China with a permanent population of over 30 million. ### Ethnic groups

Main articles: [List of ethnic groups in China](/wiki/List_of_ethnic_groups_in_China), [Ethnic minorities in China](/wiki/Ethnic_minorities_in_China), and [Ethnic groups in Chinese history](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Chinese_history)

Ethnolinguistic map of China in 1967

China legally recognizes 56 distinct ethnic groups, who comprise the *[Zhonghua minzu](/wiki/Zhonghua_minzu)*. The largest of these nationalities are the [Han Chinese](/wiki/Han_Chinese), who constitute more than 91% of the total population. The Han Chinese – the world's largest single ethnic group – outnumber other ethnic groups in every place excluding [Tibet](/wiki/Tibet_Autonomous_Region), [Xinjiang](/wiki/Xinjiang), [Linxia](/wiki/Linxia_City), and [autonomous prefectures](/wiki/Autonomous_prefecture) like [Xishuangbanna](/wiki/Xishuangbanna_Dai_Autonomous_Prefecture). Ethnic minorities account for less than 10% of the population of China, according to the 2020 census. Compared with the 2010 population census, the Han population increased by 60,378,693 persons, or 4.93%, while the population of the 55 national minorities combined increased by 11,675,179 persons, or 10.26%. The 2020 census recorded a total of 845,697 foreign nationals living in mainland China.

### Languages

Main articles: [Languages of China](/wiki/Languages_of_China) and [List of endangered languages in China](/wiki/List_of_endangered_languages_in_China)

Qing dynasty imperial patent in Manchu and Chinese scripts. Wellcome Collection, London.

There are as many as 284 [living languages](/wiki/Living_language) in China. The languages most commonly spoken belong to the [Sinitic branch](/wiki/Sinitic_languages) of the [Sino-Tibetan language family](/wiki/Sino-Tibetan_language_family), which contains [Mandarin](/wiki/Mandarin_Chinese), spoken by 80% of the population, and [other varieties](/wiki/Varieties_of_Chinese) of [Chinese language](/wiki/Chinese_language): [Jin](/wiki/Jin_Chinese), [Wu](/wiki/Wu_Chinese), [Min](/wiki/Min_Chinese), [Hakka](/wiki/Hakka_Chinese), [Yue](/wiki/Yue_Chinese), [Xiang](/wiki/Xiang_Chinese), [Gan](/wiki/Gan_Chinese), [Hui](/wiki/Huizhou_Chinese), [Ping](/wiki/Pinghua) and unclassified Tuhua ([Shaozhou Tuhua](/wiki/Shaozhou_Tuhua) and [Xiangnan Tuhua](/wiki/Xiangnan_Tuhua)). Languages of the [Tibeto-Burman branch](/wiki/Tibeto-Burman_languages), including [Tibetan](/wiki/Lhasa_Tibetan), [Qiang](/wiki/Qiang_language), [Naxi](/wiki/Naxi_language) and [Yi](/wiki/Nuosu_language), are spoken across the [Tibetan](/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau) and [Yunnan–Guizhou Plateau](/wiki/Yunnan%E2%80%93Guizhou_Plateau). Other ethnic minority languages in [southwestern China](/wiki/Southwestern_China) include [Zhuang](/wiki/Zhuang_languages), [Thai](/wiki/Thai_language), [Dong](/wiki/Kam_language) and [Sui](/wiki/Sui_language) of the [Tai-Kadai family](/wiki/Kra%E2%80%93Dai_languages), [Miao](/wiki/Hmongic_languages) and [Yao](/wiki/Mienic_languages) of the [Hmong–Mien family](/wiki/Hmong%E2%80%93Mien_languages), and [Wa](/wiki/Wa_language) of the [Austroasiatic family](/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages). Across [northeastern](/wiki/Northeast_China) and [northwestern China](/wiki/Northwestern_China), local ethnic groups speak [Altaic languages](/wiki/Altaic_languages) including [Manchu](/wiki/Manchu_language), [Mongolian](/wiki/Mongolian_language) and several [Turkic languages](/wiki/Turkic_languages): [Uyghur](/wiki/Uyghur_language), [Kazakh](/wiki/Kazakh_language), [Kyrgyz](/wiki/Kyrgyz_language), [Salar](/wiki/Salar_language) and [Western Yugur](/wiki/Western_Yugur_language). [Korean](/wiki/Korean_language) is spoken natively along the border with [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea). [Sarikoli](/wiki/Sarikoli_language), the language of [Tajiks in China](/wiki/Tajiks_in_China), is an [Indo-European language](/wiki/Indo-European_language). [Taiwanese indigenous peoples](/wiki/Taiwanese_indigenous_peoples), including a small population on the mainland, speak [Austronesian languages](/wiki/Austronesian_languages).

[Standard Chinese](/wiki/Standard_Chinese), a variety based on the [Beijing dialect](/wiki/Beijing_dialect) of Mandarin, is the national language of China, having de facto official status. It is used as a [lingua franca](/wiki/Lingua_franca) between people of different linguistic backgrounds. In the [autonomous regions of China](/wiki/Autonomous_regions_of_China), other languages may also serve as a lingua franca, such as Uyghur in Xinjiang, where governmental services in Uyghur are constitutionally guaranteed.

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in China](/wiki/Religion_in_China)

Geographic distribution of religions in China:

■ [Chinese folk religion](/wiki/Chinese_folk_religion) (including [Confucianism](/wiki/Confucianism), [Taoism](/wiki/Taoism), and groups of [Chinese Buddhism](/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism))
■ [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism) *tout court*
■ [Islam](/wiki/Islam_in_China)
■ [Ethnic minorities' indigenous religions](/wiki/Religion_in_China#Ethnic_minorities'_indigenous_religions)
■ [Mongolian folk religion](/wiki/Mongolian_folk_religion)
■ [Northeast China folk religion](/wiki/Northeast_China_folk_religion) influenced by Tungus and [Manchu shamanism](/wiki/Manchu_shamanism); widespread [Shanrendao](/wiki/Shanrendao)

[Freedom of religion](/wiki/Freedom_of_religion) is guaranteed by China's constitution, although religious organizations that lack official approval can be subject to state persecution. The government of the country is officially [atheist](/wiki/State_atheism), and the Chinese Communist Party requires its members to be atheist. Religious affairs and issues in the country are overseen by the [National Religious Affairs Administration](/wiki/National_Religious_Affairs_Administration), under the CCP's [United Front Work Department](/wiki/United_Front_Work_Department).

Zixiao Palace (紫霄宫), [Wudang Mountains](/wiki/Wudang_Mountains), Hubei. Ming dynasty.

Over the millennia, the Chinese civilization has been influenced by various religious movements. The "[three doctrines](/wiki/Three_teachings)" of [Confucianism](/wiki/Confucianism), [Taoism](/wiki/Taoism), and [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism) have historically shaped Chinese culture, enriching a [theological and spiritual framework](/wiki/Chinese_theology) of traditional religion which harks back to the early [Shang](/wiki/Shang) and [Zhou dynasty](/wiki/Zhou_dynasty). [Chinese folk religion](/wiki/Chinese_folk_religion), which is framed by the three doctrines and by other traditions, consists in allegiance to the *[shen](/wiki/Shen_(Chinese_religion))*, who can be [deities](/wiki/Deities) of the surrounding nature or [ancestral principles](/wiki/Progenitor) of human groups, concepts of civility, [culture heroes](/wiki/Culture_hero), many of whom feature in [Chinese mythology](/wiki/Chinese_mythology) and history. Amongst the most popular [cults](/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)) of folk religion are those of the [Yellow Emperor](/wiki/Yellow_Emperor), embodiment of the God of Heaven and one of the two [divine patriarchs](/wiki/Yan_Huang_Zisun) of the Chinese people, of [Mazu](/wiki/Mazu) (goddess of the seas), [Guandi](/wiki/Guandi) (god of war and business), [Caishen](/wiki/Caishen) (god of prosperity and richness), [Pangu](/wiki/Pangu) and many others. In the early decades of the 21st century, the Chinese government has been engaged in a rehabilitation of folk cults—formally recognizing them as "folk beliefs" as distinguished from doctrinal religions, and often reconstructing them into forms of "highly curated" [civil religion](/wiki/Civil_religion)—as well as in a national and international promotion of Buddhism. China is home to many of the [world's tallest religious statues](/wiki/List_of_statues_by_height), representing either deities of Chinese folk religion or enlightened beings of Buddhism; the tallest of all is the [Spring Temple Buddha](/wiki/Spring_Temple_Buddha) in [Henan](/wiki/Henan).

[Taoism](/wiki/Taoism) has served as a state religion several times throughout Chinese history.

Statistics on religious affiliation in China are difficult to gather due to complex and varying definitions of religion and the diffusive nature of Chinese religious traditions. Scholars note that in China there is no clear boundary between the three doctrines and local folk religious practices. Chinese religions or some of their currents are also definable as [non-theistic](/wiki/Non-theistic) and [humanistic](/wiki/Humanistic), since they do not hold that divine creativity is completely transcendent, but that it is inherent in the world and in particular in the human being. In 2023, according to surveys done by Pew Research, 93% of respondents were formally unaffiliated with any religion. However, in terms of practices, 75% visit family graveyards each year, 47% believe in feng shui, 33% believe in buddha, 26% burn incense to deities each year and 18% believe in taoist deities. These are not exclusive beliefs and often these will overlap as the respondents will have multiple beliefs at the same time. For example, of those 33% who believe in buddha, a significant portion also believe in figures such as Taoist immortals, Jesus Christ, Catholic God and Allah. Chinese folk religion also comprises a variety of [salvationist doctrinal organized movements](/wiki/Chinese_salvationist_religions) which emerged since the [Song dynasty](/wiki/Song_dynasty). There are also ethnic minorities in China who maintain their own [indigenous religions](/wiki/Religion_in_China#Ethnic_minorities'_indigenous_religions), while major religions characteristic of specific ethnic groups include [Tibetan Buddhism](/wiki/Tibetan_Buddhism) among [Tibetans](/wiki/Tibetans), [Mongols](/wiki/Mongols_in_China) and [Yugurs](/wiki/Yugurs), and Islam among the [Hui](/wiki/Hui), [Uyghur](/wiki/Uyghur_people), [Kazakh](/wiki/Kazakhs_in_China), and [Kyrgyz](/wiki/Kyrgyz_in_China) peoples, and other ethnicities in the northern and northwestern regions of the country.

### Education

Main articles: [Education in China](/wiki/Education_in_China) and [Higher education in China](/wiki/Higher_education_in_China)

Beijing's [Peking University](/wiki/Peking_University), one of the [top-ranked universities in China](/wiki/Rankings_of_universities_in_China)

Compulsory education in China comprises [primary](/wiki/Primary_school) and [junior secondary school](/wiki/Middle_school), which together last for nine years from the age of 6 and 15. The [Gaokao](/wiki/Gaokao), China's national university entrance exam, is a prerequisite for entrance into most higher education institutions. Vocational education is available to students at the secondary and [tertiary](/wiki/Tertiary_education) level. More than 10 million Chinese students graduated from vocational colleges every year. In 2024, about 92.0 percent of students continued their education at a three-year senior secondary school, while 60.8 percent of secondary school graduates were enrolled in higher education.

China has the largest education system in the world, with about 287 million students and 18.85 million full-time teachers in over 470,300 schools in 2024. Annual education investment went from less than US$50 billion in 2003 to more than US$817 billion in 2020. However, there remains an inequality in education spending. In 2010, the annual education expenditure per secondary school student in Beijing totalled ¥20,023, while in [Guizhou](/wiki/Guizhou), one of the [poorest provinces](/wiki/List_of_Chinese_provincial-level_divisions_by_GDP_per_capita), it only totalled ¥3,204. China's literacy rate has grown dramatically, from only 20% in 1949 and 65.5% in 1979, to 97% of the population over age 15 in 2020.

As of 2024, China has over 3,167 universities, with over 47.6 million students enrolled in mainland China, giving China the largest higher education system in the world. As of 2025, China had the world's highest [number of top universities](/wiki/Rankings_of_universities_in_China). As of 2025, there were 2 Chinese universities in the global top 20, 5 in the top 50, and 19 in the top 200, behind only the United States and the United Kingdom in terms of the overall representation in the *Aggregate Ranking of Top Universities*, a composite ranking system combining three of the world's most influential university rankings ([ARWU](/wiki/Academic_Ranking_of_World_Universities)+[QS](/wiki/QS_World_University_Rankings)+[THE](/wiki/Times_Higher_Education_World_University_Rankings)). China had five universities listed among the world's top 50, placing it third after the United States and the United Kingdom based on aggregate performance from four widely observed university rankings (THE+ARWU+QS+[US News](/wiki/U.S._News_%26_World_Report_Best_Global_Universities_Ranking)). China is home to two of the highest-ranking universities ([Tsinghua University](/wiki/Tsinghua_University) and [Peking University](/wiki/Peking_University)) in [Asia](/wiki/Asia) and [emerging economies](/wiki/Emerging_market), according to the [Times Higher Education World University Rankings](/wiki/Times_Higher_Education_World_University_Rankings) and the [Academic Ranking of World Universities](/wiki/Academic_Ranking_of_World_Universities). These universities are members of the [C9 League](/wiki/C9_League), an alliance of elite [Chinese universities](/wiki/List_of_universities_in_China) offering comprehensive and leading education.

### Health

Main article: [Health in China](/wiki/Health_in_China)

See also: [Medicine in China](/wiki/Medicine_in_China) and [Pharmaceutical industry in China](/wiki/Pharmaceutical_industry_in_China)

Chart showing the rise of China's [Human Development Index](/wiki/Human_Development_Index) from 1970 to 2010

The [National Health Commission](/wiki/National_Health_Commission), together with its counterparts in the local commissions, oversees the health needs of the population. An emphasis on public health and preventive medicine has characterized Chinese health policy since the early 1950s. The Communist Party started the [Patriotic Health Campaign](/wiki/Patriotic_Health_Campaign), which was aimed at improving sanitation and hygiene, as well as treating and preventing several diseases. Diseases such as [cholera](/wiki/Cholera), [typhoid](/wiki/Typhoid) and [scarlet fever](/wiki/Scarlet_fever), which were previously rife in China, were nearly eradicated by the campaign.

After [Deng Xiaoping](/wiki/Deng_Xiaoping) began instituting economic reforms in 1978, the health of the Chinese public improved rapidly because of better nutrition, although many of the free public health services provided in the countryside disappeared. [Healthcare in China](/wiki/Healthcare_in_China) became mostly privatized, and experienced a significant rise in quality. In 2009, the government began a three-year large-scale healthcare provision initiative worth US$124 billion. By 2011, the campaign resulted in 95% of China's population having basic health insurance coverage. By 2022, China had established itself as a key producer and exporter of [pharmaceuticals](/wiki/Medication), producing around 40 percent of [active pharmaceutical ingredients](/wiki/Active_ingredient) in 2017.

As of 2025, the life expectancy at birth exceeds 79 years. As of 2024, the [infant mortality](/wiki/Infant_mortality) rate is 4 per thousand. Both have improved significantly since the 1950s. Rates of [stunting](/wiki/Stunted_growth), a condition caused by [malnutrition](/wiki/Malnutrition), have declined from 33.1% in 1990 to 4.5% in 2024. Despite significant improvements in health and the construction of advanced medical facilities, China has several emerging public health problems, such as respiratory illnesses caused by [widespread air pollution](/wiki/Air_pollution_in_China), [hundreds of millions of cigarette smokers](/wiki/Smoking_in_China), and an increase in [obesity](/wiki/Obesity) among urban youths. [Chinese mental health](/wiki/Mental_health_in_China) services are inadequate. China's large population and densely populated cities have led to serious disease outbreaks, such as [SARS](/wiki/Severe_acute_respiratory_syndrome) in 2003, although this has since been largely contained. The [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) was first identified in [Wuhan](/wiki/Wuhan) in December 2019; China responded to the pandemic with a [zero-COVID](/wiki/Zero-COVID) approach until 2022 following [protests](/wiki/2022_COVID-19_protests_in_China).

## Culture

Main articles: [Chinese culture](/wiki/Chinese_culture) and [Culture of the People's Republic of China](/wiki/Culture_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China)

[Moon gate](/wiki/Moon_gate) at Xiaojinshan, [Slender West Lake](/wiki/Slender_West_Lake), [Yangzhou](/wiki/Yangzhou), [Jiangsu](/wiki/Jiangsu).

Since [ancient times](/wiki/Ancient_China), Chinese culture has been heavily influenced by Confucianism. Chinese culture, in turn, has heavily influenced [East Asia](/wiki/East_Asia) and [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia). For much of the country's dynastic era, opportunities for social advancement could be provided by high performance in the prestigious [imperial examinations](/wiki/Imperial_examination), which have their origins in the Han dynasty. The [literary emphasis](/wiki/Chinese_literature) of the exams affected the general perception of cultural refinement in China, such as the belief that [calligraphy](/wiki/Chinese_calligraphy), [poetry](/wiki/Classical_Chinese_poetry) and [painting](/wiki/Chinese_painting) were higher forms of art than dancing or drama. Chinese culture has long emphasized a sense of deep history and a largely inward-looking national perspective. Examinations and a [culture of merit](/wiki/Meritocracy) remain greatly valued in China today.

[Wang Family Compound](/wiki/Wang_Family_Compound), Lingshi, Shanxi. Ming and Qing dynasty.

Though the Mao Zedong attacked traditional Chinese culture during the [Cultural Revolution](/wiki/Cultural_Revolution), the Chinese government has accepted numerous elements of traditional Chinese culture as being integral to Chinese society. With the rise of [Chinese nationalism](/wiki/Chinese_nationalism) and the end of the Cultural Revolution, various forms of traditional Chinese art, literature, music, film, fashion and architecture have seen a revival, and folk and variety art in particular have sparked interest nationally and even worldwide.

### Architecture

Main articles: [Chinese architecture](/wiki/Chinese_architecture) and [List of World Heritage Sites in China](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_China)

The main hall of [Nanchan Temple](/wiki/Nanchan_Temple), Wutai County, Shanxi, completed in 782 CE, the oldest surviving timber-framed building in China.

[Chinese architecture](/wiki/Chinese_architecture) has developed over millennia in China and has remained a vestigial source of perennial influence on the development of East Asian architecture, including in [Japan](/wiki/Japanese_architecture), [Korea](/wiki/Korean_architecture), and [Mongolia](/wiki/Architecture_of_Mongolia). and minor influences on the architecture of Southeast and South Asia including the countries of [Malaysia](/wiki/Malaysia), [Singapore](/wiki/Singapore), [Indonesia](/wiki/Indonesia), [Sri Lanka](/wiki/Sri_Lanka), Thailand, Laos, [Cambodia](/wiki/Cambodia), [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnamese_architecture) and the Philippines.

Chinese architecture is characterized by bilateral symmetry, use of enclosed open spaces, [feng shui](/wiki/Feng_shui) (e.g. directional [hierarchies](/wiki/Hierarchy)), a horizontal emphasis, and an allusion to various cosmological, [mythological](/wiki/Myth) or in general symbolic elements. Chinese architecture traditionally classifies structures according to type, ranging from *[pagodas](/wiki/Pagodas)* to [palaces](/wiki/Chinese_palace).

The Sakyamuni [Pagoda of Fogong Temple](/wiki/Pagoda_of_Fogong_Temple) in Ying County, Shanxi, built in 1056 during the Liao dynasty, it is the world's tallest surviving timber-framed structure.

Chinese architecture varies widely based on status or affiliation, such as whether the structures were constructed for emperors, commoners, or for religious purposes. Other variations in Chinese architecture are shown in vernacular styles associated with different [geographic](/wiki/Geography) regions and different ethnic heritages, such as the [stilt houses](/wiki/Stilt_house) in the south, the [Yaodong buildings](/wiki/Yaodong) in the northwest, the [yurt buildings](/wiki/Yurt) of nomadic people, and the [Siheyuan buildings](/wiki/Siheyuan) in the north.

### Literature

Main article: [Chinese literature](/wiki/Chinese_literature)

Chinese literature has its roots in the Zhou dynasty's literary tradition. The [classical texts of China](/wiki/Chinese_classics) encompass a wide range of thoughts and subjects, such as the [calendar](/wiki/Chinese_calendar), [military](/wiki/List_of_Chinese_military_texts), [astrology](/wiki/Chinese_astrology), [herbology](/wiki/Chinese_herbology), and [geography](/wiki/Chinese_geography), as well as many others. Among the most significant early works are the *[I Ching](/wiki/I_Ching)* and the *[Shujing](/wiki/Classic_of_History)*, which are part of the [Four Books and Five Classics](/wiki/Four_Books_and_Five_Classics). These texts were the cornerstone of the Confucian curriculum sponsored by the state throughout the dynastic periods. Inherited from the *[Classic of Poetry](/wiki/Classic_of_Poetry)*, [classical Chinese poetry](/wiki/Classical_Chinese_poetry) developed to its [floruit](/wiki/Floruit) during the Tang dynasty. [Li Bai](/wiki/Li_Bai) and [Du Fu](/wiki/Du_Fu) opened the forking ways for the poetic circles through romanticism and realism respectively. [Chinese historiography](/wiki/Chinese_historiography) began with the *[Shiji](/wiki/Shiji)*, the overall scope of the historiographical tradition in China is termed the [Twenty-Four Histories](/wiki/Twenty-Four_Histories), which set a vast stage for Chinese fictions along with [Chinese mythology](/wiki/Chinese_mythology) and [folklore](/wiki/Chinese_folklore). Pushed by a burgeoning citizen class in the Ming dynasty, Chinese classical fiction rose to a boom of the historical, town and [gods and demons fictions](/wiki/Gods_and_demons_fiction) as represented by the [Four Great Classical Novels](/wiki/Four_Great_Classical_Novels) which include *[Water Margin](/wiki/Water_Margin)*, *[Romance of the Three Kingdoms](/wiki/Romance_of_the_Three_Kingdoms)*, *[Journey to the West](/wiki/Journey_to_the_West)* and *[Dream of the Red Chamber](/wiki/Dream_of_the_Red_Chamber)*. Along with the [wuxia](/wiki/Wuxia) fictions of [Jin Yong](/wiki/Jin_Yong) and [Liang Yusheng](/wiki/Liang_Yusheng), it remains an enduring source of popular culture in the [Chinese sphere of influence](/wiki/Sinosphere).

In the wake of the [New Culture Movement](/wiki/New_Culture_Movement) after the end of the Qing dynasty, Chinese literature embarked on a new era with [written vernacular Chinese](/wiki/Written_vernacular_Chinese) for ordinary citizens. [Hu Shih](/wiki/Hu_Shih) and [Lu Xun](/wiki/Lu_Xun) were pioneers in modern literature. Various literary genres, such as [misty poetry](/wiki/Misty_poetry), [scar literature](/wiki/Scar_literature), [young adult fiction](/wiki/Young_adult_fiction) and the [xungen literature](/wiki/Xungen_movement), which is influenced by [magic realism](/wiki/Magic_realism), emerged following the Cultural Revolution. [Mo Yan](/wiki/Mo_Yan), a xungen literature author, was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2012.

### Music

Main articles: [Music of China](/wiki/Music_of_China), [C-pop](/wiki/C-pop), [Chinese opera](/wiki/Chinese_opera), [Performance art in China](/wiki/Performance_art_in_China), and [Chinese dance](/wiki/Chinese_dance)

*The Drunken Concubine* (贵妃醉酒), a classic Peking opera

Chinese music covers a highly diverse range of music from traditional music to modern music. Chinese music dates back before the pre-imperial times. [Traditional Chinese musical instruments](/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_musical_instruments) were traditionally grouped into eight categories known as *bayin* (八音). Traditional Chinese opera is a form of musical theatre in China originating thousands of years and has regional style forms such as Beijing and [Cantonese opera](/wiki/Cantonese_opera). Chinese pop (C-Pop) includes [mandopop](/wiki/Mandopop) and [cantopop](/wiki/Cantopop). [Chinese hip hop](/wiki/Chinese_hip_hop) and [Hong Kong hip hop](/wiki/Hong_Kong_hip_hop) have become popular.

### Fashion

Main articles: [Chinese clothing](/wiki/Chinese_clothing) and [Hanfu](/wiki/Hanfu)

[Hanfu](/wiki/Hanfu) is the historical clothing of the Han people in China. The [qipao](/wiki/Cheongsam) or cheongsam is a popular Chinese female dress. The [hanfu movement](/wiki/Hanfu_movement) has been popular in contemporary times and seeks to revitalize Hanfu clothing. [China Fashion Week](/wiki/China_Fashion_Week) is the country's only national-level fashion festival.

### Media

Main articles: [Mass media in China](/wiki/Mass_media_in_China) and [Cinema of China](/wiki/Cinema_of_China)

The mass media of China primarily consists of [television](/wiki/Television_in_China), [newspapers](/wiki/Newspapers_of_China), [radio](/wiki/Radio_in_China), and [magazines](/wiki/List_of_magazines_in_China). [State media](/wiki/State_media) outlets operate under the control of the CCP. The largest media organizations are the *[People's Daily](/wiki/People%27s_Daily)*, [Xinhua News Agency](/wiki/Xinhua_News_Agency), and the [China Media Group](/wiki/China_Media_Group) consisting of [China Central Television](/wiki/China_Central_Television), [China Global Television Network](/wiki/China_Global_Television_Network), [China National Radio](/wiki/China_National_Radio) and [China Radio International](/wiki/China_Radio_International). Access to foreign media remains heavily restricted.

Cinema was first introduced to China in 1896 and the first Chinese film, *[Dingjun Mountain](/wiki/Dingjun_Mountain_(film)),* was released in 1905. China has had the largest number of movie screens in the world since 2016; China became the largest cinema market in 2020, and domestic movies dominate the market. The top three [highest-grossing films in China](/wiki/List_of_highest-grossing_films_in_China) as of 2025 were *[Ne Zha 2](/wiki/Ne_Zha_2)* (2025), *[The Battle at Lake Changjin](/wiki/The_Battle_at_Lake_Changjin)* (2021), and *[Wolf Warrior 2](/wiki/Wolf_Warrior_2)* (2017). In 2025, the video game market of China was the world's [largest by revenue](/wiki/List_of_video_games_markets_by_country).

China has the most comprehensive and sophisticated Internet censorship regime in the world called the [Great Firewall](/wiki/Great_Firewall), with numerous websites being blocked. The Great Firewall has allowed China to develop its own major internet services and companies, such as [Tencent](/wiki/Tencent), [Alibaba](/wiki/Alibaba_Group), [Baidu](/wiki/Baidu), [Douyin](/wiki/TikTok), [Xiaohongshu](/wiki/Xiaohongshu), [Bilibili](/wiki/Bilibili) and [Weibo](/wiki/Weibo). The [Cyberspace Administration of China](/wiki/Cyberspace_Administration_of_China) acts as the national internet regulator and censor. China requires a [real-name system](/wiki/Internet_real-name_system_in_China) for Internet services and online platforms.

### Cuisine

Main article: [Chinese cuisine](/wiki/Chinese_cuisine)

Map showing major regional cuisines of China

Chinese cuisine is highly diverse, drawing on several millennia of culinary history and geographical variety, in which the most influential are known as the "Eight Major Cuisines", including [Sichuan](/wiki/Sichuan_cuisine), [Cantonese](/wiki/Cantonese_cuisine), [Jiangsu](/wiki/Jiangsu_cuisine), [Shandong](/wiki/Shandong_cuisine), [Fujian](/wiki/Fujian_cuisine), [Hunan](/wiki/Hunan_cuisine), [Anhui](/wiki/Anhui_cuisine), and [Zhejiang](/wiki/Zhejiang_cuisine) cuisines. Chinese cuisine is known for its breadth of [cooking methods](/wiki/Chinese_cooking_techniques) and ingredients. China's staple food is rice in the northeast and south, and wheat-based breads and noodles in the north. Bean products such as [tofu](/wiki/Tofu) and [soy milk](/wiki/Soy_milk) remain a popular source of protein. Pork is now the most popular meat in China, accounting for about 60% of the country's total meat consumption. Pork-free [Chinese Islamic cuisine](/wiki/Chinese_Islamic_cuisine) is served at halal-certified restaurants in various regions while vegetarian [Buddhist cuisine](/wiki/Buddhist_cuisine) is commonly found at restaurants near shrines and temples.

### Sports

Main articles: [Sport in China](/wiki/Sport_in_China), [China at the Olympics](/wiki/China_at_the_Olympics), and [China at the Paralympics](/wiki/China_at_the_Paralympics)

[Go](/wiki/Go_(game)) is an abstract strategy board game for two players, in which the aim is to surround more territory than the opponent, and which was invented in China more than 2,500 years ago.

China has one of the [oldest sporting cultures](/wiki/Sport_in_China). There is evidence that [archery](/wiki/Archery) (*shèjiàn*) was practiced during the [Western Zhou dynasty](/wiki/Western_Zhou_dynasty). Swordplay (*jiànshù*) and *[cuju](/wiki/Cuju)*, a sport loosely related to [association football](/wiki/Association_football) date back to China's early dynasties as well.

[Physical fitness](/wiki/Physical_fitness) is widely emphasized in Chinese culture, with morning exercises such as *[qigong](/wiki/Qigong)* and [tai chi](/wiki/Tai_chi) widely practiced, and commercial [gyms](/wiki/Gym) and private fitness clubs are gaining popularity. Basketball is the most popular spectator sport in China. The [Chinese Basketball Association](/wiki/Chinese_Basketball_Association) and the American [National Basketball Association](/wiki/National_Basketball_Association) also have a huge national following amongst the Chinese populace, with native-born and NBA-bound Chinese players and well-known national household names such as [Yao Ming](/wiki/Yao_Ming) and [Yi Jianlian](/wiki/Yi_Jianlian) being held in high esteem. China's professional football league, known as [Chinese Super League](/wiki/Chinese_Super_League), is the largest football market in East Asia. Other popular sports include [martial arts](/wiki/Chinese_martial_arts), [table tennis](/wiki/Table_tennis), [badminton](/wiki/Badminton), [swimming](/wiki/Swimming_(sport)) and [snooker](/wiki/Snooker). China is home to a huge number of [cyclists](/wiki/Cycling), with an estimated 470 million bicycles as of 2012. China has the world's largest [esports](/wiki/Esports) market. Many more [traditional sports](/wiki/Traditional_games_of_China), such as [dragon boat](/wiki/Dragon_boat) racing, [Mongolian-style wrestling](/wiki/Mongolian_wrestling) and [horse racing](/wiki/Horse_racing) are also popular.

China has [participated in the Olympic Games](/wiki/China_at_the_Olympics) since 1932, although it has only participated as the PRC [since 1952](/wiki/China_at_the_1952_Summer_Olympics). China hosted the [2008 Summer Olympics](/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics) in Beijing, where its athletes received 48 gold medals – the [highest number of any participating nation that year](/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics_medal_table). China also won the most medals at the [2012 Summer Paralympics](/wiki/2012_Summer_Paralympics), with 231 overall, including 95 gold. In 2011, Shenzhen hosted the [2011 Summer Universiade](/wiki/2011_Summer_Universiade). China hosted the [2013 East Asian Games](/wiki/2013_East_Asian_Games) in Tianjin and the [2014 Summer Youth Olympics](/wiki/2014_Summer_Youth_Olympics) in Nanjing, the first country to host both regular and Youth Olympics. Beijing and its nearby city [Zhangjiakou](/wiki/Zhangjiakou) collaboratively hosted the [2022 Winter Olympics](/wiki/2022_Winter_Olympics), making Beijing the first dual Olympic city by holding both the Summer Olympics and the Winter Olympics. China hosted the [Asian Games](/wiki/Asian_Games) in [1990](/wiki/1990_Asian_Games) (Beijing), [2010](/wiki/2010_Asian_Games) (Guangzhou), and [2023](/wiki/2022_Asian_Games) (Hangzhou).
