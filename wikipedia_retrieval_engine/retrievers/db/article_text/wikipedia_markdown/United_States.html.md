The **United States of America** (**USA**), also known as the **United States** (**U.S.**) or **America**, is a country primarily located in [North America](/wiki/North_America). It is a [federal republic](/wiki/Federal_republic) consisting of 50 [states](/wiki/U.S._state) and a [federal capital district](/wiki/Capital_districts_and_territories), [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.) The 48 [contiguous states](/wiki/Contiguous_states) border [Canada](/wiki/Canada) to the north and [Mexico](/wiki/Mexico) to the south, with the [semi-exclave](/wiki/Semi-exclave) of [Alaska](/wiki/Alaska) in the northwest and the [archipelago](/wiki/Archipelago) of [Hawaii](/wiki/Hawaii) in the [Pacific Ocean](/wiki/Pacific_Ocean). The United States also asserts sovereignty over five [major island territories](/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States) and [various uninhabited islands](/wiki/United_States_Minor_Outlying_Islands) in [Oceania](/wiki/Oceania) and the [Caribbean](/wiki/Caribbean). It is a [megadiverse country](/wiki/Megadiverse_country), with the world's [third-largest land area](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area) and [third-largest population](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population), exceeding 341 million.

[Paleo-Indians](/wiki/Paleo-Indians) first migrated from North Asia to North America at least 15,000 years ago, and formed various civilizations. [European discovery of the Americas](/wiki/European_colonization_of_the_Americas) began in 1492, and [British colonization](/wiki/British_colonization_of_the_Americas) followed with the 1607 settlement of [Virginia](/wiki/Colony_of_Virginia), the first of the [Thirteen Colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies). The [American Enlightenment](/wiki/American_Enlightenment) that spread throughout the colonies in the 18th century valued [republicanism](/wiki/Republicanism) and [liberalism](/wiki/Liberalism_in_the_United_States). Clashes with the [British Crown](/wiki/The_Crown) over [taxation without parliamentary representation](/wiki/No_taxation_without_representation) and the denial of other [English rights](/wiki/Rights_of_Englishmen) evolved into the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution), which led to the [Declaration of Independence](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence) on July 4, 1776. Victory in the 1775–1783 [Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) brought [international recognition of the country's sovereignty](/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1783)). Rapid [westward territorial expansion](/wiki/Territorial_evolution_of_the_United_States) followed the purchase, settlement, and conquest of European-held or Indigenous-controlled territory. As more states were [admitted into the Union](/wiki/Admission_to_the_Union), a North–South [division over slavery](/wiki/Slave_states_and_free_states) led 11 Southern states to [declare secession](/wiki/Ordinance_of_Secession) and join as the [Confederate States of America](/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America) in order to [preserve slavery there](/wiki/Origins_of_the_American_Civil_War). These states fought against the [Union](/wiki/Union_(American_Civil_War)) in the [American Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War) of 1861–1865 but were defeated. With the United States' victory and reunification, slavery was [abolished nationally](/wiki/Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution). By 1900, the country had established itself as a [great power](/wiki/Great_power), a status solidified after [its involvement](/wiki/United_States_in_World_War_I) during [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I). Following [Japan](/wiki/Empire_of_Japan)'s [attack on Pearl Harbor](/wiki/Attack_on_Pearl_Harbor) in 1941, the U.S. [entered World War II](/wiki/American_entry_into_World_War_II) on the side of [the Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II). [Its aftermath](/wiki/Aftermath_of_World_War_II) left the U.S. and the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) as rival [superpowers](/wiki/Superpower), competing for [ideological dominance](/wiki/Ideological_dominance) and [international influence](/wiki/International_influence) during the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War). The [Soviet Union's collapse](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) in 1991 left the U.S. the world's sole superpower.

The [U.S. federal government](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States) is a [representative democracy](/wiki/Representative_democracy) with [a president](/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) and a constitution that creates [a separation of powers](/wiki/Separation_of_powers_under_the_United_States_Constitution) among three branches: [legislative](/wiki/Legislative), [executive](/wiki/United_States_federal_executive_departments), and [judicial](/wiki/Federal_judiciary_of_the_United_States). The [United States Congress](/wiki/United_States_Congress) is a [bicameral](/wiki/Bicameralism) national legislature composed of the [House of Representatives](/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) (a [lower house](/wiki/Lower_house) based on population) and the [Senate](/wiki/United_States_Senate) (an [upper house](/wiki/Upper_house) based on equal representation for each state). [Federalism grants substantial autonomy](/wiki/Federalism_in_the_United_States) to the 50 states. In addition, 574 Native American tribes have [sovereignty rights](/wiki/Tribal_sovereignty_in_the_United_States), and there are 326 [Native American reservations](/wiki/Native_American_reservations).

A [developed country](/wiki/Developed_country), the [U.S. ranks](/wiki/International_rankings_of_the_United_States) high in economic competitiveness, innovation, and [higher education](/wiki/Higher_education_in_the_United_States). Accounting for [over a quarter](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)) of nominal global GDP, the [U.S. economy](/wiki/Economy_of_the_United_States) has been the world's largest [since about 1890](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_largest_historical_GDP). It is the [wealthiest country](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_total_wealth), with the [highest disposable household income per capita](/wiki/Disposable_household_and_per_capita_income#Household_disposable_income_per_capita_(OECD)) among [OECD](/wiki/OECD) members, though [its wealth inequality](/wiki/Wealth_inequality_in_the_United_States) is highly pronounced. Shaped by [centuries of immigration](/wiki/History_of_immigration_to_the_United_States), the [culture of the U.S.](/wiki/Culture_of_the_United_States) is diverse and [globally influential](/wiki/Americanization). Making up [a third](/wiki/List_of_countries_with_highest_military_expenditures) of global military spending, the country is widely considered to have [the most powerful armed forces in the world](/wiki/United_States_Armed_Forces) and was the first to develop and employ [nuclear weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons). A member of [numerous international organizations](/wiki/International_organization_membership_of_the_United_States) including the [United Nations Security Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council), it plays a major role in global political, cultural, economic, and military affairs.

## Etymology

Further information: [Names of the United States](/wiki/Names_of_the_United_States), [Demonyms for the United States](/wiki/Demonyms_for_the_United_States), [United Colonies](/wiki/United_Colonies), and [Naming of the Americas](/wiki/Naming_of_the_Americas)

Documented use of the phrase "United States of America" dates back to January 2, 1776. On that day, [Stephen Moylan](/wiki/Stephen_Moylan), a [Continental Army](/wiki/Continental_Army) aide to General [George Washington](/wiki/George_Washington), wrote a letter to [Joseph Reed](/wiki/Joseph_Reed_(politician)), Washington's [aide-de-camp](/wiki/Aide-de-camp), seeking to go "with full and ample powers from the United States of America to Spain" to seek assistance in the [Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) effort. The first known public usage is an [anonymous essay](/wiki/Anonymous_work) published in the [Williamsburg](/wiki/Williamsburg,_Virginia) newspaper *[The Virginia Gazette](/wiki/The_Virginia_Gazette)* on April 6, 1776. Sometime on or after June 11, 1776, [Thomas Jefferson](/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson) wrote "United States of America" in a rough draft of the [Declaration of Independence](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence), which was adopted by the [Second Continental Congress](/wiki/Second_Continental_Congress) on July 4, 1776.

The term "United States" and its initialism "U.S.", used as nouns or as adjectives in English, are common short names for the country. The initialism "USA", a noun, is also common. "United States" and "U.S." are the established terms throughout the [U.S. federal government](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States), with prescribed rules. "The States" is an established colloquial shortening of the name, used particularly from abroad; "stateside" is the corresponding adjective or adverb.

"America" is the feminine form of the first name of *Americus Vesputius*, the Latinized name of Italian explorer [Amerigo Vespucci](/wiki/Amerigo_Vespucci) (1454–1512); It was first used as a place name by the German cartographers [Martin Waldseemüller](/wiki/Martin_Waldseem%C3%BCller) and [Matthias Ringmann](/wiki/Matthias_Ringmann) in 1507. Vespucci first proposed that the [West Indies](/wiki/West_Indies) discovered by [Christopher Columbus](/wiki/Christopher_Columbus) in 1492 were part of a previously unknown landmass and not among the Indies at the eastern limit of Asia. In English, [the term "America"](/wiki/Americas_(terminology)) (used without a qualifier) seldom refers to topics unrelated to the United States. "The [Americas](/wiki/Americas)" is the general term to describe the totality of the continents of [North](/wiki/North_America) and [South America](/wiki/South_America).

## History

Main article: [History of the United States](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States)

For a topical guide, see [Outline of the history of the United States](/wiki/Outline_of_the_history_of_the_United_States).

### Indigenous peoples

Main articles: [History of Native Americans in the United States](/wiki/History_of_Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) and [Pre-Columbian era](/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era)

[Cliff Palace](/wiki/Cliff_Palace), a settlement of [ancestors of](/wiki/Ancestral_Puebloans) the [Native American](/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) [Pueblo peoples](/wiki/Pueblo_peoples) in present-day [Montezuma County, Colorado](/wiki/Montezuma_County,_Colorado), built between c. 1200 and 1275

The [first inhabitants of North America](/wiki/Paleo-Indians) migrated from [Siberia](/wiki/Siberia) approximately 15,000 years ago, either across the [Bering land bridge](/wiki/Bering_land_bridge) or along the [now-submerged Ice Age coastline](/wiki/Coastal_migration_(Americas)). Small isolated groups of [hunter-gatherers](/wiki/Hunter-gatherer) are said to have migrated alongside herds of large [herbivores](/wiki/Herbivore) far into [Alaska](/wiki/Alaska), with ice-free corridors developing along the Pacific coast and valleys of North America in [c.](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/circa#English) 16,500 – c. 13,500 BCE (c. 18,500 – c. 15,500 BP). The [Clovis culture](/wiki/Clovis_culture), which appeared around 11,000 BCE, is believed to be the first widespread culture in the Americas. Over time, Indigenous North American cultures grew increasingly sophisticated, and some, such as the [Mississippian culture](/wiki/Mississippian_culture), developed [agriculture](/wiki/Eastern_Agricultural_Complex), [architecture](/wiki/Southeastern_Ceremonial_Complex), and [complex societies](/wiki/Complex_societies). In the [post-archaic period](/wiki/History_of_Native_Americans_in_the_United_States#Post-Archaic_stage), the Mississippian cultures were located in the [midwestern](/wiki/Midwestern_United_States), [eastern](/wiki/Eastern_United_States), and [southern](/wiki/Southern_United_States) regions, and the [Algonquian](/wiki/Algonquian_peoples) in the [Great Lakes region](/wiki/Great_Lakes_region) and along the [Eastern Seaboard](/wiki/East_Coast_of_the_United_States), while the [Hohokam culture](/wiki/Hohokam_culture) and [Ancestral Puebloans](/wiki/Ancestral_Puebloans) inhabited the [Southwest](/wiki/American_Southwest). [Native population estimates](/wiki/Population_history_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) of what is now the United States before the arrival of European colonizers range from around 500,000 to nearly 10 million.

### European exploration, colonization and conflict (1513–1765)

Main articles: [Colonial history of the United States](/wiki/Colonial_history_of_the_United_States) and [Colonial American military history](/wiki/Colonial_American_military_history)

The [colonial possessions](/wiki/Colonial_history_of_the_United_States) of [Britain](/wiki/British_colonization_of_the_Americas) (the Thirteen Colonies in pink and others in purple), [France](/wiki/New_France) (in blue), and [Spain](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Spain) (in orange) in North America, 1750

[Christopher Columbus](/wiki/Christopher_Columbus) began exploring the [Caribbean](/wiki/Caribbean) for Spain in 1492, leading to [Spanish-speaking settlements and missions](/wiki/Spanish_Empire) from what are now [Puerto Rico](/wiki/Puerto_Rico) and Florida to [New Mexico](/wiki/Santa_Fe_de_Nuevo_M%C3%A9xico) and [California](/wiki/Alta_California). The first Spanish colony in the present-day continental United States was [Spanish Florida](/wiki/Spanish_Florida), chartered in 1513. After several settlements failed there due to starvation and disease, Spain's first permanent town, [Saint Augustine](/wiki/Saint_Augustine,_Florida), was founded in 1565.

France established its own settlements in [French Florida](/wiki/French_Florida) in 1562, but they were either abandoned (Charlesfort, 1578) or destroyed by Spanish raids ([Fort Caroline](/wiki/Fort_Caroline), 1565). [Permanent French settlements](/wiki/New_France) were founded much later along the [Great Lakes](/wiki/Great_Lakes) ([Fort Detroit](/wiki/Fort_Pontchartrain_du_D%C3%A9troit), 1701), the [Mississippi River](/wiki/Mississippi_River) ([St. Louis](/wiki/St._Louis), 1764) and especially the [Gulf of Mexico](/wiki/Gulf_of_Mexico) ([New Orleans](/wiki/New_Orleans), 1718). Early European colonies also included the thriving Dutch colony of [New Nederland](/wiki/New_Nederland) (settled 1626, present-day New York) and the small Swedish colony of [New Sweden](/wiki/New_Sweden) (settled 1638 in what became Delaware). [British colonization](/wiki/British_colonization_of_the_Americas) of the [East Coast](/wiki/East_Coast_of_the_United_States) began with the [Virginia Colony](/wiki/Colony_of_Virginia) (1607) and the [Plymouth Colony](/wiki/Plymouth_Colony) (Massachusetts, 1620).

The [Mayflower Compact](/wiki/Mayflower_Compact) in Massachusetts and the [Fundamental Orders of Connecticut](/wiki/Fundamental_Orders_of_Connecticut) established precedents for local representative [self-governance](/wiki/Self-governance) and [constitutionalism](/wiki/Constitutionalism) that would develop throughout the American colonies. While European settlers in what is now the United States experienced conflicts with Native Americans, they also engaged in trade, exchanging European tools for food and animal pelts. Relations ranged from close cooperation to warfare and massacres. The colonial authorities often pursued policies that forced Native Americans to adopt European lifestyles, including conversion to Christianity. Along the eastern seaboard, settlers [trafficked Africans](/wiki/Slavery_in_the_colonial_history_of_the_United_States) through the [Atlantic slave trade](/wiki/Atlantic_slave_trade), largely to provide [manual labor on plantations](/wiki/Slave_plantation).

The original [Thirteen Colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) that would later found the United States were administered as [possessions](/wiki/Dependent_territory) of the [British Empire](/wiki/British_Empire) by Crown-appointed governors, though [local governments held elections open to most white male property owners](/wiki/Colonial_government_in_the_Thirteen_Colonies). The colonial population grew rapidly from Maine to Georgia, eclipsing Native American populations; by the 1770s, the natural increase of the population was such that only a small minority of Americans had been born overseas. The colonies' distance from Britain facilitated the entrenchment of self-governance, and the [First Great Awakening](/wiki/First_Great_Awakening), a series of [Christian revivals](/wiki/Christian_revival), fueled colonial interest in guaranteed [religious liberty](/wiki/Freedom_of_religion).

### American Revolution and the early republic (1765–1800)

Main articles: [History of the United States (1776–1789)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1776%E2%80%931789)), [History of the United States (1789–1815)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1789%E2%80%931815)), [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution), and [Founding Fathers of the United States](/wiki/Founding_Fathers_of_the_United_States)

The *[Declaration of Independence](/wiki/Declaration_of_Independence_(painting))* portrait depicts the [Committee of Five](/wiki/Committee_of_Five) presenting [the Declaration](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence) to the [Continental Congress](/wiki/Second_Continental_Congress) on June 28, 1776, in [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia).

Following its victory in the [French and Indian War](/wiki/French_and_Indian_War), Britain began to assert greater control over local affairs in the Thirteen Colonies, resulting in [growing political resistance](/wiki/American_Revolution). One of the primary grievances of the colonists was the denial of their [rights as Englishmen](/wiki/Rights_of_Englishmen), particularly the right to [representation in the British government that taxed them](/wiki/No_taxation_without_representation). To demonstrate their dissatisfaction and resolve, the [First Continental Congress](/wiki/First_Continental_Congress) met in 1774 and passed the [Continental Association](/wiki/Continental_Association), a colonial boycott of British goods enforced by local "[committees of safety](/wiki/Committee_of_safety_(American_Revolution))" that proved effective. The British attempt to then disarm the colonists resulted in the 1775 [Battles of Lexington and Concord](/wiki/Battles_of_Lexington_and_Concord), igniting the [American Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War). At the [Second Continental Congress](/wiki/Second_Continental_Congress), the colonies appointed [George Washington](/wiki/George_Washington) commander-in-chief of the [Continental Army](/wiki/Continental_Army), and created [a committee](/wiki/Committee_of_Five) that named [Thomas Jefferson](/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson) to draft the [Declaration of Independence](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence). Two days after the Second Continental Congress passed the [Lee Resolution](/wiki/Lee_Resolution) to create an independent, [sovereign](/wiki/Sovereignty) nation, the Declaration was adopted on July 4, 1776. The [political values of the American Revolution](/wiki/American_Enlightenment) evolved from an armed rebellion demanding reform within an empire to a revolution that created a new social and governing system founded on the defense of [liberty](/wiki/Liberty) and the protection of [inalienable natural rights](/wiki/Natural_law); [sovereignty of the people](/wiki/Popular_sovereignty); [republicanism](/wiki/Republicanism_in_the_United_States) over [monarchy](/wiki/Monarchy), [aristocracy](/wiki/Aristocracy), and other hereditary political power; [civic virtue](/wiki/Civic_virtue); and an intolerance of [political corruption](/wiki/Political_corruption). The [Founding Fathers of the United States](/wiki/Founding_Fathers_of_the_United_States), who included Washington, Jefferson, [John Adams](/wiki/John_Adams), [Benjamin Franklin](/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin), [Alexander Hamilton](/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton), [John Jay](/wiki/John_Jay), [James Madison](/wiki/James_Madison), [Thomas Paine](/wiki/Thomas_Paine), and many others, were inspired by [Classical](/wiki/Classical_republicanism#Classical_antecedents), [Renaissance](/wiki/Renaissance_philosophy), and [Enlightenment](/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment#Politics) philosophies and ideas.

Though in practical effect since its drafting in 1777, the [Articles of Confederation](/wiki/Articles_of_Confederation) was ratified in 1781 and formally established a decentralized government that operated until 1789. After the British surrender at the [siege of Yorktown](/wiki/Siege_of_Yorktown) in 1781, American sovereignty was internationally recognized by the [Treaty of Paris](/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1783)) (1783), through which the U.S. gained territory stretching west to the Mississippi River, north to present-day Canada, and south to [Spanish Florida](/wiki/Spanish_Florida). The [Northwest Ordinance](/wiki/Northwest_Ordinance) (1787) established the precedent by which the country's territory would expand with the [admission of new states](/wiki/Admission_to_the_Union), rather than the expansion of existing states.

The [U.S. Constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_States) was drafted at the 1787 [Constitutional Convention](/wiki/Constitutional_Convention_(United_States)) to overcome the limitations of the Articles. It went into effect in 1789, creating a [federal republic](/wiki/Federal_republic) governed by [three separate branches](/wiki/Separation_of_powers_under_the_United_States_Constitution) that together formed a system of [checks and balances](/wiki/Checks_and_balances). George Washington [was elected](/wiki/1788%E2%80%9389_United_States_presidential_election) the country's first president under the Constitution, and the [Bill of Rights](/wiki/United_States_Bill_of_Rights) was adopted in 1791 to allay skeptics' concerns about the power of the more centralized government. [His resignation as commander-in-chief](/wiki/George_Washington%27s_resignation_as_commander-in-chief) after the Revolutionary War and his later refusal to run for a third term as the country's first president established a precedent for the supremacy of civil authority in the United States and the [peaceful transfer of power](/wiki/Peaceful_transition_of_power).

### Westward expansion and Civil War (1800–1865)

Main articles: [History of the United States (1815–1849)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1815%E2%80%931849)) and [History of the United States (1849–1865)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1849%E2%80%931865))

[Territorial expansion of the United States](/wiki/Territorial_evolution_of_the_United_States)

In the late 18th century, American settlers began to [expand westward](/wiki/Territorial_evolution_of_the_United_States) in larger numbers, many with a sense of [manifest destiny](/wiki/Manifest_destiny). The [Louisiana Purchase](/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase) of 1803 from France nearly doubled the territory of the United States. [Lingering issues with Britain remained](/wiki/Origins_of_the_War_of_1812), leading to the [War of 1812](/wiki/War_of_1812), which was fought to a draw. [Spain ceded Florida](/wiki/Adams%E2%80%93On%C3%ADs_Treaty) and its Gulf Coast territory in 1819.

The [Missouri Compromise](/wiki/Missouri_Compromise) of 1820, which admitted [Missouri](/wiki/Missouri) as a [slave state](/wiki/Slave_states_and_free_states) and [Maine](/wiki/Maine#Statehood) as a free state, attempted to balance the desire of northern states to prevent the expansion of slavery into new territories with that of southern states to extend it there. Primarily, the compromise prohibited slavery in all other lands of the Louisiana Purchase north of the [36°30′ parallel](/wiki/Parallel_36%C2%B030%E2%80%B2_north).

As Americans expanded further into territory inhabited by Native Americans, the [federal government implemented policies](/wiki/Federal_Indian_Policy) of [Indian removal](/wiki/Indian_removal) or [assimilation](/wiki/Cultural_assimilation_of_Native_Americans). The most significant such legislation was the [Indian Removal Act of 1830](/wiki/Indian_Removal_Act), a key policy of President [Andrew Jackson](/wiki/Andrew_Jackson). It resulted in the [Trail of Tears](/wiki/Trail_of_Tears) (1830–1850), in which an estimated 60,000 Native Americans living east of the [Mississippi River](/wiki/Mississippi_River) were forcibly removed and displaced to lands far to the west, causing 13,200 to 16,700 deaths along the forced march. Settler expansion as well as this influx of Indigenous peoples from the East resulted in the [American Indian Wars](/wiki/American_Indian_Wars) west of the Mississippi.

During the colonial period, [slavery became legal in all the Thirteen colonies](/wiki/Slavery_in_the_colonial_history_of_the_United_States), and by 1770 it provided the main labor force in the [large-scale](/wiki/Plantation_complexes_in_the_Southern_United_States), [agriculture-dependent economies](/wiki/Antebellum_South#Economic_structure) of the [Southern Colonies](/wiki/Southern_Colonies) from Maryland to Georgia. The practice began to be significantly questioned during the American Revolution, and spurred by an active [abolitionist movement](/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_States) that had reemerged in the 1830s, states in [the North](/wiki/Northern_United_States) enacted laws to prohibit slavery within their boundaries. At the same time, [support for slavery had strengthened in Southern states](/wiki/Pro-slavery_ideology_in_the_United_States), with widespread use of inventions such as the [cotton gin](/wiki/Cotton_gin) (1793) having made slavery immensely profitable for [Southern elites](/wiki/Planter_class).

The United States [annexed the Republic of Texas](/wiki/Texas_annexation) in 1845, and the 1846 [Oregon Treaty](/wiki/Oregon_Treaty) led to U.S. control of the present-day [American Northwest](/wiki/Northwestern_United_States). Dispute with Mexico over Texas led to the [Mexican–American War](/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War) (1846–1848). After the victory of the U.S., Mexico recognized U.S. sovereignty over Texas, [New Mexico](/wiki/New_Mexico), and [California](/wiki/California) in the 1848 [Mexican Cession](/wiki/Mexican_Cession); the cession's lands also included the future states of [Nevada](/wiki/Nevada), [Colorado](/wiki/Colorado) and [Utah](/wiki/Utah). The [California gold rush](/wiki/California_gold_rush) of 1848–1849 spurred a huge migration of white settlers to the Pacific coast, leading to even more confrontations with Native populations. One of the most violent, the [California genocide](/wiki/California_genocide) of thousands of Native inhabitants, lasted into the mid-1870s. Additional western territories and states were created.

[Slave states and free states](/wiki/Slave_states_and_free_states) in 1858

Throughout the 1850s, the [sectional conflict regarding slavery](/wiki/Slave_states_and_free_states) was further inflamed by national legislation in the U.S. Congress and decisions of the Supreme Court. In Congress, the [Fugitive Slave Act of 1850](/wiki/Fugitive_Slave_Act_of_1850) mandated the forcible return to their owners in the South of slaves taking refuge in non-slave states, while the [Kansas–Nebraska Act](/wiki/Kansas%E2%80%93Nebraska_Act) of 1854 effectively gutted the anti-slavery requirements of the Missouri Compromise. In its [Dred Scott decision](/wiki/Dred_Scott_v._Sandford) of 1857, the Supreme Court ruled against a slave brought into non-slave territory, simultaneously declaring the entire Missouri Compromise to be unconstitutional. These and other [events exacerbated tensions between North and South](/wiki/Origins_of_the_American_Civil_War) that would culminate in the [American Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War) (1861–1865).

Beginning with [South Carolina](/wiki/South_Carolina), 11 slave-state governments voted to [secede from the United States](/wiki/Secession_in_the_United_States) in 1861, joining to create the [Confederate States of America](/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America). All other state governments remained loyal to the [Union](/wiki/Union_(American_Civil_War)). War broke out in April 1861 after the Confederacy [bombarded Fort Sumter](/wiki/Battle_of_Fort_Sumter). Following the [Emancipation Proclamation](/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation) on January 1, 1863, many freed slaves joined the [Union army](/wiki/Union_army). The war [began to turn in the Union's favor](/wiki/Turning_point_of_the_American_Civil_War) following the 1863 [Siege of Vicksburg](/wiki/Siege_of_Vicksburg) and [Battle of Gettysburg](/wiki/Battle_of_Gettysburg), and the Confederates surrendered in 1865 after the Union's victory in the [Battle of Appomattox Court House](/wiki/Battle_of_Appomattox_Court_House).

### Reconstruction, Gilded Age, and Progressive Era (1866–1917)

Main article: [History of the United States (1865–1917)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1865%E2%80%931917))

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b3/Emigrants_%28i.e._immigrants%29_landing_at_Ellis_Island_-.webm/250px--Emigrants_%28i.e._immigrants%29_landing_at_Ellis_Island_-.webm.jpg)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/b/b3/Emigrants_%28i.e._immigrants%29_landing_at_Ellis_Island_-.webm/Emigrants_%28i.e._immigrants%29_landing_at_Ellis_Island_-.webm.480p.vp9.webm)

An [Edison Studios](/wiki/Edison_Studios) film showing immigrants arriving at [Ellis Island](/wiki/Ellis_Island) in [New York Harbor](/wiki/New_York_Harbor), a major point of entry for European [immigrants](/wiki/Immigration_to_the_United_States) in the late 19th and early 20th centuries

Efforts toward [reconstruction in the secessionist South](/wiki/Reconstruction_era) had begun as early as 1862, but it was only after [President Lincoln's assassination](/wiki/Assassination_of_Abraham_Lincoln) that the three [Reconstruction Amendments](/wiki/Reconstruction_Amendments) to the Constitution were ratified [to protect civil rights](/wiki/Civil_rights_movement_(1865%E2%80%931896)). The amendments codified nationally the abolition of slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for crimes, promised equal protection under the law for all persons, and prohibited discrimination on the basis of race or previous enslavement. As a result, African Americans took an active political role in ex-Confederate states in the decade following the Civil War. The former Confederate states were readmitted to the Union, beginning with Tennessee in 1866 and ending with Georgia in 1870.

National infrastructure, including [transcontinental telegraph](/wiki/First_transcontinental_telegraph) and [railroads](/wiki/First_transcontinental_railroad), spurred growth in the [American frontier](/wiki/American_frontier). This was accelerated by the [Homestead Acts](/wiki/Homestead_Acts), through which nearly 10 percent of the total land area of the United States was given away free to some 1.6 million homesteaders. From 1865 through 1917, an unprecedented stream of [immigrants](/wiki/Immigrants) arrived in the United States, including 24.4 million from Europe. Most came through the [Port of New York](/wiki/Port_of_New_York_and_New_Jersey), as New York City and other large cities on the [East Coast](/wiki/East_Coast_of_the_United_States) became home to large [Jewish](/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_the_United_States), [Irish](/wiki/Irish_Americans), and [Italian](/wiki/Italian_Americans) populations. Many [Northern Europeans](/wiki/Northern_Europe) as well as significant numbers of [Germans](/wiki/German_Americans) and other [Central Europeans](/wiki/Central_Europe) moved to the [Midwest](/wiki/Midwestern_United_States). At the same time, about one million [French Canadians](/wiki/French-Canadian_Americans) migrated from [Quebec](/wiki/Quebec) to [New England](/wiki/New_England). During the [Great Migration](/wiki/Great_Migration_(African_American)), millions of African Americans [left the rural South](/wiki/Jim_Crow_economy) for urban areas in the North. [Alaska was purchased](/wiki/Alaska_Purchase) from [Russia](/wiki/Russian_Empire) in 1867.

The [Compromise of 1877](/wiki/Compromise_of_1877) is generally considered the end of the Reconstruction era, as it resolved the electoral crisis following the [1876 presidential election](/wiki/1876_United_States_presidential_election) and led President [Rutherford B. Hayes](/wiki/Rutherford_B._Hayes) to reduce the role of federal troops in the South. Immediately, the [Redeemers](/wiki/Redeemers) began evicting the [Carpetbaggers](/wiki/Carpetbaggers) and quickly regained local control of Southern politics in the name of [white supremacy](/wiki/White_supremacy). African Americans endured a period of heightened, overt racism following Reconstruction, a time often considered the [nadir of American race relations](/wiki/Nadir_of_American_race_relations). A series of Supreme Court decisions, including *[Plessy v. Ferguson](/wiki/Plessy_v._Ferguson)*, emptied the [Fourteenth](/wiki/Fourteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) and [Fifteenth Amendments](/wiki/Fifteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) of their force, allowing [Jim Crow laws](/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws) in the South to remain unchecked, [sundown towns](/wiki/Sundown_town) in the Midwest, and [segregation in communities across the country](/wiki/Racial_segregation_in_the_United_States), which would be reinforced in part by the policy of [redlining](/wiki/Redlining) later adopted by the federal [Home Owners' Loan Corporation](/wiki/Home_Owners%27_Loan_Corporation).

[An explosion of technological advancement](/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution), accompanied by the [exploitation of cheap immigrant labor](/wiki/Exploitation_of_labour), led to rapid economic expansion during the [Gilded Age](/wiki/Gilded_Age) of the late 19th century. It continued into the early 20th, when the United States already outpaced the economies of Britain, France, and Germany combined. [Tycoons](/wiki/Tycoon) led the nation's expansion in the [railroad](/wiki/History_of_rail_transportation_in_the_United_States), [petroleum](/wiki/History_of_the_petroleum_industry_in_the_United_States), and [steel](/wiki/History_of_the_steel_industry_(1850%E2%80%931970)) industries, as the United States emerged as a pioneer of the [automotive industry](/wiki/Automotive_industry_in_the_United_States). This fostered the amassing of enormous economic and political power by [a few prominent industrialists](/wiki/Robber_baron_(industrialist)), largely through the formation of [trusts](/wiki/Trust_(business)) and [monopolies](/wiki/Monopoly) to prevent competition. These changes resulted in significant increases in [economic inequality](/wiki/Economic_inequality), [slum conditions](/wiki/How_the_Other_Half_Lives), and [social unrest](/wiki/List_of_incidents_of_civil_unrest_in_the_United_States), creating a fertile environment for [labor unions](/wiki/Labor_history_of_the_United_States) to flourish and, to a more limited extent, [socialist movements](/wiki/Socialism_in_the_United_States). This period eventually ended with the advent of the [Progressive Era](/wiki/Progressive_Era), which was characterized by significant economic, legislative and social reforms.

Pro-American elements in Hawaii [overthrew the Hawaiian monarchy](/wiki/Overthrow_of_the_Hawaiian_Kingdom); the islands [were annexed](/wiki/Newlands_Resolution) in 1898. That same year, [Puerto Rico](/wiki/Puerto_Rico), [the Philippines](/wiki/The_Philippines), and [Guam](/wiki/Guam) were ceded to the U.S. by Spain after the latter's defeat in the [Spanish–American War](/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War). (The Philippines was granted full independence from the U.S. on July 4, 1946, following World War II. Puerto Rico and Guam have remained U.S. territories.) [American Samoa](/wiki/American_Samoa) was acquired by the United States in 1900 after the [Second Samoan Civil War](/wiki/Second_Samoan_Civil_War). The [U.S. Virgin Islands](/wiki/United_States_Virgin_Islands) were purchased from [Denmark](/wiki/Denmark) in 1917 after Danish voters approved the sale in [a 1916 referendum](/wiki/1916_Danish_West_Indies_status_referendum).

### World War I, Great Depression, and World War II (1917–1945)

Main article: [History of the United States (1917–1945)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1917%E2%80%931945))

The 1945 American [Trinity test](/wiki/Trinity_(nuclear_test)), the first-ever detonation of a [nuclear weapon](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon)

The United States [entered World War I](/wiki/American_entry_into_World_War_I) alongside the [Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I) in 1917 helping to turn the tide against the [Central Powers](/wiki/Central_Powers). In 1920, [a constitutional amendment](/wiki/Nineteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) granted nationwide [women's suffrage](/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_the_United_States). During the 1920s and 1930s, radio for [mass communication](/wiki/Mass_communication) and early television transformed communications nationwide. The [Wall Street Crash of 1929](/wiki/Wall_Street_Crash_of_1929) triggered the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression_in_the_United_States), to which President [Franklin D. Roosevelt](/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) responded with the [New Deal](/wiki/New_Deal) plan of "reform, recovery and relief", a series of unprecedented and sweeping [recovery programs](/wiki/Alphabet_agencies) and [employment relief projects](/wiki/Works_Progress_Administration) combined with [financial reforms and regulations](/wiki/Regulatory_economics).

[Initially neutral](/wiki/United_States_non-interventionism_before_entering_World_War_II) during [World War II](/wiki/Military_history_of_the_United_States_during_World_War_II), the U.S. began [supplying war materiel](/wiki/Lend-Lease) to the [Allies of World War II](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) in March 1941 and [entered the war](/wiki/American_entry_into_World_War_II) in December after [Japan](/wiki/Empire_of_Japan)'s [attack on Pearl Harbor](/wiki/Attack_on_Pearl_Harbor). Agreeing to a "[Europe first](/wiki/Europe_first)" policy, the U.S. concentrated its wartime efforts on Japan's allies [Italy](/wiki/Fascist_Italy) and [Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany) until their final defeat in May 1945. The U.S. [developed the first nuclear weapons](/wiki/Manhattan_Project) and [used them against the Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki](/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki) in August 1945, ending the war. The United States was one of the "[Four Policemen](/wiki/Four_Policemen)" who met to plan the [post-war world](/wiki/Aftermath_of_World_War_II), alongside the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom), the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union), and [China](/wiki/Republic_of_China_(1912%E2%80%931949)). The U.S. emerged relatively unscathed from the war, with even greater [economic power](/wiki/Economic_power) and [international political influence](/wiki/Sphere_of_influence).

### Cold War and social revolution (1945–1991)

Main articles: [History of the United States (1945–1964)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1945%E2%80%931964)), [History of the United States (1964–1980)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1964%E2%80%931980)), and [History of the United States (1980–1991)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1980%E2%80%931991))

Civil rights activists during the [March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom](/wiki/March_on_Washington_for_Jobs_and_Freedom) in [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.) in August 1963

The end of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) in 1945 left the U.S. and the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) as [superpowers](/wiki/Superpower), each with its own political, military, and economic [sphere of influence](/wiki/Sphere_of_influence). Geopolitical tensions between the two superpowers soon led to the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War). The U.S. implemented a policy of [containment](/wiki/Containment) intended to limit the Soviet Union's sphere of influence; engaged in [regime change against governments perceived to be aligned with the Soviets](/wiki/United_States_involvement_in_regime_change#1945–1991:_Cold_War); and prevailed in the [Space Race](/wiki/Space_Race), which culminated with the [first crewed Moon landing](/wiki/Apollo_11) in 1969.

Domestically, the U.S. [experienced economic growth](/wiki/Post%E2%80%93World_War_II_economic_expansion), [urbanization](/wiki/Urbanization_in_the_United_States), and [population growth following World War II](/wiki/Mid-20th_century_baby_boom). The [civil rights movement](/wiki/Civil_rights_movement) emerged, with [Martin Luther King Jr.](/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.) becoming a prominent leader in the early 1960s. The [Great Society](/wiki/Great_Society) plan of President [Lyndon B. Johnson](/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson)'s administration resulted in groundbreaking and broad-reaching laws, policies and [a constitutional amendment](/wiki/Twenty-fourth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) to counteract some of the worst effects of lingering [institutional racism](/wiki/Institutional_racism).

The [counterculture movement](/wiki/Counterculture_of_the_1960s) in the U.S. brought significant social changes, including the liberalization of attitudes toward [recreational drug use](/wiki/Recreational_drug_use) and [sexuality](/wiki/Sexual_revolution). It also encouraged [open defiance of the military draft](/wiki/Draft_evasion_in_the_Vietnam_War) (leading to the [end of conscription](/wiki/Conscription_in_the_United_States) in 1973) and [wide opposition](/wiki/Opposition_to_United_States_involvement_in_the_Vietnam_War) to [U.S. intervention in Vietnam](/wiki/United_States_in_the_Vietnam_War), with the U.S. totally withdrawing in 1975. [A societal shift in the roles of women](/wiki/Women%27s_liberation_movement_in_the_United_States) was significantly responsible for the large increase in female paid labor participation starting in the 1970s, and by 1985 the majority of American women aged 16 and older were employed.

The [Fall of Communism](/wiki/Revolutions_of_1989) and the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) from 1989 to 1991 marked the [end of the Cold War](/wiki/End_of_the_Cold_War) and [left the United States as the world's sole superpower](/wiki/Superpower#After_the_Cold_War). This cemented the United States' global influence, reinforcing the concept of the "[American Century](/wiki/American_Century)" as the U.S. dominated international political, cultural, economic, and military affairs.

### Contemporary (1991–present)

Main articles: [History of the United States (1991–2016)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1991%E2%80%932016)) and [History of the United States (2016–present)](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(2016%E2%80%93present))

The [Twin Towers](/wiki/World_Trade_Center_(1973%E2%80%932001)) in New York City during the [September 11 attacks](/wiki/September_11_attacks) of 2001

The 1990s saw the [longest recorded economic expansion in American history](/wiki/1990s_United_States_boom), a dramatic [decline in U.S. crime rates](/wiki/Crime_in_the_United_States#Crime_over_time), and [advances in technology](/wiki/Technological_and_industrial_history_of_the_United_States#Computers_and_information_networks). Throughout this decade, technological innovations such as the [World Wide Web](/wiki/World_Wide_Web), the evolution of the [Pentium](/wiki/Pentium_(original)) microprocessor in accordance with [Moore's law](/wiki/Moore%27s_law), rechargeable [lithium-ion batteries](/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery), the first [gene therapy](/wiki/Gene_therapy) trial, and [cloning](/wiki/Cloning) either emerged in the U.S. or were improved upon there. The [Human Genome Project](/wiki/Human_Genome_Project) was formally launched in 1990, while [Nasdaq](/wiki/Nasdaq) became the first stock market in the United States to trade online in 1998.

In the [Gulf War](/wiki/Gulf_War) of 1991, [an American-led international coalition of states](/wiki/Coalition_of_the_Gulf_War) expelled an [Iraqi](/wiki/Ba%27athist_Iraq) invasion force that had occupied neighboring [Kuwait](/wiki/Kuwait). The [September 11 attacks](/wiki/September_11_attacks) on the United States in 2001 by the [pan-Islamist](/wiki/Pan-Islamism) militant organization [al-Qaeda](/wiki/Al-Qaeda) led to the [war on terror](/wiki/War_on_terror) and subsequent military interventions [in Afghanistan](/wiki/War_in_Afghanistan_(2001%E2%80%932021)) and [in Iraq](/wiki/Iraq_War).

The [U.S. housing bubble](/wiki/2000s_United_States_housing_bubble) culminated in 2007 with the [Great Recession](/wiki/Great_Recession), the largest economic contraction since the Great Depression. Beginning in the 2010s, and particularly in the 2020s, the United States has experienced [increased political polarization](/wiki/Political_polarization_in_the_United_States) and [significant democratic backsliding](/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_the_United_States). The country's polarization was violently reflected in the [January 2021 Capitol attack](/wiki/January_6_United_States_Capitol_attack), when a mob of insurrectionists entered the [U.S. Capitol](/wiki/United_States_Capitol) and sought to prevent the peaceful transfer of power in an [attempted self-coup d'état](/wiki/Self-coup#Notable_events_described_as_attempted_self-coups).

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of the United States](/wiki/Geography_of_the_United_States)

A [topographic map](/wiki/Topographic_map) of the United States

The United States is the world's [third-largest country](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area) by total area behind Russia and Canada. The 48 [contiguous states and the District of Columbia](/wiki/Contiguous_United_States) have a combined area of 3,119,885 square miles (8,080,470 km2). In 2021, the United States had 8% of the Earth's permanent meadows and pastures and 10% of its cropland.

Starting in the east, [the coastal plain](/wiki/Atlantic_Plain) of the [Atlantic](/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean) seaboard gives way to inland forests and rolling hills in the [Piedmont](/wiki/Piedmont_(United_States)) plateau region. The [Appalachian Mountains](/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains) and the [Adirondack Massif](/wiki/Adirondack_Mountains) separate the [East Coast](/wiki/East_Coast_of_the_United_States) from the [Great Lakes](/wiki/Great_Lakes) and the grasslands of [the Midwest](/wiki/Midwestern_United_States). The [Mississippi River System](/wiki/Mississippi_River_System), the world's [fourth-longest river system](/wiki/List_of_rivers_by_length), runs predominantly north–south through the center of the country. The flat and fertile [prairie](/wiki/Prairie) of the [Great Plains](/wiki/Great_Plains) stretches to the west, interrupted by [a highland region](/wiki/U.S._Interior_Highlands) in the southeast.

The [Grand Canyon](/wiki/Grand_Canyon) in Arizona

The [Rocky Mountains](/wiki/Rocky_Mountains), west of the Great Plains, extend north to south across the country, peaking at over 14,000 feet (4,300 m) in [Colorado](/wiki/Colorado). The [supervolcano](/wiki/Supervolcano) underlying [Yellowstone National Park](/wiki/Yellowstone_National_Park) in the Rocky Mountains, the [Yellowstone Caldera](/wiki/Yellowstone_Caldera), is the continent's largest volcanic feature. Farther west are the rocky [Great Basin](/wiki/Great_Basin) and the [Chihuahuan](/wiki/Chihuahuan_Desert), [Sonoran](/wiki/Sonoran_Desert), and [Mojave](/wiki/Mojave_Desert) deserts. In the northwest corner of [Arizona](/wiki/Arizona), carved by the [Colorado River](/wiki/Colorado_River), is the [Grand Canyon](/wiki/Grand_Canyon), a steep-sided canyon and popular tourist destination known for its overwhelming visual size and intricate, colorful landscape. The [Cascade](/wiki/Cascade_Range) and [Sierra Nevada](/wiki/Sierra_Nevada) mountain ranges run close to the [Pacific coast](/wiki/West_Coast_of_the_United_States). The [lowest and highest points in the contiguous United States](/wiki/Extreme_points_of_the_United_States) are in the State of California, about 84 miles (135 km) apart.

At an elevation of 20,310 feet (6,190.5 m), Alaska's [Denali](/wiki/Denali) (also called Mount McKinley) is the highest peak in the country and on the continent. Active [volcanoes in the U.S.](/wiki/List_of_volcanoes_in_the_United_States) are common throughout Alaska's [Alexander](/wiki/Alexander_Archipelago) and [Aleutian Islands](/wiki/Aleutian_Islands). Located entirely outside North America, the archipelago of Hawaii consists of volcanic islands, [physiographically](/wiki/Physical_geography) and [ethnologically](/wiki/Ethnology) part of the [Polynesian](/wiki/Polynesia) subregion of [Oceania](/wiki/Oceania).

In addition to its total land area, the United States has one of the world's largest marine [exclusive economic zones](/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zones) spanning approximately 4.5 million square miles (11.7 million km2) of ocean.

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of the United States](/wiki/Climate_of_the_United_States)

The [Köppen climate types](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) of the United States

With its large size and geographic variety, the United States includes most climate types. East of the [100th meridian](/wiki/100th_meridian_west), the climate ranges from [humid continental](/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) in the north to [humid subtropical](/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) in the south. The western Great Plains are [semi-arid](/wiki/Semi-arid_climate). Many mountainous areas of the American West have an [alpine climate](/wiki/Alpine_climate). The climate is [arid](/wiki/Desert_climate) in the Southwest, [Mediterranean](/wiki/Mediterranean_climate) in [coastal California](/wiki/Coastal_California), and [oceanic](/wiki/Oceanic_climate) in coastal [Oregon](/wiki/Oregon), [Washington](/wiki/Washington_(state)), and southern [Alaska](/wiki/Alaska). Most of Alaska is [subarctic](/wiki/Subarctic_climate) or [polar](/wiki/Polar_climate). [Hawaii](/wiki/Hawaii), the [southern tip of Florida](/wiki/South_Florida) and U.S. territories in the [Caribbean](/wiki/Caribbean) and [Pacific](/wiki/Pacific_Ocean) are [tropical](/wiki/Tropical_climate).

The United States receives more high-impact [extreme weather](/wiki/Extreme_weather) incidents than any other country. States bordering the [Gulf of Mexico](/wiki/Gulf_of_Mexico) are prone to hurricanes, and most of the world's tornadoes [occur in the country](/wiki/Tornadoes_in_the_United_States), mainly in [Tornado Alley](/wiki/Tornado_Alley). Due to [climate change in the country](/wiki/Climate_change_in_the_United_States), extreme weather has become more frequent in the U.S. in the 21st century, with three times the number of reported [heat waves](/wiki/Heat_waves) compared to the 1960s. Since the 1990s, droughts in the [American Southwest](/wiki/Southwestern_United_States) have become more persistent and more severe. The regions considered as the most attractive to the population are the most vulnerable.

### Biodiversity and conservation

Main articles: [Fauna of the United States](/wiki/Fauna_of_the_United_States) and [Flora of the United States](/wiki/Flora_of_the_United_States)

The [bald eagle](/wiki/Bald_eagle), the [national emblem of the United States](/wiki/Bald_eagle#National_symbol_of_the_United_States) since 1782 and officially declared the national bird in 2024

The U.S. is one of 17 [megadiverse countries](/wiki/Megadiverse_countries) containing large numbers of [endemic species](/wiki/List_of_endangered_animals_of_North_America): about 17,000 species of [vascular plants](/wiki/Vascular_plant) occur in the contiguous United States and Alaska, and over 1,800 species of [flowering plants](/wiki/Flowering_plant) are found in Hawaii, few of which occur on the mainland. The United States is home to 428 [mammal](/wiki/Mammal) species, 784 birds, 311 reptiles, 295 [amphibians](/wiki/Amphibian), and around 91,000 insect species.

There are [63 national parks](/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_the_United_States), and [hundreds of other federally managed](/wiki/Federal_lands) monuments, forests, and [wilderness areas](/wiki/National_Wilderness_Preservation_System), administered by the [National Park Service](/wiki/National_Park_Service) and other agencies. About 28% of the country's land is publicly owned and federally managed, primarily in the [Western States](/wiki/Western_United_States). [Most of this land is protected](/wiki/Protected_areas_of_the_United_States), though some is leased for commercial use, and less than one percent is used for military purposes.

[Environmental issues in the United States](/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_the_United_States) include debates on [non-renewable resources](/wiki/Non-renewable_resource) and [nuclear energy](/wiki/Nuclear_power_debate), [air and water pollution](/wiki/Pollution_prevention_in_the_US), [biodiversity](/wiki/Biodiversity), logging and [deforestation](/wiki/Deforestation_in_the_United_States), and [climate change](/wiki/Climate_change_in_the_United_States). The [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency](/wiki/United_States_Environmental_Protection_Agency) (EPA) is the federal agency charged with [addressing most environmental-related issues](/wiki/Environmental_policy_of_the_United_States). The [idea of wilderness](/wiki/National_Wilderness_Preservation_System) has shaped the management of public lands since 1964, with the [Wilderness Act](/wiki/Wilderness_Act). The [Endangered Species Act of 1973](/wiki/Endangered_Species_Act_of_1973) provides a way to protect threatened and endangered species and their habitats. The [United States Fish and Wildlife Service](/wiki/United_States_Fish_and_Wildlife_Service) implements and enforces the Act. In 2024, the U.S. ranked 35th among 180 countries in the [Environmental Performance Index](/wiki/Environmental_Performance_Index).

## Government and politics

Main article: [Politics of the United States](/wiki/Politics_of_the_United_States)

The [Capitol Building](/wiki/United_States_Capitol), seat of legislative government, houses both chambers of [Congress](/wiki/U.S._Congress).

The [White House](/wiki/White_House), the president's [residence](/wiki/Executive_residence) and workplace, includes offices for [the executive staff](/wiki/Executive_Office_of_the_President_of_the_United_States).

The [Supreme Court Building](/wiki/United_States_Supreme_Court_Building) houses the [nation's highest court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States).

The United States is a federal republic consisting of 50 states and a federal capital district, Washington, D.C. The U.S. asserts sovereignty over five unincorporated territories and several uninhabited island possessions. It is the world's oldest surviving federation, and its [presidential system](/wiki/Presidential_system) of federal government has been adopted, in whole or in part, by many newly independent states worldwide following their [decolonization](/wiki/Decolonization). The Constitution of the United States serves as the country's [supreme legal document](/wiki/Supremacy_Clause).

The United States was the most prominent [liberal democracy](/wiki/Liberal_democracy) for much of the 20th and early 21st centuries, but has undergone [significant democratic backsliding](/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_the_United_States) and a shift toward a [hybrid regime](/wiki/Hybrid_regime)—a political system combining [autocratic](/wiki/Authoritarianism) and [democratic](/wiki/Democracy) features. There is an ongoing debate among political scientists on whether the country is more appropriately classified as an [electoral autocracy](/wiki/Electoral_autocracy) or [illiberal democracy](/wiki/Illiberal_democracy), with few still considering it a robust liberal democracy.

### Federal government

Main article: [Federal government of the United States](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States)

Composed of three branches, all headquartered in Washington, D.C., the federal government is the national government of the United States. The U.S. Constitution establishes a [separation of powers](/wiki/Separation_of_powers) intended to provide a system of [checks and balances](/wiki/Checks_and_balances) to prevent any of the three branches from becoming supreme.

#### Legislature

The [U.S. Congress](/wiki/United_States_Congress) is a [bicameral legislature](/wiki/Bicameral_legislature) made up of the [Senate](/wiki/United_States_Senate) and the [House of Representatives](/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives). The Senate has 100 members—two residents from each state and elected by that state's voters for a six-year term. The House of Representatives has 435 members, elected for a two-year term by the constituency of the [congressional district](/wiki/List_of_United_States_congressional_districts) where they reside. A [state's legislature](/wiki/State_legislature_(United_States)) decides the district boundaries, which are contiguous within the state. Every U.S. congressional district is of equivalent population and sends one representative to Congress. Election years for senators are staggered so that only one-third of them will be up for election every two years. U.S. representatives are all up for election at the same time every two years. The U.S. Congress makes [federal law](/wiki/Federal_law), [declares war](/wiki/Declaration_of_war), approves treaties, has the [power of the purse](/wiki/Power_of_the_purse), and has [the power of impeachment](/wiki/Impeachment_in_the_United_States). One of its foremost non-legislative functions is [the power to investigate](/wiki/Congressional_investigation) and oversee the executive branch. [Congressional oversight](/wiki/Congressional_oversight) is usually delegated to committees and is facilitated by [Congress' power to issue subpoenas](/wiki/Contempt_of_Congress). Much of the work of Congress is performed by [a collection of committees](/wiki/United_States_congressional_committee), each appointed for a specific purpose or function. Committee membership is by tradition and statute [bipartisan](/wiki/Bipartisan), but all committees are [chaired](/wiki/Chair_(officer)) by a member of the [majority party](/wiki/Majority_party), who sets the committee agenda.

#### Executive

[President](/wiki/President_of_the_United_States)

[Vice President](/wiki/Vice_President_of_the_United_States)

The U.S. president is the [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state), [commander-in-chief](/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the military, and [chief executive of the federal government](/wiki/Head_of_government). The president appoints the [members of the Cabinet](/wiki/Cabinet_of_the_United_States), subject to Senate approval, and names other officials who administer and enforce federal law and policy through [their respective agencies](/wiki/List_of_federal_agencies_in_the_United_States). The president has the ability to veto [legislative bills](/wiki/Bill_(law)) from the U.S. Congress before they become law. However, [presidential vetoes](/wiki/Veto_power_in_the_United_States) can be overridden by a two-thirds [supermajority](/wiki/Supermajority#Use_in_governments_around_the_world) vote in both chambers of Congress. The president also has clemency power for federal crimes and [can issue pardons](/wiki/Federal_pardons_in_the_United_States). Finally, the president has the authority to issue expansive "[executive orders](/wiki/Executive_orders)" in a number of policy areas, subject to [judicial review](/wiki/Judicial_review_in_the_United_States). Candidates for president campaign with a vice-presidential [running mate](/wiki/Running_mate). Both candidates are elected together, or defeated together, in a presidential election. Unlike other votes in American politics, this is technically an [indirect election](/wiki/Indirect_election) in which the winner will be determined by the [U.S. Electoral College](/wiki/United_States_Electoral_College). There, votes are officially cast by individual electors selected by [their state legislature](/wiki/State_legislature_(United_States)). In practice, however, each of the 50 states chooses a group of presidential electors who are required by state law to confirm the winner of their state's popular vote. Each state is allocated two electors plus one additional elector for every congressional district in the state, which in effect combines to equal the number of elected officials that state sends to Congress. The District of Columbia, with no representatives or senators, is allocated three electoral votes. Both the president and the vice president serve a four-year term, and the president may be [reelected to the office only once](/wiki/Twenty-second_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution), for one additional four-year term.

#### Judiciary

The [U.S. federal judiciary](/wiki/Federal_judiciary_of_the_United_States), whose judges are all appointed for life by the president with Senate approval, consists primarily of the [U.S. Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States), the [U.S. courts of appeals](/wiki/United_States_courts_of_appeals), and the [U.S. district courts](/wiki/United_States_district_court). The lowest level in the federal judiciary is the federal district court, which decides all cases considered to be under "[original jurisdiction](/wiki/Original_jurisdiction#Federal_and_state_courts)", such as federal statutes, constitutional law, or [international treaties](/wiki/International_treaties). After a federal district court has decided a case, its ruling may be contested and sent to a higher court, a federal court of appeals. The U.S. judicial system's 12 federal circuits divide the country into 12 separate geographic administrative regions for appeals decisions. The next and highest court in the system is the Supreme Court of the United States. The U.S. Supreme Court interprets laws and [overturns those it finds unconstitutional](/wiki/Judicial_review_in_the_United_States). On average, the Supreme Court receives about 7,000 appeals petitions for [writs of certiorari](/wiki/Writs_of_certiorari) each year, but only grants about 80. Consisting of nine members led by the [Chief Justice of the United States](/wiki/Chief_Justice_of_the_United_States), the court judges each case before it by majority decision. In the case of a presidential impeachment trial in the Senate, the chief justice presides. As with all other federal judges, the members are appointed for life by the sitting president with Senate approval when a vacancy becomes available.

The three-branch system is known as the [presidential system](/wiki/Presidential_system), in contrast to the [parliamentary system](/wiki/Parliamentary_system) where the executive is part of the legislative body. Many countries around the world adopted this aspect of the 1789 Constitution of the United States, especially in the [postcolonial](/wiki/Postcolonialism) Americas.

### Subdivisions

Main articles: [U.S. state](/wiki/U.S._state), [County (United States)](/wiki/County_(United_States)), [Indian country](/wiki/Indian_country), and [Territories of the United States](/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States)

Further information: [List of states and territories of the United States](/wiki/List_of_states_and_territories_of_the_United_States), [List of federally recognized tribes by state](/wiki/List_of_federally_recognized_tribes_by_state), and [Federally recognized tribe](/wiki/Federally_recognized_tribe)

[Territories of the United States](/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States) include [American Samoa](/wiki/American_Samoa), [Guam](/wiki/Guam), the [Northern Mariana Islands](/wiki/Northern_Mariana_Islands), [Puerto Rico](/wiki/Puerto_Rico), and the [U.S. Virgin Islands](/wiki/United_States_Virgin_Islands).

In the [U.S. federal system](/wiki/Federalism_in_the_United_States), sovereign powers are shared between three levels of government specified in the Constitution: the federal government, the states, and Indian tribes. The U.S. also asserts sovereignty over five permanently inhabited territories: [American Samoa](/wiki/American_Samoa), [Guam](/wiki/Guam), the [Northern Mariana Islands](/wiki/Northern_Mariana_Islands), [Puerto Rico](/wiki/Puerto_Rico), and the [U.S. Virgin Islands](/wiki/United_States_Virgin_Islands).

Residents of the 50 states are governed by their elected state government, under [state constitutions compatible with the national constitution](/wiki/State_constitutions_in_the_United_States), and by elected [local governments](/wiki/Local_government_in_the_United_States) that are administrative divisions of a state. States are subdivided into counties or county equivalents, which (except in Hawaii) can permit the formation of independent municipalities administered by their own elected representatives. The District of Columbia is [a federal district](/wiki/Federal_district_of_the_United_States) containing the U.S. capital, [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.) The federal district is an administrative division of the federal government.

Map of the [326 Indian reservations](/wiki/List_of_Indian_reservations_in_the_United_States) in the United States; 231 recognized [Alaska Native tribes](/wiki/List_of_Alaska_Native_tribal_entities) are not shown.

Indian country is made up of 574 [federally recognized tribes](/wiki/Federally_recognized_tribe) and [326 Indian reservations](/wiki/List_of_Indian_reservations_in_the_United_States). They hold a government-to-government relationship with the U.S. federal government in Washington and are legally defined as [domestic dependent nations](/wiki/Domestic_dependent_nations) with [inherent tribal sovereignty rights](/wiki/Tribal_sovereignty_in_the_United_States).

In addition to the five major territories, the U.S. also asserts sovereignty over the [United States Minor Outlying Islands](/wiki/United_States_Minor_Outlying_Islands) in the [Pacific Ocean](/wiki/Pacific_Ocean) and the [Caribbean](/wiki/Caribbean). The seven undisputed islands without permanent populations are [Baker Island](/wiki/Baker_Island), [Howland Island](/wiki/Howland_Island), [Jarvis Island](/wiki/Jarvis_Island), [Johnston Atoll](/wiki/Johnston_Atoll), [Kingman Reef](/wiki/Kingman_Reef), [Midway Atoll](/wiki/Midway_Atoll), and [Palmyra Atoll](/wiki/Palmyra_Atoll). U.S. sovereignty over the unpopulated [Bajo Nuevo Bank](/wiki/Bajo_Nuevo_Bank), [Navassa Island](/wiki/Navassa_Island), [Serranilla Bank](/wiki/Serranilla_Bank), and [Wake Island](/wiki/Wake_Island) is disputed.

The Constitution is silent on political parties. However, they developed independently in the 18th century with the [Federalist](/wiki/Federalist_Party) and [Anti-Federalist](/wiki/Anti-Federalist_Party) parties. ### Foreign relations

Main articles: [Foreign relations of the United States](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_the_United_States) and [Foreign policy of the United States](/wiki/Foreign_policy_of_the_United_States)

The [United Nations headquarters](/wiki/Headquarters_of_the_United_Nations) has been situated along the [East River](/wiki/East_River) in [Midtown Manhattan](/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan) since 1952; in 1945, the United States was a [founding member of the UN](/wiki/Founding_member_of_the_United_Nations).

The United States has an established structure of foreign relations, with the world's [second-largest diplomatic corps](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_diplomatic_missions) as of 2024. It is a [permanent member of the United Nations Security Council](/wiki/Permanent_members_of_the_United_Nations_Security_Council) and home to the [United Nations headquarters](/wiki/Headquarters_of_the_United_Nations). The United States is a member of the [G7](/wiki/G7), [G20](/wiki/G20), and [OECD](/wiki/OECD) intergovernmental organizations. [Almost all countries have embassies](/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_in_the_United_States) and many have [consulates](/wiki/Consul_(representative)) (official representatives) in the country. Likewise, nearly all countries host formal [diplomatic missions](/wiki/Diplomatic_mission) with the United States, except [Iran](/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea%E2%80%93United_States_relations), and [Bhutan](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Bhutan#Other_countries). Though [Taiwan](/wiki/Taiwan%E2%80%93United_States_relations) does not have formal diplomatic relations with the U.S., it maintains close unofficial relations. The United States regularly [supplies Taiwan with military equipment](/wiki/Six_Assurances) to deter potential Chinese aggression.

The United States has a "[Special Relationship](/wiki/Special_Relationship)" [with the United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom%E2%80%93United_States_relations) and strong ties [with Canada](/wiki/Canada%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [Australia](/wiki/Australia%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [the Philippines](/wiki/Philippines%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [Japan](/wiki/Japan%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [South Korea](/wiki/South_Korea%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [Israel](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93United_States_relations), and several [European Union countries](/wiki/Member_state_of_the_European_Union) such as [France](/wiki/France%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [Italy](/wiki/Italy%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [Germany](/wiki/Germany%E2%80%93United_States_relations), [Spain](/wiki/Spain%E2%80%93United_States_relations), and [Poland](/wiki/Poland%E2%80%93United_States_relations). The U.S. works closely with its [NATO](/wiki/NATO) allies on military and [national security](/wiki/National_security) issues, and with countries in the Americas through the [Organization of American States](/wiki/Organization_of_American_States) and the [United States–Mexico–Canada Free Trade Agreement](/wiki/United_States%E2%80%93Mexico%E2%80%93Canada_Agreement). The U.S. exercises full international defense authority and responsibility for [Micronesia](/wiki/Federated_States_of_Micronesia), the [Marshall Islands](/wiki/Marshall_Islands), and [Palau](/wiki/Palau) through the [Compact of Free Association](/wiki/Compact_of_Free_Association). It has increasingly conducted strategic cooperation [with India](/wiki/India%E2%80%93United_States_relations), while [its ties with China](/wiki/China%E2%80%93United_States_relations) have steadily deteriorated. Beginning in 2014, the U.S. had become [a key ally of Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine%E2%80%93United_States_relations).

### Military

Main article: [United States Armed Forces](/wiki/United_States_Armed_Forces)

[The Pentagon](/wiki/The_Pentagon), the headquarters of the [U.S. Department of Defense](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense) in [Arlington County, Virginia](/wiki/Arlington_County,_Virginia), is one of the world's largest office buildings with over 6.5 million square feet (600,000 m2) of [floor space](/wiki/Floor_space).

The president is the [commander-in-chief](/wiki/Commander-in-Chief_of_the_United_States) of the United States Armed Forces and appoints its leaders, the [secretary of defense](/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_Defense) and the [Joint Chiefs of Staff](/wiki/Joint_Chiefs_of_Staff). The [Department of Defense](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense), headquartered at [the Pentagon](/wiki/The_Pentagon) near Washington, D.C., administers five of the six service branches, which are made up of the [U.S. Army](/wiki/United_States_Army), [Marine Corps](/wiki/United_States_Marine_Corps), [Navy](/wiki/United_States_Navy), [Air Force](/wiki/United_States_Air_Force), and [Space Force](/wiki/United_States_Space_Force). The [Coast Guard](/wiki/United_States_Coast_Guard) is administered by the [Department of Homeland Security](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Homeland_Security) in peacetime and can be transferred to the [Department of the Navy](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_the_Navy) in wartime. Total strength of the entire military is about 1.3 million active duty with an additional 400,000 in reserve.

The United States military is widely regarded as the most powerful and advanced in the world. The U.S. [spent $954 billion on its military](/wiki/Military_budget_of_the_United_States) in 2025, which is by far the [largest amount of any country](/wiki/List_of_countries_with_highest_military_expenditures), making up 33% of global military spending and accounting for 3.1% of the country's GDP. The [U.S. possesses](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_the_United_States) 42% of the world's nuclear weapons—the second-largest stockpile after [that of Russia](/wiki/Russia_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction). The United States has the [third-largest combined armed forces](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_military_and_paramilitary_personnel) in the world, behind the [Chinese People's Liberation Army](/wiki/People%27s_Liberation_Army) and [Indian Armed Forces](/wiki/Indian_Armed_Forces). In addition to the vast network of military bases on its soil, the U.S. maintains approximately 800 other bases and installations around the world, and it [deploys greater than 100 active-duty personnel](/wiki/United_States_military_deployments) in each of 25 foreign countries. The United States has engaged in over 400 military interventions since its founding in 1776, with over half of these occurring between 1950 and 2019 and 25% occurring in the post–Cold War era.

[State defense forces](/wiki/State_defense_forces) (SDFs) are military units that operate under the sole authority of a state government. SDFs are authorized by state and federal law but are under the command of [the state's governor](/wiki/Governor_(United_States)).
By contrast, the 54 [U.S. National Guard](/wiki/National_Guard_(United_States)) organizations fall under the dual control of [state](/wiki/State_governments_of_the_United_States) or [territorial](/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States) governments and the [federal government](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States); their units can also become federalized entities, but SDFs cannot be federalized. The National Guard personnel of a state or territory can be federalized by the president under the [National Defense Act Amendments of 1933](/wiki/National_Defense_Act_of_1916#National_Defense_Act_Amendments_of_1933); this legislation created the Guard and provides for the integration of [Army National Guard](/wiki/Army_National_Guard) and [Air National Guard](/wiki/Air_National_Guard) units and personnel into the U.S. Army and (since 1947) the U.S. Air Force. The total number of National Guard members is about 430,000, while the estimated combined strength of SDFs is less than 10,000.

### Law enforcement and criminal justice

Main articles: [Law of the United States](/wiki/Law_of_the_United_States), [Law enforcement in the United States](/wiki/Law_enforcement_in_the_United_States), and [Crime in the United States](/wiki/Crime_in_the_United_States)

[J. Edgar Hoover Building](/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover_Building), the headquarters of the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation) (FBI), in [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.)

There are about 18,000 U.S. police agencies from local to national level in the United States. Law in the United States is mainly enforced by local police departments and [sheriff departments](/wiki/Sheriffs_in_the_United_States) in their municipal or county jurisdictions. [The state police](/wiki/State_police_(United_States)) departments [have authority in their respective state](/wiki/Police_power_(United_States_constitutional_law)), and [federal agencies](/wiki/Federal_law_enforcement_in_the_United_States) such as the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation) (FBI) and the [U.S. Marshals Service](/wiki/United_States_Marshals_Service) have national jurisdiction and specialized duties, such as protecting [civil rights](/wiki/Civil_rights), [national security](/wiki/National_security_of_the_United_States), enforcing [U.S. federal courts](/wiki/U.S._federal_courts)' rulings and federal laws, and interstate criminal activity. [State courts](/wiki/State_court_(United_States)) conduct almost all civil and criminal trials, while federal courts [adjudicate the much smaller number of civil and criminal cases that relate to federal law](/wiki/Subject-matter_jurisdiction).

There is no unified "criminal justice system" in the United States. The [American prison system](/wiki/Incarceration_in_the_United_States) is largely heterogenous, with thousands of relatively independent systems operating across federal, state, local, and tribal levels. In 2026, "these systems hold [nearly 2 million people](/wiki/United_States_incarceration_rate) in 1,566 state prisons, 98 federal prisons, 3,116 local jails, 1,277 juvenile correctional facilities, 220 immigration detention facilities, and 77 Indian country jails, as well as in military prisons, civil commitment centers, state psychiatric hospitals, and prisons in the U.S. territories—at a system-wide cost of at least $445 billion each year."

Despite disparate systems of confinement, four main institutions dominate: [federal prisons](/wiki/List_of_United_States_federal_prisons), [state prisons](/wiki/Lists_of_United_States_state_prisons), local jails, and [juvenile correctional facilities](/wiki/American_juvenile_justice_system). Federal prisons are run by the [Federal Bureau of Prisons](/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Prisons) and hold pretrial detainees as well as people who have been convicted of federal crimes. State prisons, run by the department of corrections of each state, hold people sentenced and serving prison time (usually longer than one year) for felony offenses. Local jails are county or municipal facilities that incarcerate defendants prior to trial; they also hold those serving short sentences (typically under a year). Juvenile correctional facilities are operated by local or state governments and serve as longer-term placements for any [minor adjudicated as delinquent](/wiki/Juvenile_delinquency) and ordered by a judge to be confined.

In January 2023, the United States had the [sixth-highest per capita incarceration rate](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_incarceration_rate) in the world—531 people per 100,000 inhabitants—and the largest prison and jail population in the world, with more than 1.9 million people incarcerated. An analysis of the [World Health Organization](/wiki/World_Health_Organization) Mortality Database from 2010 showed U.S. homicide rates "were 7 times higher than in other high-income countries, driven by [a gun homicide rate](/wiki/Gun_deaths_in_the_United_States) that was 25 times higher".

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of the United States](/wiki/Economy_of_the_United_States)

The [U.S. dollar](/wiki/United_States_dollar) is the most-used currency [in international transactions](/wiki/International_use_of_the_U.S._dollar) and the world's foremost [reserve currency](/wiki/Reserve_currency).

The U.S. has a highly developed [mixed economy](/wiki/Mixed_economy) that has been the world's largest nominally [since about 1890](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_largest_historical_GDP). Its 2024 [gross domestic product](/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) (GDP) of more than $29 trillion constituted over 25% of nominal [global economic output](/wiki/Gross_world_product), or 15% at [purchasing power parity](/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity) (PPP). From 1983 to 2008, U.S. real compounded annual GDP growth was 3.3%, compared to a 2.3% weighted average for the rest of the [G7](/wiki/G7). The country ranks [first in the world by nominal GDP](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)), [second when adjusted for purchasing power parities](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(PPP)) (PPP), and [ninth by PPP-adjusted GDP per capita](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(PPP)_per_capita). In February 2024, the total [U.S. federal government debt](/wiki/National_debt_of_the_United_States) was $34.4 trillion.

[New York City](/wiki/New_York_City) is the world's principal [financial center](/wiki/Financial_center), and [its metropolitan area](/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area) is the world's [largest metropolitan economy](/wiki/List_of_cities_by_GDP).

Of the world's [500 largest companies by revenue](/wiki/Fortune_Global_500), [138 were headquartered in the U.S.](/wiki/List_of_largest_companies_in_the_United_States_by_revenue) in 2025, the highest number of any country. The [U.S. dollar](/wiki/United_States_dollar) is the currency most used [in international transactions](/wiki/International_use_of_the_U.S._dollar) and the world's foremost [reserve currency](/wiki/Reserve_currency), backed by the country's dominant economy, [its military](/wiki/United_States_Armed_Forces), the [petrodollar](/wiki/Petrodollar) system, its large [U.S. treasuries market](/wiki/U.S._Treasury), and its linked [eurodollar](/wiki/Eurodollar). [Several countries use it as their official currency](/wiki/United_States_dollar#Countries_that_use_US_dollar), and in others it is the [*de facto* currency](/wiki/De_facto_currency). The U.S. has [free trade agreements](/wiki/Free_trade_agreements) with [several countries](/wiki/Free_trade_agreements_of_the_United_States), including the [USMCA](/wiki/United_States%E2%80%93Mexico%E2%80%93Canada_Agreement). Although the United States has reached a [post-industrial level of economic development](/wiki/Post-industrial_economy) and is often described as having a [service economy](/wiki/Service_economy), it remains a major industrial power; in 2024, the [U.S. manufacturing sector](/wiki/U.S._manufacturing_sector) was the world's [second-largest by value output](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_manufacturing_output) after [China's](/wiki/Manufacturing_in_China).

The [New York Stock Exchange](/wiki/New_York_Stock_Exchange) on [Wall Street](/wiki/Wall_Street), the world's [largest stock exchange by market capitalization](/wiki/List_of_stock_exchanges#Major_stock_exchanges)

[New York City](/wiki/New_York_City) is the world's principal [financial center](/wiki/Financial_center), and [its metropolitan area](/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area) is the world's [largest metropolitan economy](/wiki/List_of_cities_by_GDP). The [New York Stock Exchange](/wiki/New_York_Stock_Exchange) and [Nasdaq](/wiki/Nasdaq), both located in New York City, are the world's two [largest stock exchanges](/wiki/List_of_stock_exchanges) by [market capitalization](/wiki/Market_capitalization) and [trade volume](/wiki/Trade_volume). The United States is at the forefront of [technological advancement](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_the_United_States) and [innovation](/wiki/Innovation) in many economic fields, especially in [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence); [electronics](/wiki/Electronics) and [computers](/wiki/Computer); [pharmaceuticals](/wiki/Pharmaceuticals); and medical, [aerospace](/wiki/Aerospace) and [military equipment](/wiki/Military_equipment). The country's economy is fueled by abundant [natural resources](/wiki/Natural_resource), a well-developed [infrastructure](/wiki/Infrastructure_policy_of_the_United_States), and [high productivity](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_labour_productivity). The [largest trading partners of the United States](/wiki/List_of_the_largest_trading_partners_of_the_United_States) are the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union), Mexico, Canada, China, Japan, South Korea, the United Kingdom, Vietnam, India, and Taiwan. The United States is the world's [largest importer](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_imports) and [second-largest exporter](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_exports). It is by far the world's [largest exporter of services](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_service_exports).

Americans have the highest average [household](/wiki/Household_income) and [employee income](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_average_wage) among OECD member states, and the fourth-highest [median household income](/wiki/Median_income) in 2023, up from sixth-highest in 2013. With personal [consumption expenditures](/wiki/Consumer_spending) of over $18.5 trillion in 2023, the U.S. has a heavily [consumer-driven economy](/wiki/Consumer_economy) and is the world's [largest consumer market](/wiki/List_of_largest_consumer_markets). The U.S. [ranked first in the number of dollar billionaires](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_billionaires) and [millionaires](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_millionaires) in 2023, with 735 billionaires and nearly 22 million millionaires.

[Wealth in the United States](/wiki/Wealth_in_the_United_States) is highly concentrated; in 2011, the richest 10% of the adult population owned 72% of the country's household wealth, while the bottom 50% owned just 2%. [U.S. wealth inequality](/wiki/Wealth_inequality_in_the_United_States) increased substantially since the late 1980s, and [income inequality in the U.S.](/wiki/Income_inequality_in_the_United_States) reached a record high in 2019. In 2024, the country had some of the highest wealth and income inequality levels among [OECD](/wiki/OECD) countries. Since the 1970s, there has been a decoupling of U.S. wage gains from worker productivity. In 2016, the top fifth of earners took home more than half of all income, giving the U.S. one of the widest income distributions among OECD countries. There were about 771,480 [homeless persons in the U.S.](/wiki/Homelessness_in_the_United_States) in 2024. In 2022, 6.4 million children experienced food insecurity. [Feeding America](/wiki/Feeding_America) estimates that around one in five, or approximately 13 million, [children experience hunger in the U.S.](/wiki/Hunger_in_the_United_States#Children) and do not know where or when they will get their next meal. Also in 2022, about 37.9 million people, or 11.5% of the U.S. population, were [living in poverty](/wiki/Poverty_in_the_United_States).

The United States has a smaller [welfare state](/wiki/Welfare_state) and redistributes less income through government action than most other [high-income countries](/wiki/World_Bank_high-income_economy). It is the only [advanced economy](/wiki/Advanced_economy) that does not [guarantee its workers paid vacation](/wiki/List_of_statutory_minimum_employment_leave_by_country) nationally and one of a few countries in the world without federal [paid family leave](/wiki/Parental_leave_in_the_United_States) as a legal right. The United States has a higher percentage of low-income [workers](/wiki/Working_class_in_the_United_States) than almost any other developed country, largely because of a weak [collective bargaining](/wiki/Collective_bargaining) system and lack of government support for at-risk workers.

### Science and technology

Main articles: [Science and technology in the United States](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_the_United_States) and [Science policy of the United States](/wiki/Science_policy_of_the_United_States)

California's [Silicon Valley](/wiki/Silicon_Valley) is the largest and foremost technology and innovation hub in the world.

The United States [has been a leader in technological innovation since the late 19th century](/wiki/Technological_and_industrial_history_of_the_United_States) and scientific research since the mid-20th century. Methods for producing [interchangeable parts](/wiki/Interchangeable_parts) and the establishment of a [machine tool](/wiki/Machine_tool) industry enabled [the large-scale manufacturing](/wiki/American_system_of_manufacturing) of U.S. consumer products in the late 19th century. By the early 20th century, factory [electrification](/wiki/Electrification), the introduction of the [assembly line](/wiki/Assembly_line), and other [labor-saving techniques](/wiki/Automation) created the system of [mass production](/wiki/Mass_production).

In the 21st century, the United States continues to be one of the world's foremost scientific powers, though China has emerged as a major competitor in many fields. The U.S. has the [highest research and development expenditures of any country](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_by_research_and_development_spending) and ranks ninth as a percentage of GDP. In 2022, the United States was (after China) the country with the [second-highest number of published scientific papers](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_scientific_and_technical_journal_articles). In 2021, the U.S. ranked second (also after China) by the number of patent applications, and third by trademark and industrial design applications (after China and Germany), according to [World Intellectual Property Indicators](/wiki/World_Intellectual_Property_Indicators). In 2025 the United States ranked third (after Switzerland and Sweden) in the [Global Innovation Index](/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index). The United States is considered to be a world leader in the development of [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) technology. In 2023, the United States was ranked the second most technologically advanced country in the world (after South Korea) by *[Global Finance](/wiki/Global_Finance_(magazine))* magazine.

#### Spaceflight

Main article: [Space policy of the United States](/wiki/Space_policy_of_the_United_States)

Astronauts [Buzz Aldrin](/wiki/Buzz_Aldrin) and [Neil Armstrong](/wiki/Neil_Armstrong) (seen in visor reflection) during the 1969 [Apollo 11](/wiki/Apollo_11) mission, the first crewed [Moon landing](/wiki/Moon_landing). The United States is the only country to have landed humans on the Moon.

The United States has maintained a space program since the late 1950s, beginning with the establishment of the [National Aeronautics and Space Administration](/wiki/NASA) (NASA) in 1958. NASA's [Apollo program](/wiki/Apollo_program) (1961–1972) achieved the first crewed [Moon landing](/wiki/Moon_landing) with the 1969 [Apollo 11](/wiki/Apollo_11) mission; it remains one of the agency's most significant milestones. Other major endeavors by NASA include the [Space Shuttle program](/wiki/Space_Shuttle_program) (1981–2011), the [Voyager program](/wiki/Voyager_program) (1972–present), the [Hubble](/wiki/Hubble_Space_Telescope) and [James Webb](/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope) [space telescopes](/wiki/Space_telescope) (launched in 1990 and 2021, respectively), and the multi-mission [Mars Exploration Program](/wiki/Mars_Exploration_Program) (*[Spirit](/wiki/Spirit_(rover))*, *[Opportunity](/wiki/Opportunity_(rover))*, *[Curiosity](/wiki/Curiosity_(rover))*, and *[Perseverance](/wiki/Perseverance_(rover))*). NASA is one of five agencies collaborating on the [International Space Station](/wiki/International_Space_Station) (ISS); U.S. contributions to the ISS include several modules, including *[Destiny](/wiki/Destiny_(ISS_module))* (2001), *[Harmony](/wiki/Harmony_(ISS_module))* (2007), and *[Tranquility](/wiki/Tranquility_(ISS_module))* (2010), as well as ongoing logistical and operational support.

The United States [private sector](/wiki/Private_sector) dominates the global [commercial spaceflight industry](/wiki/Private_spaceflight). Prominent American spaceflight contractors include [Blue Origin](/wiki/Blue_Origin), [Boeing](/wiki/Boeing), [Lockheed Martin](/wiki/Lockheed_Martin), [Northrop Grumman](/wiki/Northrop_Grumman), and [SpaceX](/wiki/SpaceX). NASA programs such as the [Commercial Crew Program](/wiki/Commercial_Crew_Program), [Commercial Resupply Services](/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services), [Commercial Lunar Payload Services](/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services), and [NextSTEP](/wiki/Next_Space_Technologies_for_Exploration_Partnerships) have facilitated growing private-sector involvement in American spaceflight.

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in the United States](/wiki/Energy_in_the_United_States)

In 2023, the United States received approximately 84% of its energy from fossil fuel, and its largest source of energy was [petroleum](/wiki/Petroleum_in_the_United_States) (38%), followed by [natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas_in_the_United_States) (36%), [renewable sources](/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_the_United_States) (9%), [coal](/wiki/Coal_in_the_United_States) (9%), and [nuclear power](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_the_United_States) (9%). In 2022, the United States constituted about 4% of the [world's population](/wiki/World_population), but [consumed around 16%](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_energy_consumption) of the [world's energy](/wiki/World_energy_consumption). The U.S. ranks as the [second-highest emitter of greenhouse gases](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_greenhouse_gas_emissions) behind China.

The U.S. is the world's [largest producer of nuclear power](/wiki/Nuclear_power_by_country), generating around 30% of the world's nuclear electricity. It also has the highest number of nuclear power reactors of any country. From 2024, the U.S. plans to triple its nuclear power capacity by 2050.

### Transportation

Main article: [Transportation in the United States](/wiki/Transportation_in_the_United_States)

Interchange between [Interstate 10](/wiki/Interstate_10) and [Interstate 45](/wiki/Interstate_45) in [Houston](/wiki/Houston), Texas

The United States' 4 million miles (6.4 million kilometers) of road network, owned almost entirely by state and local governments, is the [longest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_road_network_size) in the world. The extensive [Interstate Highway System](/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System) that connects all major U.S. cities is funded mostly by the federal government but maintained by [state departments of transportation](/wiki/List_of_U.S._state_and_insular_area_departments_of_transportation). The system is further extended by [state highways](/wiki/State_highway#United_States) and some private [toll roads](/wiki/Toll_road).

The U.S. is among the top ten countries with the [highest vehicle ownership per capita](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_vehicles_per_capita) (850 vehicles per 1,000 people) in 2022. A 2022 study found that 76% of U.S. commuters drive alone and 14% ride a bicycle, including bike owners and users of [bike-sharing](/wiki/Bike-sharing) networks. About 11% use some form of public transportation.

[Public transportation in the United States](/wiki/Public_transportation_in_the_United_States) is well developed in the largest urban areas, notably New York City, Washington, D.C., Boston, Philadelphia, Chicago, and San Francisco; otherwise, coverage is generally less extensive than in most other developed countries. The U.S. also has many relatively [car-dependent](/wiki/Car-dependent) localities.

Long-distance intercity travel is provided primarily by airlines, but travel by rail is more common along the [Northeast Corridor](/wiki/Northeast_Corridor), the only [high-speed rail in the U.S.](/wiki/High-speed_rail_in_the_United_States) that meets international standards. [Amtrak](/wiki/Amtrak), the country's government-sponsored national passenger rail company, has a relatively sparse network compared to that of Western European countries. Service is concentrated in the Northeast, California, the Midwest, the Pacific Northwest, and Virginia/Southeast.

[Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport](/wiki/Hartsfield%E2%80%93Jackson_Atlanta_International_Airport), serving the [Atlanta metropolitan area](/wiki/Atlanta_metropolitan_area), is the world's [busiest airport by passenger traffic](/wiki/List_of_busiest_airports_by_passenger_traffic) with over 75 million passengers as of 2021.

The United States has an extensive air transportation network. [U.S. civilian airlines](/wiki/List_of_airlines_of_the_United_States) are all privately owned. The three largest airlines in the world, by total number of passengers carried, are U.S.-based; [American Airlines](/wiki/American_Airlines) became the global leader after its 2013 merger with [US Airways](/wiki/US_Airways). Of the 50 busiest airports in the world, 16 are in the United States, as well as five of the top 10. The world's busiest airport by passenger volume is [Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International](/wiki/Hartsfield%E2%80%93Jackson_Atlanta_International_Airport) in [Atlanta, Georgia](/wiki/Atlanta). In 2022, most of the [19,969 U.S. airports](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_the_United_States) were owned and operated by local government authorities, and there are also some private airports. Some 5,193 are designated as "public use", including for [general aviation](/wiki/General_aviation). The [Transportation Security Administration](/wiki/Transportation_Security_Administration) (TSA) has provided security at most major airports since 2001.

[The country's rail transport network](/wiki/Rail_transport_in_the_United_States), the [longest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_rail_transport_network_size) in the world at 182,412.3 mi (293,564.2 km), handles mostly [freight](/wiki/Freight_transport) (in contrast to more passenger-centered rail in Europe). Because they are often privately owned operations, U.S. railroads lag behind those of the rest of the world in terms of electrification.

[The country's inland waterways](/wiki/Inland_waterways_of_the_United_States) are the world's [fifth-longest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_waterways_length), totaling 25,482 mi (41,009 km). They are used extensively for freight, recreation, and a small amount of passenger traffic. Of the world's [50 busiest container ports](/wiki/List_of_busiest_container_ports), four are located in the United States, with the busiest in the country being the [Port of Los Angeles](/wiki/Port_of_Los_Angeles).

### Urbanization

Main articles: [Urbanization in the United States](/wiki/Urbanization_in_the_United_States) and [List of United States cities by population](/wiki/List_of_United_States_cities_by_population)

Most Americans live in [suburbs](/wiki/Suburb) and [exurbs](/wiki/Exurb), such as [Monroe Township](/wiki/Monroe_Township,_Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey), [New Jersey](/wiki/New_Jersey), in the [New York metropolitan area](/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area).

About 82% of Americans live in [metropolitan areas](/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area), particularly in [suburbs](/wiki/Suburb) and outer-ring [exurbs](/wiki/Exurb); about half of those reside in cities with populations over 50,000. In 2024, 346 [incorporated U.S. municipalities](/wiki/List_of_United_States_cities_by_population#50_states_and_District_of_Columbia) had populations over 100,000, 11 cities had more than one million residents, and four cities—[New York City](/wiki/New_York_City), [Los Angeles](/wiki/Los_Angeles), [Chicago](/wiki/Chicago), and [Houston](/wiki/Houston)—had populations exceeding two million. Some 56 U.S. metropolitan areas [have one million or more residents](/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_areas#United_States).

More recently, the fastest-growing metropolitan areas were in the South, while southern metros along the Mexican border and Gulf Coast metros susceptible to hurricanes declined the most in 2025. The [New York metro area](/wiki/New_York_metro_area), which gained the most new residents in 2024, fell to 13th in 2025, due to a fall in immigrants. The top metro areas with rising populations in 2025 were [Houston](/wiki/Houston_metropolitan_area) and [Dallas–Fort Worth](/wiki/Dallas_Metroplex), followed by [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta_metropolitan_area), [Phoenix](/wiki/Phoenix_metropolitan_area) and [Charlotte](/wiki/Charlotte_metropolitan_area).

### Language

Main article: [Languages of the United States](/wiki/Languages_of_the_United_States)

Most spoken languages in the U.S.

While many languages and [dialects](/wiki/Dialects) are spoken in the United States, [English](/wiki/American_English) is by far the most commonly spoken and written. *[De facto](/wiki/De_facto)*, English is the [official language](/wiki/Official_language) of the United States, and in 2025, [Executive Order 14224](/wiki/Executive_Order_14224) declared English official. However, the U.S. has never had a statutory official language, as Congress has never passed a law to designate English as official for all three federal branches. Some laws, such as [U.S. naturalization requirements](/wiki/Naturalized_citizen_of_the_United_States), nonetheless standardize English. Twenty-eight states and the [United States Virgin Islands](/wiki/United_States_Virgin_Islands) have laws that designate English as the sole official language; 19 states and the [District of Columbia](/wiki/District_of_Columbia) have no official language. Three states and four U.S. territories have recognized local or indigenous languages in addition to English: Hawaii ([Hawaiian](/wiki/Hawaiian_language)), Alaska ([twenty Native languages](/wiki/Alaska_Native_languages)), South Dakota ([Sioux](/wiki/Sioux_language)), American Samoa ([Samoan](/wiki/Samoan_language)), Puerto Rico ([Spanish](/wiki/Puerto_Rican_Spanish)), Guam ([Chamorro](/wiki/Chamorro_language)), and the Northern Mariana Islands ([Carolinian](/wiki/Carolinian_language) and Chamorro). In total, 169 Native American languages are spoken in the United States. In Puerto Rico, Spanish is more widely spoken than English.

According to the [American Community Survey](/wiki/American_Community_Survey) (2020), some 245.4 million people in the U.S. age five and older spoke only English at home. About 41.2 million spoke [Spanish](/wiki/Spanish_language_in_the_United_States) at home, making it the second most commonly used language. Other languages spoken at home by one million people or more include [Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_language_in_the_United_States) (3.40 million), [Tagalog](/wiki/Tagalog_language_in_the_United_States) (1.71 million), [Vietnamese](/wiki/Vietnamese_language_in_the_United_States) (1.52 million), [Arabic](/wiki/Arabic_language_in_the_United_States) (1.39 million), [French](/wiki/French_language_in_the_United_States) (1.18 million), [Korean](/wiki/Korean_language_in_the_United_States) (1.07 million), and [Russian](/wiki/Russian_language_in_the_United_States) (1.04 million). [German](/wiki/German_language_in_the_United_States), spoken by 1 million people at home in 2010, fell to 881,000 estimated total speakers in 2020.

### Immigration

Main articles: [Immigration to the United States](/wiki/Immigration_to_the_United_States) and [History of immigration to the United States](/wiki/History_of_immigration_to_the_United_States)

The [Mexico–United States border wall](/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall) between [San Diego](/wiki/San_Diego) (left) and [Tijuana](/wiki/Tijuana) (right)

America's immigrant population is by far the world's [largest in absolute terms](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_by_immigrant_and_emigrant_population). In 2022, there were 87.7 million immigrants and [U.S.-born children of immigrants](/wiki/Second-generation_immigrants_in_the_United_States) in the United States, accounting for nearly 27% of the overall U.S. population. In 2017, out of the U.S. foreign-born population, some 45% (20.7 million) were naturalized citizens, 27% (12.3 million) were lawful permanent residents, 6% (2.2 million) were temporary lawful residents, and 23% (10.5 million) were [unauthorized immigrants](/wiki/Undocumented_immigrant_population_of_the_United_States). In 2019, the top countries of origin for immigrants were Mexico (24% of immigrants), India (6%), China (5%), the Philippines (4.5%), and El Salvador (3%). In fiscal year 2022, over one million immigrants (most of whom entered through [family reunification](/wiki/Chain_migration#Legislation)) were granted [legal residence](/wiki/Permanent_residence_(United_States)). The undocumented immigrant population in the U.S. reached a record high of 14 million in 2023.

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in the United States](/wiki/Religion_in_the_United_States)

Religious affiliation in the U.S., according to a 2025 [Gallup](/wiki/Gallup,_Inc.) poll:

1. [Protestantism](/wiki/Protestantism_in_the_United_States) (32%)
2. [Catholicism](/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_the_United_States) (20%)
3. [Non-specific Christian](/wiki/Christianity_in_the_United_States) (12%)
4. [Mormonism](/wiki/Mormonism_in_the_United_States) (1%)
5. [Judaism](/wiki/Judaism_in_the_United_States) (2%)
6. Other religion (6%)
7. [Unaffiliated](/wiki/Irreligion_in_the_United_States) (24%)
8. Unanswered (3%)

The [First Amendment](/wiki/First_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) guarantees the [free exercise of religion in the country](/wiki/Free_Exercise_Clause) and forbids Congress from passing laws respecting [its establishment](/wiki/Establishment_Clause). Religious practice is widespread, among the [most diverse](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_ethnic_and_cultural_diversity_level) in the world, and profoundly vibrant.

The country has the world's [largest Christian population](/wiki/Christianity_by_country), which includes the [fourth-largest population of Catholics](/wiki/Catholic_Church_by_country). Other notable faiths include [Judaism](/wiki/Judaism), [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism), [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism), [Islam](/wiki/Islam), [New Age](/wiki/New_Age), and [Native American religions](/wiki/Native_American_religions). Religious practice varies significantly by region. "[Ceremonial deism](/wiki/Ceremonial_deism)" is common in American culture.

The overwhelming majority of [Americans](/wiki/Americans) believe in a [higher power](/wiki/Higher_Power) or spiritual force, engage in [spiritual practices](/wiki/Spiritual_practice) such as prayer, and consider themselves religious or [spiritual](/wiki/Spirituality). In the Southern United States' "[Bible Belt](/wiki/Bible_Belt)", [evangelical Protestantism](/wiki/Evangelicalism) plays a significant role culturally; [New England](/wiki/New_England) and the Western United States [tend to be more secular](/wiki/Unchurched_Belt). [Mormonism](/wiki/Mormonism), a [Restorationist](/wiki/Restorationism) movement founded in the U.S. in 1847, is the predominant religion in Utah and a major religion in Idaho.

### Health

Main article: [Health in the United States](/wiki/Health_in_the_United_States)

Further information: [Healthcare in the United States](/wiki/Healthcare_in_the_United_States) and [Health insurance in the United States](/wiki/Health_insurance_in_the_United_States)

See also: [History of public health in the United States](/wiki/History_of_public_health_in_the_United_States) and [History of medicine in the United States](/wiki/History_of_medicine_in_the_United_States)

The [Texas Medical Center](/wiki/Texas_Medical_Center) in [Houston](/wiki/Houston) is the largest medical complex in the world. In 2018, it employed 120,000 people and treated 10 million patients.

According to the [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](/wiki/Centers_for_Disease_Control_and_Prevention) (CDC), average U.S. life expectancy at birth reached 79.0 years in 2024, its highest recorded level. This was an increase of 0.6 years over 2023. The CDC attributed the improvement to a significant fall in the number of fatal drug overdoses in the country, noting that "heart disease continues to be the leading cause of death in the United States, followed by cancer and unintentional injuries." In 2024, life expectancy at birth for American men rose to 76.5 years (+0.7 years compared to 2023), while life expectancy for women was 81.4 years (+0.3 years). Starting in 1998, life expectancy in the U.S. fell [behind that of other wealthy industrialized countries](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_life_expectancy), and Americans' "health disadvantage" gap has been increasing ever since.

The Commonwealth Fund reported in 2020 that the U.S. had the [highest suicide rate](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_suicide_rate) among [high-income countries](/wiki/High-income_countries). [Approximately one-third of the U.S. adult population is obese](/wiki/Obesity_in_the_United_States), and another third is overweight. The U.S. healthcare system far [outspends that of any other country](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_total_health_expenditure_(PPP)_per_capita), measured both in per capita spending and as a percentage of GDP, but attains worse healthcare outcomes when compared to peer countries for reasons that are debated. The United States is the only developed country [without a system of universal healthcare](/wiki/Healthcare_reform_in_the_United_States), and [a significant proportion of the population that does not carry health insurance](/wiki/Health_insurance_coverage_in_the_United_States). Government-funded healthcare coverage for the poor ([Medicaid](/wiki/Medicaid)) and for those age 65 and older ([Medicare](/wiki/Medicare_(United_States))) is available to Americans who meet the programs' income or age qualifications. In 2010, President [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama) passed the [Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act](/wiki/Patient_Protection_and_Affordable_Care_Act). Since the 2022 U.S. Supreme Court decision *[Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization](/wiki/Dobbs_v._Jackson_Women%27s_Health_Organization)*, which effectively overruled *Roe v. Wade* (1973), [abortion in the United States](/wiki/Abortion_in_the_United_States) is not federally protected. It is now either illegal or restricted in 17 of the 50 states.

### Education

Main article: [Education in the United States](/wiki/Education_in_the_United_States)

Some 77% of American college students attend public institutions such as the [University of Virginia](/wiki/University_of_Virginia), founded by [Thomas Jefferson](/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson) in 1819.

American primary and secondary education, known in the U.S. as [K–12](/wiki/K%E2%80%9312_education_in_the_United_States) ("kindergarten through 12th grade"), is decentralized. School systems are operated by state, territorial, and sometimes municipal governments and regulated by the [U.S. Department of Education](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Education). In general, children are required to attend school or [an approved homeschool](/wiki/Homeschooling_in_the_United_States) from the age of five or six ([kindergarten](/wiki/Kindergarten) or [first grade](/wiki/First_grade)) until they are 18 years old. This often brings students through the [12th grade](/wiki/Twelfth_grade), the final year of a U.S. high school, but some states and territories allow them to leave school earlier, at age 16 or 17. The U.S. spends more on education per student than any other country, an average of $18,614 per year per public elementary and secondary school student in 2020–2021. Among Americans age 25 and older, 92.2% graduated from high school, 62.7% attended some college, 37.7% earned a [bachelor's degree](/wiki/Bachelor%27s_degree), and 14.2% earned a graduate degree. The [U.S. literacy rate](/wiki/Literacy_in_the_United_States) is near-universal. The U.S. has produced the [most Nobel Prize winners of any country](/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates_by_country), with [411](/wiki/List_of_American_Nobel_laureates) (having won 413 awards).

[U.S. tertiary or higher education](/wiki/Higher_education_in_the_United_States) has earned a global reputation. Many of the world's top universities, as listed by various ranking organizations, are in the United States, including 19 of the top 25. American higher education is dominated by [state university systems](/wiki/State_university_system), although [the country's many private universities and colleges](/wiki/Private_universities_in_the_United_States) enroll about 20% of all American students. Local [community colleges](/wiki/Community_colleges_in_the_United_States) generally offer [open admissions](/wiki/Open_admissions), lower tuition, and coursework leading to a two-year [associate degree](/wiki/Associate_degree) or a [non-degree certificate](/wiki/Certificate_program).

As for [public expenditures](/wiki/Public_expenditure) on higher education, the U.S. spends more per student than the [OECD](/wiki/OECD) average, and Americans spend more than all nations in combined public and private spending. Colleges and universities directly funded by the federal government do not charge tuition and are limited to military personnel and government employees, including: the [U.S. service academies](/wiki/United_States_service_academies), the [Naval Postgraduate School](/wiki/Naval_Postgraduate_School), and [military staff colleges](/wiki/US_military_staff_colleges). Despite some student [loan forgiveness](/wiki/Loan_forgiveness) programs in place, [student loan debt](/wiki/Student_debt) increased by 102% between 2010 and 2020, and exceeded $1.7 trillion in 2022.

## Culture and society

Main articles: [Culture of the United States](/wiki/Culture_of_the_United_States) and [Society of the United States](/wiki/Society_of_the_United_States)

See also: [Human rights in the United States](/wiki/Human_rights_in_the_United_States)

The [Statue of Liberty](/wiki/Statue_of_Liberty) (*Liberty Enlightening the World*) on [Liberty Island](/wiki/Liberty_Island) in [New York Harbor](/wiki/New_York_Harbor) was an 1886 gift from France that has become an iconic symbol of the [American Dream](/wiki/American_Dream).

The United States is home to [a wide variety of ethnic groups, traditions, and customs](/wiki/Multiculturalism_in_the_United_States). The country has been described as having [the values](/wiki/Americanism_(ideology)) of [individualism](/wiki/Individualism) and [personal autonomy](/wiki/Left-libertarianism), as well as a strong [work ethic](/wiki/Work_ethic) and [competitiveness](/wiki/Competition). Voluntary [altruism](/wiki/Altruism) toward others also plays a major role; according to a 2016 study by the [Charities Aid Foundation](/wiki/Charities_Aid_Foundation), Americans donated 1.44% of total GDP to charity—the [highest rate](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_charitable_donation) in the world by a large margin. Americans have traditionally [been characterized](/wiki/Stereotypes_of_Americans) by a unifying political belief in an "[American Creed](/wiki/American_Creed)" emphasizing [consent of the governed](/wiki/Consent_of_the_governed), [liberty](/wiki/Liberty), [equality under the law](/wiki/Equality_under_the_law), [democracy](/wiki/Democracy), [social equality](/wiki/Social_equality), [property rights](/wiki/Property_rights), and a preference for [limited government](/wiki/Limited_government). The U.S. has acquired significant [hard](/wiki/Hard_power) and [soft power](/wiki/Soft_power) through [its diplomatic influence](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_the_United_States), [economic power](/wiki/Economic_power), [military alliances](/wiki/Military_alliance), and [cultural exports](/wiki/Cultural_exports) such as [American movies](/wiki/Cinema_of_the_United_States), [music](/wiki/Music_of_the_United_States), [video games](/wiki/Video_games_in_the_United_States), [sports](/wiki/Sports_in_the_United_States), and [food](/wiki/American_cuisine). The influence that the United States exerts on other countries through soft power is referred to as [Americanization](/wiki/Americanization).

Nearly all present Americans or their ancestors came from [Europe, Africa, or Asia](/wiki/Afro-Eurasia) (the "[Old World](/wiki/Old_World)") within the past five centuries. [Mainstream](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/mainstream) American culture is a [Western culture](/wiki/Western_culture) largely derived from the [traditions of European immigrants](/wiki/European_American#Culture) with influences from many other sources, such as [traditions brought by slaves from Africa](/wiki/African-American_culture). More recent immigration from [Asia](/wiki/Asian_American) and especially [Latin America](/wiki/Latin_American_culture) has added to a cultural mix that has been described as a homogenizing [melting pot](/wiki/Melting_pot), and a heterogeneous [salad bowl](/wiki/Salad_bowl_(cultural_idea)), with immigrants contributing to, and often [assimilating](/wiki/Assimilation_(phonology)) into, mainstream American culture.

Under the [First Amendment to the Constitution](/wiki/First_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution), the United States is considered to have the [strongest protections of free speech of any country](/wiki/Freedom_of_speech_in_the_United_States). [Flag desecration](/wiki/Flag_desecration_in_the_United_States), [hate speech](/wiki/Hate_speech_in_the_United_States), [blasphemy](/wiki/Blasphemy_law_in_the_United_States), and [lese majesty](/wiki/L%C3%A8se-majest%C3%A9) are all forms of protected expression. A 2016 [Pew Research Center](/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) poll found that Americans were the most supportive of free expression of any polity measured. Additionally, they are the "most supportive of [freedom of the press](/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press_in_the_United_States) and the [right to use the Internet](/wiki/Right_to_Internet_access) without government censorship". The U.S. is a [socially progressive](/wiki/Cultural_liberalism) country with [permissive](/wiki/Permissive_society) attitudes surrounding [human sexuality](/wiki/Human_sexuality). [LGBTQ rights in the United States](/wiki/LGBTQ_rights_in_the_United_States) are among the most advanced by global standards.

The [American Dream](/wiki/American_Dream), or the perception that Americans enjoy high levels of [social mobility](/wiki/Socio-economic_mobility_in_the_United_States), plays a key role in attracting immigrants. Whether this perception is accurate has been a topic of debate. While mainstream culture holds that the United States is a [classless society](/wiki/Classless_society), scholars identify significant differences between [the country's social classes](/wiki/Social_class_in_the_United_States), affecting [socialization](/wiki/Socialization), language, and values. Americans tend to greatly value [socioeconomic](/wiki/Socioeconomics) achievement, but [being ordinary or average](/wiki/Average_Joe) is promoted by some as a noble condition as well.

The [National Foundation on the Arts and the Humanities](/wiki/National_Foundation_on_the_Arts_and_the_Humanities) is an agency of the United States federal government that was established in 1965 with the purpose to "develop and promote a broadly conceived national policy of support for the humanities and the arts in the United States, and for institutions which preserve the cultural heritage of the United States." It is composed of four sub-agencies:

- [National Endowment for the Arts](/wiki/National_Endowment_for_the_Arts)
- [National Endowment for the Humanities](/wiki/National_Endowment_for_the_Humanities)
- [Institute of Museum and Library Services](/wiki/Institute_of_Museum_and_Library_Services)
- [Federal Council on the Arts and the Humanities](/wiki/Federal_Council_on_the_Arts_and_the_Humanities)

### Literature

Main article: [American literature](/wiki/American_literature)

See also: [American philosophy](/wiki/American_philosophy)

[Mark Twain](/wiki/Mark_Twain), whom [William Faulkner](/wiki/William_Faulkner) called "the father of American literature"

Colonial American authors were influenced by [John Locke](/wiki/John_Locke) and other [Enlightenment](/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment) philosophers. The [American Revolutionary Period](/wiki/American_Revolution) (1765–1783) is notable for the political writings of [Benjamin Franklin](/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin), [Alexander Hamilton](/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton), [Thomas Paine](/wiki/Thomas_Paine), and [Thomas Jefferson](/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson). Shortly before and after the [Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), the newspaper rose to prominence, filling a demand for anti-British national literature. An early novel is [William Hill Brown](/wiki/William_Hill_Brown)'s *[The Power of Sympathy](/wiki/The_Power_of_Sympathy)*, published in 1791. Writer and critic [John Neal](/wiki/John_Neal) in the early- to mid-19th century helped advance America toward a unique literature and culture by criticizing predecessors such as [Washington Irving](/wiki/Washington_Irving) for imitating their British counterparts, and by influencing writers such as [Edgar Allan Poe](/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe), who took American poetry and short fiction in new directions. [Ralph Waldo Emerson](/wiki/Ralph_Waldo_Emerson) and [Margaret Fuller](/wiki/Margaret_Fuller) pioneered the influential [Transcendentalism](/wiki/Transcendentalism) movement; [Henry David Thoreau](/wiki/Henry_David_Thoreau), author of *[Walden](/wiki/Walden)*, was influenced by this movement.

The conflict surrounding [abolitionism](/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_States) inspired writers, like [Harriet Beecher Stowe](/wiki/Harriet_Beecher_Stowe), and authors of slave narratives, such as [Frederick Douglass](/wiki/Frederick_Douglass). [Nathaniel Hawthorne](/wiki/Nathaniel_Hawthorne)'s *[The Scarlet Letter](/wiki/The_Scarlet_Letter)* (1850) explored the dark side of American history, as did [Herman Melville](/wiki/Herman_Melville)'s *[Moby-Dick](/wiki/Moby-Dick)* (1851). Major American poets of the 19th century [American Renaissance](/wiki/American_Renaissance_(literature)) include [Walt Whitman](/wiki/Walt_Whitman), Melville, and [Emily Dickinson](/wiki/Emily_Dickinson). [Mark Twain](/wiki/Mark_Twain) was the first major American writer to be born in the West. [Henry James](/wiki/Henry_James) achieved international recognition with novels like *[The Portrait of a Lady](/wiki/The_Portrait_of_a_Lady)* (1881). As literacy rates rose, periodicals published more stories centered around industrial workers, women, and the rural poor. [Naturalism](/wiki/Naturalism_(literature)), [regionalism](/wiki/American_literary_regionalism), and [realism](/wiki/Literary_realism#UnitedStates) were the major literary movements of the period.

While [modernism](/wiki/Literary_modernism) generally took on an international character, modernist authors working within the United States more often rooted their work in specific regions, peoples, and cultures. Following the Great Migration to northern cities, African-American and black [West Indian](/wiki/West_Indian_Americans) authors of the [Harlem Renaissance](/wiki/Harlem_Renaissance) developed an independent tradition of literature that rebuked a history of inequality and celebrated black culture. An important cultural export during the [Jazz Age](/wiki/Jazz_Age), these writings were a key influence on *[Négritude](/wiki/N%C3%A9gritude)*, a philosophy emerging in the 1930s among francophone writers of the [African diaspora](/wiki/African_diaspora). In the 1950s, an ideal of homogeneity led many authors to attempt to write the [Great American Novel](/wiki/Great_American_Novel), while the [Beat Generation](/wiki/Beat_Generation) rejected this conformity, using styles that elevated the impact of the [spoken word](/wiki/Spoken_word) over mechanics to describe drug use, sexuality, and the failings of society. Contemporary literature is more pluralistic than in previous eras, with the closest thing to a unifying feature being a trend toward self-conscious [experiments with language](/wiki/Experimental_literature). Twelve American laureates have won the [Nobel Prize in Literature](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature).

### Mass media

Main article: [Mass media in the United States](/wiki/Mass_media_in_the_United_States)

See also: [Newspapers in the United States](/wiki/Newspapers_in_the_United_States), [Television in the United States](/wiki/Television_in_the_United_States), [Broadcasting in the United States](/wiki/Broadcasting_in_the_United_States), [Public broadcasting in the United States](/wiki/Public_broadcasting_in_the_United_States), [Internet in the United States](/wiki/Internet_in_the_United_States), [Radio in the United States](/wiki/Radio_in_the_United_States), and [Video games in the United States](/wiki/Video_games_in_the_United_States)

[Comcast Center](/wiki/Comcast_Center) in [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia), headquarters of [Comcast](/wiki/Comcast), one of the world's [largest telecommunications companies](/wiki/List_of_telecommunications_companies) and media conglomerates

Media in the United States is [broadly uncensored](/wiki/Censorship_in_the_United_States), with the [First Amendment](/wiki/First_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) providing significant protections, as reiterated in *[New York Times Co. v. United States](/wiki/New_York_Times_Co._v._United_States)*. The four major broadcasters in the U.S. are the [National Broadcasting Company](/wiki/National_Broadcasting_Company) (NBC), [Columbia Broadcasting System](/wiki/Columbia_Broadcasting_System) (CBS), [American Broadcasting Company](/wiki/American_Broadcasting_Company) (ABC), and [Fox Broadcasting Company](/wiki/Fox_Broadcasting_Company) (Fox). The four major broadcast television networks are all commercial entities. The [Public Broadcasting Service](/wiki/PBS) (PBS) is the country's major non-commercial [public broadcast network](/wiki/Public_broadcaster); it also provides [educational programming](/wiki/Educational_television) through local PBS stations. The [U.S. cable television system](/wiki/Cable_television_in_the_United_States) offers hundreds of channels catering to a variety of niches. In 2021, about 83% of Americans over age 12 listened to [broadcast radio](/wiki/Radio_broadcasting), while about 40% listened to [podcasts](/wiki/Podcast). In the prior year, there were 15,460 licensed full-power radio stations in the U.S. according to the [Federal Communications Commission](/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission) (FCC). Public radio broadcasting is largely supplied by [National Public Radio](/wiki/National_Public_Radio) (NPR), incorporated in February 1970 under the [Public Broadcasting Act of 1967](/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_Act_of_1967).

U.S. newspapers with a global reach and reputation include *[The Wall Street Journal](/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal)*, *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*, *[The Washington Post](/wiki/The_Washington_Post)*, and *[USA Today](/wiki/USA_Today)*. [About 800 publications](/wiki/List_of_Spanish-language_newspapers_published_in_the_United_States) are produced in Spanish. With few exceptions, newspapers are privately owned, either by large chains such as [Gannett](/wiki/Gannett_Company) or [McClatchy](/wiki/The_McClatchy_Company), which own dozens or even hundreds of newspapers; by small chains that own a handful of papers; or, in an increasingly rare situation, by individuals or families. Major cities often have [alternative newspapers](/wiki/Alternative_newspaper) to complement the mainstream daily papers, such as *[The Village Voice](/wiki/The_Village_Voice)* in New York City and *[LA Weekly](/wiki/LA_Weekly)* in Los Angeles. The five [most-visited websites](/wiki/List_of_most-visited_websites) in the world are [Google](/wiki/Google_Search), [YouTube](/wiki/YouTube), [Facebook](/wiki/Facebook), [Instagram](/wiki/Instagram), and [ChatGPT](/wiki/ChatGPT)—all of them American-owned. Other popular platforms used include [X](/wiki/X_(social_network)) (formerly Twitter) and [Amazon](/wiki/Amazon_(company)).

In 2025, the U.S. was the world's [second-largest video game market by revenue](/wiki/List_of_video_games_markets_by_country) (after China). In 2015, the U.S. video game industry consisted of 2,457 companies that employed around 220,000 jobs and generated $30.4 billion in revenue. There are 444 game publishers, developers, and hardware companies in California alone. According to the [Game Developers Conference](/wiki/Game_Developers_Conference) (GDC), the U.S. is the top location for [video game development](/wiki/Video_game_development), with 58% of the world's [game developers](/wiki/Video_game_developer) based there in 2025.

### Theater

Main article: [Theater in the United States](/wiki/Theater_in_the_United_States)

[Broadway theaters](/wiki/Broadway_theater) in [Theater District, Manhattan](/wiki/Theater_District,_Manhattan)

The United States is well known for its theater. Mainstream theater in the United States derives from the old European theatrical tradition and has been heavily influenced by the [British theater](/wiki/Theatre_of_the_United_Kingdom). By the middle of the 19th century, America had created new distinct dramatic forms in the [Tom Shows](/wiki/Tom_Shows), the [showboat theater](/wiki/Showboat) and the [minstrel show](/wiki/Minstrel_show). The central hub of the American theater scene is the [Theater District in Manhattan](/wiki/Theater_District,_Manhattan), with its divisions of [Broadway](/wiki/Broadway_theatre), [off-Broadway](/wiki/Off-Broadway), and [off-off-Broadway](/wiki/Off-off-Broadway).

Many movie and television [celebrities](/wiki/Celebrity) have gotten their big break working in New York productions. Outside New York City, many cities have professional [regional or resident theater companies](/wiki/Regional_theater_in_the_United_States) that produce their own seasons. The biggest-budget theatrical productions are musicals. U.S. theater has an active [community theater](/wiki/Community_theater) culture.

The [Tony Awards](/wiki/Tony_Awards) recognize excellence in live Broadway theater and are presented at an annual ceremony in [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan). The awards are given for Broadway productions and performances. One is also given for [regional theater](/wiki/Regional_theatre_in_the_United_States).

### Visual arts

Main articles: [Visual art of the United States](/wiki/Visual_art_of_the_United_States) and [Architecture of the United States](/wiki/Architecture_of_the_United_States)

*[American Gothic](/wiki/American_Gothic)* (1930) by [Grant Wood](/wiki/Grant_Wood) is one of the most famous [American paintings](/wiki/Visual_art_of_the_United_States) and is widely [parodied](/wiki/Parody).

[Folk art](/wiki/Folk_art_of_the_United_States) in [colonial America](/wiki/Colonial_america) grew out of artisanal [craftsmanship](/wiki/Workmanship) in communities that allowed commonly trained people to individually express themselves. It was distinct from Europe's tradition of [high art](/wiki/High_culture), which was less accessible and generally less relevant to early American settlers. Cultural movements in art and craftsmanship in colonial America generally lagged behind those of Western Europe. For example, the prevailing medieval style of [woodworking](/wiki/Woodworking) and primitive [sculpture](/wiki/Sculpture_of_the_United_States) became integral to early American folk art, despite the emergence of [Renaissance styles](/wiki/Renaissance_art) in England in the late 16th and early 17th centuries. The new English styles would have been early enough to make a considerable impact on American folk art, but American styles and forms had already been firmly adopted. Not only did styles change slowly in early America, but there was a tendency for rural artisans there to continue their traditional forms longer than their urban counterparts did—and far longer than those in Western Europe.

The [Hudson River School](/wiki/Hudson_River_School) was a mid-19th-century movement in the visual arts tradition of European [naturalism](/wiki/Realism_(arts)). The 1913 [Armory Show](/wiki/Armory_Show) in New York City, an exhibition of European [modernist art](/wiki/Modern_art), shocked the public and transformed the U.S. art scene.

[American Realism](/wiki/American_Realism) and [American Regionalism](/wiki/American_Regionalism) sought to reflect and give America new ways of looking at itself. [Georgia O'Keeffe](/wiki/Georgia_O%27Keeffe), [Marsden Hartley](/wiki/Marsden_Hartley), and others experimented with new and individualistic styles, which would become known as [American modernism](/wiki/American_modernism). Major artistic movements such as the [abstract expressionism](/wiki/Abstract_expressionism) of [Jackson Pollock](/wiki/Jackson_Pollock) and [Willem de Kooning](/wiki/Willem_de_Kooning) and the [pop art](/wiki/Pop_art) of [Andy Warhol](/wiki/Andy_Warhol) and [Roy Lichtenstein](/wiki/Roy_Lichtenstein) developed largely in the United States. Major photographers include [Alfred Stieglitz](/wiki/Alfred_Stieglitz), [Edward Steichen](/wiki/Edward_Steichen), [Dorothea Lange](/wiki/Dorothea_Lange), [Edward Weston](/wiki/Edward_Weston), [James Van Der Zee](/wiki/James_Van_Der_Zee), [Ansel Adams](/wiki/Ansel_Adams), and [Gordon Parks](/wiki/Gordon_Parks).

The tide of [modernism](/wiki/Modernism) and then [postmodernism](/wiki/Postmodernism) has brought global fame to American architects, including [Frank Lloyd Wright](/wiki/Frank_Lloyd_Wright), [Philip Johnson](/wiki/Philip_Johnson), and [Frank Gehry](/wiki/Frank_Gehry). The [Metropolitan Museum of Art](/wiki/Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art) in [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan) is the largest [art museum](/wiki/Art_museum) in the United States and the [fourth-largest](/wiki/List_of_largest_art_museums) in the world.

### Music

Main article: [Music of the United States](/wiki/Music_of_the_United_States)

The [Country Music Hall of Fame and Museum](/wiki/Country_Music_Hall_of_Fame_and_Museum) in [Nashville, Tennessee](/wiki/Nashville,_Tennessee)

[American folk music](/wiki/American_folk_music) encompasses numerous music genres, variously known as traditional music, traditional [folk music](/wiki/Folk_music), contemporary folk music, or roots music. Many traditional songs have been sung within the same family or folk group for generations, and sometimes trace back to such origins as the [British Isles](/wiki/British_Isles), [mainland Europe](/wiki/Mainland_Europe), or [Africa](/wiki/African-American_music). The rhythmic and lyrical styles of African-American music in particular have influenced American music. [Banjos](/wiki/Banjo) were brought to America through the slave trade. [Minstrel shows](/wiki/Minstrel_show) incorporating the instrument into their acts led to its increased popularity and widespread production in the 19th century. The [electric guitar](/wiki/Electric_guitar), first invented in the 1930s, and mass-produced by the 1940s, had an enormous influence on popular music, in particular due to the development of [rock and roll](/wiki/Rock_and_roll). The [synthesizer](/wiki/Synthesizer), [turntablism](/wiki/Turntablism), and [electronic music](/wiki/Electronic_music) were also largely developed in the U.S.

Elements from folk idioms such as the [blues](/wiki/Blues) and [old-time music](/wiki/Old-time_music) were adopted and transformed into [popular genres](/wiki/Popular_music) with global audiences. [Jazz](/wiki/Jazz) grew from blues and [ragtime](/wiki/Ragtime) in the early 20th century, developing from the innovations and recordings of composers such as [W.C. Handy](/wiki/W.C._Handy) and [Jelly Roll Morton](/wiki/Jelly_Roll_Morton). [Louis Armstrong](/wiki/Louis_Armstrong) and [Duke Ellington](/wiki/Duke_Ellington) increased its popularity early in the 20th century. [Country music](/wiki/Country_music) developed in the 1920s, [bluegrass](/wiki/Bluegrass_music) and [rhythm and blues](/wiki/Rhythm_and_blues) in the 1940s, and rock and roll in the 1950s. In the 1960s, [Bob Dylan](/wiki/Bob_Dylan) emerged from the [folk revival](/wiki/American_folk_music_revival) to become one of the country's most celebrated songwriters. The musical forms of [punk](/wiki/Punk_rock) and [hip hop](/wiki/Hip_hop) both originated in the United States in the 1970s.

The United States has the world's [largest music market](/wiki/List_of_largest_recorded_music_markets), with a total retail value of $15.9 billion in 2022. Most of the world's [major record companies](/wiki/Record_label#Major_labels) are based in the U.S.; they are represented by the [Recording Industry Association of America](/wiki/Recording_Industry_Association_of_America) (RIAA). Mid-20th-century American pop stars, such as [Frank Sinatra](/wiki/Frank_Sinatra) and [Elvis Presley](/wiki/Elvis_Presley), became [global celebrities](/wiki/Superstar) and [best-selling music artists](/wiki/List_of_best-selling_music_artists), as have artists of the late 20th century, such as [Michael Jackson](/wiki/Michael_Jackson), [Madonna](/wiki/Madonna), [Whitney Houston](/wiki/Whitney_Houston), and [Mariah Carey](/wiki/Mariah_Carey), and of the early 21st century, such as [Eminem](/wiki/Eminem), [Britney Spears](/wiki/Britney_Spears), [Lady Gaga](/wiki/Lady_Gaga), [Katy Perry](/wiki/Katy_Perry), [Taylor Swift](/wiki/Taylor_Swift) and [Beyoncé](/wiki/Beyonc%C3%A9).

### Fashion

Main article: [Fashion in the United States](/wiki/Fashion_in_the_United_States)

*[Haute couture](/wiki/Haute_couture)* [fashion models](/wiki/Fashion_model) on the [catwalk](/wiki/Catwalk) during [New York Fashion Week](/wiki/New_York_Fashion_Week)

The United States has the world's largest [apparel](/wiki/Apparel) market by revenue. Apart from professional [business attire](/wiki/Business_attire), American fashion is eclectic and predominantly informal. Americans' diverse cultural roots are reflected in their clothing; however, [sneakers](/wiki/Sneaker), [jeans](/wiki/Jeans), [T-shirts](/wiki/T-shirts), and [baseball caps](/wiki/Baseball_cap) are emblematic of American styles. New York, with [its Fashion Week](/wiki/New_York_Fashion_Week), is considered to be one of the "Big Four" global [fashion capitals](/wiki/Fashion_capital), along with [Paris](/wiki/Paris_Fashion_Week), [Milan](/wiki/Milan_Fashion_Week), and [London](/wiki/London_Fashion_Week). A study demonstrated that general proximity to [Manhattan's Garment District](/wiki/Garment_District,_Manhattan) has been synonymous with American fashion since its inception in the early 20th century.

A number of well-known [designer labels](/wiki/Designer_label), among them [Tommy Hilfiger](/wiki/Tommy_Hilfiger_(company)), [Ralph Lauren](/wiki/Ralph_Lauren_Corporation), [Tom Ford](/wiki/Tom_Ford_(brand)) and [Calvin Klein](/wiki/Calvin_Klein_(fashion_house)), are headquartered in [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan). Labels cater to [niche markets](/wiki/Niche_market), such as preteens. [New York Fashion Week](/wiki/New_York_Fashion_Week) is one of the most influential fashion shows in the world, and is held twice each year in Manhattan; the annual [Met Gala](/wiki/Met_Gala), also in Manhattan, has been called the fashion world's "biggest night".

### Cinema

Main article: [Cinema of the United States](/wiki/Cinema_of_the_United_States)

The [Hollywood Sign](/wiki/Hollywood_Sign) in the [Hollywood Hills](/wiki/Hollywood_Hills), often regarded as the symbol of the [American film industry](/wiki/American_film_industry)

The U.S. film industry has [a worldwide influence and following](/wiki/Global_Hollywood). [Hollywood](/wiki/Hollywood,_Los_Angeles), a district in central Los Angeles, the nation's second-most populous city, is also [metonymous](/wiki/Metonymous) for the American filmmaking industry. The [major film studios](/wiki/Major_film_studios) of the United States are the primary source of the [most commercially successful](/wiki/List_of_highest-grossing_films) movies selling the most tickets in the world.

Largely centered in the New York City region from its beginnings in the late 19th century through the first decades of the 20th century, the U.S. film industry has since been primarily based in and around Hollywood. Nonetheless, American film companies have been subject to the forces of [globalization](/wiki/Globalization) in the 21st century, and an increasing number of films are made elsewhere. The [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Awards), popularly known as "the Oscars", have been held annually by the [Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences](/wiki/Academy_of_Motion_Picture_Arts_and_Sciences) since 1929, and the [Golden Globe Awards](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Award) have been held annually since January 1944.

The industry peaked in what is commonly referred to as the "[Golden Age of Hollywood](/wiki/Classical_Hollywood_cinema)", from the early sound period until the early 1960s, with screen actors such as [John Wayne](/wiki/John_Wayne) and [Marilyn Monroe](/wiki/Marilyn_Monroe) becoming iconic figures. In the 1970s, "[New Hollywood](/wiki/New_Hollywood)", or the "Hollywood Renaissance", was defined by grittier films influenced by French and Italian realist pictures of the [post-war period](/wiki/Aftermath_of_World_War_II). The 21st century has been marked by the rise of American [streaming platforms](/wiki/Streaming_platform), which came to rival traditional cinema.

### Cuisine

Main article: [American cuisine](/wiki/American_cuisine)

A [Thanksgiving dinner](/wiki/Thanksgiving_dinner) with [roast turkey](/wiki/Roast_turkey), [mashed potatoes](/wiki/Mashed_potatoes), [pickles](/wiki/Pickled_cucumber), [corn](/wiki/Corn), [candied yams](/wiki/Candied_yams), [cranberry jelly](/wiki/Cranberry_jelly), [shrimps](/wiki/Shrimp_and_prawn_as_food), [stuffing](/wiki/Stuffing), [green peas](/wiki/Green_peas), [deviled eggs](/wiki/Deviled_eggs), [green salad](/wiki/Green_salad), and [apple sauce](/wiki/Apple_sauce)

Early settlers were introduced by Native Americans to foods such as [turkey](/wiki/Turkey_as_food), [sweet potatoes](/wiki/Sweet_potato), [corn](/wiki/Maize), [squash](/wiki/Cucurbita), and [maple syrup](/wiki/Maple_syrup). Of the most enduring and pervasive examples are variations of the native dish called [succotash](/wiki/Succotash). Early settlers and later immigrants combined these with foods they were familiar with, such as [wheat flour](/wiki/Wheat_flour), beef, and milk, to create a distinctive American cuisine. [New World crops](/wiki/New_World_crops), especially [pumpkin](/wiki/Pumpkin), corn, [potatoes](/wiki/Potatoes), and turkey as the main course are part of a shared national menu on [Thanksgiving](/wiki/Thanksgiving_(United_States)), when many Americans prepare or purchase traditional dishes to celebrate the occasion.

Characteristic American dishes such as [apple pie](/wiki/Apple_pie), [fried chicken](/wiki/Fried_chicken), [doughnuts](/wiki/Doughnut), [french fries](/wiki/French_fries), [macaroni and cheese](/wiki/Macaroni_and_cheese), [ice cream](/wiki/Ice_cream), [hamburgers](/wiki/Hamburger), [hot dogs](/wiki/Hot_dog), and [American pizza](/wiki/Pizza_in_the_United_States) derive from the recipes of various immigrant groups. [Mexican dishes](/wiki/Mexican-American_cuisine) such as [burritos](/wiki/Burritos) and [tacos](/wiki/Tacos) preexisted the United States in areas later annexed from Mexico, and [adaptations of Chinese cuisine](/wiki/American_Chinese_cuisine) as well as [pasta dishes freely adapted from Italian sources](/wiki/Italian-American_cuisine) are all widely consumed.

American [chefs](/wiki/Chef) have had a significant impact on society both domestically and internationally. In 1946, the [Culinary Institute of America](/wiki/Culinary_Institute_of_America) was founded by [Katharine Angell](/wiki/Katharine_Cramer_Angell) and [Frances Roth](/wiki/Frances_Roth). This would become the United States' most prestigious culinary school, where many of the most talented American chefs would study prior to successful careers. The [United States restaurant industry](/wiki/United_States_restaurant_industry) was projected at $899 billion in sales for 2020, and employed more than 15 million people, representing 10% of the nation's workforce directly. It is the country's second-largest private employer and the third-largest employer overall. The United States is home to over 220 [Michelin-starred](/wiki/Michelin_star) restaurants, [14 of which were awarded three stars](/wiki/List_of_Michelin_3-star_restaurants_in_the_United_States).

[Wine](/wiki/American_wine) has been produced in what is now the United States since the 1500s, with the [first widespread production beginning in what is now New Mexico](/wiki/New_Mexico_wine) in 1628. In the modern U.S., wine production is undertaken in all fifty states, with [California producing 84 percent of all U.S. wine](/wiki/California_wine). With more than 1,100,000 acres (4,500 km2) under vine, the United States is the [fourth-largest wine-producing country](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_wine_production) in the world, after [Italy](/wiki/Italian_wine), [Spain](/wiki/Spanish_wine), and [France](/wiki/French_wine).

The [classic American diner](/wiki/Diner), a casual restaurant type originally intended for the working class, emerged during the 19th century from converted railroad [dining cars](/wiki/Dining_car) made stationary. The diner soon evolved into purpose-built structures whose number expanded greatly in the 20th century. The American [fast-food](/wiki/Fast-food) industry developed alongside the nation's [car culture](/wiki/Car_culture). American restaurants developed the [drive-in](/wiki/Drive-in) format in the 1920s, which they began to replace with the [drive-through](/wiki/Drive-through) format by the 1940s. American [fast-food restaurant](/wiki/Fast-food_restaurant) chains, such as [McDonald's](/wiki/McDonald%27s), [Burger King](/wiki/Burger_King), [Chick-fil-A](/wiki/Chick-fil-A), [Kentucky Fried Chicken](/wiki/Kentucky_Fried_Chicken), [Dunkin' Donuts](/wiki/Dunkin%27_Donuts) and [many others](/wiki/List_of_fast_food_restaurant_chains#United_States), have numerous outlets around the world.

### Sports

Main articles: [Sports in the United States](/wiki/Sports_in_the_United_States) and [United States at the Olympics](/wiki/United_States_at_the_Olympics)

[American football](/wiki/American_football) is the most popular sport in the United States; in this 2009 [National Football League](/wiki/National_Football_League) game, [Carolina Panthers](/wiki/Carolina_Panthers) quarterback [Jake Delhomme](/wiki/Jake_Delhomme) (number 17) throws a forward pass against the [Dallas Cowboys](/wiki/Dallas_Cowboys).

The most popular spectator sports in the U.S. are [American football](/wiki/American_football_in_the_United_States), [basketball](/wiki/Basketball_in_the_United_States), [baseball](/wiki/Baseball_in_the_United_States), [soccer](/wiki/Soccer_in_the_United_States), and [ice hockey](/wiki/Ice_hockey_in_the_United_States). Their premier leagues are, respectively, the [National Football League](/wiki/National_Football_League), the [National Basketball Association](/wiki/National_Basketball_Association), [Major League Baseball](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball), [Major League Soccer](/wiki/Major_League_Soccer), and the [National Hockey League](/wiki/National_Hockey_League), All these leagues enjoy wide-ranging domestic media coverage and, except for the MLS, all are considered the preeminent leagues in their respective sports in the world. While most major U.S. sports such as baseball and American football have evolved out of European practices, basketball, [volleyball](/wiki/Volleyball_in_the_United_States), [skateboarding](/wiki/Skateboarding), and [snowboarding](/wiki/Snowboarding) are American inventions, many of which have become popular worldwide. [Lacrosse](/wiki/Lacrosse_in_the_United_States) and [surfing](/wiki/Surfing_in_the_United_States) arose from Native American and Native Hawaiian activities that predate European contact. The [market for professional sports in the United States](/wiki/Professional_sports_market_in_the_United_States) was approximately $69 billion in July 2013, roughly 50% larger than that of Europe, the Middle East, and Africa combined. [Professional wrestling](/wiki/Professional_wrestling) was widely popularized in the country, with the United States serving as the home country for [World Wrestling Entertainment](/wiki/World_Wrestling_Entertainment) and [All Elite Wrestling](/wiki/All_Elite_Wrestling), the two largest wrestling promotions in the world.

American football is by several measures the most popular spectator sport in the United States. Although American football does not have a substantial following in other nations, the NFL does have the highest average attendance (67,254) of any professional sports league in the world. In the year 2024, the NFL generated over $23 billion, making them the most valued professional sports league in the United States and the world. Baseball has been regarded as the U.S. "[national sport](/wiki/National_sport)" since the late 19th century. The most-watched [individual sports](/wiki/Individual_sport) in the U.S. are [golf](/wiki/Golf_in_the_United_States) and [auto racing](/wiki/Auto_racing), particularly [NASCAR](/wiki/NASCAR) and [IndyCar](/wiki/IndyCar).

On the [collegiate level](/wiki/College_athletics_in_the_United_States), earnings for the member institutions exceed $1 billion annually, and [college football](/wiki/College_football) and [basketball](/wiki/College_basketball) attract large audiences, as the [NCAA March Madness tournament](/wiki/NCAA_Division_I_men%27s_basketball_tournament) and the [College Football Playoff](/wiki/College_Football_Playoff) are some of the most watched national sporting events. In the U.S., the intercollegiate sports level serves as the main feeder system for professional and Olympic sports, with significant exceptions such as [Minor League Baseball](/wiki/Minor_League_Baseball). This differs greatly from practices in nearly all other countries, where publicly and privately funded sports organizations serve this function.

Eight [Olympic Games](/wiki/Olympic_Games) have taken place in the United States. The [1904 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1904_Summer_Olympics) in [St. Louis, Missouri](/wiki/St._Louis,_Missouri), were the first-ever Olympic Games held outside of Europe. The Olympic Games will be held in the U.S. for a ninth time when Los Angeles hosts the [2028 Summer Olympics](/wiki/2028_Summer_Olympics). [U.S. athletes](/wiki/United_States_at_the_Olympics) have won a total of 2,968 medals (1,179 gold) at the Olympic Games, the most of any country.

In other international competition, the United States is the home of a number of prestigious events, including the [America's Cup](/wiki/America%27s_Cup), [World Baseball Classic](/wiki/World_Baseball_Classic), the [U.S. Open](/wiki/U.S._Open_(tennis)), and the [Masters Tournament](/wiki/Masters_Tournament). The [U.S. men's national soccer team](/wiki/United_States_men%27s_national_soccer_team) has qualified for [eleven World Cups](/wiki/United_States_at_the_FIFA_World_Cup), while the [women's national team](/wiki/United_States_women%27s_national_soccer_team) has [won](/wiki/United_States_at_the_FIFA_Women%27s_World_Cup) the [FIFA Women's World Cup](/wiki/FIFA_Women%27s_World_Cup) and [Olympic soccer tournament](/wiki/Football_at_the_Summer_Olympics) four and five times, respectively. The [1999 FIFA Women's World Cup](/wiki/1999_FIFA_Women%27s_World_Cup) was hosted by the United States. [Its final match](/wiki/1999_FIFA_Women%27s_World_Cup_final) was attended by 90,185, setting the world record for largest women's sporting event crowd at the time. The United States hosted the [1994 FIFA World Cup](/wiki/1994_FIFA_World_Cup) and will co-host, along with Canada and Mexico, the [2026 FIFA World Cup](/wiki/2026_FIFA_World_Cup).
