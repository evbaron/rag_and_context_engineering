# United States federal executive departments

"Executive Department" redirects here. For the idea of executive departments in general, see [Cabinet (government)](/wiki/Cabinet_(government)).

The **United States federal executive departments** are the principal units of the [executive branch](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States#Executive_branch) of the [federal government of the United States](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States). The executive departments are the administrative arms of the [president of the United States](/wiki/President_of_the_United_States). They are analogous to [ministries](/wiki/Ministry_(government_department)) common in [parliamentary](/wiki/Parliamentary_system) or [semi-presidential](/wiki/Semi-presidential_republic) systems but (the United States being a [presidential system](/wiki/Presidential_system)) they are led by a [head of government](/wiki/Head_of_government) who is also the [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state). There are currently 15 executive departments.

## Overview

### Structure

Each department is headed by a [secretary](/wiki/Secretary_(title)) whose title echoes the title of their respective department, with the exception of the [Department of Justice](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice), whose head is known as the [attorney general](/wiki/United_States_Attorney_General). The heads of the executive departments are appointed by the president and take office after [confirmation](/wiki/Appointments_Clause) by the [United States Senate](/wiki/United_States_Senate), and serve [at the pleasure of the president](/wiki/Powers_of_the_president_of_the_United_States). The heads of departments are members of the [Cabinet of the United States](/wiki/Cabinet_of_the_United_States), an executive organ that normally acts as an advisory body to the president. In the [Opinion Clause](/wiki/Opinion_Clause) (Article II, section 2, clause 1) of the [U.S. Constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_States), heads of executive departments are referred to as "principal Officer in each of the executive Departments".

The heads of executive departments are included in the [line of succession](/wiki/United_States_presidential_line_of_succession) to the president, in the event of a vacancy in the presidency, after the [vice president](/wiki/Vice_President_of_the_United_States), the [speaker of the House](/wiki/Speaker_of_the_United_States_House_of_Representatives), and the [president pro tempore of the Senate](/wiki/President_pro_tempore_of_the_United_States_Senate). They are included in order of their respective department's formation, with the exception of the secretary of defense, whose position in the line of succession is based on when the Department of War was formed.

### Separation of powers

To enforce a strong [separation of powers](/wiki/Separation_of_powers_under_the_United_States_Constitution), the federal Constitution's [Ineligibility Clause](/wiki/Ineligibility_Clause) expressly prohibits executive branch employees (including heads of executive departments) from simultaneously serving in [Congress](/wiki/United_States_Congress), and vice versa. Accordingly, in sharp contrast to parliamentary systems where [ministers](/wiki/Minister_(government)) are often selected to form a government from [members of parliament](/wiki/Member_of_parliament), U.S. legislators who are appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate to serve as heads of executive departments must resign from Congress before assuming their new positions. If the emoluments for a new appointee's executive branch position were increased while the appointee was previously serving in Congress (e.g., [cost of living](/wiki/Cost_of_living) adjustments), the president must implement a [Saxbe fix](/wiki/Saxbe_fix).

### Contracting and grantmaking roles

The chart below shows that several executive departments (Education, Health and Human Services, Housing and Urban Development, and Transportation) have disproportionately small employee headcounts in contrast to the size of their budgets. This is because many of their employees merely supervise contracts with private [independent contractors](/wiki/Independent_contractor) or [grants](/wiki/Federal_grants_in_the_United_States) (especially [categorical grants](/wiki/Categorical_grant)) to [state](/wiki/State_governments_of_the_United_States) or [local government](/wiki/Local_government_in_the_United_States) agencies who are primarily responsible for providing services directly to the general public. In the 20th century, when the federal government began to provide funding and supervision for matters which were historically seen as the domain of state governments (i.e., [education](/wiki/Education_in_the_United_States), [health](/wiki/Healthcare_in_the_United_States) and [welfare services](/wiki/Social_programs_in_the_United_States), [housing](/wiki/Housing_in_the_United_States), and [transportation](/wiki/Transportation_in_the_United_States)), Congress frequently authorized only funding for grants which were voluntary, in the sense that state or local government agencies could choose to apply for such grants (and accept conditions attached by Congress) or they could decline to apply. In the case of HHS's [Medicare](/wiki/Medicare_(United_States)) program, Congress chose to contract with private health insurers because they "already possessed the requisite expertise for administering complex [health insurance](/wiki/Health_insurance_in_the_United_States) programs", and because American hospitals preferred to continue dealing with private insurers instead of a new federal bureaucracy.

## Proposed departments

### Mergers of existing departments

- Department of Natural Resources, proposed by the [Eisenhower administration](/wiki/Presidency_of_Dwight_D._Eisenhower), President [Richard Nixon](/wiki/Richard_Nixon), the [1976 GOP national platform](/wiki/1976_Republican_National_Convention), and by [Bill Daley](/wiki/William_M._Daley) (as a consolidation of the Departments of the Interior and Energy, and the Environmental Protection Agency)
- Department of Economic Affairs, proposed by President Richard Nixon; essentially a consolidation of the Departments of Commerce, Labor, and Agriculture.
- Department of Business, proposed by President [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama) as a consolidation of the U.S. Department of Commerce's core business and trade functions, the [Small Business Administration](/wiki/Small_Business_Administration), the [Office of the U.S. Trade Representative](/wiki/Office_of_the_United_States_Trade_Representative), the [Export-Import Bank](/wiki/Export%E2%80%93Import_Bank_of_the_United_States), the [Overseas Private Investment Corporation](/wiki/Overseas_Private_Investment_Corporation), and the [U.S. Trade and Development Agency](/wiki/United_States_Trade_and_Development_Agency)
- Department of Commerce and the Workforce, a merger of the Department of Commerce and the Department of Labor proposed in 2011 and 2013 by Senator [Richard Burr](/wiki/Richard_Burr) ([R](/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States))-[NC](/wiki/North_Carolina)) in [S. 1116](https://www.congress.gov/bill/112th-congress/senate-bill/1116)
- Department of Education and the Workforce, proposed by President [Donald Trump](/wiki/Donald_Trump) as a consolidation of the Departments of Education and Labor
- Department of Economic Development, proposed by Senator [Elizabeth Warren](/wiki/Elizabeth_Warren) to replace the Commerce Department, subsume other agencies like the Small Business Administration and the [Patent and Trademark Office](/wiki/United_States_Patent_and_Trademark_Office), and include research and development programs, worker training programs, and export and trade authorities like the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative with the single goal of creating and defending good American jobs

### New departments

- Department of Industry and Commerce, proposed by Secretary of the Treasury [William Windom](/wiki/William_Windom_(politician)) in a speech given at a Chamber of Commerce dinner in May 1881
- [Department of Peace](/wiki/Department_of_Peace), proposed by [Founding Father](/wiki/Founding_Fathers_of_the_United_States) and signer of the [United States Declaration of Independence](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence) [Benjamin Rush](/wiki/Benjamin_Rush) in 1793, Senator [Matthew Neely](/wiki/Matthew_M._Neely) in the 1930s, Congressman [Dennis Kucinich](/wiki/Dennis_Kucinich) in the 2000s, and other members of the U.S. Congress
- Department of Social Welfare, proposed by President [Franklin D. Roosevelt](/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) in January 1937
- Department of Public Works, proposed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in January 1937
- Department of Conservation (a renaming of the [Department of the Interior](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_the_Interior)), proposed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in January 1937
- Department of Urban Affairs and Housing, proposed by President [John F. Kennedy](/wiki/John_F._Kennedy)
- Department of Business and Labor, proposed by President [Lyndon B. Johnson](/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson)
- Department of Community Development, proposed by President Richard Nixon; to be chiefly concerned with rural infrastructure development
- Department of Human Resources, proposed by President Richard Nixon; essentially a revised Department of Health, Education, and Welfare
- Department of Environmental Protection, proposed by Senator [Arlen Specter](/wiki/Arlen_Specter) and others
- Department of Intelligence, proposed by former [director of national intelligence](/wiki/Director_of_National_Intelligence) [Mike McConnell](/wiki/Mike_McConnell_(U.S._Naval_officer))
- Department of Global Development, proposed by the [Center for Global Development](/wiki/Center_for_Global_Development)
- Department of Art, proposed by [Quincy Jones](/wiki/Quincy_Jones)
- Department of Health and Public Welfare, proposed by President Donald Trump as a renamed Department of Health and Human Services
- Department of Technology, proposed by businessman and 2020 Democratic presidential candidate [Andrew Yang](/wiki/Andrew_Yang)
- Department of Culture, patterned on similar departments in many foreign nations, proposed by, among others, [Murray Moss](/wiki/Murray_Moss) and Jeva Lange
