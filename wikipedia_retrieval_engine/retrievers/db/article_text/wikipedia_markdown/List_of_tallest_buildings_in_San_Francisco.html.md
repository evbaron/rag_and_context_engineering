[San Francisco](/wiki/San_Francisco), a major city in the [U.S state](/wiki/U.S._state) of [California](/wiki/California), has over 480 high-rises, 124 of which are at least 300 feet (91 m) tall as of 2026. The tallest building in the city is [Salesforce Tower](/wiki/Salesforce_Tower), the city's sole [supertall skyscraper](/wiki/List_of_supertall_skyscrapers). Headquarters of software company [Salesforce](/wiki/Salesforce), it was completed in 2018 at a height of 1,070 ft (326.1 m). It is the [18th-tallest building](/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_the_United_States) in the United States and the second tallest in California. San Francisco has the second largest skyline in the [Western United States](/wiki/Western_United_States). It has the second most skyscrapers taller than 492 ft (150 m) in California, with 27, after [Los Angeles](/wiki/Los_Angeles). When ranked by buildings that reach 300 ft (91 m), San Francisco has more skyscrapers than Los Angeles, and is tied with [Atlanta](/wiki/Atlanta) as having the [fifth-most in the United States](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_the_United_States_with_the_most_skyscrapers#Cities_with_the_most_skyscrapers_by_height), after [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City), [Chicago](/wiki/Chicago), [Miami](/wiki/Miami), and [Houston](/wiki/Houston).

The history of skyscrapers in San Francisco began with the 218-foot (66 m), ten-story [Chronicle Building](/wiki/Ritz-Carlton_Club_and_Residences), which was completed in 1890. During the 1920s, San Francisco underwent one of the largest pre-war skyscraper booms in the United States, constructing ten structures greater than 300 ft (91 m), including the [Telephone Building](/wiki/140_New_Montgomery) and the [Russ Building](/wiki/Russ_Building)**.** The Great Depression and World War II halted skyscraper development for two decades until the 1950s. Many of San Francisco's tallest buildings, particularly its office [skyscrapers](/wiki/Skyscraper), were built in a major construction boom between the 1960s and the late 1980s. This included the iconic [Transamerica Pyramid](/wiki/Transamerica_Pyramid) in 1972, which rises to 853 feet (260 m).

After a pause in the 1990s, high-rise construction resumed from the 2000s onward, with an increased share of residential buildings. The rate of development increased in the second half of the 2010s. New additions expanded the skyline towards the south and southeast, particularly in the neighborhoods of [SoMa](/wiki/South_of_Market,_San_Francisco) and [Rincon Hill](/wiki/Rincon_Hill,_San_Francisco). The [Transbay development](/wiki/San_Francisco_Transbay_development) resulted in the completion of the Salesforce Tower and the city's third-tallest building, [181 Fremont](/wiki/181_Fremont). The [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) of the early 2020s heavily curtailed high-rise construction in the city, while commercial real estate in existing skyscrapers was heavily affected. A number of planned projects could extend the skyline significantly in the future, such as the redevelopment of the Caltrain railyards and the ongoing [Treasure Island Development](/wiki/Treasure_Island_Development).

The large majority of tall buildings in San Francisco are concentrated in the city's [Financial District](/wiki/Financial_District,_San_Francisco), located in the northeastern corner of the [San Francisco Peninsula](/wiki/San_Francisco_Peninsula). There is also a notable high-rise cluster at the intersection of [Market Street](/wiki/Market_Street_(San_Francisco)) and [Van Ness Avenue](/wiki/Van_Ness_Avenue). Shorter high-rises are spread more sparsely in nearby neighborhoods, such as in [Russina Hill](/wiki/Russian_Hill,_San_Francisco), [Nob Hill](/wiki/Nob_Hill,_San_Francisco), [Pacific Heights](/wiki/Pacific_Heights,_San_Francisco), and in [Mission Bay](/wiki/Mission_Bay,_San_Francisco), where the Mission Rock project is under development. The western half of the city, including the [Richmond](/wiki/Richmond_District,_San_Francisco) and [Sunset](/wiki/Sunset_District,_San_Francisco) districts that surround [Golden Gate Park](/wiki/Golden_Gate_Park), is almost entirely devoid of high-rises. San Francisco's skyline is a common photography subject, with a popular viewpoint being that from the [Twin Peaks](/wiki/Twin_Peaks_(San_Francisco)) as well as the [Marin Headlands](/wiki/Marin_Headlands), where it can be viewed alongside the [Golden Gate Bridge](/wiki/Golden_Gate_Bridge).

California's first skyscraper was the 218-foot (66 m) [Chronicle Building](/wiki/Ritz-Carlton_Club_and_Residences) in San Francisco, which was completed in 1890. [M. H. de Young](/wiki/M._H._de_Young), owner of the *[San Francisco Chronicle](/wiki/San_Francisco_Chronicle)*, commissioned [Burnham and Root](/wiki/Burnham_and_Root) to design a signature tower to convey the power of his newspaper. Not to be outdone, de Young's rival, industrialist [Claus Spreckels](/wiki/Claus_Spreckels), purchased the *[San Francisco Call](/wiki/San_Francisco_Call)* in 1895 and commissioned a tower of his own that would dwarf the Chronicle Building. The 315-foot (96 m) [Call Building](/wiki/Central_Tower_(San_Francisco)) was completed in 1898 and stood across [Market Street](/wiki/Market_Street_(San_Francisco)) from the Chronicle Building. The Call Building (later named the Spreckels Building, and Central Tower today) would remain the city's tallest for nearly a quarter century.

Both steel-framed structures survived the [1906 earthquake](/wiki/1906_San_Francisco_earthquake), demonstrating that tall buildings could be safely constructed in earthquake country. Other early twentieth-century skyscrapers above 200 feet (61 m) include the [Merchants Exchange Building](/wiki/Merchants_Exchange_Building_(San_Francisco)) (1903), [Humboldt Bank Building](/wiki/Humboldt_Bank_Building) (1908), [Hobart Building](/wiki/Hobart_Building) (1914), and [Southern Pacific Building](/wiki/Southern_Pacific_Building) (1916). Another skyscraper boom took hold during the 1920s, when several [Neo-Gothic](/wiki/Gothic_Revival_architecture) and [Art Deco](/wiki/Art_Deco) high rises, reaching three to four hundred feet (90 to 120 m) in height, were constructed, including the [Standard Oil Building](/wiki/225_Bush_Street) (1922), [Pacific Telephone Building](/wiki/140_New_Montgomery) (1925), [Russ Building](/wiki/Russ_Building) (1927), [Hunter-Dulin Building](/wiki/Hunter-Dulin_Building) (1927), [450 Sutter Medical Building](/wiki/450_Sutter_Street) (1929), [Shell Building](/wiki/Shell_Building_(San_Francisco)) (1929), and [McAllister Tower](/wiki/McAllister_Tower_Apartments) (1930).

The [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression) and [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) halted any further skyscraper construction until the 1950s when the [Equitable Life Building](/wiki/100_Montgomery_Street) (1955) and [Crown-Zellerbach Building](/wiki/One_Bush_Plaza) (1959) were completed. Many of San Francisco's tallest buildings, particularly its office [skyscrapers](/wiki/Skyscraper), were completed in a building boom from the late 1960s until the late 1980s. During the 1960s, at least 40 new skyscrapers were built, and the [Hartford Building](/wiki/650_California_Street) (1965), [44 Montgomery](/wiki/44_Montgomery) (1967), [Bank of America Center](/wiki/555_California_Street) (1969), and [Transamerica Pyramid](/wiki/Transamerica_Pyramid) (1972) each, in turn, took the title of the [tallest building in California](/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_California) upon completion. At 853 feet (260 m) tall, the Transamerica Pyramid was one of the most controversial, with critics suggesting that it be torn down even before it was completed.

Aerial photo of downtown San Francisco, 2015. The [Transamerica Pyramid](/wiki/Transamerica_Pyramid) is visible on the right. The foundation of the [Salesforce Tower](/wiki/Salesforce_Tower) is visible on the lower left.

This surge of construction was dubbed "[Manhattanization](/wiki/Manhattanization)" by opponents and led to local legislation that set some of the strictest building height limits and regulations in the country. In 1985, San Francisco adopted the Downtown Plan, which slowed development in the [Financial District](/wiki/Financial_District,_San_Francisco) north of [Market Street](/wiki/Market_Street_(San_Francisco)) and directed it to the area [South of Market](/wiki/South_of_Market,_San_Francisco) around the [Transbay Terminal](/wiki/San_Francisco_Transbay_Terminal). Over 250 historic buildings were protected from development and developers were required to set aside open space for new projects. To prevent excessive growth and smooth the boom-and-bust building cycle, the Plan included an annual limit of 950,000 square feet (88,000 m2) for new office development, although it grandfathered millions of square feet of proposals already in the development pipeline. In response, voters approved Proposition M in November 1986 that reduced the annual limit to 475,000 square feet (44,100 m2) until the grandfathered square footage was accounted for, which occurred in 1999.

These limits, combined with the [early 1990s recession](/wiki/Early_1990s_recession), led to a significant slowdown of skyscraper construction during the late 1980s and 1990s. To guide new development, the city passed several neighborhood plans, such as the [Rincon Hill Plan](/wiki/Rincon_Hill,_San_Francisco) in 2005 and [Transit Center District Plan](/wiki/San_Francisco_Transbay_development) in 2012, which allow taller skyscrapers in certain specific locations in the South of Market area. Since the early 2000s, the city has been undergoing another building boom, with numerous buildings over 400 feet (122 m) proposed, approved, or under construction; some, such as the two-towered [One Rincon Hill](/wiki/One_Rincon_Hill) and mixed-use [181 Fremont](/wiki/181_Fremont), have been completed. Multiple skyscrapers have been constructed near the new [Salesforce Transit Center](/wiki/Salesforce_Transit_Center), including [Salesforce Tower](/wiki/Salesforce_Tower), which topped-out in 2017 at a height of 1,070 feet (330 m). This building is the first [supertall](/wiki/Supertall) skyscraper in San Francisco and among the [tallest in the United States](/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_the_United_States).
