The **University of Edinburgh** ([Scots](/wiki/Scots_language): *University o Edinburgh*, [Scottish Gaelic](/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic_language): *Oilthigh Dhùn Èideann*; abbreviated as ***Edin.*** in [post-nominals](/wiki/Post-nominal_letters)) is a [public](/wiki/Public_university) [research university](/wiki/Research_university) based in [Edinburgh](/wiki/Edinburgh), [Scotland](/wiki/Scotland). Founded by the [town council](/wiki/City_of_Edinburgh_Council) under the authority of a [royal charter](/wiki/Royal_charter) from King [James VI](/wiki/James_VI_and_I) in 1582 and officially opened in 1583, it is one of Scotland's [four ancient universities](/wiki/Ancient_universities_of_Scotland) and the [sixth-oldest university](/wiki/List_of_oldest_universities_in_continuous_operation) in continuous operation in the [English-speaking world](/wiki/English-speaking_world). The university played a crucial role in Edinburgh becoming a leading intellectual centre during the [Scottish Enlightenment](/wiki/Scottish_Enlightenment) and contributed to the city being nicknamed the "[Athens of the North](/wiki/Etymology_of_Edinburgh#Athens_of_the_North)".

The three main global university rankings ([ARWU](/wiki/Academic_Ranking_of_World_Universities), [THE](/wiki/Times_Higher_Education_World_University_Rankings), and [QS](/wiki/QS_World_University_Rankings)) place the University of Edinburgh within their respective top 40. It is a member of several associations of research-intensive universities, including the [Coimbra Group](/wiki/Coimbra_Group), [League of European Research Universities](/wiki/League_of_European_Research_Universities), [Russell Group](/wiki/Russell_Group), [Una Europa](/wiki/Una_Europa), and [Universitas 21](/wiki/Universitas_21). In the [fiscal year](/wiki/Fiscal_year) ending 31 July 2025, the university had a total income of £1.437 billion, with £375.4 million from research grants and contracts. It has the [third-largest endowment](/wiki/List_of_universities_in_the_United_Kingdom_by_endowment) in the UK, behind only [Cambridge](/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) and [Oxford](/wiki/University_of_Oxford). The university occupies five main campuses in the city of Edinburgh, which include many buildings of historical and architectural significance, such as those in the [Old Town](/wiki/Old_Town,_Edinburgh).

Edinburgh is the [fourth-largest university](/wiki/List_of_universities_in_the_United_Kingdom_by_enrolment) in the United Kingdom by total enrolment and the [largest university in Scotland](/wiki/List_of_universities_in_Scotland), receiving over 68,000 undergraduate applications per year, making it the fifth-most popular university in the UK by application volume. In 2021, Edinburgh had the seventh-highest average [UCAS points](/wiki/UCAS_Tariff) among British universities for new entrants. The university maintains strong links to the [royal family](/wiki/British_royal_family), with [Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh](/wiki/Prince_Philip,_Duke_of_Edinburgh), serving as its chancellor from 1953 to 2010, and [Anne, Princess Royal](/wiki/Anne,_Princess_Royal), holding the position since March 2011.

[Notable alumni of the University of Edinburgh](/wiki/List_of_University_of_Edinburgh_people) include inventor [Alexander Graham Bell](/wiki/Alexander_Graham_Bell), [naturalist](/wiki/Natural_history#Before_1900) [Charles Darwin](/wiki/Charles_Darwin), philosopher [David Hume](/wiki/David_Hume), physicist [James Clerk Maxwell](/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell), and writers such as [Oliver Goldsmith](/wiki/Oliver_Goldsmith), Sir [J. M. Barrie](/wiki/J._M._Barrie), Sir [Arthur Conan Doyle](/wiki/Arthur_Conan_Doyle), Sir [Walter Scott](/wiki/Walter_Scott), and [Robert Louis Stevenson](/wiki/Robert_Louis_Stevenson). The university has produced several heads of state and government, including [three British prime ministers](/wiki/List_of_prime_ministers_of_the_United_Kingdom_by_education). Additionally, four [UK Supreme Court justices](/wiki/Justice_of_the_Supreme_Court_of_the_United_Kingdom) were educated at Edinburgh. As of April 2026, the university has been affiliated with 20 [Nobel Prize](/wiki/Nobel_Prize) laureates, four [Pulitzer Prize](/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize) winners, three [Turing Award](/wiki/Turing_Award) winners, an [Abel Prize](/wiki/Abel_Prize) laureate, and a [Fields Medalist](/wiki/Fields_Medal). Edinburgh alumni have also won a total of ten [Olympic gold medals](/wiki/Olympic_medal).

## History

### Early history

[Robert Rollock](/wiki/Robert_Rollock), Regent (1583–1586) and first principal (1586–1599) of the University of Edinburgh

In 1557, Bishop [Robert Reid](/wiki/Robert_Reid_(bishop)) of [St Magnus Cathedral](/wiki/St_Magnus_Cathedral) made a will containing an endowment of 8,000 [merks](/wiki/Merk_(coin)) to build a college in Edinburgh. Unusually for his time, Reid's vision for the college included the teaching of [rhetoric](/wiki/Rhetoric) and [poetry](/wiki/Poetry), alongside more traditional subjects such as [philosophy](/wiki/Philosophy).

However, the bequest was delayed by more than 25 years due to the religious revolution that led to the [Reformation Parliament](/wiki/Scottish_Reformation_Parliament) of 1560. The plans were revived in the late 1570s through efforts by the [Edinburgh Town Council](/wiki/Edinburgh_Town_Council), first minister of Edinburgh [James Lawson](/wiki/James_Lawson_(minister)), and [Lord Provost](/wiki/Lord_Provost_of_Edinburgh) [William Little](/wiki/William_Little_(Lord_Provost)). When Reid's descendants were unwilling to pay out the sum, the town council petitioned King [James VI](/wiki/James_VI_and_I) and his [Privy Council](/wiki/Privy_Council_of_Scotland). The King arranged a monetary compromise and granted a [royal charter](/wiki/Royal_charter) on 14 April 1582, empowering the town council to create a college of higher education. A college established by secular authorities was unprecedented in [newly Presbyterian](/wiki/Scottish_Reformation) Scotland, as all previous Scottish universities had been founded through [papal bulls](/wiki/Papal_bull).

Main buildings of King James' College in 1647, lying in a double courtyard on the lower left

Frontispiece to earliest laureation (graduation) register (1587)

Named *Tounis College* (Town's College), the university opened its doors to students on 14 October 1583, with an attendance of 80–90. At the time, the college mainly covered [liberal arts](/wiki/Liberal_arts_education) and [divinity](/wiki/Divinity_(academic_discipline)). Instruction began under the charge of a graduate from the [University of St Andrews](/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews), theologian [Robert Rollock](/wiki/Robert_Rollock), who first served as Regent, and from 1586 as principal of the college. Initially, Rollock was the sole instructor for first-year students, and he was expected to tutor the 1583 intake for all four years of their degree in every subject. The first cohort finished their studies in 1587, and 47 students graduated (or 'laureated') with an [M.A.](/wiki/Master_of_Arts_(Scotland)) degree. When King James VI visited Scotland in 1617, he held a [disputation](/wiki/Disputation) with the college's professors, after which he decreed that it should henceforth be called the "Colledge *[sic]* of King James". The university was known as both *Tounis College* and *King James' College* until it gradually assumed the name of the University of Edinburgh during the 17th century.

After the deposition of King [James II and VII](/wiki/James_II_of_England) during the [Glorious Revolution](/wiki/Glorious_Revolution) of 1688, the [Parliament of Scotland](/wiki/Parliament_of_Scotland) passed legislation designed to root out [Jacobite](/wiki/Jacobitism) sympathisers amongst university staff. In Edinburgh, this led to the dismissal of Principal [Alexander Monro](/wiki/Alexander_Monro_(educator)) and several professors and regents after a government visitation in 1690. The university was subsequently led by Principal [Gilbert Rule](/wiki/Gilbert_Rule), one of the inquisitors on the visitation committee.

### 18th and 19th century

> "You are now in a place where the best courses upon earth are within your reach... Such an opportunity you will never again have. I would therefore strongly press on you to fix no other limit to your stay in Edinborough than your having got thro this whole course. The omission of any one part of it will be an affliction & loss to you as long as you live."

— [Thomas Jefferson](/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson), writing to his son-in-law [Thomas Mann Randolph, Jr.](/wiki/Thomas_Mann_Randolph,_Jr.) in 1786.

The late 17th and early 18th centuries were marked by a power struggle between the university and town council, which had ultimate authority over staff appointments, curricula, and examinations. After a series of challenges by the university, the conflict culminated in the council seizing the college records in 1704. Relations were only gradually repaired over the next 150 years and suffered repeated setbacks.

In 1708 the Principal [William Carstares](/wiki/William_Carstares) restructured the University, abolishing [the regenting system](/wiki/The_Regenting_System_(Edinburgh)) and establishing a Dutch style system of Professors. The newly created Chairs, such as that of [Greek and Humanity](/wiki/Professor_of_Classics_(Edinburgh)), were offered to the existing Regents. The university expanded by founding a Faculty of Law in 1707, a Faculty of Arts in 1708, and a Faculty of Medicine in 1726. In 1762, Reverend [Hugh Blair](/wiki/Hugh_Blair) was appointed by King [George III](/wiki/George_III) as the first [Regius Professor of Rhetoric and Belles-Lettres](/wiki/Regius_Chair_of_Rhetoric_and_English_Literature). This formalised literature as a subject and marks the foundation of the English Literature department, making Edinburgh the oldest centre of literary education in Britain.

During the 18th century, the university was at the centre of the [Scottish Enlightenment](/wiki/Scottish_Enlightenment). The ideas of the [Age of Enlightenment](/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment) fell on especially fertile ground in Edinburgh because of the university's democratic and secular origin; its organisation as a single entity instead of loosely connected colleges, which encouraged academic exchange; its adoption of the more flexible Dutch model of professorship, rather than having student cohorts taught by a single regent; and the lack of land endowments as its source of income, which meant its faculty operated in a more competitive environment. Between 1750 and 1800, this system produced and attracted key Enlightenment figures such as chemist [Joseph Black](/wiki/Joseph_Black), economist [Adam Smith](/wiki/Adam_Smith), historian [William Robertson](/wiki/William_Robertson_(historian)), philosophers [David Hume](/wiki/David_Hume) and [Dugald Stewart](/wiki/Dugald_Stewart), physician [William Cullen](/wiki/William_Cullen), and early sociologist [Adam Ferguson](/wiki/Adam_Ferguson), many of which taught concurrently. By the time the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh) was founded in 1783, the university was regarded as one of the world's preeminent scientific institutions, and [Voltaire](/wiki/Voltaire) called Edinburgh a "hotbed of genius" as a result. [Benjamin Franklin](/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin) believed that the university possessed "a set of as truly great men, Professors of the Several Branches of Knowledge, as have ever appeared in any Age or Country". [Thomas Jefferson](/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson) felt that as far as science was concerned, "no place in the world can pretend to a competition with Edinburgh".

The east facade of [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh) facing onto [South Bridge](/wiki/South_Bridge,_Edinburgh), as built in 1827. A [dome](/wiki/Dome) similar to Adam's original design was added in 1887 by Sir [Robert Rowand Anderson](/wiki/Robert_Rowand_Anderson).

A [Charter of Novodamus](/wiki/Charter_of_novodamus) from King [James VI of Scotland](/wiki/James_VI_and_I) in 1582, to establish a college

In 1785, [Henry Dundas](/wiki/Henry_Dundas,_1st_Viscount_Melville) introduced the [South Bridge Act](/wiki/South_Bridge_Act_1785) in the [House of Commons](/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom); one of the bill's goals was to use [South Bridge](/wiki/South_Bridge,_Edinburgh) as a location for the university, which had existed in a hotchpotch of buildings since its establishment. The site was used to construct [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh), the university's first custom-built building, by architect [William Henry Playfair](/wiki/William_Henry_Playfair) to plans by [Robert Adam](/wiki/Robert_Adam). During the 18th century, the university developed a particular forte in teaching [anatomy](/wiki/Anatomy) and the developing science of [surgery](/wiki/Surgery), and it was considered one of the best medical schools in the English-speaking world. Bodies to be used for [dissection](/wiki/Dissection) were brought to the university's Anatomy Theatre through a secret tunnel from a nearby house (today's College Wynd student accommodation), which was also used by murderers [Burke and Hare](/wiki/Burke_and_Hare_murders) to deliver the corpses of their victims during the 1820s.

The [Edinburgh snowball riots of 1838](/wiki/Edinburgh_snowball_riots_of_1838) – also known as the *'Wars of the Quadrangle'* – occurred when University of Edinburgh students started a snowball fight in "a spirit of harmless amusement". However, this turned into a two-day 'battle' at Old College with local Edinburgh residents on South Bridge, which led to the Lord Provost asking for an infantry regiment to be brought from [Edinburgh Castle](/wiki/Edinburgh_Castle) to quell the disturbance. The riot was immortalised in a humorous 92-page account written by the students entitled *The University Snowdrop*. In 1853, English landscape artist [Sam Bough](/wiki/Sam_Bough) painted the event in watercolour.

*Snowballing Outside Edinburgh University* (1853) – Sam Bough

After 275 years of governance by the town council, the [Universities (Scotland) Act 1858](/wiki/Ancient_university_governance_in_Scotland) gave the university full authority over its own affairs. The act established governing bodies including a university court and a general council, and redefined the roles of key officials like the chancellor, rector, and principal.

Plaque commemorating the [Edinburgh Seven](/wiki/Edinburgh_Seven) at the [Royal College of Surgeons of Edinburgh](/wiki/Royal_College_of_Surgeons_of_Edinburgh)

The [Edinburgh Seven](/wiki/Edinburgh_Seven) were the first group of matriculated undergraduate female students at any British university. Led by [Sophia Jex-Blake](/wiki/Sophia_Jex-Blake), they began studying medicine at the University of Edinburgh in 1869. Although the university blocked them from graduating and qualifying as doctors, their campaign gained national attention and won them many supporters, including [Charles Darwin](/wiki/Charles_Darwin). Their efforts put the rights of women to higher education on the national political agenda, which eventually resulted in legislation allowing women to study at all Scottish universities in 1889. The university admitted women to graduate in medicine in 1893. In 2015, the Edinburgh Seven were commemorated with a plaque at the university, and in 2019 they were posthumously awarded with medical degrees.

Buildings of the [Old Medical School](/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh_Medical_School#Teviot_Place) at Teviot Place, photographed in the late 19th century

Exterior of the [McEwan Hall](/wiki/McEwan_Hall)

Towards the end of the 19th century, Old College was becoming overcrowded. After a bequest from Sir [David Baxter](/wiki/Sir_David_Baxter,_1st_Baronet), the university started planning new buildings in earnest. Sir [Robert Rowand Anderson](/wiki/Robert_Rowand_Anderson) won the public architectural competition and was commissioned to design new premises for the [Medical School](/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh_Medical_School) in 1877. Initially, the design incorporated a [campanile](/wiki/Bell_tower) and a hall for examination and graduation, but this was seen as too ambitious. The new Medical School opened in 1884, but the building was not completed until 1888. After funds were donated by politician and brewer [William McEwan](/wiki/William_McEwan) in 1894, a separate graduation building was constructed after all, also designed by Anderson. The resulting [McEwan Hall](/wiki/McEwan_Hall) on [Bristo Square](/wiki/Bristo_Square) was presented to the university in 1897.

[Teviot Row House](/wiki/Teviot_Row_House), drawn by architect [Sydney Mitchell](/wiki/Sydney_Mitchell) in 1888

The [Students' Representative Council](/wiki/Students%27_Representative_Council) (SRC) was founded in 1884 by student [Robert Fitzroy Bell](/wiki/Robert_Fitzroy_Bell). In 1889, the SRC voted to establish Edinburgh University Union (EUU), to be housed in [Teviot Row House](/wiki/Teviot_Row_House) on Bristo Square. [Edinburgh University Sports Union](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Sports_Union) (EUSU) was founded in 1866, and [Edinburgh University Women's Union](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Women%27s_Union) (renamed the Chambers Street Union in 1964) in October 1905. The SRC, EUU and Chambers Street Union merged to form [Edinburgh University Students' Association](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Students%27_Association) (EUSA) on 1 July 1973.

### 20th century

During [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I), the Science and Medicine buildings had suffered from a lack of repairs or upgrades, which was exacerbated by an influx of students after the end of the war. In 1919, the university bought the land of West Mains Farm in the south of the city for the development of a new satellite campus specialising in the sciences. On 6 July 1920, King [George V](/wiki/George_V) laid the foundation of the first new building (now called the [Joseph Black](/wiki/Joseph_Black) Building), housing the [Department of Chemistry](/wiki/School_of_Chemistry,_University_of_Edinburgh). The campus was named [King's Buildings](/wiki/King%27s_Buildings) in honour of George V.

Facade of [New College](/wiki/New_College,_Edinburgh) facing onto [The Mound](/wiki/The_Mound) in 1910

[New College](/wiki/New_College,_University_of_Edinburgh) on [The Mound](/wiki/The_Mound) was originally opened in 1846 as a [Free Church of Scotland](/wiki/Free_Church_of_Scotland_(1843%E2%80%931900)) college, later of the [United Free Church of Scotland](/wiki/United_Free_Church_of_Scotland). Since the 1930s it has been the home of the School of Divinity. Prior to the 1929 reunion of the [Church of Scotland](/wiki/Church_of_Scotland), candidates for the ministry in the United Free Church studied at New College, whilst candidates for the Church of Scotland studied in the university's Faculty of Divinity. In 1935 the two institutions merged, with all operations moved to the New College site in Old Town. This freed up Old College for [Edinburgh Law School](/wiki/Edinburgh_Law_School).

Plaque honouring the [Polish School of Medicine](/wiki/Polish_School_of_Medicine) at the old Medical School

The [Polish School of Medicine](/wiki/Polish_School_of_Medicine) was established in 1941 as a wartime academic initiative. While it was originally intended for students and doctors in the [Polish Armed Forces in the West](/wiki/Polish_Armed_Forces_in_the_West), civilians were also allowed to take the courses, which were taught in Polish and awarded Polish medical degrees. When the school was closed in 1949, 336 students had matriculated, of which 227 students graduated with the equivalent of an [MBChB](/wiki/Bachelor_of_Medicine,_Bachelor_of_Surgery) and a total of 19 doctors obtained a doctorate or [MD.](/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine) A bronze plaque commemorating the Polish School of Medicine is located in the Quadrangle of the old Medical School in Teviot Place.

On 10 May 1951, the *Royal (Dick) Veterinary College*, founded in 1823 by [William Dick](/wiki/William_Dick_(veterinary_surgeon)), was reconstituted as the [Royal (Dick) School of Veterinary Studies](/wiki/Royal_(Dick)_School_of_Veterinary_Studies) and officially became part of the university. It achieved full faculty status as Faculty of Veterinary Medicine in 1964.

In 1955 the university opened the first department of [nursing](/wiki/Nursing) in Europe for academic study. This department was inspired by the work of [Gladys Beaumont Carter](/wiki/Gladys_Beaumont_Carter) and a grant from the [Rockefeller Foundation](/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation).

By the end of the 1950s, there were around 7,000 students matriculating annually, more than doubling the numbers from the turn of the century. The university addressed this partially through the redevelopment of [George Square](/wiki/George_Square,_Edinburgh), demolishing much of the area's historic houses and erecting modern buildings such as [40 George Square](/wiki/40_George_Square), [Appleton Tower](/wiki/Appleton_Tower) and the [Main Library](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Library).

On 1 August 1998, the *Moray House Institute of Education*, founded in 1848, merged with the University of Edinburgh, becoming its Faculty of Education. Following the internal restructuring of the university in 2002, Moray House became known as the [Moray House School of Education](/wiki/Moray_House_School_of_Education). It was renamed the Moray House School of Education and Sport in August 2019.

### 21st century

In the 1990s it became apparent that the old [Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh](/wiki/Royal_Infirmary_of_Edinburgh) buildings in [Lauriston Place](/wiki/Lauriston) were no longer adequate for a modern teaching hospital. [Donald Dewar](/wiki/Donald_Dewar), the [Scottish Secretary](/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Scotland) at the time, authorised a joint project between private finance, local authorities, and the university to create a modern hospital and medical campus in the [Little France](/wiki/Little_France) area of Edinburgh. The new campus was named the [BioQuarter](/wiki/Edinburgh_BioQuarter). The Chancellor's Building was opened on 12 August 2002 by [Prince Philip](/wiki/Prince_Philip,_Duke_of_Edinburgh), housing the new [Edinburgh Medical School](/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh_Medical_School) alongside the new Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh. In 2007, the campus saw the addition of the [Euan MacDonald Centre](/wiki/Euan_MacDonald_Centre) as a research centre for [motor neuron diseases](/wiki/Motor_neuron_disease), which was part-funded by Scottish entrepreneur [Euan MacDonald](/wiki/Euan_MacDonald) and his father Donald. In August 2010, author [J. K. Rowling](/wiki/J._K._Rowling) provided £10 million in funding to create the Anne Rowling Regenerative Neurology Clinic, which was officially opened in October 2013. The [Centre for Regenerative Medicine](/wiki/Centre_for_Regenerative_Medicine) (CRM) is a [stem cell](/wiki/Stem_cell) research centre dedicated to the development of [regenerative](/wiki/Regenerative_medicine) treatments, which was opened in 2012. CRM is also home to applied scientists working with the [Scottish National Blood Transfusion Service](/wiki/Scottish_National_Blood_Transfusion_Service) (SNBTS) and Roslin Cells.

[Atrium](/wiki/Atrium_(architecture)) of the [Informatics Forum](/wiki/Informatics_Forum)

In December 2002, the [Edinburgh Cowgate fire](/wiki/Edinburgh_Cowgate_fire) destroyed a number of university buildings, including some 3,000 m2 (32,000 sq ft) of the [School of Informatics](/wiki/School_of_Informatics,_University_of_Edinburgh) at 80 [South Bridge](/wiki/South_Bridge,_Edinburgh). This was replaced with the [Informatics Forum](/wiki/Informatics_Forum) on [Bristo Square](/wiki/Bristo_Square), completed in July 2008. Also in 2002, the [Edinburgh Cancer Research Centre](/wiki/Edinburgh_Cancer_Research_Centre) (ECRC) was opened on the [Western General Hospital](/wiki/Western_General_Hospital) site. In 2007, the [MRC Human Genetics Unit](/wiki/MRC_Human_Genetics_Unit) formed a partnership with the Centre for Genomic & Experimental Medicine and the ECRC to create the Institute of Genetics and Molecular Medicine (renamed the Institute of Genetics and Cancer in 2021) on the same site.

In April 2008, the [Roslin Institute](/wiki/Roslin_Institute) – an [animal sciences](/wiki/Animal_science) research centre known for cloning [Dolly the sheep](/wiki/Dolly_(sheep)) – became part of the Royal (Dick) School of Veterinary Studies. In 2011, the school moved into a new £60 million building on the Easter Bush campus, which now houses research and teaching facilities, and a hospital for small and farm animals.

[Edinburgh College of Art](/wiki/Edinburgh_College_of_Art)

[Edinburgh College of Art](/wiki/Edinburgh_College_of_Art), founded in 1760, formally merged with the university's School of Arts, Culture and Environment on 1 August 2011. In 2014, the [Zhejiang University-University of Edinburgh Institute](/wiki/Zhejiang_University-University_of_Edinburgh_Institute) (ZJE) was founded as an international joint institute offering degrees in biomedical sciences, taught in English. The campus, located in [Haining](/wiki/Haining), Zhejiang Province, China, was established on 15 March 2016.

The university began hosting a [Wikimedian in Residence](/wiki/Wikimedian_in_Residence) in 2016. The residency was made into a full-time position in 2019, with the Wikimedian involved in teaching and learning activities within the scope of the [University of Edinburgh WikiProject](/wiki/Wikipedia:University_of_Edinburgh).

In 2018, the University of Edinburgh was a signatory to the £1.3 billion *Edinburgh and South East Scotland City Region Deal*, in partnership with the UK and Scottish governments, six local authorities and all universities and colleges in the region. The university committed to delivering a range of economic benefits to the region through the *Data-Driven Innovation* initiative. In conjunction with [Heriot-Watt University](/wiki/Heriot-Watt_University), the deal created five innovation hubs: the Bayes Centre, Edinburgh Futures Institute, Usher Institute, Easter Bush, and one further hub based at Heriot-Watt, the National Robotarium. The deal also included creation of the Edinburgh International Data Facility, which performs high-speed data processing in a secure environment.

In September 2020, the university completed work on the *Richard Verney Health Centre* at its central area campus on Bristo Square. The facility houses a health centre and pharmacy, and the university's disability and counselling services. The university's largest expansion in the 2020s was the conversion of some of the historic Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh buildings in Lauriston Place, which had been vacated in 2003 and partially developed into the [Quartermile](/wiki/Quartermile). The £120 million project created a home for the [Edinburgh Futures Institute](/wiki/Edinburgh_Futures_Institute) (EFI), an interdisciplinary hub linking arts, humanities, and social sciences with other disciplines in the research and teaching of complex, multi-stakeholder societal challenges. The EFI officially opened its doors to the public on 4 June 2024.

### Historical links

Edinburgh has several historical links to other universities, chiefly through its influential Medical School and its graduates, who established and developed institutions elsewhere in the world.

- [Columbia University](/wiki/Columbia_University): had its [Medical School](/wiki/Columbia_University_Vagelos_College_of_Physicians_and_Surgeons) founded by [Samuel Bard](/wiki/Samuel_Bard_(physician)), an Edinburgh medical graduate.
- [Dalhousie University](/wiki/Dalhousie_University): Edinburgh alumnus [George Ramsay](/wiki/George_Ramsay,_9th_Earl_of_Dalhousie), the 22nd [Lieutenant Governor of Nova Scotia](/wiki/List_of_lieutenant_governors_of_Nova_Scotia), wanted to establish a non-denominational college in [Halifax](/wiki/Halifax,_Nova_Scotia) open to all. The school was modelled after the University of Edinburgh, which students could attend regardless of religion or nationality.
- [Dartmouth College](/wiki/Dartmouth_College): had its [School of Medicine](/wiki/Geisel_School_of_Medicine) founded by [Nathan Smith](/wiki/Nathan_Smith_(physician,_born_1762)), an alumnus of Edinburgh Medical School.
- [Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_University): had its [Medical School](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School) founded by three surgeons, one of whom was [Benjamin Waterhouse](/wiki/Benjamin_Waterhouse), an alumnus of Edinburgh Medical School.
- [McGill University](/wiki/McGill_University): had its [Faculty of Medicine](/wiki/McGill_University_Faculty_of_Medicine_and_Health_Sciences) founded by four physicians, which included Edinburgh alumni [Andrew Fernando Holmes](/wiki/Andrew_Fernando_Holmes) and [John Stephenson](/wiki/John_Stephenson_(physician)).
- [University of Pennsylvania](/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania): had its [School of Medicine](/wiki/Perelman_School_of_Medicine_at_the_University_of_Pennsylvania) founded by Edinburgh graduate [John Morgan](/wiki/John_Morgan_(physician)), who modelled it after Edinburgh Medical School.
- [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University): had its academic syllabus and structure reformed along the lines of the University of Edinburgh and other Scottish universities by its sixth president [John Witherspoon](/wiki/John_Witherspoon), an Edinburgh theology graduate.
- [University of Sydney](/wiki/University_of_Sydney): founded in 1850 by Sir [Charles Nicholson](/wiki/Charles_Nicholson), a graduate of Edinburgh Medical School.
- [College of William & Mary](/wiki/College_of_William_%26_Mary): the [second-oldest college](/wiki/Colonial_colleges) in the US was founded in 1693 by Edinburgh graduate [James Blair](/wiki/James_Blair_(clergyman)), who served as the college's founding president for fifty years.
- [Yale University](/wiki/Yale_University): had its [School of Medicine](/wiki/Yale_School_of_Medicine) co-founded by [Nathan Smith](/wiki/Nathan_Smith_(physician,_born_1762)), an alumnus of Edinburgh Medical School.

### Central Area

The Central Area is spread around numerous squares and streets in Edinburgh's *Southside*, with some buildings in Old Town. It is the university's oldest area, occupied primarily by the College of Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences and the [School of Informatics](/wiki/School_of_Informatics,_University_of_Edinburgh). The highest concentration of university buildings is around [George Square](/wiki/George_Square,_Edinburgh), which includes [40 George Square](/wiki/40_George_Square) (formerly David Hume Tower), [Appleton Tower](/wiki/Appleton_Tower), [Main Library](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Library), and [Gordon Aikman Lecture Theatre](/wiki/Gordon_Aikman_Lecture_Theatre), the area's largest lecture hall. North of George Square lies the university's [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh) housing [Edinburgh Law School](/wiki/Edinburgh_Law_School), [New College](/wiki/New_College,_Edinburgh) on [The Mound](/wiki/The_Mound) housing the School of Divinity, and [St Cecilia's Hall](/wiki/St_Cecilia%27s_Hall). Some of these buildings are used to host events during the [Edinburgh International Festival](/wiki/Edinburgh_International_Festival) and the [Edinburgh Festival Fringe](/wiki/Edinburgh_Festival_Fringe) every summer.

#### Pollock Halls

Main article: [Pollock Halls of Residence](/wiki/Pollock_Halls_of_Residence)

[St Leonard's Hall](/wiki/St_Leonard%27s_Hall)

Pollock Halls, adjoining [Holyrood Park](/wiki/Holyrood_Park) to the east, is the university's largest residence hall for undergraduate students in their first year. The complex houses over 2,000 students during term time and consists of ten named buildings with communal green spaces between them. The two original buildings, [St Leonard's Hall](/wiki/St_Leonard%27s_Hall) and [Salisbury Green](/wiki/Salisbury_Green), were built in the 19th century, while the majority of Pollock Halls dates from the 1960s and early 2000s. Two of the older houses in Pollock Halls were demolished in 2002, and a new building, Chancellor's Court, was built in their place and opened in 2003. Self-catered flats elsewhere account for the majority of university-provided accommodation. The area also includes the John McIntyre Conference Centre opened in 2009, which is the university's premier conference space.

#### Holyrood

See also: [Moray House School of Education](/wiki/Moray_House_School_of_Education)

The Holyrood campus, just off the [Royal Mile](/wiki/Royal_Mile), used to be the site for *Moray House Institute for Education* until it merged with the university on 1 August 1998. The university has since extended this campus. The buildings include redeveloped and extended Sports Science, Physical Education and Leisure Management facilities at St Leonard's Land linked to the Sports Institute on the [Pleasance](/wiki/Pleasance_(street)). The £80 million O'Shea Hall at Holyrood was named after the former principal of the university Sir [Timothy O'Shea](/wiki/Timothy_O%27Shea) and was opened by [Princess Anne](/wiki/Anne,_Princess_Royal) in 2017, providing a living and social environment for postgraduate students. The Outreach Centre, Institute for Academic Development (University Services Group), and Edinburgh Centre for Professional Legal Studies are also located at Holyrood.

### King's Buildings

Main article: [King's Buildings](/wiki/King%27s_Buildings)

[Royal Observatory, Edinburgh](/wiki/Royal_Observatory,_Edinburgh)

The King's Buildings campus is located in the south of the city. Most of the Science and Engineering College's research and teaching activities take place at the campus, which occupies a 35-hectare site. It includes the [Alexander Graham Bell](/wiki/Alexander_Graham_Bell) Building (for mobile phones and digital communications systems), [James Clerk Maxwell](/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell) Building (the administrative and teaching centre of the [School of Physics and Astronomy](/wiki/School_of_Physics_and_Astronomy,_University_of_Edinburgh) and School of Mathematics), [Joseph Black](/wiki/Joseph_Black) Building (home to the [School of Chemistry](/wiki/School_of_Chemistry,_University_of_Edinburgh)), [Royal Observatory](/wiki/Royal_Observatory,_Edinburgh), [Swann](/wiki/Michael_Swann) Building (the Wellcome Trust Centre for Cell Biology), [Waddington](/wiki/C._H._Waddington) Building (the Centre for Systems Biology at Edinburgh), [William Rankine](/wiki/William_Rankine) Building (School of Engineering's Institute for Infrastructure and Environment), and others. Until 2012, the KB campus was served by three libraries: Darwin Library, James Clerk Maxwell Library, and Robertson Engineering and Science Library. These were replaced by the Noreen and Kenneth Murray Library opened for the academic year 2012/13. The campus also hosts the National e-Science Centre (NeSC), [Scotland's Rural College](/wiki/Scotland%27s_Rural_College) (SRUC), Scottish Institute for Enterprise (SIE), Scottish Microelectronics Centre (SMC), and Scottish Universities Environmental Research Centre (SUERC).

### BioQuarter

Main article: [Edinburgh BioQuarter](/wiki/Edinburgh_BioQuarter)

[Edinburgh BioQuarter](/wiki/Edinburgh_BioQuarter)

The BioQuarter campus, based in the Little France area, is home to the majority of medical facilities of the university, alongside the Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh. The campus houses the Anne Rowling Regenerative Neurology Clinic, [Centre for Regenerative Medicine](/wiki/Centre_for_Regenerative_Medicine), Chancellor's Building, [Euan MacDonald Centre](/wiki/Euan_MacDonald_Centre), and Queen's Medical Research Institute, which opened in 2005. The Chancellor's Building has two large lecture theatres and a medical library connected to the Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh by a series of corridors.

### Easter Bush

The Easter Bush campus, located seven miles south of the city, houses the Jeanne Marchig International Centre for Animal Welfare Education, [Roslin Institute](/wiki/Roslin_Institute), Royal (Dick) School of Veterinary Studies, and Veterinary Oncology and Imaging Centre.

The [Roslin Institute](/wiki/Roslin_Institute) is an animal sciences research institute which is sponsored by [BBSRC](/wiki/Biotechnology_and_Biological_Sciences_Research_Council). The Institute won international fame in 1996, when its researchers Sir [Ian Wilmut](/wiki/Ian_Wilmut), [Keith Campbell](/wiki/Keith_Campbell_(biologist)) and their colleagues created [Dolly the sheep](/wiki/Dolly_(sheep)), the first [mammal](/wiki/Mammal) to be cloned from an adult cell. A year later [Polly and Molly](/wiki/Polly_and_Molly) were cloned, both sheep contained a human gene.

### Western General

The Western General campus, in proximity to the [Western General Hospital](/wiki/Western_General_Hospital), contains the Biomedical Research Facility, Edinburgh Clinical Research Facility, and Institute of Genetics and Cancer (formerly the Institute of Genetics and Molecular Medicine).

## Organisation and administration

### Governance

Further information: [Ancient university governance in Scotland](/wiki/Ancient_university_governance_in_Scotland)

In common with the other [ancient universities of Scotland](/wiki/Ancient_universities_of_Scotland), and in contrast to nearly all other pre-1992 universities which are established by [royal charters](/wiki/Royal_charter), the University of Edinburgh is constituted by the [Universities (Scotland) Acts 1858 to 1966](/wiki/Universities_(Scotland)_Acts_1858_to_1966). These acts provide for three major bodies in the governance of the university: the [University Court](/wiki/University_Court), the [General Council](/wiki/General_council_(Scottish_university)), and the [*Senatus Academicus*](/wiki/Academic_Senate).

#### University Court

The University Court is the university's governing body and the [legal person](/wiki/Legal_person) of the university, chaired by the [rector](/wiki/Rector_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh) and consisting of the principal, [Lord Provost of Edinburgh](/wiki/Lord_Provost_of_Edinburgh), and of [Assessors](/wiki/Assessor_(law)) appointed by the rector, chancellor, [Edinburgh Town Council](/wiki/Edinburgh_Town_Council), General Council, and *Senatus Academicus*. By the Universities (Scotland) Act 1889, it is a body corporate, with perpetual succession and a common seal. All property belonging to the university at the passing of the Act was vested in the Court. The present powers of the Court are further defined in the Universities (Scotland) Act 1966, including the administration and management of the university's revenue and property, the regulation of staff salaries, and the establishment and composition of committees of its own members or others.

#### General Council

The General Council consists of [graduates](/wiki/Alumnus), [academic staff](/wiki/Academic_personnel), current and former University Court members. It was established to ensure that graduates have a continuing voice in the management of the university. The Council is required to meet twice per year to consider matters affecting the wellbeing and prosperity of the university. The Universities (Scotland) Act 1966 gave the Council the power to consider draft ordinances and resolutions, to be presented with an [annual report](/wiki/Annual_report) of the work and activities of the university, and to receive an audited [financial statement](/wiki/Financial_statement). The Council elects the chancellor of the university and three Assessors on the University Court.

#### *Senatus Academicus*

The *Senatus Academicus* is the university's supreme academic body, with its core function being the regulation and supervision of teaching and overall planning of the academic work of the institution. It is chaired by the principal and consists of approximately 300 heads of schools and colleges, heads of departments, and elected professorial and non-professorial academic staff; the *Senatus* also includes 30 elected students. The *Senatus* elects four Assessors on the University Court. The *Senatus* typically meets four times per year; most documents for discussion and each meeting's minutes are accessible to all members of the university.

#### University officials

Main articles: [Chancellor of the University of Edinburgh](/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh), [List of principals of the University of Edinburgh](/wiki/List_of_principals_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh), and [Rector of the University of Edinburgh](/wiki/Rector_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh)

[Princess Anne](/wiki/Anne,_Princess_Royal), current [Chancellor](/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh)

[Sir Peter Mathieson](/wiki/Peter_Mathieson_(nephrologist)), current [Principal and Vice-Chancellor](/wiki/List_of_Principals_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh)

[Simon Fanshawe](/wiki/Simon_Fanshawe), current [Lord Rector](/wiki/Rector_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh)

The university's three most significant officials are its chancellor, rector, and principal, whose rights and responsibilities are largely derived from the Universities (Scotland) Act 1858.

The office of [chancellor](/wiki/Chancellor_(education)) serves as the titular head and highest office of the university. Their duties include conferring degrees and enhancing the profile and reputation of the university on national and global levels. The chancellor is elected by the university's [General Council](/wiki/General_council_(Scottish_university)), and a person generally remains in the office for life. Previous chancellors include former [prime minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom) [Arthur Balfour](/wiki/Arthur_Balfour) and novelist Sir [J. M. Barrie](/wiki/J._M._Barrie). [Princess Anne](/wiki/Anne,_Princess_Royal) has held the position since March 2011 succeeding [Prince Philip](/wiki/Prince_Philip,_Duke_of_Edinburgh). She is also Patron of the university's Royal (Dick) School of Veterinary Studies.

The [principal](/wiki/Principal_(academia)) is responsible for the overall operation of the university in a [chief executive](/wiki/Chief_executive_officer) role. The principal is formally nominated by the Curators of Patronage and appointed by the University Court. They are the President of the [*Senatus Academicus*](/wiki/Academic_Senate) and a member of the University Court [*ex officio*](/wiki/Ex_officio_member). The principal is also automatically appointed vice-chancellor, in which role they confer degrees on behalf of the chancellor. Previous principals include physicist Sir [Edward Appleton](/wiki/Edward_Victor_Appleton) and [religious philosopher](/wiki/Religious_philosophy) [Stewart Sutherland](/wiki/Stewart_Sutherland,_Baron_Sutherland_of_Houndwood). The current principal is [nephrologist](/wiki/Nephrology) [Sir Peter Mathieson](/wiki/Peter_Mathieson_(nephrologist)), who has held the position since February 2018.

The office of [rector](/wiki/Rector_(academia)) is elected every three years by the staff and matriculated students. The primary role of the rector is to preside at the University Court. The rector also chairs meetings of the General Council in absence of the chancellor. They work closely with students and [Edinburgh University Students' Association](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Students%27_Association). Previous rectors include [microbiologist](/wiki/Microbiologist) Sir [Alexander Fleming](/wiki/Alexander_Fleming), and former Prime Ministers Sir [Winston Churchill](/wiki/Winston_Churchill) and [David Lloyd George](/wiki/David_Lloyd_George). The current rector is activist and writer [Simon Fanshawe](/wiki/Simon_Fanshawe), who has held the position since March 2024.

### Colleges and schools

In 2002, the university was reorganised from its nine [faculties](/wiki/Faculty_(division)) into three 'Colleges'. While technically not a [collegiate university](/wiki/Collegiate_university), it comprises the Colleges of [Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences](#Arts,_Humanities_and_Social_Sciences) (CAHSS), [Science & Engineering](#Science_and_Engineering) (CSE) and [Medicine & Vet Medicine](#Medicine_and_Veterinary_Medicine) (CMVM). Within these colleges are 'Schools', which either represent one academic discipline such as Informatics or assemble adjacent academic disciplines such as the School of History, Classics and Archaeology. While bound by College-level policies, individual Schools can differ in their organisation and governance. As of 2021, the university has 21 schools in total.

#### Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences

Department of Psychology building at 7 [George Square](/wiki/George_Square,_Edinburgh)

[Elsie Inglis](/wiki/Elsie_Inglis) Quad at the Old Medical School, currently hosting the [School of History, Classics and Archaeology](/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh_School_of_History,_Classics_and_Archaeology)

The College took on its current name of Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences in 2016 after absorbing the Edinburgh College of Art in 2011. CAHSS offers more than 280 undergraduate degree programmes, 230 taught postgraduate programmes, and 200 research postgraduate programmes. Twenty subjects offered by the college were ranked within the top 10 nationally in the 2022 *Complete University Guide*. It includes the oldest English Literature department in Britain, which was ranked 7th globally in the 2021 *[QS](/wiki/QS_World_University_Rankings) Rankings by Subject* in English Language & Literature. The college hosts Scotland's [ESRC](/wiki/Economic_and_Social_Research_Council) [Doctoral Training Centre](/wiki/Doctoral_Training_Centre) (DTC), the Scottish Graduate School of Social Science. The college is the largest of the three colleges by enrolment, with 26,130 students and 3,089 academic staff.

#### Medicine and Veterinary Medicine

Main article: [University of Edinburgh College of Medicine and Veterinary Medicine](/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh_College_of_Medicine_and_Veterinary_Medicine)

Members of the medical faculty at Edinburgh in the first half of the 19th century. *Seated* (L–R): [J. Y. Simpson](/wiki/James_Young_Simpson), [J. Miller](/wiki/James_Miller_(surgeon)), [J. H. Balfour](/wiki/John_Hutton_Balfour) and [J. H. Bennett](/wiki/John_Hughes_Bennett). *Standing* (L–R): [R. Jameson](/wiki/Robert_Jameson), [W. Alison](/wiki/William_Alison) and [T. S. Traill](/wiki/Thomas_Stewart_Traill).

[Edinburgh Medical School](/wiki/Edinburgh_Medical_School) was widely considered the best medical school in the English-speaking world throughout the 18th century and the first half of the 19th century and contributed significantly to the university's international reputation. Its graduates founded medical schools all over the world, including at five of the seven [Ivy League](/wiki/Ivy_League) universities ([Columbia](/wiki/Columbia_University_Vagelos_College_of_Physicians_and_Surgeons), [Dartmouth](/wiki/Geisel_School_of_Medicine), [Harvard](/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School), [Pennsylvania](/wiki/Perelman_School_of_Medicine_at_the_University_of_Pennsylvania), and [Yale](/wiki/Yale_School_of_Medicine)); those in [McGill](/wiki/McGill_University_Faculty_of_Medicine_and_Health_Sciences), [Montréal](/wiki/Universit%C3%A9_de_Montr%C3%A9al_Faculty_of_Medicine), [Sydney](/wiki/Sydney_Medical_School), and [Vermont](/wiki/Robert_Larner_College_of_Medicine); the [Royal Postgraduate Medical School](/wiki/Royal_Postgraduate_Medical_School) (now part of [Imperial College London](/wiki/Imperial_College_School_of_Medicine)), and the [London School of Medicine for Women](/wiki/London_School_of_Medicine_for_Women) (both now part of [UCL](/wiki/UCL_Medical_School)).

In the 21st century, the medical school has continued to excel, and it is associated with 13 Nobel Prize recipients: seven recipients of the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine) and six of the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry). In 2021, it was ranked third in the UK by [The Times](/wiki/The_Times) University Guide, and the Complete University Guide. In 2022, it was ranked the UK's best medical school by the [Guardian](/wiki/The_Guardian) University Guide, It also ranked 21st in the world by both the [Times Higher Education World University Rankings](/wiki/Times_Higher_Education_World_University_Rankings) and the [QS World University Rankings](/wiki/QS_World_University_Rankings) in 2021.

The [Royal (Dick) School of Veterinary Studies](/wiki/Royal_(Dick)_School_of_Veterinary_Studies) is a world leader in veterinary education, research and practice. The eight original faculties formed four Faculty Groups in August 1992. Medicine and Veterinary Medicine became one of these, and in 2002 became the smallest of the three colleges, with 7,740 students and 1,896 academic staff. The university's teaching hospitals include the [Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh](/wiki/Royal_Infirmary_of_Edinburgh), [Western General Hospital](/wiki/Western_General_Hospital), [St John's Hospital, Livingston](/wiki/St_John%27s_Hospital,_Livingston), [Roodlands Hospital](/wiki/East_Lothian_Community_Hospital), and [Royal Hospital for Children and Young People](/wiki/Royal_Hospital_for_Children_and_Young_People).

#### Science and Engineering

Main article: [University of Edinburgh College of Science and Engineering](/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh_College_of_Science_and_Engineering)

Old Surgical Hospital in [Drummond Street](/wiki/Drummond_Street,_Edinburgh), once part of the [Royal Infirmary](/wiki/Royal_Infirmary_of_Edinburgh), today houses the university's Institute of Geography.

In the 16th century, science was taught as "[natural philosophy](/wiki/Natural_philosophy)" in the university. The 17th century saw the institution of the University Chairs of Mathematics and Botany, followed the next century by Chairs of Natural History, Astronomy, Chemistry and Agriculture. It was Edinburgh's professors who took a leading part in the formation of the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh) in 1783. [Joseph Black](/wiki/Joseph_Black), Professor of Medicine and Chemistry at the time, founded the world's first Chemical Society in 1785. The first named degrees of Bachelor and Doctor of Science was instituted in 1864, and a separate Faculty of Science was created in 1893 after three centuries of scientific advances at Edinburgh. The [Regius Chair in Engineering](/wiki/Regius_Professor_of_Engineering_(Edinburgh)) was established in 1868, and the Regius Chair in Geology in 1871. In 1991 the Faculty of Science was renamed the Faculty of Science and Engineering, and in 2002 it became the College of Science and Engineering. The college has 11,745 students and 2,937 academic staff.

### Finances

In the financial year ending 31 July 2024, the University of Edinburgh had a total income of £1.386 billion (2022/23 – £1.341 billion) and total expenditure of £1.014 billion (2022/23 – £1.181 billion). Key sources of income included £527.2 million from tuition fees and education contracts (2022/23 – £513.7 million), £208.7 million from funding body grants (2022/23 – £210.9 million), £365.2 million from research grants and contracts (2022/23 – £339.5 million), £62.7 million from investment income (2022/23 – £42.2 million) and £25 million from donations and endowments (2022/23 – £55.7 million).

At year end, Edinburgh had endowments of £580.4 million (2023 – £559.8 million) and total net assets of £3.003 billion (2023 – £2.615 billion). It holds the [third-largest endowment](/wiki/List_of_UK_universities_by_endowment) of any university in the UK.

#### Industrial action

Staff at the university engaged in the sector-wide [2018–2023 UK higher education strikes](/wiki/2018%E2%80%932023_UK_higher_education_strikes) called by the [University and College Union](/wiki/University_and_College_Union) over disputes regarding [USS pensions](/wiki/Universities_Superannuation_Scheme), pay, and working conditions. A Marking and Assessment Boycott that commenced on 20 April 2023 was called off on 6 September 2023. However, the UCU voted to continue strike action throughout the rest of September.

In November 2024, Principal Mathieson warned staff of impending redundancies, citing "a multi-million pound increase to our salary bill" he said had been created by the [Labour](/wiki/Labour_Party_UK) administration's National Insurance hike. Following Mathieson's announcement of £140 million in cuts on 25 February 2025, the UCU said that 59% of its members had turned out for a consulative ballot, with 75% willing to strike, and a further 10% prepared to [work to rule](/wiki/Work-to-rule) including refusing to cover for absent colleagues. Mathieson's [annual salary package](/wiki/Peter_Mathieson_(nephrologist)#University_of_Edinburgh) exceeding £418,000 and comparable remunerations of other university executive staff also fell under heavy criticism in light of cuts staff were being asked to absorb. The UCU announced strike action for the university's Open Day in June 2025, and further actions to be taken in September 2025. On 28 October, in a re-ballot of members of the UCU at the university, with a turnout of 60%, 85% backed strike action, and 94% backed work to rule.

## Academic profile

The university is a member of the [Russell Group](/wiki/Russell_Group) of research-led British universities, and the *[Sutton 13](/wiki/Sutton_13)* group of top-ranked universities in the UK. It is the only British university to be a member of both the [Coimbra Group](/wiki/Coimbra_Group) and the [League of European Research Universities](/wiki/League_of_European_Research_Universities), and it is a founding member of [Una Europa](/wiki/Una_Europa) and [Universitas 21](/wiki/Universitas_21), both international associations of research-intensive universities. The university maintains historically strong ties with the neighbouring [Heriot-Watt University](/wiki/Heriot-Watt_University) for teaching and research. Edinburgh also offers a wide range of free online [MOOC](/wiki/Massive_open_online_course) courses on three global platforms [Coursera](/wiki/Coursera), [Edx](/wiki/EdX) and [FutureLearn](/wiki/FutureLearn).

The university is consistently designated as a 'high-tariff' institution by the [Department for Education](/wiki/Department_for_Education), with the average undergraduate entrant to the university in recent years amassing between 155–159 [UCAS Tariff](/wiki/UCAS_Tariff) points in their top three pre-university qualifications – the equivalent of A\*AA to A\*A\*A at [A-Level](/wiki/A-level_(United_Kingdom)). Based on 2022/23 [HESA](/wiki/Higher_Education_Statistics_Agency) entry standards data published in domestic league tables, which include a broad range of qualifications beyond the top three exam grades, the average student at the University of Edinburgh achieved 197 points – the seventh highest in the United Kingdom. It gave offers of admission to 33% of its 18 year old applicants in 2022, the fourth lowest amongst the [Russell Group](/wiki/Russell_Group).

In 2025, excluding courses which received fewer than 50 applications and within the [Edinburgh College of Art](/wiki/Edinburgh_College_of_Art), the most competitive courses for Scottish applicants were Business Studies (18% offer rate), International Business (22%), Law (29%), International Relations (30%), and Nursing (33%). For students from the rest of the UK and Ireland, the most competitive courses were Veterinary Medicine (12%), International Relations (15%), Psychology MA (19%), Nursing (21%), and Psychology BSc (21%). For international students, the most competitive courses were Medicine (10%), Nursing (12%), Social Work (21%), Veterinary Medicine (32%), and Informatics (41%).

For the academic year 2019/20, 36.8% of Edinburgh's new undergraduates were [privately educated](/wiki/Independent_school_(United_Kingdom)), the second-highest proportion among mainstream British universities, behind only [Oxford](/wiki/University_of_Oxford). As of August 2021, it has a higher proportion of female than male students with a male to female ratio of 38:62 in the undergraduate population, and the undergraduate student body is composed of 30% Scottish students, 32% from the rest of the UK, 10% from the EU, and 28% from outside the EU.

### Graduation

See also: [Academic dress of the University of Edinburgh](/wiki/Academic_dress_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh)

Graduation ceremony in McEwan Hall, 2025.

Graduation ceremonies are held in the purpose-built [McEwan Hall](/wiki/McEwan_Hall). At the ceremonies, graduates are 'capped' with the *Geneva Bonnet*, which involves the university's principal tapping them on the head with the cap while they receive their graduation certificate. The velvet-and-silk hat has been used for over 150 years, and legend says that it was originally made from cloth taken from the breeches of 16th-century scholars [John Knox](/wiki/John_Knox) or [George Buchanan](/wiki/George_Buchanan). When the hat was restored in the early 2000s, a label dated 1849 was discovered bearing the name of Edinburgh tailor Henry Banks, casting doubt on the supposed 16th-century origin; however, it is unknown whether Banks manufactured or restored the hat. In 2006, a university emblem that had been taken into space by astronaut and Edinburgh graduate [Piers Sellers](/wiki/Piers_Sellers) was incorporated into the *Geneva Bonnet*.

### Library system

Main article: [Edinburgh University Library](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Library)

Playfair Library Hall in [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh)

Pre-dating the university by three years, Edinburgh University Library was founded in 1580 through the donation of a large collection by Clement Litill, and today is the largest academic library collection in Scotland. The [Brutalist](/wiki/Brutalist_architecture) style eight-storey Main Library building in [George Square](/wiki/George_Square,_Edinburgh) was designed by Sir [Basil Spence](/wiki/Basil_Spence). At the time of its completion in 1967, it was the largest building of its type in the UK, and today is a [category A listed building](/wiki/Category_B_listed_building). The library system also includes many specialised libraries at the college and school level.

In the 2021 [Research Excellence Framework](/wiki/Research_Excellence_Framework) (REF), which evaluated work produced between 2014 and 2021, Edinburgh ranked 4th by research power and 15th by GPA amongst British universities. The university fell four places in GPA when compared to the 2014 REF, but retained its place in research power. 90 per cent of the university's research activity was judged to be 'world leading' (4\*) or 'internationally excellent' (3\*), and five departments – Computer Science, Informatics, Sociology, Anthropology, and Development Studies – were ranked as the best in the UK.

In the 2015 *THE Global Employability University Ranking*, Edinburgh ranked 23rd in the world and 4th in the UK for graduate employability as voted by international recruiters. A 2015 government report found that Edinburgh was one of only two Scottish universities (along with [St Andrews](/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews)) that some London-based elite recruitment firms considered applicants from, especially in the field of financial services and investment banking. When *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* ranked universities based on the employability of graduates as evaluated by recruiters from top companies in 20 countries in 2012, Edinburgh was placed at 42nd in the world and 7th in Britain.

In 2026, Edinburgh was ranked 4th in the UK and 16th in the world universities ranking by [*Time*](/wiki/Time_(magazine)) magazine and [Statista](/wiki/Statista). It was ranked 24th in the world and 5th in the UK by the 2021 [*Aggregate Ranking of Top Universities*](/wiki/College_and_university_rankings#Aggregate_Ranking_of_Top_Universities), a league table based on the three major world university rankings, [*ARWU*](/wiki/Academic_Ranking_of_World_Universities), [*QS*](/wiki/QS_World_University_Rankings) and [*THE*](/wiki/Times_Higher_Education_World_University_Rankings). In the 2022 [*U.S. News & World Report*](/wiki/U.S._News_%26_World_Report_Best_Global_University_Ranking), Edinburgh ranked 32nd globally and 5th nationally. The 2022 [*World Reputation Rankings*](/wiki/Times_Higher_Education_World_Reputation_Rankings) placed Edinburgh at 32nd worldwide and 5th nationwide. In 2023, it ranked 73rd amongst the universities around the world by the *[SCImago Institutions Rankings](/wiki/SCImago_Institutions_Rankings)*.

The disparity between Edinburgh's research capacity, [endowment](/wiki/List_of_universities_in_the_United_Kingdom_by_endowment) and international status on the one hand, and its ranking in national league tables on the other, is largely due to the impact of measures of 'student satisfaction'. Edinburgh was ranked last in the UK for teaching quality in the 2012 [National Student Survey](/wiki/National_Student_Survey), with the 2015 *[Good University Guide](/wiki/Times_Good_University_Guide)* stating that this stemmed from "questions to do with the promptness, usefulness and extent of academic feedback", and that the university "still has a long way to go to turn around a poor position". Edinburgh improved only marginally over the next years, with the 2021 *Good University Guide* still ranking it in the bottom 10 domestically in both teaching quality and student experience. Edinburgh was ranked 122nd out of 128 universities for student satisfaction in the 2022 *[Complete University Guide](/wiki/The_Complete_University_Guide)*, although it was ranked 12th overall. The 2024 *[Guardian University Guide](/wiki/The_Guardian_University_Guide)* ranked Edinburgh 14th overall, but 50th out of 120 universities in teaching satisfaction, and lowest among all universities in satisfaction with feedback.

In the 2022 *Complete University Guide*, 32 out of the 49 subjects offered by Edinburgh were ranked within the top 10 in the UK, with Asian Studies (4th), Chemical Engineering (4th), Education (2nd), Geology (5th), Linguistics (5th), Mechanical Engineering (5th), Medicine (5th), Music (5th), Nursing (1st), Physics & Astronomy (5th), Social Policy (5th), Theology & Religious Studies (4th), and Veterinary Medicine (2nd) within the top 5. The 2021 *THE World University Rankings by Subject* ranked Edinburgh 10th worldwide in Arts and Humanities, 15th in Law, 16th in Psychology, 21st in Clinical, Pre-clinical & Health, 22nd in Computer Science, 28th in Education, 28th in Life Science, 43rd in Business & Economics, 44th in Social Sciences, 45th in Physical Sciences, and 86th in Engineering & Technology. The 2023 *QS World University Rankings by Subject* placed Edinburgh at 10th globally in Arts & Humanities, 23rd in Life Sciences & Medicine, 36th in Natural Sciences, 50th in Social Sciences & Management, and 59th in Engineering & Technology. According to *CSRankings*, computer science at Edinburgh was ranked 1st in the UK and 36th globally, and Edinburgh was the best in [natural language processing](/wiki/Natural_language_processing) (NLP) in the world.

## Student life

### Students' Association

Main article: [Edinburgh University Students' Association](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Students%27_Association)

[The Pleasance](/wiki/The_Pleasance), one of EUSA's main buildings, is a theatre, bar, sports and recreation complex.

Edinburgh University Students' Association (EUSA) consists of the [students' union](/wiki/Students%27_union) and the [students' representative council](/wiki/Students%27_representative_council). EUSA's buildings include [Teviot Row House](/wiki/Teviot_Row_House), [The Pleasance](/wiki/The_Pleasance), [Potterrow Student Centre](/wiki/Potterrow_Student_Centre), [Kings Buildings House](/wiki/King%27s_Buildings#Other_Facilities), as well as shops, cafés and refectories across the various campuses. Teviot Row House is considered the oldest purpose-built student union building in the world. Most of these buildings are operated as [Edinburgh Festival Fringe](/wiki/Edinburgh_Festival_Fringe) venues during August. EUSA represents students to the university and the wider world, and is responsible for over 250 student societies at the university. The association has five [sabbatical](/wiki/Sabbatical) office bearers – a president and four vice presidents. EUSA is affiliated with the [National Union of Students](/wiki/National_Union_of_Students_(United_Kingdom)) (NUS).

### Debate

The University of Edinburgh is home to [the Diagnostic Society of Edinburgh](/wiki/The_Diagnostic_Society_of_Edinburgh) which was founded in 1787. It claims to be the oldest student debating society in the UK. The society is indirectly associated with the university through the students union as an unincorporated society.

### Performing arts

Amateur dramatic societies benefit from Edinburgh being an important cultural hub for [comedy](/wiki/Comedy), [amateur](/wiki/Amateur_Theatre) and [fringe theatre](/wiki/Fringe_Theatre) throughout the UK, most prominently through the [Edinburgh Festival Fringe](/wiki/Edinburgh_Festival_Fringe).

[EMUS Symphony Orchestra](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Music_Society), performing [Mahler](/wiki/Gustav_Mahler)'s [Symphony No. 1](/wiki/Symphony_No._1_(Mahler)) at [Greyfriars Kirk](/wiki/Greyfriars_Kirk)

The [Edinburgh University Music Society](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Music_Society) (EUMS) is a student-run musical organisation, which is Scotland's oldest student's musical society; it can be traced back to a concert in February 1867. It performs three concert series throughout the year whilst also undertaking a programme of charity events and education projects.

The student-run [Bedlam Theatre](/wiki/Bedlam_Theatre), home to the Edinburgh University Theatre Company

The Edinburgh University Theatre Company (EUTC), founded in 1890 as the Edinburgh University Drama Society, is known for running [Bedlam Theatre](/wiki/Bedlam_Theatre), the oldest student-run theatre in Britain and venue for the Fringe. EUTC also funds acclaimed [improvisational comedy](/wiki/Improvisational_theatre) troupe [The Improverts](/wiki/The_Improverts) during term time and the Fringe. Alumni include Sir [Michael Boyd](/wiki/Michael_Boyd_(director)), [Ian Charleson](/wiki/Ian_Charleson), [Kevin McKidd](/wiki/Kevin_McKidd), and [Greg Wise](/wiki/Greg_Wise).

The Edinburgh Studio Opera (formerly Edinburgh University Opera Club) is a student [opera](/wiki/Opera) company in Edinburgh. It performs at least one fully staged opera each year. The Edinburgh University Savoy Opera Group (EUSOG) is an opera and [musical theatre](/wiki/Musical_theatre) company founded by students in 1961 to promote and perform the comic operettas of Sir [William Gilbert](/wiki/W._S._Gilbert) and Sir [Arthur Sullivan](/wiki/Arthur_Sullivan), collectively known as [Savoy Operas](/wiki/Savoy_Opera) after the theatre in which they were originally staged.

The Edinburgh University Footlights are a musical theatre company founded in 1989 and produce two large scale shows a year. One of the founders is the Theatre Producer Colin Ingram. Theatre Parodok, founded in 2004, is a student theatre company that aims to produce shows that are "experimental without being exclusive". They stage one large show each semester and one for the festival.

### Media

[*The Student*](/wiki/Student_(newspaper)) is a fortnightly [student newspaper](/wiki/List_of_student_newspapers_in_the_United_Kingdom). Founded in 1887 by writer [Robert Louis Stevenson](/wiki/Robert_Louis_Stevenson), it is the oldest student newspaper in the United Kingdom. Former writers of the newspaper include politicians [Gordon Brown](/wiki/Gordon_Brown), [Robin Cook](/wiki/Robin_Cook), and [Lord Steel of Aikwood](/wiki/David_Steel). It has been independent of the university since 1992, but was forced to temporarily fold in 2002 due to increasing debts. The newspaper won a number of student newspaper awards in the years following its relaunch.

*[The Journal](/wiki/The_Journal_(student_newspaper))* was an independent publication, established in 2007 by three students and former writers for *The Student.* It was also distributed to other higher education institutions in the city, such as [Heriot-Watt University](/wiki/Heriot-Watt_University), [Edinburgh Napier University](/wiki/Edinburgh_Napier_University), and [Telford College](/wiki/Telford_College). It was the largest such publication in Scotland, with a print run of 10,000 copies. Despite winning a number of awards for its journalism, the magazine folded in 2015 due to financial difficulties.

[FreshAir](/wiki/FreshAir.org.uk), launched on 3 October 1992, is an alternative music [student radio](/wiki/Student_radio) station. The station is one of the oldest surviving student radio stations in the UK, and won the "Student Radio Station of the Year" award at the annual [Student Radio Awards](/wiki/Student_Radio_Association#The_Student_Radio_Awards_(SRAs)) in 2004.

In September 2015, the Edinburgh University Student Television (EUTV) became the newest addition to the student media scene at the university, producing a regular magazine-style programme, documentaries and other special events.

### Sport

Main article: [Edinburgh University Sports Union](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Sports_Union)

Edinburgh University Mountaineering Club at the [cairn](/wiki/Cairn) on [Ciste Dhubh](/wiki/Ciste_Dhubh), 1964

Student sport at Edinburgh consists of clubs covering the more traditional [rugby](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_RFC), [football](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_A.F.C.), [rowing](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Boat_Club) and [judo](/wiki/Judo), to the more unconventional [korfball](/wiki/Korfball), [gliding](/wiki/Gliding) and [mountaineering](/wiki/Mountaineering). In 2021, the university had over 65 sports clubs run by Edinburgh University Sports Union (EUSU).

[The Scottish Varsity](/wiki/The_Scottish_Varsity), known as the "world's oldest varsity match", is a rugby match played annually against the [University of St Andrews](/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews_RFC) dating back over 150 years. Discontinued in the 1950s, the match was resurrected in 2011 and was staged in London at the home of [London Scottish RFC](/wiki/London_Scottish_F.C.). It is played at the beginning of the academic year, and since 2015 has been staged at [Murrayfield Stadium](/wiki/Murrayfield_Stadium) in Edinburgh.

[The Scottish Boat Race](/wiki/Scottish_Boat_Race) is an annual [rowing](/wiki/Rowing_(sport)) race between the [Glasgow University Boat Club](/wiki/Glasgow_University_Boat_Club) and the [Edinburgh University Boat Club](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Boat_Club), rowed between competing [eights](/wiki/Eight_(rowing)) on the [River Clyde](/wiki/River_Clyde) in [Glasgow](/wiki/Glasgow), Scotland. Started in 1877, it is believed to be the third-oldest university boat race in the world, predated by the [Oxford and Cambridge Boat Race](/wiki/The_Boat_Race) and the [Harvard–Yale Regatta](/wiki/Harvard%E2%80%93Yale_Regatta).

Edinburgh athletes have repeatedly been successful at the [Olympic Games](/wiki/Olympic_Games): Sprinter [Eric Liddell](/wiki/Eric_Liddell) won gold and bronze at the [1924 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1924_Summer_Olympics). At the [1948 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1948_Summer_Olympics), alumnus [Jackie Robinson](/wiki/Jackie_Robinson_(basketball,_born_1927)) won a gold medal with the American Basketball team. [Trap shooter](/wiki/Trap_shooting) [Bob Braithwaite](/wiki/Bob_Braithwaite) secured a gold medal at the [1968 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics). Cyclist Sir [Chris Hoy](/wiki/Chris_Hoy) won six gold and one silver medal between 2000 and 2012. Rower Dame [Katherine Grainger](/wiki/Katherine_Grainger) won a gold medal at the [2012 Summer Olympics](/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics), and four further silver medals between 2000 and 2016. Edinburgh was the most successful UK university at the 2012 Games with two gold medals from Hoy and one from Grainger.

### Student activism

There are a number of campaigning societies at the university, including but not limited to the environment and poverty campaigning group [People & Planet](/wiki/People_%26_Planet) and the [Amnesty International](/wiki/Amnesty_International) Society. International development organisations include [Edinburgh Global Partnerships](/wiki/Edinburgh_Global_Partnerships), which was established as a student-led charity in 1990. There is also a significant [left-wing](/wiki/Left-wing_politics) presence on campus, including an [anti-austerity](/wiki/Anti-austerity_movement) group, Edinburgh University [Anarchist](/wiki/Anarchism) Society, [Edinburgh University Socialist Society](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Socialist_Society), Edinburgh [Young Greens](/wiki/Scottish_Green_Party), Feminist Society, [LGBT+ Pride](/wiki/LGBT_Pride), [Marxist](/wiki/Marxism) Society, and Justice for Palestine Society.

Protests, demonstrations and occupations are regular occurrences at the university. The activist group People & Planet took over Charles Stewart House in 2015 and again in 2016 in protest over the university's investment in companies active in arms manufacturing or fossil fuel extraction. In May 2015, a security guard was charged in relation to the occupations.

#### Gaza protest

Edinburgh University student Gaza protest, Old College Quad encampment, May 2024.

In May 2024, [student activists](/wiki/Student_activism) set up a protest camp in the Old College Quad, with some also beginning a [hunger strike](/wiki/Hunger_strike), and demanded divestment from companies they alleged supported the [Israeli invasion of the Gaza Strip](/wiki/Israeli_invasion_of_the_Gaza_Strip_(2023%E2%80%93present)). The campaign, backed by more than 600 staff and the [students' union](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Students%27_Association), specifically targeted the university's investments in [Alphabet Inc.](/wiki/Alphabet_Inc.) and [Amazon](/wiki/Amazon_(company)), and its relationship with investment firm [BlackRock](/wiki/BlackRock). In response, Principal Mathieson emphasised that the university would respect peaceful protest rights, while also defending its investment practices. The university subsequently established a working group to review its investment policies related to "armaments and controversial weapons" and launched a consultation on ethical investing principles. The protest camp was dismantled in June. In March 2025, a student was attacked with a sharp object during a Gaza protest outside the main library; Edinburgh University Students' Association alleged that the university security's slow response was reflective of "the University's attitude towards students showing their support for Palestine." Nevertheless, Vice Principal Colm Harmon condemned the attack, and stated the university was taking the matter seriously. The attacker was arrested and subsequently sentenced to a 22-month jail term.

### Student co-operatives

There are three student-run [co-operatives](/wiki/Cooperative) associated with the University: [Edinburgh Student Housing Co-operative](/wiki/Edinburgh_Student_Housing_Co-operative) (ESHC), providing affordable housing for 106 students; the Hearty Squirrel Food Cooperative, providing local, organic and affordable food to students and staff; and the SHRUB Coop, a swap and re-use hub aimed at reducing waste and promoting [sustainability](/wiki/Sustainability). Of these, only the Hearty Squirrel Co-operative operates on campus. ESHC is based on the Bruntsfield Links south of the University's central campus, and hosts students from all three city universities and Edinburgh College. The SHRUB co-operative was formed partly by University of Edinburgh students but is now run by interested members from across Edinburgh. The co-operatives form part of the [Students for Cooperation](/wiki/Students_for_Cooperation) network.

## In popular culture

The University of Edinburgh has featured prominently in a number of works of [popular culture](/wiki/Popular_culture).

- The events of the [Burke and Hare murders](/wiki/Burke_and_Hare_murders), involving Edinburgh lecturer [Robert Knox](/wiki/Robert_Knox_(surgeon)) and the anatomical department, have made a wide range of appearances in popular culture. They became the basis for [Robert Louis Stevenson](/wiki/Robert_Louis_Stevenson)'s short story *[The Body Snatcher](/wiki/The_Body_Snatcher)* (1884), and most recently in 2010 for *[Burke & Hare](/wiki/Burke_%26_Hare_(2010_film))*, a [black comedy](/wiki/Black_comedy) film starring [Simon Pegg](/wiki/Simon_Pegg) and [Andy Serkis](/wiki/Andy_Serkis). Scenes were filmed at the [old School of Anatomy](/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh_Medical_School).
- Many of [Arthur Conan Doyle](/wiki/Arthur_Conan_Doyle)'s works drew inspiration from his mentors at the university. [Joseph Bell](/wiki/Joseph_Bell), a lecturer and surgeon famous for drawing conclusions from minute observations, became the archetype for Conan Doyle's fictional detective [Sherlock Holmes](/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes). [William Rutherford](/wiki/William_Rutherford_(physiologist)), Conan Doyle's physiology professor, provided the template for [Professor Challenger](/wiki/Professor_Challenger), the protagonist of his science fiction work *[The Lost World](/wiki/The_Lost_World_(Doyle_novel))* (1912). Edinburgh is also Challenger's *[alma mater](/wiki/Alma_mater)* in the books.
- [Dr. Fu Manchu](/wiki/Fu_Manchu), a fictional supervillain created by [Sax Rohmer](/wiki/Sax_Rohmer) in 1912, stated that "I am a doctor of philosophy from Edinburgh, a doctor of law from [Christ's College](/wiki/Christ%27s_College,_Cambridge), a doctor of medicine from [Harvard](/wiki/Harvard_University). My friends, out of courtesy, call me 'Doctor'." In 2010, Fu Manchu's connections with the University where he supposedly obtained a doctorate were investigated in a mockumentary by [Miles Jupp](/wiki/Miles_Jupp) (also an Edinburgh alumnus) for [BBC Radio 4](/wiki/BBC_Radio_4).
- In the movie *[Journey to the Center of the Earth](/wiki/Journey_to_the_Center_of_the_Earth_(1959_film))* (1959), an adaptation of [Jules Verne](/wiki/Jules_Verne)'s [novel of the same name](/wiki/Journey_to_the_Center_of_the_Earth), the protagonist Sir Oliver Lindenbrook is a Professor of Geology at the university. An early scene where Lindenbrook addresses the students is filmed at the central [quadrangle](/wiki/Quadrangle_(architecture)) of [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh).
- The historical film *[Chariots of Fire](/wiki/Chariots_of_Fire)* (1981) is based on the story of Olympic runner and Edinburgh graduate [Eric Liddell](/wiki/Eric_Liddell), and includes scenes filmed outside of [Assembly Hall](/wiki/General_Assembly_Hall_of_the_Church_of_Scotland), New College. Liddell is played by [Ian Charleson](/wiki/Ian_Charleson), who is also an Edinburgh alumnus.
- In the novel *[The Last King of Scotland](/wiki/The_Last_King_of_Scotland)* (1998) by [Giles Foden](/wiki/Giles_Foden), the fictional protagonist Dr. Nicholas Garrigan is a medical doctor recently graduated from Edinburgh. The 2006 [film of the same name](/wiki/The_Last_King_of_Scotland_(film)) stars [James McAvoy](/wiki/James_McAvoy) in the role of Dr. Garrigan with the same background.
- In the American television show *[NCIS](/wiki/NCIS_(TV_series))* (2003–present), the chief medical examiner, [Dr. Donald "Ducky" Mallard](/wiki/Dr._Donald_%22Ducky%22_Mallard) studied medicine at Edinburgh. [Ari Haswari](/wiki/Ari_Haswari), the show's main antagonist for the first two seasons, also studied medicine at Edinburgh.
- In the novel *[One Day](/wiki/One_Day_(novel))* (2009), the lead characters Dexter and Emma meet at their graduation from Edinburgh. A feature film based on the book, also titled *[One Day](/wiki/One_Day_(2011_film))* and starring [Anne Hathaway](/wiki/Anne_Hathaway) and [Jim Sturgess](/wiki/Jim_Sturgess) was released in August 2011, with some scenes filmed at the university. In 2021, [Netflix](/wiki/Netflix) commissioned a limited series adaptation of the movie, also called *[One Day](/wiki/One_Day_(TV_series)),* with filming occurring in the grounds of [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh) in 2022; the series released in February 2024.
- The [BBC](/wiki/BBC) legal drama *[Garrow's Law](/wiki/Garrow%27s_Law)* (2009–2011) was largely filmed in Edinburgh, despite being set in London. [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh) and the [Playfair Library](/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Library) are prominently featured.
- The thriller television series [*Clique*](/wiki/Clique_(TV_series)) (2017–2019) produced by [BBC Three](/wiki/BBC_Three) focuses on two students at the university. The series was shot largely on location in Edinburgh, including [The Meadows](/wiki/The_Meadows,_Edinburgh), [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh), and [Potterrow](/wiki/Potterrow_Student_Centre).
- *[Fast & Furious 9](/wiki/F9_(film))* (2021), partly set in Edinburgh, featured scenes in and around [Old College](/wiki/Old_College,_University_of_Edinburgh) filmed in September 2019.
