# List of British monarchs

This article is about monarchs of Great Britain (1707–1800) and the United Kingdom (1801–present). For earlier monarchs, see [List of legendary kings of Britain](/wiki/List_of_legendary_kings_of_Britain), [List of English monarchs](/wiki/List_of_English_monarchs), and [List of Scottish monarchs](/wiki/List_of_Scottish_monarchs).

British monarchs

[Anne](/wiki/Anne,_Queen_of_Great_Britain) became the first monarch of Great Britain in 1707.

[Edward VIII](/wiki/Edward_VIII) had the shortest reign at 326 days.

[Elizabeth II](/wiki/Elizabeth_II) had the longest reign at 70 years 214 days.

[Charles III](/wiki/Charles_III) is the current King of the United Kingdom.

There have been 13 [British monarchs](/wiki/Monarchy_of_the_United_Kingdom) since the [political union](/wiki/Political_union) of the [Kingdom of England](/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) and the [Kingdom of Scotland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Scotland), which occurred on 1 May 1707 upon the commencement of the [Acts of Union](/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1707). The first British monarch was [Anne](/wiki/Anne,_Queen_of_Great_Britain), who reigned between 1707 and 1714; the current monarch is [Charles III](/wiki/Charles_III), who [acceded to the throne](/wiki/Proclamation_of_accession_of_Charles_III) in September 2022. Although the style of "King of Great Britain" had been in use since the [personal union of England and Scotland](/wiki/Personal_union_of_England_and_Scotland) on 24 March 1603 under [James VI and I](/wiki/James_VI_and_I), the official title came into effect legislatively in 1707 and therefore, apart from Anne, this list of British monarchs does not include the four earlier kings and one queen who ruled as [monarchs of England](/wiki/Monarchs_of_England) and [Scotland](/wiki/Monarch_of_Scotland) at the same time.

On 1 January 1801, the [Kingdom of Great Britain](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain) and the [Kingdom of Ireland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland) merged to create the [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland](/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland). This later became the [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland](/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Northern_Ireland) upon the secession of the [Irish Free State](/wiki/Irish_Free_State) (now the [Republic of Ireland](/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland)) in the 1920s. British monarchs have also held various other titles reflecting their claims outside of the United Kingdom, including [King of Hanover](/wiki/King_of_Hanover), [Emperor of India](/wiki/Emperor_of_India), and [Head of the Commonwealth](/wiki/Head_of_the_Commonwealth). The title of monarch also comes with various secondary titles for land within the United Kingdom, such as the [Duchy of Lancaster](/wiki/Duchy_of_Lancaster).

## Background

Further information: [Union of the Crowns](/wiki/Union_of_the_Crowns)

Before 1603, the [Kingdom of England](/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) and the [Kingdom of Scotland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Scotland) were independent states with different monarchs. Upon [James VI of Scotland](/wiki/James_VI_of_Scotland) inheriting the monarchy of England from his cousin [Elizabeth I](/wiki/Elizabeth_I), however, the two independent countries began sharing a monarch in a [personal union](/wiki/Personal_union) known as the [Union of the Crowns](/wiki/Union_of_the_Crowns). Between November 1606 and July 1607 unification between the two countries was discussed in Parliament. While the English Parliament agreed to certain concessions to the Scots, it refused union. In October 1604, James VI and I declared himself to be the 'King of Great Britain'. The [style](/wiki/Style_(form_of_address)) was used on coins, stamps, and elsewhere, but the [Parliament of England](/wiki/Parliament_of_England) did not use it officially, nor did they consider him the King of a single unified country.

[Anne](/wiki/Anne,_Queen_of_Great_Britain) ascended both thrones on 8 March 1702, upon the death of her [brother-in-law](/wiki/Brother-in-law) and cousin [William III](/wiki/William_III_of_England), becoming Queen of England and Queen of Scotland. In November that year, Anne began negotiations with the [Parliament of Scotland](/wiki/Parliament_of_Scotland) about a possible union of the two countries, but by 1704 they had ended without a deal. In 1706, a new proposal was debated that involved the merger of Scotland and England into a new country called 'Great Britain'. A final version of the proposal was presented to Anne in July that year. In January and March 1707, the [Treaty of Union](/wiki/Treaty_of_Union) was passed by the Scottish and English parliaments respectively, with the union beginning from May.

Despite having eighteen pregnancies, Anne did not produce an heir that survived her or to adulthood. Before union, England had passed the [Act of Settlement 1701](/wiki/Act_of_Settlement_1701) which defined Anne's cousin [Sophia of Hanover](/wiki/Sophia_of_Hanover) and her heirs as Anne's successor in England and Ireland and disqualified [Catholics](/wiki/Catholics) from becoming monarch. However, Scotland had passed its own law, the [Act of Security 1704](/wiki/Act_of_Security_1704), which allowed its parliament to choose an heir upon Anne's death. On union, Great Britain adopted the English succession and the Act of Security was repealed. When Anne died in 1714, she was succeeded by [George I](/wiki/George_I_of_Great_Britain), Sophia of Hanover's eldest son.

## Statistics

The thirteen monarchs consist of ten kings and three queens. Only two monarchs were born outside of the United Kingdom; these were George I and [George II](/wiki/George_II_of_Great_Britain), who were both born in Germany. All but two monarchs died in the United Kingdom: George I died while he was still King, during a trip to his birthplace; [Edward VIII](/wiki/Edward_VIII) (by then Prince Edward, Duke of Windsor) died in [Villa Windsor](/wiki/Villa_Windsor), Paris, while living in [exile](/wiki/Exile) in France having abdicated over 35 years beforehand. The longest reigning monarch was [Elizabeth II](/wiki/Elizabeth_II), who reigned for 70 years and 214 days between 1952 and 2022; the shortest reigning monarch was Edward VIII, who reigned for 326 days between January and December 1936. Only two of the thirteen monarchs have not been crowned as a monarch of Great Britain or the United Kingdom: Anne was crowned in 1702 before the Treaty of Union; and Edward VIII abdicated before he [could be crowned](/wiki/Abandoned_coronation_of_Edward_VIII).

### Other titles

Between [George I](/wiki/George_I_of_Great_Britain) and [William IV](/wiki/William_IV), all monarchs had the additional office of [Elector of Hanover](/wiki/Elector_of_Hanover) (later [King of Hanover](/wiki/King_of_Hanover)). Hanover was a separate kingdom with its own government and army. When [Victoria](/wiki/Queen_Victoria) acceded to the throne in 1837, she could not become the Queen of Hanover *[suo jure](/wiki/Suo_jure)* as it followed [Salic law](/wiki/Salic_law), meaning that it could only be inherited by men through the male-line. Instead, her uncle [Ernest Augustus](/wiki/Ernest_Augustus,_King_of_Hanover) became King of Hanover.

As well as being the monarch of Great Britain (1707–1801) or the United Kingdom (1801–), monarchs have held various other titles *[ex officio](/wiki/Ex_officio)*. On 1 May 1876, Victoria accepted the title of [Empress of India](/wiki/Empress_of_India) from parliament under then-[Prime Minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom) [Benjamin Disraeli](/wiki/Benjamin_Disraeli). Monarchs held this title until [George VI](/wiki/George_VI) issued a Royal Proclamation renouncing it on 22 June 1948, in line with [India's independence from the United Kingdom](/wiki/Indian_Independence_Act_1947). Since 1949, the monarch has instead been the ceremonial [Head of the Commonwealth](/wiki/Head_of_the_Commonwealth). The monarch also holds all titles that have been [merged into the Crown](/wiki/Merged_into_the_Crown), such as [Duke of Lancaster](/wiki/Duke_of_Lancaster), which has been a secondary title of the monarch since 1399, and [Duke of Normandy](/wiki/Duke_of_Normandy), which has been a secondary title of the monarch since 1106.
