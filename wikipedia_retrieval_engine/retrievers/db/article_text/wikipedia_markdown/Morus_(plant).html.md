# Morus (plant)

"Mulberry" redirects here. For other plants called mulberry, see [List of plants known as mulberry](/wiki/List_of_plants_known_as_mulberry). For other uses, see [Mulberry (disambiguation)](/wiki/Mulberry_(disambiguation)).

For the bird genus, see [Gannet](/wiki/Gannet).

***Morus***, a [genus](/wiki/Genus) of flowering plants in the family [Moraceae](/wiki/Moraceae), consists of 19 species of [deciduous](/wiki/Deciduous) trees commonly known as **mulberries**, growing wild and under cultivation in many [temperate](/wiki/Temperate) world regions. Generally, the genus has 64 subordinate taxa, though the three most common are referred to as white, red, and black, originating from the color of their dormant buds and not necessarily the fruit color (*[Morus alba](/wiki/Morus_alba)*, *[M. rubra](/wiki/Morus_rubra)*, and *[M. nigra](/wiki/Morus_nigra)*, respectively), with numerous [cultivars](/wiki/Cultivar) and some taxa currently unchecked and awaiting taxonomic scrutiny. *M. alba* is native to [South Asia](/wiki/South_Asia), but is widely distributed across [Europe](/wiki/Europe), [Southern Africa](/wiki/Southern_Africa), [South America](/wiki/South_America), and [North America](/wiki/North_America). *M. alba* is also the species most preferred by the [silkworm](/wiki/Bombyx_mori). It is regarded as an [invasive species](/wiki/Invasive_species) in Brazil, the United States and some states of Australia.

The closely related genus *[Broussonetia](/wiki/Broussonetia)* is also commonly known as mulberry, notably the [paper mulberry](/wiki/Paper_mulberry) (*Broussonetia papyrifera*).

Despite their similar appearance, mulberries are not closely related to [raspberries](/wiki/Raspberries) or [blackberries](/wiki/Blackberries). All three species belong to the [Rosales](/wiki/Rosales) order. But while the mulberry is a tree belonging to the [Moraceae](/wiki/Moraceae) family (also including the [fig](/wiki/Common_fig), [jackfruit](/wiki/Jackfruit), and other fruits), raspberries and blackberries are [brambles](/wiki/Brambles) and belong to the [Rosaceae](/wiki/Rosaceae) family.

## Description

Mulberries are fast-growing when young, and can grow to 24 metres (79 feet) tall. The [leaves](/wiki/Leaves) are alternately arranged, simple, and often lobed and serrated on the margin. Lobes are more common on juvenile shoots than on mature trees. The trees can be [monoecious](/wiki/Monoecious) or [dioecious](/wiki/Dioecious).

The mulberry fruit is a [multiple](/wiki/Multiple_fruit), about 2–3 centimetres (3⁄4–1+1⁄4 inches) long. Immature fruits are white, green, or pale yellow. The fruit turns from pink to red while ripening, then dark purple or black, and has a sweet flavor when fully ripe.

## Taxonomy

The taxonomy of *Morus* is complex and disputed. [Fossils](/wiki/Fossil) of *Morus* appear in the [Pliocene](/wiki/Pliocene) record of the [Netherlands](/wiki/Netherlands). Over 150 species names have been published, and although differing sources may cite different selections of accepted names, fewer than 20 are accepted by the vast majority of botanical authorities. *Morus* classification is further complicated by widespread [hybridisation](/wiki/Hybrid_(biology)), wherein the hybrids are fertile. Recent chromosome-level genome assemblies have been published for several *Morus* species, including *[Morus alba](/wiki/Morus_alba)*, *[Morus atropurpurea](/wiki/Morus_atropurpurea)*, *[Morus notabilis](/wiki/Morus_notabilis)*, and *[Morus macroura](/wiki/Morus_macroura)*, providing comparative genomic resources for the genus.

## Distribution

Mulberry fruit in [Libya](/wiki/Libya)

Black, red, and white mulberries are widespread in [Southern Europe](/wiki/Southern_Europe), the [Middle East](/wiki/Middle_East), [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia), [Northern Africa](/wiki/Northern_Africa), and the [Indian subcontinent](/wiki/Indian_subcontinent), where the tree and the fruit have names under regional [dialects](/wiki/Dialect).

Black mulberry was imported to Britain in the 17th century in the hopes that it would be useful in the cultivation of silkworms. It was much used in [folk medicine](/wiki/Folk_medicine), especially in the treatment of [tapeworms](/wiki/Tapeworm).

The United States has native red mulberries, as well as imported black and white mulberries. In North America, the white mulberry is considered an invasive exotic and has taken over extensive tracts from native plant species, including the red mulberry.

Mulberries are also widespread in [Greece](/wiki/Greece), particularly in the [Peloponnese](/wiki/Peloponnese), which in the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages) was known as [Morea](/wiki/Morea), deriving from the Greek word for the tree ([μουριά](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BC%CE%BF%CF%85%CF%81%CE%B9%CE%AC), **mouria**).

Australia has two types of native mulberries: *[Hedycarya angustifolia](/wiki/Hedycarya_angustifolia)*, and *[Pipturus argenteus](/wiki/Pipturus_argenteus)*, which are both from different families to [Moraceae](/wiki/Moraceae). The exotic black, red and white *Morus* mulberries are also commonly grown in Australian backyards. White mulberry is considered an [environmental weed](/wiki/Environmental_weed) in the states of [Queensland](/wiki/Queensland) and [New South Wales](/wiki/New_South_Wales).

## Cultivation

A mulberry tree in [England](/wiki/England)

Mulberries can be grown from seed, and this is often advised, as seedling-grown trees are generally of better shape and health. Mulberry trees grown from seed can take up to ten years to bear fruit. Mulberries are most often planted from large cuttings, which root readily. The mulberry plants allowed to grow tall have a [crown](/wiki/Tree_crown) height of 1.5 to 1.8 m (5 to 6 ft)  from ground level and a stem girth of 10–13 cm (4–5 in). They are specially raised with the help of well-grown [saplings](/wiki/Sapling) 8–10 months old of any of the varieties recommended for rainfed areas like S-13 (for red loamy soil) or S-34 (black cotton soil), which are tolerant to drought or soil-moisture stress conditions. Usually, the plantation is raised and in block formation with a spacing of 1.8 by 1.8 m (6 by 6 ft), or 2.4 by 2.4 m (8 by 8 ft), as plant-to-plant and row-to-row distances. The plants are usually pruned once a year during the [monsoon season](/wiki/Monsoon_season) to a height of 1.5–1.8 m (5–6 ft) and allowed to grow with a maximum of 8–10 shoots at the crown.

Mulberry tree [scion wood](/wiki/Scion_(grafting)) can easily be [grafted](/wiki/Grafting) onto other mulberry trees during the winter, when the tree is dormant. One common scenario is converting a problematic male mulberry tree to an allergy-free female tree, by grafting all-female mulberry tree scions to a male mulberry that has been pruned back to the trunk. However, any new growth from below the graft(s) must be removed, as they would be from the original male mulberry tree.

## Toxicity and allergenicity

All parts of the plant besides the ripe fruit can exude a milky sap (latex) which is mildly toxic if ingested, causing digestive distress and, at larger doses, hallucinations. It is also an irritant and may cause a skin rash on contact. Unripe green fruit may cause nausea, cramps, and be [hallucinogenic](/wiki/Hallucinogen). The berries have a [laxative](/wiki/Laxative) effect; too many will cause diarrhea.

Some North American cities have banned the planting of mulberries because of the large amounts of pollen they produce, posing a potential health hazard for some [pollen allergy](/wiki/Pollen_allergy) sufferers. Only the male mulberry trees produce pollen; this lightweight pollen can be inhaled deeply into the lungs, sometimes triggering [asthma](/wiki/Asthma). Conversely, female mulberry trees produce all-female flowers, which draw pollen and dust from the air. Because of this pollen-absorbing feature, all-female mulberry trees have an [OPALS allergy scale](/wiki/OPALS_(Ogren_Plant_Allergy_Scale)) rating of just 1 (lowest level of allergy potential), and some consider it "allergy-free".

## Uses

### Agricultural

The leaves are harvested three or four times a year by a leaf-picking method under rain-fed or semi-arid conditions, depending on the monsoon. The leaves are useful as animal fodder. The tree branches pruned in the fall (autumn; after the leaves have fallen) are cut and used to make durable baskets supporting agriculture and [animal husbandry](/wiki/Animal_husbandry).

### Culinary

As the fruit matures, mulberries change in texture and color, becoming succulent, plump, and juicy, resembling a [blackberry](/wiki/Blackberry). The color of the fruit does not distinguish the mulberry species, as mulberries may be white, lavender or black in color. The fruit of the black mulberry (native to southwest Asia) and the red mulberry (native to eastern North America) have distinct flavors. White mulberry fruits are typically sweet, but not tart, while red mulberries are usually deep red, sweet, and juicy. Black mulberries are large and juicy, with balanced sweetness and tartness. The fruit of the East Asian white mulberry – a species extensively naturalized in urban regions of eastern North America – has a different flavor, sometimes characterized as refreshing and a little tart, with a bit of gumminess to it and a hint of [vanilla](/wiki/Vanilla).

Mulberries are used in pies, tarts, wines, [cordials](/wiki/Cordial_(drink)), and [herbal teas](/wiki/Herbal_tea). [Jams](/wiki/Jam) and [sherbets](/wiki/Sharbat_(beverage)) are often made from the fruit in the [Old World](/wiki/Old_World). In spring, new tender twigs are semisweet and can be eaten raw or cooked.

In [Armenia](/wiki/Armenia), mulberries are common, with wide usage in homemade *[oghi](/wiki/Oghi_(drink))* ([moonshine](/wiki/Moonshine)) [production](/wiki/Moonshine_by_country#Armenia), known as *tti oghi*, and syrup known as doshab.

### Supplement

The fruit and leaves are sold in various forms as [dietary supplements](/wiki/Dietary_supplement).

### Silk industry

A silkworm, *[Bombyx mori](/wiki/Bombyx_mori)*, feeding on a mulberry tree

Mulberry leaves, particularly those of the white mulberry, are ecologically important as the sole food source of the [silkworm](/wiki/Silkworm) (*Bombyx mori*, named after the mulberry genus *Morus*), the [cocoon](/wiki/Cocoon_(silk)) of which is used to make [silk](/wiki/Silk). The [wild silk moth](/wiki/Bombyx_mandarina) also eats mulberry. Other [Lepidoptera](/wiki/Lepidoptera) [larvae](/wiki/Larva)—which include the [common emerald](/wiki/Common_emerald), [lime hawk-moth](/wiki/Mimas_tiliae), [sycamore moth](/wiki/Sycamore_(moth)), and [fall webworm](/wiki/Fall_webworm)—also eat the plant.

The Ancient Greeks and Romans cultivated the mulberry for silkworms; at least as early as 220AD, Emperor [Elagabalus](/wiki/Elagabalus) wore a silk robe. English clergy wore silk vestments from about 1500 onwards. Mulberry and the silk industry played a role in [colonial Virginia](/wiki/Colony_of_Virginia).

### Pigment

Mulberry fruit color derives from [anthocyanins](/wiki/Anthocyanin), which have unknown effects in humans. Anthocyanins are responsible for the attractive colors of fresh plant foods, including orange, red, purple, black, and blue. These colors are water-soluble and easily extractable, yielding natural [food colorants](/wiki/Food_colorant). Due to a growing demand for natural food colorants, they have numerous applications in the food industry.

A cheap and industrially feasible method has been developed to extract anthocyanins from mulberry fruit that could be used as a fabric dye or food colorant of high [color value](/wiki/Color_value). Scientists found that, of 31 Chinese mulberry cultivars tested, the total anthocyanin yield varied from 148 to 2725 mg/L of fruit juice. Sugars, acids, and vitamins of the fruit remained intact in the residual juice after removal of the anthocyanins, indicating that the juice may be used for other food products.

Mulberry [germplasm](/wiki/Germplasm) resources may be used for:

- exploration and collection of fruit yielding mulberry species
- their characterization, cataloging, and evaluation for anthocyanin content by using traditional, as well as modern, means and [biotechnology](/wiki/Biotechnology) tools
- developing an information system about these cultivars and varieties
- training and global coordination of genetic stocks
- evolving suitable breeding strategies to improve the anthocyanin content in potential breeds by collaboration with various research stations in the field of sericulture, plant genetics, and breeding, biotechnology and [pharmacology](/wiki/Pharmacology)

### Paper

During the Angkorian age of the [Khmer Empire](/wiki/Khmer_Empire) of [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia), monks at Buddhist temples made paper from the bark of mulberry trees. The paper was used to make books, known as *[kraing](/wiki/Kraing)*.

[Tengujo](/wiki/Tengujo) is the thinnest paper in the world. It is produced in Japan and made with kozo (stems of mulberry trees). Traditional Japanese [washi paper](/wiki/Washi) is often created from parts of the mulberry tree.

### Wood

The wood of mulberry trees is used for [barrel aging](/wiki/Barrel_aging) of [Țuică](/wiki/%C8%9Auic%C4%83), a traditional Romanian plum brandy.

## Culture

*Mulberry Tree* by [Vincent van Gogh](/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh)

A [Babylonian](/wiki/Babylonia) [etiological myth](/wiki/Etiological_myth), which [Ovid](/wiki/Ovid) incorporated into his *[Metamorphoses](/wiki/Metamorphoses)*, attributes the reddish-purple colour of the mulberry fruits to the tragic deaths of the lovers [Pyramus and Thisbe](/wiki/Pyramus_and_Thisbe). Meeting under a mulberry tree (probably the native *[Morus nigra](/wiki/Morus_nigra)*), Thisbe dies by suicide by sword after Pyramus does the same, he having believed, on finding her bloodstained cloak, that she was killed by a lion. Their splashed blood stained the previously white fruit, and the gods forever changed the mulberry's colour to honour their forbidden love.

In the Old Testament's [1 Maccabees](/wiki/1_Maccabees), the [Seleucids](/wiki/Seleucid_army) used the "blood of grapes and mulberries" to provoke their [war elephants](/wiki/War_elephant) in preparation for battle against [Jewish rebels](/wiki/Maccabees). In the [New Testament](/wiki/New_Testament), [Luke 17:6](/wiki/Luke_17#Verse_6) refers to the power of a little faith to move a mulberry tree into the sea.

The [nursery rhyme](/wiki/Nursery_rhyme) "[Here We Go Round the Mulberry Bush](/wiki/Here_We_Go_Round_the_Mulberry_Bush)" uses the tree in the refrain, as do some contemporary American versions of the nursery rhyme "[Pop Goes the Weasel](/wiki/Pop_Goes_the_Weasel)".

[Vincent van Gogh](/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh) featured the mulberry tree in some of his paintings, notably *[The Mulberry Tree](/wiki/The_Mulberry_Tree_(Van_Gogh))* (*Mûrier*, 1889, now in [Pasadena](/wiki/Pasadena,_California)'s [Norton Simon Museum](/wiki/Norton_Simon_Museum)). He painted it after a stay at an asylum, and he considered it a technical success.
