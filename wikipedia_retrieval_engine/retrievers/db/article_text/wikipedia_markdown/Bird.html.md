# Bird

Several terms redirect here. For other uses, see [Bird (disambiguation)](/wiki/Bird_(disambiguation)), [Birds (disambiguation)](/wiki/Birds_(disambiguation)), [Aves (disambiguation)](/wiki/Aves_(disambiguation)), [Avian (disambiguation)](/wiki/Avian_(disambiguation)), and [Avifauna (disambiguation)](/wiki/Avifauna_(disambiguation)).

**Birds** are a group of [warm-blooded](/wiki/Warm-blooded) [vertebrate](/wiki/Vertebrate) [animals](/wiki/Animal) constituting the [class](/wiki/Class_(biology)) **Aves**, characterised by [feathers](/wiki/Feather), toothless [beaked](/wiki/Beak) jaws, the [laying](/wiki/Oviparity) of [hard-shelled](/wiki/Eggshell) eggs, a high [metabolic](/wiki/Metabolism) rate, a four-chambered [heart](/wiki/Heart), and a strong yet lightweight [skeleton](/wiki/Bird_skeleton). Birds live worldwide and range in size from the 5.5 cm (2.2 in) [bee hummingbird](/wiki/Bee_hummingbird) to the 2.8 m (9 ft 2 in) [common ostrich](/wiki/Common_ostrich). There are over 11,000 living species and they are split into 44 [orders](/wiki/Order_(biology)). More than half are [passerine](/wiki/Passerine) or "perching" birds. Birds have [wings](/wiki/Bird_wing) whose development varies according to species; the only known groups without wings are the extinct [moa](/wiki/Moa) and [elephant birds](/wiki/Elephant_bird). Wings, which are modified [forelimbs](/wiki/Forelimb), gave birds the ability to fly, although further evolution has led to the [loss of flight in some birds](/wiki/Flightless_bird), including [ratites](/wiki/Ratite), [penguins](/wiki/Penguin), and diverse [endemic](/wiki/Endemism) island species. The digestive and respiratory systems of birds are also uniquely adapted for flight. Some bird species of aquatic environments, particularly [seabirds](/wiki/Seabird) and some [waterbirds](/wiki/Water_bird), have further evolved for swimming. The study of birds is called [ornithology](/wiki/Ornithology).

Birds [evolved](/wiki/Evolution_of_birds) from earlier [theropods](/wiki/Theropods), and thus constitute the only known living [dinosaurs](/wiki/Dinosaurs). Likewise, birds are considered [reptiles](/wiki/Reptile) in the modern [cladistic](/wiki/Cladistics) sense of the term, and their closest living relatives are the [crocodilians](/wiki/Crocodilia). Birds are descendants of the primitive [avialans](/wiki/Avialae) (whose members include *[Archaeopteryx](/wiki/Archaeopteryx)*) which first appeared during the [Late Jurassic](/wiki/Late_Jurassic). According to some estimates, modern birds (**Neornithes**) evolved in the [Late Cretaceous](/wiki/Late_Cretaceous) or between the Early and Late Cretaceous (100 Ma) and diversified dramatically around the time of the [Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event](/wiki/Cretaceous%E2%80%93Paleogene_extinction_event) 66 million years ago, which killed off the [pterosaurs](/wiki/Pterosaur) and all non-[ornithuran](/wiki/Ornithuran) dinosaurs.

Many [social species](/wiki/Social_animal) preserve knowledge across generations ([culture](/wiki/Animal_culture#Examples_of_culturally_transmitted_behaviors_in_birds)). Birds are social, communicating with visual signals, calls, and [songs](/wiki/Bird_vocalization), and participating in such behaviour as [cooperative breeding](/wiki/Helpers_at_the_nest) and hunting, [flocking](/wiki/Flocking_(behavior)), and [mobbing](/wiki/Mobbing_(animal_behavior)) of predators. The vast majority of bird species are socially (but not necessarily sexually) [monogamous](/wiki/Monogamy_in_animals), usually for one breeding season at a time, sometimes for years, and rarely for life. Other species have breeding systems that are [polygynous](/wiki/Polygyny_in_animals) (one male with many females) or, rarely, [polyandrous](/wiki/Polyandry_in_nature) (one female with many males). Birds produce offspring by laying eggs which are fertilised through [sexual reproduction](/wiki/Sexual_reproduction). They are usually laid in a nest and [incubated](/wiki/Avian_incubation) by the parents. Most birds have an extended period of parental care after hatching.

Many species of birds are economically important as food for human consumption and raw material in manufacturing, with [domesticated](/wiki/Poultry) and [undomesticated](/wiki/Game_(hunting)) birds being important sources of eggs, meat, and feathers. [Songbirds](/wiki/Songbird), parrots, and other species are popular as pets. [Guano](/wiki/Guano) (bird excrement) is harvested for use as a fertiliser. Birds figure throughout human culture. About 120 to 130 species have become [extinct](/wiki/Bird_extinction) due to human activity since the 17th century, and hundreds more before then. Human activity threatens about 1,200 bird species with extinction, though efforts are underway to protect them. Recreational [birdwatching](/wiki/Birdwatching) is an important part of the [ecotourism](/wiki/Ecotourism) industry.

## Evolution and classification

Main article: [Evolution of birds](/wiki/Evolution_of_birds)

*[Archaeopteryx](/wiki/Archaeopteryx)* is often considered the oldest known true bird.

The first [classification](/wiki/Biological_classification) of birds was developed by [Francis Willughby](/wiki/Francis_Willughby) and [John Ray](/wiki/John_Ray) in their 1676 volume *Ornithologiae*.
[Carl Linnaeus](/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus) modified that work in 1758 to devise the [taxonomic classification](/wiki/Taxonomic_classification) system currently in use. Birds are usually categorised as a [biological class](/wiki/Class_(biology)) in traditional [evolutionary taxonomy](/wiki/Evolutionary_taxonomy). Modern [phylogenetic taxonomy](/wiki/Phylogenetic_taxonomy) places Aves in the [clade](/wiki/Clade) [Theropoda](/wiki/Theropoda), sometimes with a Linnean rank lower than class, such as subclass or infraclass.

### Definition

Aves and a sister group, the order [Crocodilia](/wiki/Crocodilia), contain the only living representatives of the reptile clade [Archosauria](/wiki/Archosauria). During the late 1990s, Aves was most commonly defined [phylogenetically](/wiki/Phylogenetics) as all descendants of the [most recent common ancestor](/wiki/Most_recent_common_ancestor) of modern birds and *[Archaeopteryx lithographica](/wiki/Archaeopteryx)*. However, an earlier definition proposed by [Jacques Gauthier](/wiki/Jacques_Gauthier) gained wide currency in the 21st century, and is used by many scientists including adherents to the *[PhyloCode](/wiki/PhyloCode)*. Gauthier defined Aves to include only the [crown group](/wiki/Crown_group) of the set of modern birds. This was done by excluding most groups known only from [fossils](/wiki/Fossils), and assigning them, instead, to the broader group Avialae, on the principle that a clade based on extant species should be limited to those extant species and their closest extinct relatives.

Gauthier and de Queiroz identified four different definitions for the same biological name "Aves", which is a problem. The authors proposed to reserve the term Aves only for the crown group consisting of the last common ancestor of all living birds and all of its descendants, which corresponds to meaning number 4 below. They assigned other names to the other groups.

1. Aves can mean all [archosaurs](/wiki/Archosaur) closer to birds than to [crocodiles](/wiki/Crocodile) (alternately [Avemetatarsalia](/wiki/Avemetatarsalia))
2. Aves can mean those advanced archosaurs with feathers (alternately [Avifilopluma](/wiki/Avifilopluma))
3. Aves can mean those feathered dinosaurs that fly (alternately [Avialae](/wiki/Avialae))

Under the fourth definition *Archaeopteryx*, traditionally considered one of the earliest members of Aves, is removed from this group, becoming a non-avian dinosaur instead. These proposals have been adopted by many researchers in the field of palaeontology and [bird evolution](/wiki/Bird_evolution), though the exact definitions applied have been inconsistent. Avialae, initially proposed to replace the traditional fossil content of Aves, is often used synonymously with the vernacular term "bird" by these researchers.

Most researchers define Avialae as branch-based clade, though definitions vary. Many authors have used a definition similar to "all [theropods](/wiki/Theropod) closer to birds than to *[Deinonychus](/wiki/Deinonychus)*", with *[Troodon](/wiki/Troodon)* being sometimes added as a second external specifier in case it is closer to birds than to *Deinonychus*. Avialae is also occasionally defined as an [apomorphy-based clade](/wiki/Phylogenetic_nomenclature#Phylogenetic_definitions) (that is, one based on physical characteristics). [Jacques Gauthier](/wiki/Jacques_Gauthier), who named Avialae in 1986, re-defined it in 2001 as all dinosaurs that possessed feathered wings used in flapping [flight](/wiki/Bird_flight), and the birds that descended from them.

Despite being currently one of the most widely used, the crown-group definition of Aves has been criticised by some researchers. Lee and Spencer (1997) argued that, contrary to what Gauthier defended, this definition would not increase the stability of the clade and the exact content of Aves will always be uncertain because any defined clade (either crown or not) will have few synapomorphies distinguishing it from its closest relatives. Their alternative definition is synonymous to Avifilopluma.

Simplified [phylogenetic tree](/wiki/Phylogenetic_tree) showing the relationship between modern birds and other dinosaurs

Based on [fossil](/wiki/Fossil) and biological evidence, most scientists accept that birds are a specialised subgroup of [theropod](/wiki/Theropod) [dinosaurs](/wiki/Dinosaur) and, more specifically, members of [Maniraptora](/wiki/Maniraptora), a group of theropods which includes [dromaeosaurids](/wiki/Dromaeosauridae) and [oviraptorosaurs](/wiki/Oviraptorosauria), among others. As scientists have discovered more theropods closely related to birds, the previously clear distinction between non-birds and birds has become blurred. By the 2000s, discoveries in the [Liaoning](/wiki/Liaoning) Province of northeast China, which demonstrated many small theropod [feathered dinosaurs](/wiki/Feathered_dinosaur), contributed to this ambiguity.

*[Anchiornis huxleyi](/wiki/Anchiornis_huxleyi)* is an important source of information on the early evolution of birds in the [Late Jurassic](/wiki/Late_Jurassic) period.

The consensus view in contemporary [palaeontology](/wiki/Paleontology) is that the flying theropods, or [avialans](/wiki/Avialae), are the closest relatives of the [deinonychosaurs](/wiki/Deinonychosaur), which include dromaeosaurids and [troodontids](/wiki/Troodontid). Together, these form a group called [Paraves](/wiki/Paraves). Some [basal](/wiki/Basal_(phylogenetics)) members of Deinonychosauria, such as *[Microraptor](/wiki/Microraptor)*, have features which may have enabled them to glide or fly. The most basal deinonychosaurs were very small. This evidence raises the possibility that the ancestor of all paravians may have been [arboreal](/wiki/Arboreal), have been able to glide, or both. Unlike *Archaeopteryx* and the non-avialan feathered dinosaurs, who primarily ate meat, studies suggest that the first avialans were [omnivores](/wiki/Omnivore).

The [Late Jurassic](/wiki/Late_Jurassic) *Archaeopteryx* is well known as one of the first [transitional fossils](/wiki/Transitional_fossil) to be found, and it provided support for the [theory of evolution](/wiki/Theory_of_evolution) in the late 19th century. *Archaeopteryx* was the first fossil to display both clearly traditional reptilian characteristics—teeth, clawed fingers, and a long, lizard-like tail—as well as wings with flight feathers similar to those of modern birds. It is not considered a direct ancestor of birds, though it is possibly closely related to the true ancestor.

### Early evolution

See also: [List of fossil bird genera](/wiki/List_of_fossil_bird_genera)

*[Confuciusornis sanctus](/wiki/Confuciusornis_sanctus)*, a Cretaceous bird from China that lived 125 million years ago, is the oldest known bird to have a beak.

Over 40% of key traits found in modern birds evolved during the 60 million year transition from the earliest [bird-line archosaurs](/wiki/Avemetatarsalia) to the first [maniraptoromorphs](/wiki/Maniraptoromorpha), i.e. the first dinosaurs closer to living birds than to *[Tyrannosaurus rex](/wiki/Tyrannosaurus)*. The loss of osteoderms otherwise common in archosaurs and acquisition of primitive feathers might have occurred early during this phase. After the appearance of Maniraptoromorpha, the next 40 million years marked a continuous reduction of body size and the accumulation of [neotenic](/wiki/Neoteny) (juvenile-like) characteristics. [Hypercarnivory](/wiki/Hypercarnivore) became increasingly less common while braincases enlarged and forelimbs became longer. The [integument](/wiki/Integument) evolved into complex, [pennaceous feathers](/wiki/Pennaceous_feather).

The oldest known paravian (and probably the earliest avialan) fossils come from the [Tiaojishan Formation](/wiki/Tiaojishan_Formation) of China, which has been dated to the late [Jurassic](/wiki/Jurassic) period ([Oxfordian](/wiki/Oxfordian_(stage)) stage), about 160 million years ago. The avialan species from this time period include *[Anchiornis huxleyi](/wiki/Anchiornis_huxleyi)*, *[Xiaotingia zhengi](/wiki/Xiaotingia_zhengi)*, and *[Aurornis xui](/wiki/Aurornis_xui)*.

The well-known probable early avialan, *Archaeopteryx*, dates from slightly later Jurassic rocks (about 155 million years old) from [Germany](/wiki/Germany). Many of these early avialans shared unusual anatomical features that may be ancestral to modern birds but were later lost during bird evolution. These features include enlarged claws on the second toe which may have been held clear of the ground in life, and long feathers or "hind wings" covering the hind limbs and feet, which may have been used in aerial maneuvering.

Avialans diversified into a wide variety of forms during the [Cretaceous](/wiki/Cretaceous) period. Many groups retained [primitive characteristics](/wiki/Symplesiomorphy), such as clawed wings and teeth, though the latter were lost independently in a number of avialan groups, including modern birds (Aves). Increasingly stiff tails (especially the outermost half) can be seen in the evolution of maniraptoromorphs, and this process culminated in the appearance of the [pygostyle](/wiki/Pygostyle), an ossification of fused tail vertebrae. In the late Cretaceous, about 100 million years ago, the ancestors of all modern birds evolved a more open pelvis, allowing them to lay larger eggs compared to body size. Around 95 million years ago, they evolved a better sense of smell.

A third stage of bird evolution starting with [Ornithothoraces](/wiki/Ornithothoraces) (the "bird-chested" avialans) can be associated with the refining of aerodynamics and flight capabilities, and the loss or co-ossification of several skeletal features. Particularly significant are the development of an enlarged, [keeled](/wiki/Keel_(bird_anatomy)) sternum and the [alula](/wiki/Alula), and the loss of grasping hands.

*[Ichthyornis](/wiki/Ichthyornis)*, which lived 93 million years ago, was the first known prehistoric bird relative preserved with teeth.

The first large, diverse lineage of short-tailed avialans to evolve were the [Enantiornithes](/wiki/Enantiornithes), or "opposite birds", so named because the construction of their shoulder bones was in reverse to that of modern birds. Enantiornithes occupied a wide array of [ecological niches](/wiki/Ecological_niche), from sand-probing shorebirds and fish-eaters to tree-dwelling forms and seed-eaters. While they were the dominant group of avialans during the Cretaceous period, Enantiornithes became extinct along with many other dinosaur groups at the end of the [Mesozoic](/wiki/Mesozoic) era.

Many species of the second major avialan lineage to diversify, the [Euornithes](/wiki/Euornithes) (meaning "true birds", because they include the ancestors of modern birds), were semi-aquatic and specialised in eating fish and other small aquatic organisms. Unlike the Enantiornithes, which dominated land-based and arboreal habitats, most early euornithians lacked [perching](/wiki/Perching_bird) adaptations and likely included shorebird-like species, waders, and swimming and diving species.

The latter included the superficially [gull](/wiki/Gull)-like *[Ichthyornis](/wiki/Ichthyornis)* and the [Hesperornithiformes](/wiki/Hesperornithiformes), which became so well adapted to hunting fish in marine environments that they lost the ability to fly and became primarily aquatic. The early euornithians also saw the development of many traits associated with modern birds, like strongly keeled breastbones, toothless, beaked portions of their jaws (though most non-avian euornithians retained teeth in other parts of the jaws). Euornithes also included the first avialans to develop true [pygostyle](/wiki/Pygostyle) and a fully mobile fan of tail feathers, which may have replaced the "hind wing" as the primary mode of aerial maneuverability and braking in flight.

A study on [mosaic evolution](/wiki/Mosaic_evolution) in the avian skull found that the [last common ancestor](/wiki/Most_recent_common_ancestor) of all Neornithes might have had a beak similar to that of the modern [hook-billed vanga](/wiki/Hook-billed_vanga) and a skull similar to that of the [Eurasian golden oriole](/wiki/Eurasian_golden_oriole). As both species are small aerial and canopy foraging omnivores, a similar ecological niche was inferred for this hypothetical ancestor.

### Diversification of modern birds

Most studies agree on a [Cretaceous](/wiki/Cretaceous) age for the most recent common ancestor of modern birds but estimates range from the Early Cretaceous to the latest Cretaceous. Similarly, there is no agreement on whether most of the early diversification of modern birds occurred in the Cretaceous and associated with breakup of the supercontinent [Gondwana](/wiki/Gondwana) or occurred later and potentially as a consequence of the [Cretaceous–Palaeogene extinction event](/wiki/Cretaceous%E2%80%93Paleogene_extinction_event). This disagreement is in part caused by a divergence in the evidence; most molecular dating studies suggests a Cretaceous [evolutionary radiation](/wiki/Evolutionary_radiation), while fossil evidence points to a Cenozoic radiation (the so-called 'rocks' versus 'clocks' controversy).

The discovery in 2005 of *[Vegavis](/wiki/Vegavis)* from the [Maastrichtian](/wiki/Maastrichtian), the last stage of the [Late Cretaceous](/wiki/Late_Cretaceous), proved that the diversification of modern birds started before the [Cenozoic](/wiki/Cenozoic) era. The affinities of an earlier fossil, the possible [galliform](/wiki/Galliformes) *[Austinornis](/wiki/Austinornis) lentus*, dated to about 85 million years ago, are still too controversial to provide a fossil evidence of modern bird diversification. In 2020, *[Asteriornis](/wiki/Asteriornis)* from the Maastrichtian was described, it appears to be a close relative of [Galloanserae](/wiki/Galloanserae), the earliest diverging lineage within Neognathae.

Attempts to reconcile molecular and fossil evidence using genomic-scale DNA data and comprehensive fossil information have not resolved the controversy. However, a 2015 estimate that used a new method for calibrating [molecular clocks](/wiki/Molecular_clocks) confirmed that while modern birds originated early in the Late Cretaceous, likely in Western [Gondwana](/wiki/Gondwana), a pulse of diversification in all major groups occurred around the Cretaceous–Palaeogene extinction event. Modern birds would have expanded from West Gondwana through two routes. One route was an Antarctic interchange in the Paleogene. The other route was probably via Paleocene land bridges between South America and North America, which allowed for the rapid expansion and diversification of Neornithes into the [Holarctic](/wiki/Holarctic) and [Paleotropics](/wiki/Paleotropics). On the other hand, the occurrence of *Asteriornis* in the Northern Hemisphere suggest that Neornithes dispersed out of East Gondwana before the Paleocene.

### Classification of bird orders

See also: [List of birds](/wiki/List_of_birds)

All modern birds lie within the [crown group](/wiki/Crown_group) Aves (alternately Neornithes), which has two subdivisions: the [Palaeognathae](/wiki/Palaeognathae), which includes the flightless [ratites](/wiki/Ratite) (such as the [ostriches](/wiki/Ostrich)) and the weak-flying [tinamous](/wiki/Tinamou), and the extremely diverse [Neognathae](/wiki/Neognathae), containing all other birds. These two subdivisions have variously been given the [rank](/wiki/Taxonomic_rank) of [superorder](/wiki/Superorder), cohort, or infraclass. The number of known living bird species is around 11,000 although sources may differ in their precise numbers.

The classification of birds is a contentious issue. [Sibley](/wiki/Charles_Sibley) and [Ahlquist](/wiki/Jon_Ahlquist)'s *Phylogeny and Classification of Birds* (1990) is a landmark work on the subject. Most evidence seems to suggest the assignment of orders is accurate, but scientists disagree about the relationships among the orders themselves; evidence from modern bird anatomy, fossils and DNA have all been brought to bear on the problem, but no strong consensus has emerged. Fossil and molecular evidence from the 2010s is providing an increasingly clear picture of the evolution of modern bird orders.

### Genomics

See also: [list of sequenced animal genomes § Birds](/wiki/List_of_sequenced_animal_genomes#Birds)

In 2010, the [genome](/wiki/Genome) had been sequenced for only two birds, the [chicken](/wiki/Chicken) and the [zebra finch](/wiki/Zebra_finch). As of 2022, the genomes of 542 species of birds had been completed. At least one genome has been sequenced from every order. These include at least one species in about 90% of extant avian families (218 out of 236 families recognised by the *Howard and Moore Checklist*).

Being able to sequence and compare whole genomes gives researchers many types of information, about genes, the DNA that regulates the genes, and their evolutionary history. This has led to reconsideration of some of the classifications that were based solely on the identification of protein-coding genes. Waterbirds such as [pelicans](/wiki/Pelicans) and [flamingos](/wiki/Flamingos), for example, may have in common specific adaptations suited to their environment that were developed independently.

## Distribution

See also: [Lists of birds by region](/wiki/Lists_of_birds_by_region) and [List of birds by population](/wiki/List_of_birds_by_population)

The range of the [house sparrow](/wiki/House_sparrow) has expanded dramatically due to human activities.

Birds live and breed in most terrestrial habitats and on all seven continents, reaching their southern extreme in the [snow petrel](/wiki/Snow_petrel)'s breeding colonies up to 440 kilometres (270 mi) inland in [Antarctica](/wiki/Antarctica). The highest bird [diversity](/wiki/Biodiversity) occurs in tropical regions. It was earlier thought that this high diversity was the result of higher [speciation](/wiki/Speciation) rates in the tropics; however studies from the 2000s found higher speciation rates in the high latitudes that were offset by greater [extinction](/wiki/Extinction) rates than in the tropics. Many species migrate annually over great distances and across oceans; several families of birds have adapted to life both on the world's oceans and in them, and some [seabird](/wiki/Seabird) species come ashore only to breed, while some [penguins](/wiki/Penguin) have been recorded diving up to 300 metres (980 ft) deep.

Many bird species have established breeding populations in areas to which they have been [introduced](/wiki/Introduced_species) by humans. Some of these introductions have been deliberate; the [ring-necked pheasant](/wiki/Ring-necked_pheasant), for example, has been introduced around the world as a [game bird](/wiki/Game_bird). Others have been accidental, such as the establishment of wild [monk parakeets](/wiki/Monk_parakeet) in several North American cities after their escape from captivity. Some species, including [cattle egret](/wiki/Cattle_egret), [yellow-headed caracara](/wiki/Yellow-headed_caracara) and [galah](/wiki/Galah), have [spread naturally](/wiki/Avian_range_expansion) far beyond their original ranges as [agricultural expansion](/wiki/Agriculture) created alternative habitats although modern practices of intensive agriculture have negatively impacted farmland bird populations.

Compared with other vertebrates, birds have a [body plan](/wiki/Body_plan) that shows many unusual adaptations, mostly to facilitate [flight](/wiki/Bird_flight).

### Skeletal system

Main article: [Bird anatomy § Skeletal system](/wiki/Bird_anatomy#Skeletal_system)

The skeleton consists of very lightweight bones. They have large air-filled cavities (called pneumatic cavities) which connect with the [respiratory system](/wiki/Respiratory_system). The skull bones in adults are fused and do not show [cranial sutures](/wiki/Cranial_sutures). The [orbital cavities](/wiki/Orbit_(anatomy)) that house the eyeballs are large and separated from each other by a bony [septum](/wiki/Septum) (partition). The [spine](/wiki/Vertebral_column) has cervical, thoracic, lumbar and caudal regions with the number of cervical (neck) vertebrae highly variable and especially flexible, but movement is reduced in the anterior [thoracic vertebrae](/wiki/Thoracic_vertebrae) and absent in the later vertebrae. The last few are fused with the [pelvis](/wiki/Pelvis) to form the [synsacrum](/wiki/Synsacrum). The ribs are flattened and the [sternum](/wiki/Sternum) is keeled for the attachment of flight muscles except in the flightless bird orders. The forelimbs are modified into wings. The wings are more or less developed depending on the species; the only known groups that lost their wings are the [extinct](/wiki/Extinct) [moa](/wiki/Moa) and [elephant birds](/wiki/Elephant_bird).

### Excretory system

Like [reptiles](/wiki/Reptile), birds are primarily [uricotelic](/wiki/Metabolic_waste#Uricotelism); that is, their [kidneys](/wiki/Kidney) extract [nitrogenous waste](/wiki/Nitrogenous_waste) from their bloodstream and excrete it as [uric acid](/wiki/Uric_acid), instead of [urea](/wiki/Urea) or [ammonia](/wiki/Ammonia), through the ureters into the intestine. Birds do not have a [urinary bladder](/wiki/Urinary_bladder) or external urethral opening. With the exception of the [ostrich](/wiki/Ostrich#Description), uric acid is excreted along with faeces as a semisolid waste. However, birds such as hummingbirds can be facultatively ammonotelic, excreting most of the nitrogenous wastes as ammonia. They also excrete [creatine](/wiki/Creatine), rather than [creatinine](/wiki/Creatinine) like mammals. This material, as well as the output of the intestines, emerges from the bird's [cloaca](/wiki/Cloaca). The cloaca is a multi-purpose opening: waste is expelled through it, most birds mate by [joining cloaca](/wiki/Bird_anatomy#Reproduction), and females lay eggs from it. In addition, many species of birds regurgitate [pellets](/wiki/Pellet_(ornithology)).

It is a common but not universal feature of [altricial](/wiki/Altriciality) [passerine](/wiki/Passerine) nestlings (born helpless, under constant parental care) that instead of excreting directly into the nest, they produce a [fecal sac](/wiki/Fecal_sac). This is a mucus-covered pouch that allows parents to either dispose of the waste outside the nest or to recycle the waste through their own digestive system.

Adult [eastern phoebe](/wiki/Eastern_phoebe) removing [fecal sac](/wiki/Fecal_sac) of a fledgling

### Reproductive system

Most male birds do not have [intromittent](/wiki/Intromittent_organ) penises. Males within [Palaeognathae](/wiki/Palaeognathae) (with the exception of the [kiwis](/wiki/Kiwi_(bird))), the [Anseriformes](/wiki/Anseriformes) (with the exception of [screamers](/wiki/Screamer)), and in rudimentary forms in [Galliformes](/wiki/Galliformes) (but fully developed in [Cracidae](/wiki/Cracidae)) possess a [penis](/wiki/Bird_penis), which is never present in [Neoaves](/wiki/Neoaves). Its length is thought to be related to [sperm competition](/wiki/Sperm_competition) and it fills with lymphatic fluid instead of blood when erect. When not copulating, it is hidden within the [proctodeum](/wiki/Proctodeum) compartment within the cloaca, just inside the vent. Female birds have [sperm storage](/wiki/Female_sperm_storage) tubules that allow sperm to remain viable long after copulation, a hundred days in some species. Sperm from multiple males may [compete](/wiki/Sperm_competition) through this mechanism. Most female birds have a single [ovary](/wiki/Ovary) and a single [oviduct](/wiki/Oviduct), both on the left side, but there are exceptions: species in at least 16 different orders of birds have two ovaries. Even these species, however, tend to have a single oviduct. It has been speculated that this might be an adaptation to flight, but males have two testes, and it is also observed that the gonads in both sexes decrease dramatically in size outside the breeding season. Also, terrestrial birds generally have a single ovary, as does the [platypus](/wiki/Platypus), an egg-laying mammal. A more likely explanation is that the egg develops a shell while passing through the oviduct over a period of about a day, so that if two eggs were to develop at the same time, there would be a risk to survival. While rare, mostly abortive, [parthenogenesis](/wiki/Parthenogenesis) is not unknown in birds and eggs can be [diploid](/wiki/Ploidy#Diploid), [automictic](/wiki/Automixis) and results in male offspring.

Birds are solely [gonochoric](/wiki/Gonochorism), meaning they have two sexes: either [female](/wiki/Female) or [male](/wiki/Male). The sex of birds follows the [Z and W sex-determination system](/wiki/ZW_sex-determination_system), where the [ovum](/wiki/Egg_cell) determines the sex of the offspring. Male birds have two Z chromosomes (ZZ), and female birds have a W chromosome and a Z chromosome (WZ). In [mammals](/wiki/Mammal), by contrast, the [sperm](/wiki/Sperm) determines the sex of the offspring in the [X and Y sex-determination](/wiki/XY_sex-determination_system) system. A complex system of [disassortative mating](/wiki/Disassortative_mating) with two morphs is involved in the [white-throated sparrow](/wiki/White-throated_sparrow) *Zonotrichia albicollis*, where white- and tan-browed morphs of opposite sex pair, making it appear as if four sexes were involved since any individual is compatible with only a fourth of the population. In all species of birds, an individual's sex is determined at fertilisation. One 2007 study claimed to demonstrate [temperature-dependent sex determination](/wiki/Temperature-dependent_sex_determination) among the [Australian brushturkey](/wiki/Australian_brushturkey), for which higher temperatures during incubation resulted in a higher female-to-male [sex ratio](/wiki/Sex_ratio). This, however, was later proven to not be the case. These birds do not exhibit temperature-dependent sex determination, but temperature-dependent sex mortality.

### Respiratory and circulatory systems

Birds have one of the most complex [respiratory systems](/wiki/Respiratory_system) of all animal groups. Upon inhalation, 75% of the fresh air bypasses the lungs and flows directly into a posterior [air sac](/wiki/Parabronchi) which extends from the lungs and connects with air spaces in the bones and fills them with air. The other 25% of the air goes directly into the lungs. When the bird exhales, the used air flows out of the lungs and the stored fresh air from the posterior air sac is simultaneously forced into the lungs. Thus, a bird's lungs receive a constant supply of fresh air during both inhalation and exhalation. Sound production is achieved using the [syrinx](/wiki/Syrinx_(biology)), a muscular chamber incorporating multiple tympanic membranes which diverges from the lower end of the trachea; the trachea being elongated in some species, increasing the volume of vocalisations and the perception of the bird's size.

In birds, the main arteries taking blood away from the heart originate from the right [aortic arch](/wiki/Aortic_arches) (or pharyngeal arch), unlike in the mammals where the left aortic arch forms this part of the [aorta](/wiki/Aorta). The postcava receives blood from the limbs via the renal portal system. Unlike in mammals, the circulating [red blood cells](/wiki/Red_blood_cells) in birds retain their [nucleus](/wiki/Cell_nucleus).

#### Heart type and features

[Didactic model](/wiki/Educational_toy) of an avian heart

The avian circulatory system is driven by a four-chambered, myogenic heart contained in a fibrous pericardial sac. This pericardial sac is filled with a [serous fluid](/wiki/Serous_fluid) for lubrication. The heart itself is divided into a right and left half, each with an [atrium](/wiki/Atrium_(heart)) and [ventricle](/wiki/Ventricle_(heart)). The atrium and ventricles of each side are separated by [atrioventricular valves](/wiki/Atrioventricular_valves) which prevent back flow from one chamber to the next during contraction. Being myogenic, the heart's pace is maintained by pacemaker cells found in the sinoatrial node, located on the right atrium.

The [sinoatrial node](/wiki/Sinoatrial_node) uses calcium to cause a [depolarising](/wiki/Depolarization) [signal transduction pathway](/wiki/Signal_transduction_pathway) from the atrium through right and left atrioventricular bundle which communicates contraction to the ventricles. The avian heart also consists of muscular arches that are made up of thick bundles of muscular layers. Much like a mammalian heart, the avian heart is composed of [endocardial](/wiki/Endocardial), [myocardial](/wiki/Myocardial) and [epicardial](/wiki/Epicardial) layers. The atrium walls tend to be thinner than the ventricle walls, due to the intense ventricular contraction used to pump oxygenated blood throughout the body. Avian hearts are generally larger than mammalian hearts when compared to body mass. This adaptation allows more blood to be pumped to meet the high metabolic need associated with flight.

#### Organisation

Birds have a very efficient system for diffusing oxygen into the blood; birds have a ten times greater surface area to [gas exchange](/wiki/Gas_exchange) volume than mammals. As a result, birds have more blood in their capillaries per unit of volume of lung than a mammal. The arteries are composed of thick elastic muscles to withstand the pressure of the ventricular contractions, and become more rigid as they move away from the heart. Blood moves through the arteries, which undergo [vasoconstriction](/wiki/Vasoconstriction), and into arterioles which act as a transportation system to distribute primarily oxygen as well as nutrients to all tissues of the body. As the arterioles move away from the heart and into individual organs and tissues they are further divided to increase surface area and slow blood flow. Blood travels through the arterioles and moves into the capillaries where gas exchange can occur.

Capillaries are organised into capillary beds in tissues; it is here that blood exchanges oxygen for carbon dioxide waste. In the capillary beds, blood flow is slowed to allow maximum [diffusion](/wiki/Diffusion) of oxygen into the tissues. Once the blood has become deoxygenated, it travels through venules then veins and back to the heart. Veins, unlike arteries, are thin and rigid as they do not need to withstand extreme pressure. As blood travels through the venules to the veins a funneling occurs called [vasodilation](/wiki/Vasodilation) bringing blood back to the heart. Once the blood reaches the heart, it moves first into the right atrium, then the right ventricle to be pumped through the lungs for further gas exchange of carbon dioxide waste for oxygen. Oxygenated blood then flows from the lungs through the left atrium to the left ventricle where it is pumped out to the body.

### Nervous system

Main article: [Bird anatomy § Nervous system](/wiki/Bird_anatomy#Nervous_system)

The [nervous system](/wiki/Nervous_system) is large relative to the bird's size. The most developed part of the [brain of birds](/wiki/Avian_brain) is the one that controls the flight-related functions, while the [cerebellum](/wiki/Cerebellum) coordinates movement and the [cerebrum](/wiki/Cerebrum) controls behaviour patterns, navigation, mating and [nest](/wiki/Bird_nest) building. Most birds have a poor [sense of smell](/wiki/Olfaction) with notable exceptions including [kiwis](/wiki/Kiwi_(bird)), [New World vultures](/wiki/New_World_vulture) and [tubenoses](/wiki/Procellariiformes). The [avian visual system](/wiki/Bird_vision) is usually highly developed. Water birds have special flexible lenses, allowing [accommodation](/wiki/Accommodation_(eye)) for vision in air and water. Some species also have dual [fovea](/wiki/Fovea_centralis). Birds are [tetrachromatic](/wiki/Tetrachromacy), possessing [ultraviolet](/wiki/Ultraviolet) (UV) sensitive [cone cells](/wiki/Cone_cell) in the eye as well as green, red and blue ones. They also have [double cones](/wiki/Double_cone_(biology)), likely to mediate [achromatic vision](/wiki/Monochromacy).

The [nictitating membrane](/wiki/Nictitating_membrane) as it covers the eye of a [masked lapwing](/wiki/Masked_lapwing)

Many birds show plumage patterns in [ultraviolet](/wiki/Ultraviolet) that are invisible to the human eye; some birds whose sexes appear similar to the naked eye are distinguished by the presence of ultraviolet reflective patches on their feathers. Male [blue tits](/wiki/Blue_tit) have an ultraviolet reflective crown patch which is displayed in courtship by posturing and raising of their nape feathers. Ultraviolet light is also used in foraging—[kestrels](/wiki/Kestrel) have been shown to search for prey by detecting the UV fluorescent urine trail marks left on the ground by rodents. With the exception of pigeons and a few other species, the eyelids of birds are not used in blinking. Instead the eye is lubricated by the [nictitating membrane](/wiki/Nictitating_membrane), a third eyelid that moves horizontally. The nictitating membrane also covers the eye and acts as a [contact lens](/wiki/Contact_lens) in many aquatic birds. The bird [retina](/wiki/Retina) has a fan shaped blood supply system called the [pecten](/wiki/Pecten_oculi).

[Eyes](/wiki/Eye) of most birds are large, not very round and capable of only limited movement in the orbits, typically 10–20°. Birds with eyes on the sides of their heads have a wide [visual field](/wiki/Visual_field), while birds with eyes on the front of their heads, such as owls, have [binocular vision](/wiki/Binocular_vision) and can estimate the [depth of field](/wiki/Depth_of_field). The avian [ear](/wiki/Ear) lacks external [pinnae](/wiki/Pinna_(anatomy)) but is covered by feathers, although in some birds, such as the *[Asio](/wiki/Asio)*, *[Bubo](/wiki/Horned_owl)* and *[Otus](/wiki/Scops_owl)* [owls](/wiki/Owl), these feathers form tufts which resemble ears. The [inner ear](/wiki/Inner_ear) has a [cochlea](/wiki/Cochlea), but it is not a spiral as in mammals. Several species have been demonstrated to hear infrasound (below 20 Hz) and a few cave-dwelling swifts and oilbirds emit ultrasound (above 20 kHz) and [echolocate](/wiki/Animal_echolocation) in darkness.

### Defence and intraspecific combat

A few species are able to use chemical defences against predators; some [Procellariiformes](/wiki/Procellariiformes) can eject an unpleasant [stomach oil](/wiki/Stomach_oil) against an aggressor, and some species of [pitohuis](/wiki/Pitohui) from [New Guinea](/wiki/New_Guinea) have a powerful [neurotoxin](/wiki/Neurotoxin) in their skin and feathers.

A lack of field observations limit our knowledge, but intraspecific conflicts are known to sometimes result in injury or death. The screamers ([Anhimidae](/wiki/Anhimidae)), some jacanas (*[Jacana](/wiki/Jacana_(genus))*, *[Hydrophasianus](/wiki/Hydrophasianus)*), the spur-winged goose (*[Plectropterus](/wiki/Plectropterus)*), the torrent duck (*[Merganetta](/wiki/Merganetta)*) and nine species of lapwing (*[Vanellus](/wiki/Vanellus)*) use a sharp spur on the wing as a weapon. The steamer ducks (*[Tachyeres](/wiki/Tachyeres)*), geese and swans (*[Anserinae](/wiki/Anserinae)*), the solitaire (*[Pezophaps](/wiki/Pezophaps)*), sheathbills (*[Chionis](/wiki/Chionis)*), some guans (*[Crax](/wiki/Crax)*) and stone curlews (*[Burhinus](/wiki/Burhinus)*) use a bony knob on the [alular](/wiki/Alula) metacarpal to punch and hammer opponents. The jacanas *[Actophilornis](/wiki/Actophilornis)* and *[Irediparra](/wiki/Irediparra)* have an expanded, blade-like radius. The extinct *[Xenicibis](/wiki/Xenicibis)* was unique in having an elongate forelimb and massive hand which likely functioned in combat or defence as a jointed club or flail. [Swans](/wiki/Cygnus_olor), for instance, may strike with the bony spurs and bite when defending eggs or young.

### Feathers, plumage, and scales

Main articles: [Feather](/wiki/Feather), [Flight feather](/wiki/Flight_feather), and [Down feather](/wiki/Down_feather)

The [disruptively patterned](/wiki/Disruptively_patterned) plumage of the [African scops owl](/wiki/African_scops_owl) allows it to blend in with its surroundings.

Feathers are a feature characteristic of birds (though also present in [some dinosaurs](/wiki/Feathered_dinosaurs) not currently considered to be true birds). They facilitate [flight](/wiki/Bird_flight), provide insulation that aids in [thermoregulation](/wiki/Thermoregulation), and are used in display, camouflage, and signalling. There are several types of feathers, each serving its own set of purposes. Feathers are epidermal growths attached to the skin and arise only in specific tracts of skin called [pterylae](/wiki/Pterylography). The distribution pattern of these feather tracts (pterylosis) is used in taxonomy and systematics. The arrangement and appearance of feathers on the body, called [plumage](/wiki/Plumage), may vary within species by age, [social status](/wiki/Social_status), and [sex](/wiki/Sexual_dimorphism).

Plumage is regularly [moulted](/wiki/Moult); the standard plumage of a bird that has moulted after breeding is known as the "[non-breeding](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#basic_plumage)" plumage, or—in the [Humphrey–Parkes terminology](/wiki/Humphrey%E2%80%93Parkes_terminology)—"basic" plumage; breeding plumages or variations of the basic plumage are known under the Humphrey–Parkes system as "[alternate](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#alternate_plumage)" plumages. Moulting is annual in most species, although some may have two moults a year, and large birds of prey may moult only once every few years. Moulting patterns vary across species. In passerines, [flight feathers](/wiki/Flight_feather) are replaced one at a time with the innermost [primary](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#primary) being the first. When the fifth of sixth primary is replaced, the outermost [tertiaries](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#tertiaries) begin to drop. After the innermost tertiaries are moulted, the [secondaries](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#secondaries) starting from the innermost begin to drop and this proceeds to the outer feathers (centrifugal moult). The greater primary [coverts](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#coverts) are moulted in synchrony with the primary that they overlap.

[Red lory](/wiki/Red_lory) preening

A small number of species, such as ducks and geese, lose all of their flight feathers at once, temporarily becoming flightless. As a general rule, the tail feathers are moulted and replaced starting with the innermost pair. Centripetal moults of tail feathers are however seen in the [Phasianidae](/wiki/Phasianidae). The centrifugal moult is modified in the tail feathers of [woodpeckers](/wiki/Woodpecker) and [treecreepers](/wiki/Treecreeper), in that it begins with the second innermost pair of feathers and finishes with the central pair of feathers so that the bird maintains a functional climbing tail. The general pattern seen in [passerines](/wiki/Passerine) is that the primaries are replaced outward, secondaries inward, and the tail from centre outward. Before nesting, the females of most bird species gain a bare [brood patch](/wiki/Brood_patch) by losing feathers close to the belly. The skin there is well supplied with blood vessels and helps the bird in incubation.

Feathers require maintenance and birds [preen](/wiki/Preening) or groom them daily, spending an average of around 9% of their daily time on this. The bill is used to brush away foreign particles and to apply [waxy](/wiki/Wax) secretions from the [uropygial gland](/wiki/Uropygial_gland); these secretions protect the feathers' flexibility and act as an [antimicrobial agent](/wiki/Antimicrobial), inhibiting the growth of feather-degrading [bacteria](/wiki/Bacteria). This may be supplemented with the secretions of [formic acid](/wiki/Formic_acid) from ants, which birds receive through a behaviour known as [anting](/wiki/Anting_(bird_activity)), to remove feather parasites.

The [scales](/wiki/Bird_anatomy#Scales) of birds are composed of the same keratin as beaks, claws, and spurs. They are found mainly on the toes and [metatarsus](/wiki/Metatarsus), but may be found further up on the ankle in some birds. Most bird scales do not overlap significantly, except in the cases of [kingfishers](/wiki/Kingfisher) and [woodpeckers](/wiki/Woodpecker). The scales of birds are thought to be [homologous](/wiki/Homology_(biology)) to those of reptiles and mammals.

### Flight

Main articles: [Bird flight](/wiki/Bird_flight) and [Flightless birds](/wiki/Flightless_birds)

[Restless flycatcher](/wiki/Restless_flycatcher) in the downstroke of flapping flight

Most birds can [fly](/wiki/Flying_and_gliding_animals), which distinguishes them from almost all other vertebrate classes. Flight is the primary means of locomotion for most bird species and is used for searching for food and for escaping from predators. Birds have various adaptations for flight, including a lightweight skeleton, two large flight muscles, the pectoralis (which accounts for 15% of the total mass of the bird) and the supracoracoideus, as well as a modified forelimb ([wing](/wiki/Bird_wing)) that serves as an [aerofoil](/wiki/Airfoil).

Wing shape and size generally determine a bird's flight style and performance; many birds combine powered, flapping flight with less energy-intensive soaring flight. About 60 extant bird species are [flightless](/wiki/Flightless_bird), as were many extinct birds. Flightlessness often arises in birds on isolated islands, most likely due to limited resources and the absence of [mammalian](/wiki/Mammal) land predators. Flightlessness is almost exclusively correlated with [gigantism](/wiki/Island_gigantism) due to an island's inherent condition of isolation. Although flightless, penguins use similar musculature and movements to "fly" through the water, as do some flight-capable birds such as [auks](/wiki/Auk), [shearwaters](/wiki/Shearwater) and [dippers](/wiki/Dipper).

## Behaviour

Most birds are [diurnal](/wiki/Diurnal_animal), but some birds, such as many species of [owls](/wiki/Owl) and [nightjars](/wiki/Nightjar), are [nocturnal](/wiki/Nocturnal) or [crepuscular](/wiki/Crepuscular) (active during twilight hours), and many coastal [waders](/wiki/Wader) feed when the tides are appropriate, by day or night.

### Diet and feeding

See also: [Bird food](/wiki/Bird_food)

Feeding adaptations in beaks

[Feral pigeon](/wiki/Feral_pigeon) eating bread.

[Birds' diets](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#dietary_classification_terms_(-vores)) are varied and often include [nectar](/wiki/Nectar_(plant)), fruit, plants, seeds, [carrion](/wiki/Carrion), and various small animals, including other birds. The [digestive system of birds](/wiki/Digestive_system_of_birds) is unique, with a [crop](/wiki/Crop_(anatomy)) for storage and a [gizzard](/wiki/Gizzard) that contains swallowed stones for grinding food to compensate for the lack of teeth. Some species such as pigeons and some psittacine species do not have a [gallbladder](/wiki/Gallbladder). Most birds are highly adapted for rapid digestion to aid with flight. Some migratory birds have adapted to use protein stored in many parts of their bodies, including protein from the intestines, as additional energy during migration.

Birds that employ many strategies to obtain food or feed on a variety of food items are called generalists, while others that concentrate time and effort on specific food items or have a single strategy to obtain food are considered specialists. [Avian foraging](/wiki/Avian_foraging) strategies can vary widely by species. Many birds [glean](/wiki/Gleaning_(birds)) for insects, invertebrates, fruit, or seeds. Some hunt insects by suddenly attacking from a branch. Those species that seek [pest](/wiki/Pest_(organism)) [insects](/wiki/Insect) are considered beneficial 'biological control agents' and their presence encouraged in [biological pest control](/wiki/Biological_pest_control) programmes. Combined, insectivorous birds eat 400–500 million metric tons of arthropods annually.

Nectar feeders such as [hummingbirds](/wiki/Hummingbird), [sunbirds](/wiki/Sunbird), [lories, and lorikeets](/wiki/Lories_and_lorikeets) amongst others have specially adapted brushy tongues and in many cases bills designed to fit [co-adapted](/wiki/Coevolution) flowers. [Kiwis](/wiki/Kiwi_(bird)) and [shorebirds](/wiki/Shorebird) with long bills probe for invertebrates; shorebirds' varied bill lengths and feeding methods result in the separation of [ecological niches](/wiki/Ecological_niche). [Divers](/wiki/Loon), [diving ducks](/wiki/Diving_duck), [penguins](/wiki/Penguin) and [auks](/wiki/Auks) pursue their prey underwater, using their wings or feet for propulsion, while aerial predators such as [sulids](/wiki/Sulidae), [kingfishers](/wiki/Kingfisher) and [terns](/wiki/Tern) plunge dive after their prey. [Flamingos](/wiki/Flamingo), three species of [prion](/wiki/Prion_(bird)), and some ducks are [filter feeders](/wiki/Filter_feeder). [Geese](/wiki/Geese) and [dabbling ducks](/wiki/Dabbling_duck) are primarily grazers.

Some species, including [frigatebirds](/wiki/Frigatebird), [gulls](/wiki/Gull), and [skuas](/wiki/Skua), engage in [kleptoparasitism](/wiki/Kleptoparasitism), stealing food items from other birds. Kleptoparasitism is thought to be a supplement to food obtained by hunting, rather than a significant part of any species' diet; a study of [great frigatebirds](/wiki/Great_frigatebird) stealing from [masked boobies](/wiki/Masked_booby) estimated that the frigatebirds stole at most 40% of their food and on average stole only 5%. Other birds are [scavengers](/wiki/Scavenger); some of these, like [vultures](/wiki/Vulture), are specialised carrion eaters, while others, like gulls, [corvids](/wiki/Corvid), or other birds of prey, are opportunists.

### Water and drinking

Water is needed by many birds although their mode of excretion and lack of [sweat glands](/wiki/Sweat_gland) reduces the physiological demands. Some desert birds can obtain their water needs entirely from moisture in their food. Some have other adaptations such as allowing their body temperature to rise, saving on moisture loss from evaporative cooling or panting. Seabirds can drink seawater and have [salt glands](/wiki/Salt_gland) inside the head that eliminate excess salt out of the nostrils.

Most birds scoop water in their beaks and raise their head to let water run down the throat. Some species, especially of arid zones, belonging to the [pigeon](/wiki/Columbidae), [finch](/wiki/Estrildidae), [mousebird](/wiki/Coliidae), [button-quail](/wiki/Turnicidae) and [bustard](/wiki/Otididae) families are capable of sucking up water without the need to tilt back their heads. Some desert birds depend on water sources and [sandgrouse](/wiki/Sandgrouse) are particularly well known for congregating daily at waterholes. Nesting sandgrouse and many plovers carry water to their young by wetting their belly feathers. Some birds carry water for chicks at the nest in their crop or regurgitate it along with food. The pigeon family, flamingos and penguins have adaptations to produce a nutritive fluid called [crop milk](/wiki/Crop_milk) that they provide to their chicks.

### Migration

Main article: [Bird migration](/wiki/Bird_migration)

A flock of [Canada geese](/wiki/Canada_geese) in [V formation](/wiki/V_formation)

Many bird species migrate to take advantage of global differences of [seasonal](/wiki/Season) temperatures, therefore optimising availability of food sources and breeding habitat. These migrations vary among the different groups. Many landbirds, [shorebirds](/wiki/Shorebird), and [waterbirds](/wiki/Waterbird) undertake annual long-distance migrations, usually triggered by the length of daylight as well as weather conditions. These birds are characterised by a breeding season spent in the [temperate](/wiki/Temperate) or [polar regions](/wiki/Polar_region) and a non-breeding season in the [tropical](/wiki/Tropical) regions or opposite hemisphere. Before migration, birds substantially increase body fats and reserves and reduce the size of some of their organs.

Migration is highly demanding energetically, particularly as birds need to cross deserts and oceans without refuelling. Landbirds have a flight range of around 2,500 km (1,600 mi) and shorebirds can fly up to 4,000 km (2,500 mi), although the [bar-tailed godwit](/wiki/Bar-tailed_godwit) is capable of non-stop flights of up to 10,200 km (6,300 mi). Some [seabirds](/wiki/Seabird) undertake long migrations, with the longest annual migrations including those of [Arctic terns](/wiki/Arctic_terns), which were recorded travelling an average of 70,900 km (44,100 mi) between their [Arctic](/wiki/Arctic) breeding grounds in [Greenland](/wiki/Greenland) and [Iceland](/wiki/Iceland) and their wintering grounds in [Antarctica](/wiki/Antarctica), with one bird covering 81,600 km (50,700 mi), and [sooty shearwaters](/wiki/Sooty_shearwater), which nest in [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand) and [Chile](/wiki/Chile) and make annual round trips of 64,000 km (39,800 mi) to their summer feeding grounds in the North Pacific off Japan, [Alaska](/wiki/Alaska) and [California](/wiki/California).
Other seabirds disperse after breeding, travelling widely but having no set migration route. [Albatrosses](/wiki/Albatross) nesting in the [Southern Ocean](/wiki/Southern_Ocean) often undertake circumpolar trips between breeding seasons.

The routes of satellite-tagged [bar-tailed godwits](/wiki/Bar-tailed_godwit) migrating north from [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand). This species has the longest known non-stop migration of any species, up to 10,200 km (6,300 mi).

Some bird species undertake shorter migrations, travelling only as far as is required to avoid bad weather or obtain food. [Irruptive](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/irruptive) species such as the boreal [finches](/wiki/Finch) are one such group and can commonly be found at a location in one year and absent the next. This type of migration is normally associated with food availability. Species may also travel shorter distances over part of their range, with individuals from higher latitudes travelling into the existing range of conspecifics; others undertake partial migrations, where only a fraction of the population, usually females and subdominant males, migrates. Partial migration can form a large percentage of the migration behaviour of birds in some regions; in Australia, surveys found that 44% of non-passerine birds and 32% of passerines were partially migratory.

[Altitudinal migration](/wiki/Altitudinal_migration) is a form of short-distance migration in which birds spend the breeding season at higher altitudes and move to lower ones during suboptimal conditions. It is most often triggered by temperature changes and usually occurs when [the normal territories](/wiki/Territory_(animal)) also become inhospitable due to lack of food. Some species may also be nomadic, holding no fixed territory and moving according to weather and food availability. [Parrots](/wiki/True_parrots) as a [family](/wiki/Family_(taxonomy)) are overwhelmingly neither migratory nor sedentary but considered to either be dispersive, irruptive, nomadic or undertake small and irregular migrations.

The ability of birds to return to precise locations across vast distances has been known for some time; in an experiment conducted in the 1950s, a [Manx shearwater](/wiki/Manx_shearwater) released in [Boston](/wiki/Boston) in the United States returned to its colony in [Skomer](/wiki/Skomer), in Wales within 13 days, a distance of 5,150 km (3,200 mi). Birds navigate during migration using a variety of methods. For [diurnal](/wiki/Diurnal_animal) migrants, the [sun](/wiki/Sun) is used to navigate by day, and a stellar compass is used at night. Birds that use the sun compensate for the changing position of the sun during the day by the use of an [internal clock](/wiki/Chronobiology). Orientation with the stellar compass depends on the position of the [constellations](/wiki/Constellation) surrounding [Polaris](/wiki/Polaris). These are backed up in some species by their ability to sense the Earth's [geomagnetism](/wiki/Geomagnetism) through specialised [photoreceptors](/wiki/Photoreceptor_cell).

### Communication

See also: [Bird vocalisation](/wiki/Bird_vocalisation)

Birds [communicate](/wiki/Animal_communication) primarily using visual and auditory signals. Signals can be interspecific (between species) and intraspecific (within species).

Birds sometimes use plumage to assess and assert social dominance, to display breeding condition in sexually selected species, or to make threatening displays, as in the [sunbittern](/wiki/Sunbittern)'s mimicry of a large predator to ward off [hawks](/wiki/Hawk) and protect young chicks.

The startling display of the [sunbittern](/wiki/Sunbittern) mimics a large predator.

Visual communication among birds may also involve ritualised displays, which have developed from non-signalling actions such as preening, the adjustments of feather position, pecking, or other behaviour. These displays may signal aggression or submission or may contribute to the formation of pair-bonds. The most elaborate displays occur during courtship, where "dances" are often formed from complex combinations of many possible component movements; males' breeding success may depend on the quality of such displays.

[Bird calls and songs](/wiki/Bird_vocalization), which are produced in the [syrinx](/wiki/Syrinx_(biology)), are the major means by which birds communicate with [sound](/wiki/Sound). This communication can be very complex; some species can operate the two sides of the syrinx independently, allowing the simultaneous production of two different songs. Calls are used for a variety of purposes, including mate attraction, evaluation of potential mates, bond formation, the claiming and maintenance of territories, the identification of other individuals (such as when parents look for chicks in colonies or when mates reunite at the start of breeding season), and the warning of other birds of potential predators, sometimes with specific information about the nature of the threat. Some birds also use mechanical sounds for auditory communication. The *[Coenocorypha](/wiki/Coenocorypha)* [snipes](/wiki/Snipe) of [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand) drive air through their feathers, [woodpeckers](/wiki/Woodpecker) drum for long-distance communication, and [palm cockatoos](/wiki/Palm_cockatoo) use tools to drum.

### Flocking and other associations

[Red-billed queleas](/wiki/Red-billed_quelea), the most numerous species of wild bird, form enormous flocks – sometimes tens of thousands strong.

While some birds are essentially territorial or live in small family groups, other birds may form large [flocks](/wiki/Flock_(birds)). The principal benefits of flocking are [safety in numbers](/wiki/Safety_in_numbers) and increased foraging efficiency. Defence against predators is particularly important in closed habitats like forests, where [ambush predation](/wiki/Ambush_predation) is common and multiple eyes can provide a valuable [early warning system](/wiki/Early_warning_system). This has led to the development of many [mixed-species feeding flocks](/wiki/Mixed-species_feeding_flock), which are usually composed of small numbers of many species; these flocks provide safety in numbers but increase potential competition for resources. Costs of flocking include bullying of socially subordinate birds by more dominant birds and the reduction of feeding efficiency in certain cases. Some species have a mixed system with breeding pairs maintaining territories, while unmated or young birds live in flocks where they secure mates prior to finding territories.

Birds sometimes also form associations with non-avian species. Plunge-diving [seabirds](/wiki/Seabird) associate with [dolphins](/wiki/Dolphin) and [tuna](/wiki/Tuna), which push shoaling fish towards the surface. Some species of [hornbills](/wiki/Hornbill) have a [mutualistic relationship](/wiki/Mutualism_(biology)) with [dwarf mongooses](/wiki/Dwarf_mongoose), in which they forage together and warn each other of nearby [birds of prey](/wiki/Birds_of_prey) and other predators.

### Resting and roosting

"Roosting" redirects here. For other uses, see [Roost](/wiki/Roost_(disambiguation)).

Many birds, like this [American flamingo](/wiki/American_flamingo), tuck their head into their back when sleeping.

The high metabolic rates of birds during the active part of the day is supplemented by rest at other times. [Sleeping birds](/wiki/Avian_sleep) often use a type of sleep known as vigilant sleep, where periods of rest are interspersed with quick eye-opening "peeks", allowing them to be sensitive to disturbances and enable rapid escape from threats. [Swifts](/wiki/Swift_(bird)) are believed to be able to sleep in flight and radar observations suggest that they orient themselves to face the wind in their roosting flight. It has been suggested that there may be certain kinds of sleep which are possible even when in flight.

Some birds have also demonstrated the capacity to fall into [slow-wave sleep](/wiki/Slow-wave_sleep) one [hemisphere](/wiki/Cerebral_hemisphere) of the brain at a time. The birds tend to exercise this ability depending upon its position relative to the outside of the flock. This may allow the eye opposite the sleeping hemisphere to remain vigilant for [predators](/wiki/Predator) by viewing the outer margins of the flock. This adaptation is also known from [marine mammals](/wiki/Marine_mammal). [Communal roosting](/wiki/Communal_roosting) is common because it lowers the [loss of body heat](/wiki/Thermoregulation) and decreases the risks associated with predators. Roosting sites are often chosen with regard to thermoregulation and safety. Unusual mobile roost sites include large herbivores on the African savanna that are used by [oxpeckers](/wiki/Oxpecker).

Many sleeping birds bend their heads over their backs and tuck their [bills](/wiki/Beak) in their back feathers, although others place their beaks among their breast feathers. Many birds rest on one leg, while some may pull up their legs into their feathers, especially in cold weather. [Perching birds](/wiki/Passerine) have a tendon-locking mechanism that helps them hold on to the perch when they are asleep. Many ground birds, such as quails and pheasants, roost in trees. A few parrots of the genus *[Loriculus](/wiki/Loriculus)* roost hanging upside down. Some [hummingbirds](/wiki/Hummingbird) go into a nightly state of [torpor](/wiki/Torpor) accompanied with a reduction of their metabolic rates. This [physiological adaptation](/wiki/Adaptation) shows in nearly a hundred other species, including [owlet-nightjars](/wiki/Owlet-nightjar), [nightjars](/wiki/Nightjar), and [woodswallows](/wiki/Woodswallow). One species, the [common poorwill](/wiki/Common_poorwill), even enters a state of [hibernation](/wiki/Hibernation). Birds do not have sweat glands, but can lose water directly through the skin, and they may cool themselves by moving to shade, standing in water, panting, increasing their surface area, fluttering their throat or using special behaviours like [urohidrosis](/wiki/Urohidrosis) to cool themselves.

### Breeding

See also: [Category:Avian sexuality](/wiki/Category:Avian_sexuality), [Animal sexual behaviour § Birds](/wiki/Animal_sexual_behaviour#Birds), [Seabird breeding behaviour](/wiki/Seabird_breeding_behaviour), and [Sexual selection in birds](/wiki/Sexual_selection_in_birds)

#### Social systems

Like others of its family, the male [Raggiana bird-of-paradise](/wiki/Raggiana_bird-of-paradise) has elaborate breeding plumage used to impress females.

95 per cent of bird species are socially monogamous. These species pair for at least the length of the breeding season or—in some cases—for several years or until the death of one mate. Monogamy allows for both [paternal care](/wiki/Paternal_care) and [biparental care](/wiki/Parental_investment), which is especially important for species in which care from both the female and the male parent is required in order to successfully rear a brood. Among many socially monogamous species, [extra-pair copulation](/wiki/Extra-pair_copulation) (infidelity) is common. Such behaviour typically occurs between dominant males and females paired with subordinate males, but may also be the result of [forced copulation](/wiki/Forced_copulation) in ducks and other [anatids](/wiki/Anatidae).

For females, possible benefits of extra-pair copulation include getting better genes for her offspring and insuring against the possibility of infertility in her mate. Males of species that engage in extra-pair copulations will closely guard their mates to ensure the parentage of the offspring that they raise.

Other mating systems, including [polygyny](/wiki/Polygyny_in_animals), [polyandry](/wiki/Polyandry_in_animals), [polygamy](/wiki/Animal_sexual_behaviour#Polygamy), [polygynandry](/wiki/Polygynandry), and [promiscuity](/wiki/Promiscuity#Other_animals), also occur. Polygamous breeding systems arise when females are able to raise broods without the help of males. Mating systems vary across bird families but variations within species are thought to be driven by environmental conditions. A unique system is the formation of trios where a third individual is allowed by a breeding pair temporarily into the territory to assist with brood raising thereby leading to higher fitness.

Breeding usually involves some form of courtship display, typically performed by the male. Most displays are rather simple and involve some type of [song](/wiki/Bird_vocalization). Some displays, however, are quite elaborate. Depending on the species, these may include wing or tail drumming, dancing, aerial flights, or communal [lekking](/wiki/Lek_mating). Females are generally the ones that drive partner selection, although in the polyandrous [phalaropes](/wiki/Phalaropes), this is reversed: plainer males choose brightly coloured females. [Courtship feeding](/wiki/Courtship_feeding), [billing](/wiki/Billing_(birds)) and [allopreening](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#allopreening) are commonly performed between partners, generally after the birds have paired and mated.

[Homosexual behaviour has been observed](/wiki/Homosexuality_in_animals#Birds) in males or females in numerous species of birds, including copulation, pair-bonding, and joint parenting of chicks. Over 130 avian species around the world engage in sexual interactions between the same sex or homosexual behaviours. "Same-sex courtship activities may involve elaborate displays, synchronised dances, gift-giving ceremonies, or behaviours at specific display areas including bowers, arenas, or leks."

#### Territories, nesting and incubation

See also: [Bird nest](/wiki/Bird_nest)

A bird nest which fell from a tree.

Many birds actively defend a territory from others of the same species during the breeding season; maintenance of territories protects the food source for their chicks. Species that are unable to defend feeding territories, such as [seabirds](/wiki/Seabird) and [swifts](/wiki/Swift_(bird)), often breed in [colonies](/wiki/Bird_colony) instead; this is thought to offer protection from predators. Colonial breeders defend small nesting sites, and competition between and within species for nesting sites can be intense.

All birds lay [amniotic eggs](/wiki/Amniotic_egg) with hard shells made mostly of [calcium carbonate](/wiki/Calcium_carbonate). Hole and burrow nesting species tend to lay white or pale eggs, while open nesters lay [camouflaged](/wiki/Camouflage) eggs. There are many exceptions to this pattern, however; the ground-nesting [nightjars](/wiki/Nightjar) have pale eggs, and camouflage is instead provided by their plumage. Species that are victims of [brood parasites](/wiki/Brood_parasites) have varying egg colours to improve the chances of spotting a parasite's egg, which forces female parasites to match their eggs to those of their hosts.

Male [golden-backed weavers](/wiki/Golden-backed_weaver) construct elaborate suspended nests out of grass.

Bird eggs are usually laid in a [nest](/wiki/Bird_nest). Most species create somewhat elaborate nests, which can be cups, domes, plates, mounds, or burrows. Some bird nests can be a simple scrape, with minimal or no lining; most seabird and wader nests are no more than a scrape on the ground. Most birds build nests in sheltered, hidden areas to avoid predation, but large or colonial birds—which are more capable of defence—may build more open nests. During nest construction, some species seek out plant matter from plants with parasite-reducing toxins to improve chick survival, and feathers are often used for nest insulation. Some bird species have no nests; the cliff-nesting [common guillemot](/wiki/Common_guillemot) lays its eggs on bare rock, and male [emperor penguins](/wiki/Emperor_penguin) keep eggs between their body and feet. The absence of nests is especially prevalent in open habitat ground-nesting species where any addition of nest material would make the nest more conspicuous. Many ground nesting birds lay a clutch of eggs that hatch synchronously, with [precocial](/wiki/Precocial) chicks led away from the nests ([nidifugous](/wiki/Nidifugous)) by their parents soon after hatching.

Nest of an [eastern phoebe](/wiki/Eastern_phoebe) that has been parasitised by a [brown-headed cowbird](/wiki/Brown-headed_cowbird)

[Incubation](/wiki/Egg_incubation), which regulates temperature for chick development, usually begins after the last egg has been laid. In monogamous species incubation duties are often shared, whereas in polygamous species one parent is wholly responsible for incubation. Warmth from parents passes to the eggs through [brood patches](/wiki/Brood_patch), areas of bare skin on the abdomen or breast of the incubating birds. Incubation can be an energetically demanding process; adult albatrosses, for instance, lose as much as 83 grams (2.9 oz) of body weight per day of incubation. The warmth for the incubation of the eggs of [megapodes](/wiki/Megapode) comes from the sun, decaying vegetation or volcanic sources. Incubation periods range from 10 days (in [woodpeckers](/wiki/Woodpecker), [cuckoos](/wiki/Cuckoo) and [passerine](/wiki/Passerine) birds) to over 80 days (in albatrosses and [kiwis](/wiki/Kiwi_(bird))).

#### Parental care and fledging

Main article: [Parental care in birds](/wiki/Parental_care_in_birds)

At the time of their hatching, chicks range in development from helpless to independent, depending on their species. Helpless chicks are termed *[altricial](/wiki/Altricial)*, and tend to be born small, [blind](/wiki/Blindness), immobile and naked; chicks that are mobile and feathered upon hatching are termed *[precocial](/wiki/Precocial)*. Altricial chicks need help [thermoregulating](/wiki/Thermoregulation) and must be brooded for longer than precocial chicks. The young of many bird species do not precisely fit into either the precocial or altricial category, having some aspects of each and thus fall somewhere on an "altricial-precocial spectrum". Chicks at neither extreme but favouring one or the other may be termed [semi-precocial](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#semi-precocial) or [semi-altricial](/wiki/Glossary_of_bird_terms#semi-altricial).

[Altricial](/wiki/Altricial) chicks of a [white-breasted woodswallow](/wiki/White-breasted_woodswallow)

The length and nature of parental care varies widely amongst different orders and species. At one extreme, parental care in [megapodes](/wiki/Megapode) ends at hatching; the newly hatched chick digs itself out of the nest mound without parental assistance and can fend for itself immediately. At the other extreme, many seabirds have extended periods of parental care, the longest being that of the [great frigatebird](/wiki/Great_frigatebird), whose chicks take up to six months to [fledge](/wiki/Fledge) and are fed by the parents for up to an additional 14 months. The *chick guard stage* describes the period of breeding during which one of the adult birds is permanently present at the nest after chicks have hatched. The main purpose of the guard stage is to aid offspring to thermoregulate and protect them from predation.

A female [calliope hummingbird](/wiki/Calliope_hummingbird) feeding fully grown chicks

In some species, both parents care for nestlings and fledglings; in others, such care is the responsibility of only one sex. In some species, [other members](/wiki/Helpers_at_the_nest) of the same species—usually close relatives of the [breeding pair](/wiki/Breeding_pair), such as offspring from previous broods—will help with the raising of the young. Such [alloparenting](/wiki/Alloparenting) is particularly common among the [Corvida](/wiki/Corvida), which includes such birds as the true [crows](/wiki/Corvidae), [Australian magpie](/wiki/Australian_magpie) and [fairy-wrens](/wiki/Fairy-wren), but has been observed in species as different as the [rifleman](/wiki/Rifleman_(bird)) and [red kite](/wiki/Red_kite). Among most groups of animals, [male parental care](/wiki/Paternal_care) is rare. In birds, however, it is quite common—more so than in any other vertebrate class. Although territory and nest site defence, incubation, and chick feeding are often shared tasks, there is sometimes a [division of labour](/wiki/Division_of_labour) in which one mate undertakes all or most of a particular duty.

The point at which chicks [fledge](/wiki/Fledge) varies dramatically. The chicks of the *[Synthliboramphus](/wiki/Synthliboramphus)* murrelets, like the [ancient murrelet](/wiki/Ancient_murrelet), leave the nest the night after they hatch, following their parents out to sea, where they are raised away from terrestrial predators. Some other species, such as ducks, move their chicks away from the nest at an early age. In most species, chicks leave the nest just before, or soon after, they are able to fly. The amount of parental care after fledging varies; albatross chicks leave the nest on their own and receive no further help, while other species continue some supplementary feeding after fledging. Chicks may also follow their parents during their first [migration](/wiki/Bird_migration).

#### Brood parasites

Main article: [Brood parasite](/wiki/Brood_parasite)

[Reed warbler](/wiki/Reed_warbler) raising a [common cuckoo](/wiki/Common_cuckoo), a [brood parasite](/wiki/Brood_parasite)

[Brood parasitism](/wiki/Brood_parasitism), in which an egg-layer leaves her eggs with another individual's brood, is more common among birds than any other type of organism. After a parasitic bird lays her eggs in another bird's nest, they are often accepted and raised by the host at the expense of the host's own brood. Brood parasites may be either *obligate brood parasites*, which must lay their eggs in the nests of other species because they are incapable of raising their own young, or *non-obligate brood parasites*, which sometimes lay eggs in the nests of [conspecifics](/wiki/Conspecific) to increase their reproductive output even though they could have raised their own young. One hundred bird species, including [honeyguides](/wiki/Honeyguide), [icterids](/wiki/Icterid), and [ducks](/wiki/Black-headed_duck), are obligate parasites, though the most famous are the [cuckoos](/wiki/Cuckoo). Some brood parasites are adapted to hatch before their host's young, which allows them to destroy the host's eggs by pushing them out of the nest or to kill the host's chicks; this ensures that all food brought to the nest will be fed to the parasitic chicks.

#### Sexual selection

The peacock tail in flight, the classic example of a [Fisherian runaway](/wiki/Fisherian_runaway)

Main article: [Sexual selection in birds](/wiki/Sexual_selection_in_birds)

Birds have [evolved](/wiki/Evolution) a variety of [mating](/wiki/Mating) behaviours, with the [peacock](/wiki/Peafowl) tail being perhaps the most famous example of [sexual selection](/wiki/Sexual_selection) and the [Fisherian runaway](/wiki/Fisherian_runaway). Commonly occurring [sexual dimorphisms](/wiki/Sexual_dimorphism) such as size and colour differences are energetically costly attributes that signal competitive breeding situations. Many types of avian [sexual selection](/wiki/Sexual_selection) have been identified; intersexual selection, also known as female choice; and intrasexual competition, where individuals of the more abundant sex compete with each other for the privilege to mate. Sexually selected traits often evolve to become more pronounced in competitive breeding situations until the trait begins to limit the individual's fitness. Conflicts between an individual fitness and signalling adaptations ensure that sexually selected ornaments such as plumage colouration and [courtship behaviour](/wiki/Courtship_behavior) are "honest" traits. Signals must be costly to ensure that only good-quality individuals can present these exaggerated sexual ornaments and behaviours.

#### Inbreeding depression

Main article: [Inbreeding depression](/wiki/Inbreeding_depression)

[Inbreeding](/wiki/Inbreeding) causes early death ([inbreeding depression](/wiki/Inbreeding_depression)) in the [zebra finch](/wiki/Zebra_finch) *Taeniopygia guttata*. Embryo survival (that is, hatching success of fertile eggs) was significantly lower for [sib-sib](/wiki/Sibling) mating pairs than for unrelated pairs.

[Darwin's finch](/wiki/Darwin%27s_finches) *Geospiza scandens* experiences [inbreeding depression](/wiki/Inbreeding_depression) (reduced survival of offspring) and the magnitude of this effect is influenced by environmental conditions such as low food availability.

#### Inbreeding avoidance

Main article: [Inbreeding avoidance](/wiki/Inbreeding_avoidance)

Incestuous matings by the [purple-crowned fairy wren](/wiki/Purple-crowned_fairywren) *Malurus coronatus* result in severe fitness costs due to [inbreeding depression](/wiki/Inbreeding_depression) (greater than 30% reduction in hatchability of eggs). Females paired with related males may undertake extra pair matings (see [Promiscuity#Other animals](/wiki/Promiscuity#Other_animals) for 90% frequency in avian species) that can reduce the negative effects of inbreeding. However, there are ecological and demographic constraints on extra pair matings. Nevertheless, 43% of broods produced by incestuously paired females contained extra pair young.

Inbreeding depression occurs in the [great tit](/wiki/Great_tit) (*Parus major*) when the offspring produced as a result of a mating between close relatives show reduced fitness. In natural populations of *Parus major*, inbreeding is avoided by dispersal of individuals from their birthplace, which reduces the chance of mating with a close relative.

[Southern pied babblers](/wiki/Southern_pied_babbler) *Turdoides bicolor* appear to avoid inbreeding in two ways. The first is through dispersal, and the second is by avoiding familiar group members as mates.

[Cooperative breeding](/wiki/Cooperative_breeding) in birds typically occurs when offspring, usually males, delay dispersal from their natal group in order to remain with the family to help rear younger kin. Female offspring rarely stay at home, dispersing over distances that allow them to breed independently, or to join unrelated groups. In general, inbreeding is avoided because it leads to a reduction in progeny fitness ([inbreeding depression](/wiki/Inbreeding_depression)) due largely to the homozygous expression of deleterious recessive alleles. [Cross-fertilisation](/wiki/Outcrossing) between unrelated individuals ordinarily leads to the masking of deleterious recessive alleles in progeny.

## Ecology

[Gran Canaria blue chaffinch](/wiki/Gran_Canaria_blue_chaffinch), an example of a bird highly specialised in its habitat, in this case in the [Canarian pine](/wiki/Pinus_canariensis) forests

Birds occupy a wide range of ecological positions. While some birds are generalists, others are highly specialised in their habitat or food requirements. Even within a single habitat, such as a forest, the [niches](/wiki/Ecological_niche) occupied by different species of birds vary, with some species feeding in the [forest canopy](/wiki/Forest_canopy), others beneath the canopy, and still others on the forest floor. Forest birds may be [insectivores](/wiki/Insectivore), [frugivores](/wiki/Frugivore), or [nectarivores](/wiki/Nectarivore). Aquatic birds generally feed by fishing, plant eating, and piracy or [kleptoparasitism](/wiki/Kleptoparasitism). Many grassland birds are granivores. Birds of prey specialise in hunting mammals or other birds, while vultures are specialised [scavengers](/wiki/Scavenger). Birds are also preyed upon by a range of mammals including a few [avivorous](/wiki/Avivore) bats. A wide range of endo- and ectoparasites depend on birds and some parasites that are transmitted from parent to young have [co-evolved](/wiki/Coevolution) and show host-specificity.

Some nectar-feeding birds are important pollinators, and many frugivores play a key role in seed dispersal. Plants and pollinating birds often [coevolve](/wiki/Coevolution), and in some cases a flower's primary pollinator is the only species capable of reaching its nectar.

Birds are often important to island ecology. Birds have frequently reached islands that mammals have not; on those islands, birds may fulfil ecological roles typically played by larger animals. For example, in New Zealand nine species of [moa](/wiki/Moa) were important browsers, as are the [kererū](/wiki/Kerer%C5%AB) and [kōkako](/wiki/K%C5%8Dkako) today. Today the plants of New Zealand retain the defensive adaptations evolved to protect them from the extinct moa.

Many birds act as [ecosystem engineers](/wiki/Ecosystem_engineer) through the construction of nests, which provide important microhabitats and food for hundreds of species of invertebrates. Nesting [seabirds](/wiki/Seabird) may affect the ecology of islands and surrounding seas, principally through the concentration of large quantities of [guano](/wiki/Guano), which may enrich the local soil and the surrounding seas.

A wide variety of [avian ecology field methods](/wiki/Avian_ecology_field_methods), including counts, nest monitoring, and capturing and marking, are used for researching avian ecology.

## Relationship with humans

Main article: [Human uses of birds](/wiki/Human_uses_of_birds)

[Industrial farming](/wiki/Industrial_farming) of [chickens](/wiki/Chicken)

Since birds are highly visible and common animals, humans have had a relationship with them since the dawn of man. Sometimes, these relationships are [mutualistic](/wiki/Mutualism_(biology)), like the cooperative honey-gathering among [honeyguides](/wiki/Honeyguide) and African peoples such as the [Borana](/wiki/Borana_people). Other times, they may be [commensal](/wiki/Commensalism), as when species such as the [house sparrow](/wiki/House_sparrow) have benefited from human activities. Several species have reconciled to habits of farmers who practice traditional farming. Examples include the [Sarus Crane](/wiki/Sarus_crane) that begins nesting in India when farmers flood the fields in anticipation of rains, and the [woolly-necked storks](/wiki/Asian_woolly-necked_stork) that have taken to nesting on a short tree grown for agroforestry beside fields and canals. Several bird species have become commercially significant agricultural pests, and some pose an [aviation hazard](/wiki/Bird_strike). Human activities can also be detrimental, and have threatened numerous bird species with extinction ([hunting](/wiki/Hunting), [avian lead poisoning](/wiki/Avian_lead_poisoning), [pesticides](/wiki/Pesticide), [roadkill](/wiki/Roadkill), [wind turbine](/wiki/Wind_turbine) kills and predation by pet [cats](/wiki/Cat) and [dogs](/wiki/Dog) are common causes of death for birds).

Birds can act as vectors for spreading diseases such as [psittacosis](/wiki/Psittacosis), [salmonellosis](/wiki/Salmonellosis), [campylobacteriosis](/wiki/Campylobacteriosis), mycobacteriosis (avian [tuberculosis](/wiki/Tuberculosis)), [avian influenza](/wiki/Avian_influenza) (bird flu), [giardiasis](/wiki/Giardiasis), and [cryptosporidiosis](/wiki/Cryptosporidiosis) over long distances. Some of these are [zoonotic diseases](/wiki/Zoonosis) that can also be transmitted to humans.

### Economic importance

See also: [Pet § Birds](/wiki/Pet#Birds)

The use of cormorants by Asian fishermen is in steep decline but survives in some areas as a tourist attraction.

Domesticated birds raised for meat and eggs, called [poultry](/wiki/Poultry), are the largest source of animal protein eaten by humans; in 2003, 76 million tons of poultry and 61 million tons of eggs were produced worldwide. [Chickens](/wiki/Chicken) account for much of human poultry consumption, though domesticated [turkeys](/wiki/Domesticated_turkey), [ducks](/wiki/Domestic_duck), and [geese](/wiki/Domestic_goose) are also relatively common. Many species of birds are also hunted for meat. Bird hunting is primarily a recreational activity except in extremely undeveloped areas. The most important birds hunted in North and South America are waterfowl; other widely hunted birds include [pheasants](/wiki/Pheasant), [wild turkeys](/wiki/Wild_turkey), quail, [doves](/wiki/Dove), [partridge](/wiki/Partridge), [grouse](/wiki/Grouse), [snipe](/wiki/Snipe), and [woodcock](/wiki/Woodcock). [Muttonbirding](/wiki/Muttonbirding) is also popular in Australia and New Zealand. Although some hunting, such as that of muttonbirds, may be sustainable, hunting has led to the extinction or endangerment of dozens of species.

Other commercially valuable products from birds include feathers (especially the [down](/wiki/Down_feather) of geese and ducks), which are used as insulation in clothing and bedding, and seabird faeces ([guano](/wiki/Guano)), which is a valuable source of phosphorus and nitrogen. The [War of the Pacific](/wiki/War_of_the_Pacific), sometimes called the Guano War, was fought in part over the control of guano deposits.

Birds have been domesticated by humans both as pets and for practical purposes. Colourful birds, such as [parrots](/wiki/Parrot_(family)) and [mynas](/wiki/Myna), are bred in [captivity](/wiki/Aviculture) or kept as pets, a practice that has led to the illegal trafficking of some [endangered species](/wiki/Endangered_species). [Falcons](/wiki/Falcon) and [cormorants](/wiki/Cormorant) have long been used for [hunting](/wiki/Falconry) and [fishing](/wiki/Cormorant_fishing), respectively. [Messenger pigeons](/wiki/Messenger_pigeon), used since at least 1 AD, remained important as recently as [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II). Today, such activities are more common either as hobbies, for entertainment and tourism.

Amateur bird enthusiasts (called birdwatchers, twitchers or, more commonly, [birders](/wiki/Birding)) number in the millions. Many homeowners erect [bird feeders](/wiki/Bird_feeder) near their homes to attract various species. [Bird feeding](/wiki/Bird_feeding) has grown into a multimillion-dollar industry; for example, an estimated 75% of households in Britain provide food for birds at some point during the winter.

### In religion and mythology

The *3 of Birds* by the [Master of the Playing Cards](/wiki/Master_of_the_Playing_Cards), 15th-century Germany

Birds play prominent and diverse roles in religion and mythology.
In religion, birds may serve as either messengers or priests and leaders for a [deity](/wiki/Deity), such as in the Cult of [Makemake](/wiki/Makemake_(mythology)), in which the [Tangata manu](/wiki/Tangata_manu) of [Easter Island](/wiki/Easter_Island) served as chiefs or as attendants, as in the case of [Hugin and Munin](/wiki/Hugin_and_Munin), the two [common ravens](/wiki/Common_raven) who whispered news into the ears of the [Norse god](/wiki/Norse_god) [Odin](/wiki/Odin). In several civilisations of [ancient Italy](/wiki/History_of_Italy), particularly [Etruscan](/wiki/Etruscan_mythology) and [Roman](/wiki/Ancient_Rome) [religion](/wiki/Religion_in_Ancient_Rome), priests were involved in [augury](/wiki/Augur), or interpreting the words of birds while the "auspex" (from which the word "auspicious" is derived) watched their activities to foretell events.

They may also serve as [religious symbols](/wiki/Religious_symbolism), as when [Jonah](/wiki/Jonah) ([Hebrew](/wiki/Hebrew_language): יונה, [dove](/wiki/Dove)) embodied the fright, passivity, mourning, and beauty traditionally associated with doves. Birds have themselves been deified, as in the case of the [common peacock](/wiki/Common_peacock), which is perceived as Mother Earth by the people of southern India. In the ancient world, doves were used as symbols of the [Mesopotamian goddess](/wiki/Mesopotamian_goddess) [Inanna](/wiki/Inanna) (later known as Ishtar), the [Canaanite](/wiki/Ancient_Canaanite_religion) mother goddess [Asherah](/wiki/Asherah), and the Greek goddess [Aphrodite](/wiki/Aphrodite). In [ancient Greece](/wiki/Ancient_Greece), [Athena](/wiki/Athena), the goddess of wisdom and patron deity of the city of [Athens](/wiki/Athens), had a [little owl](/wiki/Little_owl) as [her symbol](/wiki/Owl_of_Athena). In religious images preserved from the Inca and Tiwanaku empires, birds are depicted in the process of transgressing boundaries between earthly and underground spiritual realms. Indigenous peoples of the central Andes maintain legends of birds passing to and from metaphysical worlds.

### In culture and folklore

Further information: [Birds in culture](/wiki/Birds_in_culture)

Painted [tiles](/wiki/Tiles) with design of birds from [Qajar dynasty](/wiki/Qajar_dynasty), [Iran](/wiki/Iran)

Birds have featured in culture and art since prehistoric times, when they were represented in early [cave painting](/wiki/Cave_painting) and carvings. Some birds have been perceived as monsters, including the mythological [Roc](/wiki/Roc_(mythology)) and the [Māori](/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people)'s legendary *[Pouākai](/wiki/Poukai)*, a giant bird capable of snatching humans. Birds were later used as symbols of power, as in the magnificent [Peacock Throne](/wiki/Peacock_Throne) of the [Mughal](/wiki/Mughal_era) and [Persian](/wiki/History_of_Iran) emperors. With the advent of scientific interest in birds, many paintings of birds were commissioned for books.

Among the most famous of these bird artists was [John James Audubon](/wiki/John_James_Audubon), whose paintings of [North American birds](/wiki/List_of_North_American_birds) were a great commercial success in Europe and who later lent his name to the [National Audubon Society](/wiki/National_Audubon_Society). Birds are also important figures in poetry; for example, [Homer](/wiki/Homer) incorporated [nightingales](/wiki/Nightingale) into his *[Odyssey](/wiki/Odyssey)*, and [Catullus](/wiki/Catullus) used a [sparrow](/wiki/Old_World_sparrow) as an erotic symbol in his [Catullus 2](/wiki/Catullus_2). The relationship between an [albatross](/wiki/Albatross) and a sailor is the central theme of [Samuel Taylor Coleridge](/wiki/Samuel_Taylor_Coleridge)'s *[The Rime of the Ancient Mariner](/wiki/The_Rime_of_the_Ancient_Mariner)*, which led to the use of the [term as a metaphor for a 'burden'](/wiki/Albatross_(metaphor)). Other [English](/wiki/English_language) metaphors derive from birds; [vulture funds](/wiki/Vulture_fund) and vulture investors, for instance, take their name from the scavenging vulture. Aircraft, particularly military aircraft, are frequently named after birds. The predatory nature of raptors make them popular choices for fighter aircraft such as the [F-16 Fighting Falcon](/wiki/F-16_Fighting_Falcon) and the [Harrier Jump Jet](/wiki/Harrier_Jump_Jet), while the names of seabirds may be chosen for aircraft primarily used by naval forces such as the [HU-16 Albatross](/wiki/HU-16_Albatross) and the [V-22 Osprey](/wiki/V-22_Osprey).

The [flag](/wiki/Flag_of_Dominica) of [Dominica](/wiki/Dominica) prominently features the [Sisserou Parrot](/wiki/Sisserou_Parrot), its national bird.

Perceptions of bird species vary across cultures. [Owls](/wiki/Owl) are associated with bad luck, [witchcraft](/wiki/Witchcraft), and death in parts of Africa, but are regarded as wise across much of Europe. [Hoopoes](/wiki/Hoopoe) were considered sacred in [Ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) and symbols of virtue in [Persia](/wiki/Persia), but were thought of as thieves across much of Europe and harbingers of war in [Scandinavia](/wiki/Scandinavia). In [heraldry](/wiki/Heraldry), birds, especially [eagles](/wiki/Eagle_(heraldry)), often appear in [coats of arms](/wiki/Coats_of_arms) In [vexillology](/wiki/Vexillology), birds are a popular choice on [flags](/wiki/List_of_national_flags_by_design#Birds). Birds feature in the flag designs of 17 countries and numerous subnational entities and territories. Birds are used by nations to symbolise a country's identity and heritage, with 91 countries officially recognising a [national bird](/wiki/List_of_national_birds). Birds of prey are highly represented, though some nations have chosen other species of birds with parrots being popular among smaller, tropical nations.

### In music

Main article: [Birds in music](/wiki/Birds_in_music)

[In music](/wiki/Birds_in_music), birdsong has influenced composers and musicians in several ways: they can be inspired by birdsong; they can intentionally imitate bird song in a composition, as [Vivaldi](/wiki/Antonio_Vivaldi), [Messiaen](/wiki/Olivier_Messiaen), and [Beethoven](/wiki/Beethoven) did, along with many later composers; they can incorporate recordings of birds into their works, as [Ottorino Respighi](/wiki/Ottorino_Respighi) first did; or like [Beatrice Harrison](/wiki/Beatrice_Harrison) and [David Rothenberg](/wiki/David_Rothenberg), they can duet with birds.

A 2023 archaeological excavation of a 10,000-year-old site in Israel yielded hollow wing bones of coots and ducks with perforations made on the side that are thought to have allowed them to be used as flutes or whistles possibly used by [Natufian](/wiki/Natufian_culture) people to lure birds of prey.

## Threats and conservation

Main article: [Bird conservation](/wiki/Bird_conservation)

See also: [Late Quaternary prehistoric birds](/wiki/Late_Quaternary_prehistoric_birds), [List of extinct birds](/wiki/List_of_extinct_birds), and [Raptor conservation](/wiki/Raptor_conservation)

The [California condor](/wiki/California_condor) once numbered only 22 birds, but conservation measures have raised that to over 500 today.

Human activities have caused population decreases or [extinction](/wiki/Extinction) in many bird species. Over a hundred bird species have gone extinct in historical times, although the most dramatic human-caused avian extinctions, eradicating an estimated 750–1800 species, occurred during the human colonisation of [Melanesian](/wiki/Melanesia), [Polynesian](/wiki/Polynesia), and [Micronesian](/wiki/Micronesia) islands. Many bird populations are declining worldwide, with 1,227 species listed as [threatened](/wiki/Threatened_species) by [BirdLife International](/wiki/BirdLife_International) and the [IUCN](/wiki/IUCN) in 2009. There have been long-term declines in North American bird populations, with an estimated loss of 2.9 billion breeding adults, about 30% of the total, since 1970.

The most commonly cited human threat to birds is [habitat loss](/wiki/Habitat_destruction). Other threats include overhunting, accidental mortality due to collisions with [buildings](/wiki/Bird-skyscraper_collisions) or [vehicles](/wiki/Bird_strike), [long-line fishing](/wiki/Long-line_fishing) [bycatch](/wiki/Bycatch), pollution (including [oil spills](/wiki/Oil_spill) and pesticide use), competition and predation from nonnative [invasive species](/wiki/Invasive_species), and [climate change](/wiki/Climate_change_and_birds).

Governments and [conservation](/wiki/Conservation_biology) groups work to protect birds, either by passing laws that [preserve](/wiki/In-situ_conservation) and [restore](/wiki/Ecological_restoration) bird habitat or by establishing [captive populations](/wiki/Ex-situ_conservation) for reintroductions. Such projects have produced some successes; one study estimated that conservation efforts saved 16 species of bird that would otherwise have gone extinct between 1994 and 2004, including the [California condor](/wiki/California_condor) and [Norfolk parakeet](/wiki/Norfolk_parakeet).

Human activities have allowed the expansion of a few temperate area species, such as the [barn swallow](/wiki/Barn_swallow) and [European starling](/wiki/European_starling). In the tropics and sub-tropics, relatively more species are expanding due to human activities, particularly due to the spread of crops such as rice whose expansion in south Asia has benefitted at least 64 bird species, though may have harmed many more species.
