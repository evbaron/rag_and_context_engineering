**Pakistan**, officially the **Islamic Republic of Pakistan**, is a country in [South Asia](/wiki/South_Asia). It is the [fifth-most populous country](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population), with a population of over 241.5 million, having the [second-largest Muslim population](/wiki/Islam_by_country#Countries) as of 2023. [Islamabad](/wiki/Islamabad) is the nation's capital, while [Karachi](/wiki/Karachi) is [its largest city](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Pakistan_by_population) and [financial centre](/wiki/Financial_centre). Pakistan is the [33rd-largest country by area](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area). Bounded by the [Arabian Sea](/wiki/Arabian_Sea) on the south, the [Gulf of Oman](/wiki/Gulf_of_Oman) on the southwest, and the [Sir Creek](/wiki/Sir_Creek) on the southeast, it shares land borders with [India](/wiki/India) to the east; [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan) to the west; [Iran](/wiki/Iran) to the southwest; and [China](/wiki/China) to the northeast. It shares a maritime border with [Oman](/wiki/Oman) in the Gulf of Oman, and is separated from [Tajikistan](/wiki/Tajikistan) in the northwest by Afghanistan's narrow [Wakhan Corridor](/wiki/Wakhan_Corridor).

Pakistan is the site of [several ancient cultures](/wiki/History_of_Pakistan), including the 8,500-year-old [Neolithic](/wiki/Neolithic) site of [Mehrgarh](/wiki/Mehrgarh) in [Balochistan](/wiki/Balochistan,_Pakistan), the [Indus Valley Civilisation](/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation) of the [Bronze Age](/wiki/Bronze_Age), and the ancient [Gandhara civilisation](/wiki/Gandhara_civilisation). The regions that compose the modern state of Pakistan were the realm of multiple empires and dynasties, including the [Achaemenid](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire), the [Maurya](/wiki/Maurya_Empire), the [Kushan](/wiki/Kushan_Empire), the [Gupta](/wiki/Gupta_Empire); the [Umayyad Caliphate](/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate) in its southern regions, the [Hindu Shahis](/wiki/Hindu_Shahis), the [Ghaznavids](/wiki/Ghaznavids), the [Delhi Sultanate](/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate), the [Samma](/wiki/Samma_dynasty), the [Shah Miris](/wiki/Shah_Miris), the [Mughals](/wiki/Mughal_Empire), and finally, the [British Raj](/wiki/British_Raj) from 1858 to 1947.

Spurred by the [Pakistan Movement](/wiki/Pakistan_Movement), which sought a homeland for the Muslims of [British India](/wiki/Presidencies_and_provinces_of_British_India), and election victories in 1946 by the [All-India Muslim League](/wiki/All-India_Muslim_League), Pakistan gained independence in 1947 after the [partition of British India](/wiki/Partition_of_British_India), which awarded separate statehood to its Muslim-majority regions and was accompanied by an unparalleled mass migration and loss of life. Initially [a dominion](/wiki/Dominion_of_Pakistan) of the [British Commonwealth](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations), Pakistan [adopted a republican constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan_(1956%E2%80%931958)) in 1956 and became an [Islamic republic](/wiki/Islamic_republic) with two geographically separate provinces, [East Pakistan](/wiki/East_Pakistan) and [West Pakistan](/wiki/West_Pakistan). East Pakistan seceded as the new country of [Bangladesh](/wiki/Bangladesh) in 1971 after a [nine-month-long civil war](/wiki/Bangladesh_Liberation_War). In the following four decades, Pakistan has been ruled by governments that alternated between civilian and military, democratic and authoritarian, relatively [secular](/wiki/Secularism_in_Pakistan) and Islamist.

Pakistan is considered a [middle power](/wiki/Middle_power) and is a declared [nuclear-weapon state](/wiki/List_of_states_with_nuclear_weapons), with the world's [seventh-largest standing armed forces](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_military_and_paramilitary_personnel). The [Pakistani economy](/wiki/Economy_of_Pakistan) is ranked amongst the world's emerging and growth-leading economies, with a large and rapidly growing middle class. Pakistan's political history since independence has been characterised by periods of significant economic and military growth as well as those of political and economic instability. It is an [ethnically](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Pakistan) and [linguistically](/wiki/Languages_of_Pakistan) diverse country, with similarly diverse [geography](/wiki/Geography_of_Pakistan) and [wildlife](/wiki/Wildlife_of_Pakistan). The country continues to face [poverty](/wiki/Poverty_in_Pakistan), [illiteracy](/wiki/Literate_Pakistan_Foundation), [corruption](/wiki/Corruption_in_Pakistan), and [terrorism](/wiki/Terrorism_in_Pakistan). Pakistan is a member of the United Nations, the [Shanghai Cooperation Organisation](/wiki/Shanghai_Cooperation_Organisation), the [Organisation of Islamic Cooperation](/wiki/Organisation_of_Islamic_Cooperation), the [Commonwealth of Nations](/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations), the [South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation](/wiki/South_Asian_Association_for_Regional_Cooperation), and the [Islamic Military Counter-Terrorism Coalition](/wiki/Islamic_Military_Counter_Terrorism_Coalition), and is designated as a [major non-NATO ally](/wiki/Major_non-NATO_ally) by the United States.

## Etymology

The name *Pakistan* was coined by [Choudhry Rahmat Ali](/wiki/Choudhry_Rahmat_Ali), a [Pakistan Movement](/wiki/Pakistan_Movement) activist, who in January 1933 first published it (originally as "Pakstan") in a pamphlet *[Now or Never](/wiki/Pakistan_Declaration)*, using it as an [acronym](/wiki/Acronym). Rahmat Ali explained: "It is composed of letters taken from the names of all our homelands, Indian and Asian, [**P**anjab](/wiki/Punjab_Province_(British_India)), [**A**fghania](/wiki/North-West_Frontier_Province_(1901%E2%80%932010)), [**K**ashmir](/wiki/Jammu_and_Kashmir_(princely_state)), [**S**indh](/wiki/Sind_Division), and [Baluchis**tan**](/wiki/Baluchistan_Agency)." He added, "Pakistan is both a [Persian](/wiki/Persian_language) and [Urdu](/wiki/Urdu) word... It means the land of the Paks, the spiritually pure and clean." Etymologists note that [پاک](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D9%BE%D8%A7%DA%A9#Persian) *pāk*, is 'pure' in Persian and [Pashto](/wiki/Pashto) and the Persian suffix [ـستان](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D9%80%D8%B3%D8%AA%D8%A7%D9%86#Persian) *[-stan](/wiki/-stan)* means 'land' or 'place of'.

Rahmat Ali's concept of Pakistan only related to the northwestern area of the [Indian subcontinent](/wiki/Indian_subcontinent). He also proposed the name "Banglastan" for the Muslim areas of [Bengal](/wiki/Bengal) and "Osmanistan" for [Hyderabad State](/wiki/Hyderabad_State), as well as a political federation between the three.

### Prehistory and antiquity

*[Priest-King](/wiki/Priest-king_(sculpture))* from [Mohenjo-daro](/wiki/Mohenjo-daro) (c. 2500 BCE)

Cremation urn, [Gandhara grave culture](/wiki/Gandhara_grave_culture), Swat Valley, c. 1200 BCE

Some of the earliest ancient human civilisations in [South Asia](/wiki/South_Asia) originated from areas encompassing present-day Pakistan. The earliest known stone tools in the region, dating to the [Lower Palaeolithic](/wiki/Lower_Palaeolithic) (~2 million years ago), were discovered in the [Soan Valley](/wiki/Soan_Valley) of [northern Pakistan](/wiki/Northern_Pakistan). The [Indus plain](/wiki/Indus_River) region, which covers most of the present-day Pakistan, was the site of several successive ancient cultures including the [Neolithic](/wiki/Neolithic) (7000–4300 [BCE](/wiki/BCE)) site of [Mehrgarh](/wiki/Mehrgarh), and the 5,000-year history of urban life in South Asia to the various sites of the [Indus Valley Civilisation](/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation), including [Mohenjo-daro](/wiki/Mohenjo-daro) and [Harappa](/wiki/Harappa).

Following the decline of the Indus Valley Civilisation, semi-nomadic [Indo-European Aryans](/wiki/Indo-Aryan_peoples) migrated into the [Indian subcontinent](/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) around 2000 BCE, perhaps by way of the [Khyber Pass](/wiki/Khyber_Pass). They fused with the indigenous Harappan culture of the Indus Valley, and elements of the pre-Aryan spiritual traditions were assimilated into the developing Vedic tradition. This cultural milieu shaped the [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara) Civilisation, which flourished at the crossroads of India, Central Asia, and the Middle East, connecting [trade routes](/wiki/Silk_Road) and absorbing cultural influences from diverse civilisations. By the early Vedic period, parts of the Indus region in present-day Pakistan were populated by numerous tribes that were beginning to coalesce into chieftain-led clans and early kingdoms. During this period, the [Vedas](/wiki/Vedas), the oldest [scriptures](/wiki/Scriptures) of [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism), were composed.

### Classical period

*[Standing Buddha](/wiki/Standing_Buddha)* from [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara) (1st–2nd century CE)

The western regions of Pakistan [became part](/wiki/Achaemenid_conquest_of_the_Indus_Valley) of [Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) around 517 BCE. In 326 BCE, [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) conquered the region by defeating various local rulers, most notably, the King [Porus](/wiki/Porus), at [Jhelum](/wiki/Battle_of_Hydaspes). Among the major powers that ruled the region were the [Mauryas](/wiki/Mauryas) (322–185 BCE), during which [Ashoka the Great](/wiki/Ashoka_the_Great) extended the empire. The [Indo-Greek Kingdom](/wiki/Indo-Greek_Kingdom) founded by [Demetrius of Bactria](/wiki/Demetrius_I_of_Bactria) (180–165 BCE) included [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara) and [Punjab](/wiki/Punjab) and reached its greatest extent under [Menander](/wiki/Menander_I) (165–150 BCE), prospering the [Greco-Buddhist](/wiki/Greco-Buddhism) culture in the region. [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila) had one of the earliest universities and centres of higher education in the world, which was established during the late Vedic period in the 6th century BCE. The ancient university was documented by the invading forces of Alexander the Great and was also recorded by Chinese pilgrims in the 4th or 5th century CE. At its zenith, the [Rai dynasty](/wiki/Rai_dynasty) (489–632 CE) ruled [Sindh](/wiki/Sindh) and the surrounding territories.

### Medieval period

[Makran](/wiki/Makran) came under Muslim rule in the 7th century CE when it was conquered and a garrison was established under [Mu'awiya I](/wiki/Mu%27awiya_I) (r. 661–680). The Arab general [Muhammad ibn Qasim](/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Qasim) conquered Sindh and some regions of Punjab in 711 CE. The Pakistan government's official chronology claims this as the time when the foundation of Pakistan was laid. The early medieval period (642–1219 CE) witnessed the spread of Islam in the region. Before the arrival of Islam beginning in the 8th century, the region of Pakistan was home to a diverse plethora of faiths, including [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism), [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism), [Jainism](/wiki/Jainism) and [Zoroastrianism](/wiki/Zoroastrianism). During this period, [Sufi](/wiki/Sufi) [missionaries](/wiki/Dawah) played a pivotal role in converting a majority of the regional population to Islam. Upon the defeat of the [Turk](/wiki/Turk_Shahis) and [Hindu Shahi](/wiki/Hindu_Shahi) dynasties which governed the [Kabul Valley](/wiki/Kabul_Valley), [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara), and western Punjab in the 7th to 11th centuries CE, [several successive Muslim empires](/wiki/Muslim_conquest_in_the_Indian_subcontinent) ruled over the region, including the [Ghaznavid Empire](/wiki/Ghaznavid_Empire) (975–1187 CE), the [Ghorid Empire](/wiki/Ghorid_Empire), and the [Delhi Sultanate](/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate) (1206–1526 CE). The [Lodi dynasty](/wiki/Lodi_dynasty), the last of the Delhi Sultanate, was replaced by the [Mughal Empire](/wiki/Mughal_Empire) (1526–1857 CE).

[Makli Necropolis](/wiki/Makli_Necropolis), a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](/wiki/UNESCO_World_Heritage_Site) rose to prominence as a major funerary site during the [Samma dynasty](/wiki/Samma_dynasty).

The Mughals introduced Persian literature and high culture, establishing the roots of [Indo-Persian culture](/wiki/Indo-Persian_culture) in the region. In the region of modern-day Pakistan, key cities during the Mughal period were [Multan](/wiki/Multan), [Lahore](/wiki/Lahore), [Peshawar](/wiki/Peshawar) and [Thatta](/wiki/Thatta), which were chosen as the site of impressive [Mughal buildings](/wiki/Mughal_architecture). In the early 16th century, the region remained under the Mughal Empire. In the 18th century, the slow disintegration of the Mughal Empire was hastened by the emergence of the rival powers like the [Maratha Empire](/wiki/Maratha_Empire) and later the [Sikh Empire](/wiki/Sikh_Empire), as well as invasions by [Nader Shah](/wiki/Nader_Shah) from Iran in 1739, and the [Durrani Empire](/wiki/Durrani_Empire) of Afghanistan in 1748. The growing political power of the British in Bengal had not yet reached the territories of modern Pakistan.

### Colonial rule

Main articles: [British India](/wiki/British_India), [British Raj](/wiki/British_Raj), [Aligarh Movement](/wiki/Aligarh_Movement), and [Two-nation theory](/wiki/Two-nation_theory)

Sir [Syed Ahmad Khan](/wiki/Syed_Ahmad_Khan) (1817–1898), whose [vision](/wiki/Two-nation_theory) formed the basis of Pakistan.

[Muhammad Ali Jinnah](/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_Jinnah) (1876–1948) served as Pakistan's first Governor-General and the leader of the [Pakistan Movement](/wiki/Pakistan_Movement).

None of modern Pakistan was under British rule until 1839 when [Karachi](/wiki/Karachi), a small fishing village governed by [Talpurs](/wiki/Talpur_dynasty) of [Sindh](/wiki/Sindh) with a mud fort guarding the harbour, was [taken](/wiki/History_of_Karachi#Company_rule), and used as an enclave with a port and [military base](/wiki/Karachi_Cantonment) for the [First Afghan War](/wiki/First_Afghan_War) that ensued. The remainder of [Sindh](/wiki/Sindh) was acquired in 1843, and subsequently, through a series of wars and treaties, the [East India Company](/wiki/British_East_India_Company), and later, after the post-[Sepoy Mutiny](/wiki/Sepoy_Mutiny) (1857–1858), direct rule by [Queen Victoria](/wiki/Queen_Victoria) of the [British Empire](/wiki/British_Empire), acquired most of the region. Key conflicts included those against the [Baloch](/wiki/Baloch_people) [Talpur dynasty](/wiki/Talpur_dynasty), resolved by the [Battle of Miani](/wiki/Battle_of_Miani) (1843) in Sindh, the [Anglo-Sikh Wars](/wiki/Anglo-Sikh_War_(disambiguation)) (1845–1849), and the [Anglo-Afghan Wars](/wiki/Anglo-Afghan_Wars) (1839–1919). By 1893, all modern Pakistan was part of the [British Indian Empire](/wiki/British_Indian_Empire), and remained so until independence in 1947.

Under British rule, modern Pakistan was primarily divided into the [Sind Division](/wiki/Sind_Division), [Punjab Province](/wiki/Punjab_Province_(British_India)), and the [Baluchistan Agency](/wiki/Baluchistan_Agency). The region also included various [princely states](/wiki/Princely_state), with the largest being [Bahawalpur](/wiki/Bahawalpur_(princely_state)).

An important uprising against the British in the region was the [Indian Rebellion of 1857](/wiki/Indian_Rebellion_of_1857), known at the time as the [Sepoy Mutiny](/wiki/Sepoy_Mutiny). Divergence in the [relationship](/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Islamic_relations) between [Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism) and Islam resulted in significant tension in [British India](/wiki/British_Raj), leading to religious violence. The [language controversy](/wiki/Hindi%E2%80%93Urdu_controversy) further exacerbated tensions between Hindus and Muslims. A [Muslim intellectual movement](/wiki/Aligarh_Movement), led by Sir [Syed Ahmed Khan](/wiki/Syed_Ahmed_Khan) to counter the [Hindu renaissance](/wiki/Bengali_Renaissance), advocated for the [two-nation theory](/wiki/Two-nation_theory) and led to the establishment of the [All-India Muslim League](/wiki/All-India_Muslim_League) in 1906.

In March 1929, in response to the [Nehru Report](/wiki/Nehru_Report), [Muhammad Ali Jinnah](/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_Jinnah), the founder of Pakistan, issued his [fourteen points](/wiki/Fourteen_Points_of_Jinnah), which included proposals to safeguard the interests of the Muslim minority in a united India. These proposals were rejected. In his 29 December 1930 address, [Allama Iqbal](/wiki/Muhammad_Iqbal) advocated the amalgamation of Muslim-majority states in [North-West](/wiki/Northwest_India_(pre-1947)) India, including [Punjab](/wiki/Punjab_Province_(British_India)), [North-West Frontier Province](/wiki/North-West_Frontier_Province_(1901%E2%80%9355)), [Sind](/wiki/Sind_Province_(1936%E2%80%9355)), and [Baluchistan](/wiki/Baluchistan_(Chief_Commissioner%27s_Province)). The perception that Congress-led British [provincial governments](/wiki/1937_Indian_provincial_elections) neglected the Muslim League from 1937 to 1939 motivated Jinnah and other Muslim League leaders to embrace the two-nation theory. This led to the adoption of the [Lahore Resolution](/wiki/Lahore_Resolution) of 1940, presented by [Sher-e-Bangla](/wiki/Sher-e-Bangla) [A.K. Fazlul Haque](/wiki/A.K._Fazlul_Haque), also known as the Pakistan Resolution.

By 1942, Britain faced considerable strain during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), with India directly threatened by Japanese forces. Britain had pledged voluntary independence for India in exchange for support during the war. However, this pledge included a clause stating that no part of British India would be compelled to join the resulting dominion, which could be interpreted as support for an independent Muslim nation. Congress under the leadership of [Mahatma Gandhi](/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi) launched the [Quit India Movement](/wiki/Quit_India_Movement), demanding an immediate end to British rule. In contrast, the Muslim League chose to support the [UK's war efforts](/wiki/United_Kingdom_in_World_War_II), thereby nurturing the possibility of establishing a Muslim nation.

### Independence

Main article: [Pakistan Movement](/wiki/Pakistan_Movement)

Further information: [Indian independence movement](/wiki/Indian_independence_movement) and [Partition of India](/wiki/Partition_of_India)

The [partition of India](/wiki/Partition_of_India): green regions were all part of Pakistan by 1948, and orange ones part of India. The darker-shaded regions represent the [Punjab](/wiki/Punjab_Province_(British_India)) and [Bengal](/wiki/Bengal_Presidency) provinces partitioned by the [Radcliffe Line](/wiki/Radcliffe_Line). The grey areas represent some of the key [princely states](/wiki/Princely_state) that were eventually integrated into India or Pakistan.

The [1946 elections](/wiki/1946_Indian_provincial_elections) saw the Muslim League secure 90 percent of the Muslim seats, supported by the landowners of Sindh and Punjab. This forced the Indian National Congress, initially skeptical of the League's representation of Indian Muslims, to acknowledge its significance. Jinnah's emergence as the voice of the Indian Muslims, compelled the British to consider their stance, despite their [reluctance to partition India](/wiki/Opposition_to_the_partition_of_India). In a final attempt to prevent partition, they proposed the [Cabinet Mission Plan](/wiki/Cabinet_Mission_Plan).

As the Cabinet Mission failed, the British announced their intention to end rule by June 1948. Following rigorous discussions involving [Viceroy of India](/wiki/Governor-General_of_India), [Lord Mountbatten of Burma](/wiki/Louis_Mountbatten,_1st_Earl_Mountbatten_of_Burma), [Muhammad Ali Jinnah](/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_Jinnah) of the [All-India Muslim League](/wiki/All-India_Muslim_League), and [Jawaharlal Nehru](/wiki/Jawaharlal_Nehru) of Congress, the formal declaration to partition British India into two independent dominions—namely Pakistan and India—was issued by Mountbatten on the evening of 3 June 1947. In Mountbatten's oval office, the prime ministers of around a dozen major princely states gathered to receive their copies of the plan before its worldwide broadcast. At 7:00 P.M., [All India Radio](/wiki/All_India_Radio) transmitted the public announcement, starting with the viceroy's address, followed by individual speeches from Nehru, and Jinnah. The founder of Pakistan [Muhammad Ali Jinnah](/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_Jinnah) concluded his address with the slogan *Pakistan Zindabad* (Long Live Pakistan).

As the United Kingdom agreed to the [partitioning of India](/wiki/Partition_of_India), the modern state of Pakistan was established on [14 August 1947](/wiki/Independence_Day_(Pakistan)) (27th of [Ramadan](/wiki/Ramadan) in 1366 of the [Islamic calendar](/wiki/Islamic_calendar), [a holy time regarded as auspicious and noted for its religious importance](/wiki/Night_of_Power)). This new nation amalgamated the [Muslim-majority](/wiki/Islam_in_India) eastern and [northwestern regions](/wiki/Northwest_India_(pre-1947)) of [British India](/wiki/British_India), comprising the provinces of [Balochistan](/wiki/Balochistan_(Pakistan)), [East Bengal](/wiki/East_Bengal), the [North-West Frontier Province](/wiki/North-West_Frontier_Province_(1901%E2%80%931955)), [West Punjab](/wiki/Punjab,_Pakistan), and Sindh.

In the riots that accompanied the partition in Punjab Province, between 200,000 and 2,000,000 people were killed in what some have described as a retributive genocide between the religions. Around 50,000 Muslim women were [abducted and raped](/wiki/Violence_against_women_during_the_partition_of_India) by Hindu and Sikh men, while 33,000 Hindu and Sikh women experienced the same fate at the hands of Muslims. Around 6.5 million Muslims moved from India to West Pakistan and 4.7 million Hindus and Sikhs moved from West Pakistan to India. It was the largest mass migration in human history. A subsequent dispute over the [princely state](/wiki/Princely_state) of [Jammu and Kashmir](/wiki/Jammu_and_Kashmir_(princely_state)) eventually sparked the [Indo-Pakistani War of 1947–1948](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1947%E2%80%931948).

### Post-independence

Main articles: [History of Pakistan (1947–present)](/wiki/History_of_Pakistan_(1947%E2%80%93present)) and [Dominion of Pakistan](/wiki/Dominion_of_Pakistan)

[Liaquat Ali Khan](/wiki/Liaquat_Ali_Khan) was elected the first Prime Minister of Pakistan.

After [independence](/wiki/Creation_of_Pakistan) in 1947, Jinnah, the [President](/wiki/Party_chair) of the Muslim League, became Pakistan's first [Governor-General](/wiki/Governor-General_of_Pakistan) and the first [President-Speaker](/wiki/Speaker_of_the_National_Assembly_of_Pakistan) of the [Parliament](/wiki/Parliament_of_Pakistan), but he died of tuberculosis on 11 September 1948. Meanwhile, Pakistan's founding fathers agreed to appoint [Liaquat Ali Khan](/wiki/Liaquat_Ali_Khan), the [secretary-general](/wiki/Secretary_General) of the [party](/wiki/All-India_Muslim_League), the nation's [first Prime Minister](/wiki/List_of_Prime_Ministers_of_Pakistan). From 1947 to 1956, [Pakistan was a monarchy](/wiki/Monarchy_of_Pakistan) within the Commonwealth of Nations, and had two monarchs before it became a republic.

The creation of Pakistan was never fully accepted by many British leaders including [Lord Mountbatten](/wiki/Louis_Mountbatten,_1st_Earl_Mountbatten_of_Burma). Mountbatten expressed his lack of support and faith in the Muslim League's idea of Pakistan. Jinnah refused Mountbatten's offer to serve as Governor-General of Pakistan. When Mountbatten was asked by [Collins](/wiki/Larry_Collins_(writer)) and [Lapierre](/wiki/Dominique_Lapierre) if he would have sabotaged Pakistan had he known that Jinnah was dying of tuberculosis, he replied 'most probably'.

— [Muhammad Ali Jinnah](/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_Jinnah)'s first speech to the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan.

Maulana [Shabbir Ahmad Usmani](/wiki/Shabbir_Ahmad_Usmani), a respected Deobandi *alim* (scholar) who held the position of Shaykh al-Islam in Pakistan in 1949, and [Maulana Mawdudi](/wiki/Abul_A%27la_Maududi) of [Jamaat-i-Islami](/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami) played key roles in advocating for an Islamic constitution. Mawdudi insisted that the Constituent Assembly declare the "supreme sovereignty of God" and the supremacy of the *shariah* in Pakistan.

The efforts of Jamaat-i-Islami and the *[ulama](/wiki/Ulama)* led to the passage of the [Objectives Resolution](/wiki/Objectives_Resolution) in March 1949. This resolution, described by Liaquat Ali Khan as the second most significant step in Pakistan's history, affirmed that "sovereignty over the entire universe belongs to God Almighty alone and the authority which He has delegated to the State of Pakistan through its people for being exercised within the limits prescribed by Him is a sacred trust". It was later included as a preamble to the constitutions of 1956, 1962, and 1973.

[Democracy](/wiki/Democracy_in_Pakistan) faced setbacks due to the [martial law](/wiki/1958_Pakistani_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat) imposed by President [Iskander Mirza](/wiki/Iskander_Mirza), who was succeeded by General [Ayub Khan](/wiki/Ayub_Khan_(President_of_Pakistan)). After adopting a [presidential system](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan_of_1962) in 1962, Pakistan witnessed significant growth until the [second war](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1965) with India in 1965, resulting in an economic downturn and widespread public discontent in 1967. In 1969, President [Yahya Khan](/wiki/Yahya_Khan) consolidated control, but faced a devastating [cyclone](/wiki/1970_Bhola_cyclone) in East Pakistan resulting in 500,000 deaths.

In 1970, Pakistan conducted its [first democratic elections](/wiki/1970_Pakistani_general_election) since independence, intending to transition from [military rule](/wiki/Legal_Framework_Order,_1970) to democracy. However, after the East Pakistani [Awami League](/wiki/Awami_League) emerged victorious over the [Pakistan Peoples Party](/wiki/Pakistan_Peoples_Party) (PPP), Yahya Khan and the military refused to transfer power. This led to [Operation Searchlight](/wiki/Operation_Searchlight), a military crackdown, and eventually sparked the [war of liberation](/wiki/Bangladesh_Liberation_War) by Bengali [Mukti Bahini](/wiki/Mukti_Bahini) forces in East Pakistan, described in West Pakistan as a civil war rather than a liberation struggle.

Signing of the [Tashkent Declaration](/wiki/Tashkent_Declaration) to end [hostilities](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1965) with India in 1965 in [Tashkent](/wiki/Tashkent), [USSR](/wiki/Union_of_Soviet_Socialist_Republics_(USSR)), by President [Ayub](/wiki/Field_Marshal_Ayub_Khan) alongside [Bhutto](/wiki/Zulfikar_Ali_Bhutto) (centre) and [Aziz Ahmed](/wiki/Aziz_Ahmed_(civil_servant)) (left)

Independent researchers estimate that between 300,000 and 500,000 civilians died during this period while the Bangladesh government puts the number of dead at three million, a figure that is now nearly universally regarded as excessively inflated. Some academics such as [Rudolph Rummel](/wiki/Rudolph_Rummel) and [Rounaq Jahan](/wiki/Rounaq_Jahan) say both sides committed genocide; others such as [Richard Sisson](/wiki/John_Richard_Sisson) and Leo E. Rose believe there was no genocide. In response to India's support for the insurgency in East Pakistan, [preemptive strikes](/wiki/Preemptive_strikes) on India by Pakistan's [air force](/wiki/Pakistan_Air_Force), [navy](/wiki/Pakistan_Navy), and [marines](/wiki/Pakistan_Marines) sparked [a conventional war](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1971) in 1971 that resulted in an Indian victory and East Pakistan gaining [independence](/wiki/Independence_of_Bangladesh) as [Bangladesh](/wiki/Bangladesh).

Yahya Khan was replaced by [Zulfikar Ali Bhutto](/wiki/Zulfikar_Ali_Bhutto) as president; the country worked towards promulgating [its constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan) and putting the country on the road to democracy. In 1972 Pakistan embarked on an ambitious plan to develop its [nuclear deterrence](/wiki/Pakistan_and_its_Nuclear_Deterrent_Program) capability with [the goal of preventing](/wiki/Mutually_assured_destruction) any [foreign invasion](/wiki/Foreign_interventionism); the country's [first](/wiki/KANUPP) [nuclear power plant](/wiki/Nuclear_power_plant) was inaugurated in that same year. India's [first nuclear test](/wiki/Operation_Smiling_Buddha) in 1974 gave Pakistan additional justification to accelerate its [nuclear program](/wiki/Pakistan_and_Nuclear_Weapons).

Democracy ended with a [military coup](/wiki/Operation_Fair_Play) in 1977 against the [leftist](/wiki/Socialism_in_Pakistan) PPP, which saw [General Zia-ul-Haq](/wiki/General_Zia-ul-Haq) become the president in 1978. From 1977 to 1988, President Zia's [corporatisation](/wiki/Corporate_sector_of_Pakistan) and [economic Islamisation](/wiki/Islamization_of_Economy) initiatives led to Pakistan becoming one of the fastest-growing economies in South Asia. While building up the country's [nuclear program](/wiki/Pakistan_and_its_Nuclear_Deterrent_Program), increasing [Islamisation](/wiki/Zia-ul-Haq%27s_Islamization), and the rise of a homegrown [conservative](/wiki/Conservatism_in_Pakistan) philosophy, Pakistan helped subsidise and distribute US [resources to factions](/wiki/Operation_Cyclone) of the [mujahideen](/wiki/Afghan_mujahideen) against the [USSR](/wiki/Soviet_Union)'s [intervention](/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War) in [communist Afghanistan](/wiki/Communist_Afghanistan). Pakistan's [North-West Frontier Province](/wiki/Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) became a base for the anti-Soviet Afghan fighters, with the province's influential [Deobandi](/wiki/Deobandi) ulama playing a significant role in encouraging and organising the 'jihad'.

[President Zia died](/wiki/Death_of_Muhammad_Zia-ul-Haq) in a plane crash in 1988, and [Benazir Bhutto](/wiki/Benazir_Bhutto), daughter of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, was elected as the country's [first female](/wiki/Women_in_Muslim_societies) Prime Minister. The PPP was followed by conservative [Pakistan Muslim League (N)](/wiki/Pakistan_Muslim_League_(N)) (PML (N)), and over the next decade the leaders of the two parties fought for power, alternating in office. This period is marked by prolonged [stagflation](/wiki/Periods_of_stagflation_in_Pakistan), political instability, [corruption](/wiki/Corruption_in_Pakistan), misgovernment, [geopolitical](/wiki/Geostrategy) rivalry with India, and the clash of [left wing](/wiki/Socialism_in_Pakistan)-[right wing](/wiki/Conservatism_in_Pakistan) ideologies. As PML (N) secured a [supermajority](/wiki/Supermajority) in [elections](/wiki/1997_Pakistani_general_election) in 1997, [Nawaz Sharif](/wiki/Nawaz_Sharif) authorised [nuclear testings](/wiki/List_of_nuclear_weapons_tests_of_Pakistan), as a [retaliation](/wiki/Tit-for-tat) to the [second nuclear tests](/wiki/Pokhran-II) conducted by India in May 1998.

President [Musharraf](/wiki/Pervez_Musharraf) meets with Indian Prime Minister [Vajpayee](/wiki/Atal_Bihari_Vajpayee) in [Islamabad](/wiki/Islamabad) at the sidelines of 12th [SAARC](/wiki/SAARC) summit in 2004.

Military tension between the two countries in the [Kargil district](/wiki/Kargil_district) led to the [Kargil War](/wiki/Kargil_War) of 1999, and turmoil in [civil-military relations](/wiki/Civil-military_relations) allowed General [Pervez Musharraf](/wiki/Pervez_Musharraf) to take over through a [bloodless coup d'état](/wiki/1999_Pakistani_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat). Musharraf governed Pakistan as [chief executive](/wiki/Head_of_government) from 1999 to 2002 and as president from 2001 to 2008—a period of [enlightenment](/wiki/Enlightened_moderation), social [liberalism](/wiki/Liberal_islam), extensive [economic reforms](/wiki/Economic_reforms_in_Pakistan), and [direct involvement in the US-led war on terrorism](/wiki/Pakistan%27s_role_in_the_War_on_Terror). By its own financial calculations, Pakistan's [involvement in the war on terrorism](/wiki/Pakistan%27s_role_in_the_War_on_Terror) has cost up to $118 billion, over [eighty one thousand casualties](/wiki/List_of_terrorist_incidents_in_Pakistan_since_2001), and more than 1.8 million displaced civilians.

The [National Assembly](/wiki/National_Assembly_(Pakistan)) historically completed its first full five-year term on 15 November 2007. After the [assassination of Benazir Bhutto](/wiki/Assassination_of_Benazir_Bhutto) in 2007, the PPP secured the [most votes](/wiki/Majority) in the [elections](/wiki/2008_Pakistani_general_election) of 2008, appointing party member [Yusuf Raza Gilani](/wiki/Yusuf_Raza_Gilani) as Prime Minister. Threatened with [impeachment](/wiki/Movement_to_impeach_Pervez_Musharraf), President Musharraf resigned on 18 August 2008, and was succeeded by [Asif Ali Zardari](/wiki/Asif_Ali_Zardari). Clashes with the [judicature](/wiki/Judiciary_of_Pakistan) prompted Gilani's disqualification from the [Parliament](/wiki/Parliament_of_Pakistan) and as the Prime Minister in June 2012. The [general election](/wiki/2013_Pakistani_general_election) held in 2013 saw the PML (N) achieve victory, following which Nawaz Sharif was elected as Prime Minister for the third time. In 2018, [PTI](/wiki/Pakistan_Tehreek-e-Insaf) won the [general election](/wiki/2018_Pakistan_general_election) and [Imran Khan](/wiki/Imran_Khan) became the 22nd Prime Minister. In April 2022, [Shehbaz Sharif](/wiki/Shehbaz_Sharif) was elected as prime minister, after Imran Khan lost a no-confidence vote. During [2024 general election](/wiki/2024_Pakistani_general_election), PTI-backed independents became the largest bloc, but Shehbaz Sharif was elected prime minister for a second term, as a result of a coalition between PML (N) and PPPP.

## Geography

Main articles: [Geography of Pakistan](/wiki/Geography_of_Pakistan), [Environment of Pakistan](/wiki/Environment_of_Pakistan), [Climate of Pakistan](/wiki/Climate_of_Pakistan), [Extreme weather records in Pakistan](/wiki/List_of_extreme_weather_records_in_Pakistan), [List of tropical cyclones in Pakistan](/wiki/List_of_tropical_cyclones_in_Pakistan), and [List of beaches in Pakistan](/wiki/List_of_beaches_in_Pakistan)

[Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) of Pakistan

Pakistan's diverse [geography](/wiki/Geography_of_Pakistan) and [climate](/wiki/Climate_of_Pakistan) host a wide array of [wildlife](/wiki/Wildlife_of_Pakistan). Covering 881,913 km2 (340,509 sq mi), Pakistan ranks as the [33rd-largest nation by total area](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_area), but this varies based on Kashmir's disputed status. Pakistan boasts a 1,046 km (650 mi) coastline along the Arabian Sea and the Gulf of Oman, and shares land borders totalling 6,774 km (4,209 mi), including 2,430 km (1,510 mi) [with Afghanistan](/wiki/Durand_Line), 523 km (325 mi) [with China](/wiki/China%E2%80%93Pakistan_border), 2,912 km (1,809 mi) [with India](/wiki/India%E2%80%93Pakistan_border), and 909 km (565 mi) [with Iran](/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Pakistan_border). It has a [maritime border with Oman](/wiki/Oman%E2%80%93Pakistan_maritime_boundary) and is separated from Tajikistan via the narrow strip of the [Wakhan Corridor](/wiki/Wakhan_Corridor). Situated at the crossroads of South Asia, the Middle East, and Central Asia, Pakistan's location is geopolitically significant. Geologically, Pakistan lies at the interaction of the [Indian](/wiki/Indian_Plate) and [Eurasian tectonic plates](/wiki/Eurasian_Plate) within the Indus–Tsangpo Suture Zone, a region of high seismic activity. The collision between these two plates occurs in [northwest Pakistan](/wiki/Northwest_Pakistan), particularly in [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](/wiki/Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa). Sindh and Punjab are located on the north-western corner of the Indian Plate, while Balochistan and much of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa lie along its north-western margin. [Northern areas of Pakistan](/wiki/Northern_Pakistan), including Azad Kashmir, lie along the northern edge of the Indian Plate and are [prone to powerful earthquakes](/wiki/List_of_earthquakes_in_Pakistan).

A satellite image showing the topography of Pakistan

Pakistan's landscapes vary from coastal plains to glaciated mountains, offering deserts, forests, hills, and plateaus. Pakistan is divided into three major geographic areas: the northern highlands, the Indus River plain, and the Balochistan Plateau. The northern highlands feature the [Karakoram](/wiki/Karakoram), [Hindu Kush](/wiki/Hindu_Kush), and [Pamir](/wiki/Pamir_Mountains) mountain ranges, hosting some of the world's highest peaks, including five of the fourteen [eight-thousanders](/wiki/Eight-thousanders) (mountain peaks over 8,000 metres or 26,250 feet), notably [K2](/wiki/K2) (8,611 m or 28,251 ft) and [Nanga Parbat](/wiki/Nanga_Parbat) (8,126 m or 26,660 ft). The Balochistan Plateau lies in the west and the [Thar Desert](/wiki/Thar_Desert) in the east. The 1,609 km (1,000 mi) Indus River and its tributaries traverse the nation from Kashmir to the Arabian Sea, sustaining alluvial plains along the Punjab and Sindh regions.

The climate varies from tropical to temperate, with arid conditions in the coastal south. There is a monsoon season with frequent flooding due to heavy rainfall, and a dry season with significantly less rainfall or none at all. Pakistan experiences four distinct seasons: a cool, dry winter from December through February; a hot, dry spring from March through May; the summer rainy season, or southwest monsoon period, from June through September; and the retreating monsoon period of October and November. Rainfall varies greatly from year to year, with patterns of alternate flooding and drought common.

### Flora and fauna

Main articles: [Wildlife of Pakistan](/wiki/Wildlife_of_Pakistan), [Flora of Pakistan](/wiki/Flora_of_Pakistan), and [Fauna of Pakistan](/wiki/Fauna_of_Pakistan)

The diverse landscape and climate in Pakistan support a wide range of trees and plants. From coniferous [alpine](/wiki/Alpine_plant) and [subalpine](/wiki/Subalpine) trees like [spruce](/wiki/Spruce), [pine](/wiki/Pine), and [deodar cedar](/wiki/Deodar_cedar) in the northern mountains to [deciduous trees](/wiki/Deciduous_trees) like [shisham](/wiki/Shisham) in the [Sulaiman Mountains](/wiki/Sulaiman_Mountains), and palms such as [coconut](/wiki/Coconut) and [date](/wiki/Phoenix_dactylifera) in the southern regions. The western hills boast [juniper](/wiki/Juniper), [tamarisk](/wiki/Tamarisk), coarse grasses, and scrub plants. [Mangrove](/wiki/Mangrove) forests dominate the coastal wetlands in the south. Coniferous forests span altitudes from 1,000 to 4,000 metres (3,300 to 13,100 feet) in most northern and northwestern highlands. In Balochistan's xeric regions, date palms and *[Ephedra](/wiki/Ephedra_(genus))* are prevalent. In Punjab and Sindh's Indus plains, tropical and subtropical dry and moist broadleaf forests as well as tropical and xeric shrublands thrive. Approximately 4.8% or 36,845.6 square kilometres (3,684,560 ha) of Pakistan was forested in 2021.

[Markhor](/wiki/Markhor) is the national animal of Pakistan.

Pakistan's fauna mirrors its diverse climate. The country boasts around 668 bird species, including [crows](/wiki/Crow), [sparrows](/wiki/Old_World_sparrow), [mynas](/wiki/Acridotheres), [hawks](/wiki/Hawk), [falcons](/wiki/Falcon), and [eagles](/wiki/Eagle). [Palas, Kohistan](/wiki/Palas,_Kohistan), is home to the [western tragopan](/wiki/Western_tragopan), with many migratory birds visiting from Europe, Central Asia, and India. The southern plains harbor [mongooses](/wiki/Mongoose), [small Indian civet](/wiki/Small_Indian_civet), hares, the [Asiatic jackal](/wiki/Asiatic_jackal), the [Indian pangolin](/wiki/Indian_pangolin), the [jungle cat](/wiki/Jungle_cat), and the [sand cat](/wiki/Sand_cat). Indus is home to [mugger crocodiles](/wiki/Mugger_crocodile), while surrounding areas host [wild boars](/wiki/Wild_boar), deer, and [porcupines](/wiki/Indian_porcupine). Central Pakistan's sandy scrublands shelter Asiatic jackals, [striped hyenas](/wiki/Striped_hyena), wildcats, and [leopards](/wiki/Leopard). The mountainous north hosts a variety of animals like the [Marco Polo sheep](/wiki/Marco_Polo_sheep), [urial](/wiki/Urial), [markhor](/wiki/Markhor) goat, [ibex](/wiki/Ibex) goat, [Asian black bear](/wiki/Asian_black_bear), and [Himalayan brown bear](/wiki/Himalayan_brown_bear).

The lack of vegetative cover, severe climate, and grazing impact on deserts have endangered wild animals. The [chinkara](/wiki/Chinkara) is the only animal found in significant numbers in [Cholistan](/wiki/Cholistan), with a few [nilgai](/wiki/Nilgai) along the Pakistan–India border and in some parts of Cholistan. Rare animals include the [snow leopard](/wiki/Snow_leopard) and the blind [Indus river dolphin](/wiki/Indus_river_dolphin), of which there are believed to be about 1,816 remaining, protected at the [Indus Dolphin Reserve](/wiki/Indus_Dolphin_Reserve) in Sindh. In total, 174 species of mammals, 177 species of reptiles, 22 species of amphibians, 198 species of freshwater fish, 668 species of birds, over 5,000 species of insects, and over 5,700 species of plants have been recorded in Pakistan. Pakistan faces deforestation, hunting, and pollution, with a 2019 [Forest Landscape Integrity Index](/wiki/Forest_Landscape_Integrity_Index) mean score of 7.42/10, ranking 41st globally out of 172 countries.

## Government and politics

Main articles: [Government of Pakistan](/wiki/Government_of_Pakistan), [Politics of Pakistan](/wiki/Politics_of_Pakistan), and [Elections in Pakistan](/wiki/Elections_in_Pakistan)

[Parliament House](/wiki/Parliament_of_Pakistan)

Pakistan operates as a democratic [parliamentary](/wiki/Parliamentary_republic) [federal republic](/wiki/Federal_republic), with Islam designated as the [state religion](/wiki/State_religion). The military's influence in mainstream politics has been significant throughout Pakistan's history. Initially adopting a [constitution in 1956](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan_of_1956), Pakistan saw it suspended by Ayub Khan in 1958, replaced by a [second constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan_of_1962) in 1962. Under the leadership of [Zulfikar Ali Bhutto](/wiki/Zulfikar_Ali_Bhutto), the [1973 constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan) established a parliamentary system, but it was suspended under [Zia-ul-Haq](/wiki/Zia-ul-Haq)'s martial law in 1977 and restored, with amendments, in 1985. The eras of 1958–1971, [1977–1988](/wiki/1977_Pakistani_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat), and 1999–2008 witnessed [military coups](/wiki/Military_coups_in_Pakistan), leading to [martial law](/wiki/Martial_law_in_Pakistan) and military leaders governing de facto as presidents. Presently, Pakistan operates a [multi-party](/wiki/Multi-party_system) [parliamentary system](/wiki/Parliamentary_system), with distinct [checks and balances](/wiki/Check_and_balance) among government branches. The first successful [democratic transition](/wiki/2013_Pakistani_general_election) occurred in May 2013. Pakistani politics revolves around a blend of [socialism](/wiki/Socialism_in_Pakistan), [conservatism](/wiki/Conservatism_in_Pakistan), and the [third way](/wiki/Third_way), with the three main political parties being the conservative [Pakistan Muslim League (N)](/wiki/Pakistan_Muslim_League_(N)), socialist [Pakistani People's Party](/wiki/Pakistani_People%27s_Party), and centrist [Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf](/wiki/Pakistan_Tehreek-e-Insaf). Constitutional amendments in 2010 curtailed presidential powers, enhancing the role of the prime minister.

- [Head of State](/wiki/Head_of_State): The ceremonial head of the state and civilian [commander-in-chief](/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the [Pakistan Armed Forces](/wiki/Pakistan_Armed_Forces) is the [President](/wiki/President_of_Pakistan), elected by an [Electoral College](/wiki/Electoral_College_of_Pakistan). The [Prime Minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Pakistan) advises the President on key appointments, including military and judicial positions, and the President is constitutionally bound to act on this advice. The President also holds powers to pardon and grant clemency.
- [Legislative](/wiki/Legislative): The [bicameral](/wiki/Bicameral) legislature includes a 96-member [Senate](/wiki/Senate_of_Pakistan) ([upper house](/wiki/Upper_House)) and a 336-member [National Assembly](/wiki/National_Assembly_of_Pakistan) ([lower house](/wiki/Lower_House)). [National Assembly members](/wiki/Member_of_the_National_Assembly_of_Pakistan) are elected via [first-past-the-post](/wiki/First-past-the-post) under [universal adult suffrage](/wiki/Universal_adult_suffrage), representing National Assembly [constituencies](/wiki/Constituencies_of_Pakistan). The constitution reserves 70 seats for women and religious minorities, allocated to political parties based on proportional representation. Senate members are elected by provincial legislators, ensuring equal representation across all provinces.

[Prime Minister's Office](/wiki/Prime_Minister%27s_Office_(Pakistan))

- Executive: The Prime Minister, typically the leader of the [majority rule](/wiki/Majority_rule) party or coalition in the National Assembly (the lower house), serves as the country's chief executive and head of government. Responsibilities include forming a [cabinet](/wiki/Cabinet_of_Pakistan), making executive decisions, and appointing senior civil servants, subject to executive confirmation.
- [Provincial governments](/wiki/List_of_provincial_governments_of_Pakistan): Each of the [four provinces](/wiki/Four_Provinces_(Pakistan)) follows a similar governance system, with a directly elected [Provincial Assembly](/wiki/Category:Provincial_Assemblies_of_Pakistan) choosing the [Chief Minister](/wiki/Chief_Minister_(Pakistan)), usually from the largest party or coalition. Chief Ministers lead the provincial cabinet and oversee provincial governance. The [Chief Secretary](/wiki/Chief_Secretary_(Pakistan)), appointed by the Prime Minister, heads the provincial bureaucracy. Provincial assemblies legislate and approve the provincial budget, typically presented by the provincial finance minister annually. Ceremonial heads of provinces, the Provincial Governors, are appointed by the President based on the binding advice of the Prime Minister.

[Supreme Court of Pakistan](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Pakistan)

- [Judicature](/wiki/Judiciary_of_Pakistan): The judiciary in Pakistan has two classes: the superior and subordinate judiciary. The superior judiciary includes the [Supreme Court of Pakistan](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Pakistan), [Federal Shariat Court](/wiki/Federal_Shariat_Court), and five [high courts](/wiki/High_courts_of_Pakistan), with the Supreme Court at the top. It's responsible for safeguarding the constitution. Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan have their own court systems.

### Role of Islam

See also: [Islam in Pakistan](/wiki/Islam_in_Pakistan)

Pakistan, the only country established in the name of Islam, had overwhelming support among Muslims, especially in provinces like the [United Provinces](/wiki/United_Provinces_(1937%E2%80%9350)), where Muslims were a minority. This idea, articulated by the Muslim League, the [Islamic clergy](/wiki/Ulama), and Jinnah, envisioned an [Islamic state](/wiki/Islamic_state). Jinnah, closely associated with the *ulama*, was described upon his death by [Maulana Shabbir Ahmad Usmani](/wiki/Maulana_Shabbir_Ahmad_Usmani) as the greatest Muslim after [Aurangzeb](/wiki/Aurangzeb), aspiring to unite Muslims worldwide under Islam.

The Objectives Resolution of March 1949 marked the initial step towards this goal, affirming God as the sole sovereign. Muslim League leader [Chaudhry Khaliquzzaman](/wiki/Chaudhry_Khaliquzzaman) asserted that Pakistan could only truly become an Islamic state after bringing all believers of Islam into a single political unit. Keith Callard observed that Pakistanis believed in the essential unity of purpose and outlook in the Muslim world, expecting similar views on religion and nationality from Muslims worldwide.

[Eid prayers](/wiki/Eid_prayers) at the [Badshahi Mosque](/wiki/Badshahi_Mosque) in Lahore

Pakistan's desire for a united Islamic bloc, called Islamistan, wasn't supported by other Muslim governments, though figures like the Grand Mufti of Palestine, [Al-Haj Amin al-Husseini](/wiki/Amin_al-Husseini), and leaders of the [Muslim Brotherhood](/wiki/Muslim_Brotherhood) were drawn to the country. Pakistan's desire for an international organisation of Muslim countries was fulfilled in the 1970s when the [Organisation of Islamic Conference](/wiki/Organisation_of_Islamic_Cooperation) (OIC) was formed. In East Pakistan, Bengali Muslims increasingly prioritised Bengali ethno-nationalism over religious nationalism. The Islamist party [Jamaat-e-Islami](/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami) backed an Islamic state and opposed [Bengali nationalism](/wiki/Bengali_nationalism).

After the 1970 general elections, the Parliament crafted the [1973 Constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan). It declared Pakistan an Islamic Republic, with Islam as the state religion, and mandated laws to comply with Islamic teachings laid down in the [Quran](/wiki/Quran) and [Sunnah](/wiki/Sunnah) and that no law repugnant to such injunctions could be enacted. Additionally, it established institutions like the Shariat Court and the [Council of Islamic Ideology](/wiki/Council_of_Islamic_Ideology) to interpret and apply Islam.

Zulfikar Ali Bhutto faced opposition under the banner of *Nizam-e-Mustafa* ("Rule of the [Prophet](/wiki/Muhammad)"), advocating an Islamic state. Bhutto conceded to some Islamist demands before being ousted in a coup.

General Zia-ul-Haq, after seizing power, committed to establishing an Islamic state and enforcing *[sharia](/wiki/Sharia)* law. He instituted Shariat judicial courts, and court benches, to adjudicate using Islamic doctrine. Zia aligned with Deobandi institutions, exacerbating sectarian tensions with anti-Shia policies.

Most Pakistanis, according to a [Pew Research Center](/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) (PEW) poll, favour Sharia law as the official law, and 94 percent of them identify more with religion than nationality compared to Muslims in other nations.

Pakistan, a [federal](/wiki/Federal_republic) [parliamentary republic](/wiki/Parliamentary_republic), consists of [four provinces](/wiki/Four_Provinces_(Pakistan)): Punjab, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Sindh, and Balochistan, along with three [territories](/wiki/Administrative_units_of_Pakistan): [Islamabad Capital Territory](/wiki/Islamabad_Capital_Territory), [Gilgit-Baltistan](/wiki/Gilgit-Baltistan), and [Azad Kashmir](/wiki/Azad_Kashmir). The Government of Pakistan governs the [western parts](/wiki/Western_Kashmir) of the [Kashmir Region](/wiki/Kashmir_Region), organised into separate political entities, Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan. In 2009, the [constitutional assignment](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan) (the *Gilgit–Baltistan Empowerment and Self-Governance Order*) granted Gilgit-Baltistan [semi-provincial status](/wiki/Provinces_of_Pakistan), providing it with self-government.

The [local government](/wiki/Local_government_in_Pakistan) system consists of [districts](/wiki/Districts_of_Pakistan), [tehsils](/wiki/Tehsil), and [union councils](/wiki/Union_Councils_of_Pakistan), with an elected body at each tier.

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of Pakistan](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Pakistan)

Since independence, Pakistan has aimed to maintain an independent foreign policy. Pakistan's [foreign policy](/wiki/Foreign_policy_of_Pakistan) and [geostrategy](/wiki/Pakistani_geostrategy) focus on the economy, security, [national identity](/wiki/Nationalism_in_Pakistan), and territorial integrity, as well as building close ties with other Muslim nations. According to [Hasan Askari Rizvi](/wiki/Hasan_Askari_Rizvi), a foreign policy expert, "Pakistan highlights sovereign equality of states, bilateralism, mutuality of interests, and non-interference in each other's domestic affairs as the cardinal features of its foreign policy."

President [Ayub Khan](/wiki/Ayub_Khan) meeting with U.S. President [John F. Kennedy](/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) in 1961

The [Kashmir conflict](/wiki/Kashmir_conflict) remains a major issue between Pakistan and India, with three of their [four wars](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_wars_and_conflicts) fought over it. Due partly to strained relations with India, Pakistan has close ties with Turkey and Iran, both focal points in its foreign policy. Saudi Arabia also holds importance in Pakistan's foreign relations.

As a non-signatory of the [Treaty on Nuclear Non-Proliferation](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Non-Proliferation_of_Nuclear_Weapons), Pakistan holds influence in the [IAEA](/wiki/International_Atomic_Energy_Agency). For years, Pakistan has blocked an [international treaty](/wiki/Fissile_Material_Cut-off_Treaty) to limit [fissile material](/wiki/Fissile_material), arguing that its stockpile does not meet its long-term needs. Pakistan's nuclear program in the 20th century aimed to counter India's nuclear ambitions in the [region](/wiki/South_Asia), and [reciprocal nuclear tests](/wiki/Pakistan%27s_nuclear_testing_series) ensued after India's [nuclear tests](/wiki/Pokhran-II), solidifying Pakistan as a [nuclear power](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Pakistan). Pakistan maintains a policy of [Full spectrum deterrence](/wiki/N-deterrence), considering its nuclear program vital for [deterring](/wiki/Pakistan_and_its_Nuclear_Deterrent_Program) foreign aggression.

Prime Minister [Shehbaz Sharif](/wiki/Shehbaz_Sharif) at the 2022 [Shanghai Cooperation Organisation](/wiki/Shanghai_Cooperation_Organisation) summit

Located strategically in the world's major maritime oil supply lines and communication [fiber optic corridors](/wiki/Optical_fiber), Pakistan also enjoys proximity to the natural resources of Central Asian countries. Pakistan actively participates in the United Nations with a [Permanent Representative](/wiki/Permanent_Representative_of_Pakistan_to_the_United_Nations) representing its positions in international politics. It has advocated for the concept of "[enlightened moderation](/wiki/Enlightened_moderation)" in the Muslim world. Pakistan is a member of the Commonwealth of Nations, [SAARC](/wiki/South_Asian_Association_for_Regional_Cooperation), [ECO](/wiki/Economic_Cooperation_Organization), and the [G20 developing nations](/wiki/G20_developing_nations).

Pakistan is designated as an "Iron Brother" by China, emphasising the significance of their close and supportive [relationship](/wiki/China%E2%80%93Pakistan_relations). In the 1950s, Pakistan opposed the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) for geopolitical reasons. During the [Soviet–Afghan War](/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War) in the 1980s, it was a close ally of the United States. Relations with Russia have improved since the end of the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War), but Pakistan's relationship with the United States has been "on-and-off". Initially a close ally during the Cold War, Pakistan's relations with the US soured in the 1990s due to [sanctions](/wiki/Pressler_amendment) over its secretive nuclear program. Since [9/11](/wiki/September_11_attacks), Pakistan has been a US ally on [counterterrorism](/wiki/Counterterrorism), but their relationship has been strained due to diverging interests and mistrust during the [20-year war](/wiki/War_in_Afghanistan_(2001%E2%80%932021)) and terrorism issues. Although Pakistan was granted [major non-NATO ally](/wiki/Major_non-NATO_ally) status by the U.S. in 2004, it faced accusations of supporting the [Taliban insurgents](/wiki/Taliban_insurgency) in Afghanistan.

Pakistan does not have formal [diplomatic relations](/wiki/Pakistan-Israel_relations) with [Israel](/wiki/Israel); nonetheless, an exchange occurred between the two countries in 2005, with Turkey acting as an intermediary.

#### Relations with China

Main article: [China–Pakistan relations](/wiki/China%E2%80%93Pakistan_relations)

Pakistani Prime Minister [Huseyn Shaheed Suhrawardy](/wiki/Huseyn_Shaheed_Suhrawardy) with Chinese Premier [Zhou Enlai](/wiki/Zhou_Enlai) signing the Treaty of Friendship Between China and Pakistan. Pakistan is host to China's largest embassy.

Pakistan was among the first nations to establish formal diplomatic ties with China, forging a strong relationship since China's [1962 conflict](/wiki/Sino-Indian_War) with India, culminating in a special bond. During the 1970s, Pakistan acted as an intermediary in U.S.-China rapprochement, facilitating US President [Richard Nixon](/wiki/Richard_Nixon)'s [historic visit](/wiki/1972_Nixon_visit_to_China) to China. Despite changes in Pakistani governance and regional/global dynamics, China's influence in Pakistan remains paramount. In reciprocation, China stands as Pakistan's largest trading partner, with substantial investment in Pakistani infrastructure, notably the [Gwadar port](/wiki/Gwadar_port). In 2015 alone, they inked 51 agreements and Memorandums of Understanding (MoUs) for cooperative efforts. Both nations signed a [Free Trade Agreement](/wiki/China%E2%80%93Pakistan_Free_Trade_Agreement) in 2006, with China making its largest investment in Pakistan's history through [CPEC](/wiki/CPEC). Pakistan acts as China's liaison to the Muslim world, and both nations support each other on sensitive issues like Kashmir, Taiwan, Xinjiang, and more.

#### Relations with the Muslim world

Pakistani Prime Minister [Zulfikar Ali Bhutto](/wiki/Zulfikar_Ali_Bhutto) with leaders from [Algeria](/wiki/Algeria), [Bangladesh](/wiki/Bangladesh), and [Saudi Arabia](/wiki/Saudi_Arabia) in [Lahore](/wiki/Lahore), February 1974. That year, Pakistan hosted 36 Muslim countries at the [Second Islamic Summit](/wiki/Islamic_Summit_of_the_Organisation_of_Islamic_Cooperation#List_of_summits) of the [Organisation of Islamic Cooperation](/wiki/Organisation_of_Islamic_Cooperation).

After Independence, Pakistan vigorously pursued bilateral relations with other Muslim countries. The [Ali](/wiki/Maulana_Muhammad_Ali_Jauhar) brothers sought to project Pakistan as the natural leader of the Islamic world, partly due to its significant manpower and military strength. [Khaliquzzaman](/wiki/Chaudhry_Khaliquzzaman), a prominent [Muslim League](/wiki/All_India_Muslim_League) leader, declared Pakistan's ambition to unite all Muslim countries into [Islamistan](/wiki/Islamistan), a pan-Islamic entity.

These developments, alongside Pakistan's creation, didn't receive approval from the United States, with British Prime Minister [Clement Attlee](/wiki/Clement_Attlee) expressing a hope for India and Pakistan to reunite. However, due to a nationalist awakening in the Arab world at that time, there was little interest in Pakistan's Pan-Islamic aspirations. Some Arab countries perceived the 'Islamistan' project as Pakistan's bid to dominate other Muslim states.

Pakistan's founder, [Muhammad Ali Jinnah](/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_Jinnah), consistently advocated for the [Palestinian cause](/wiki/Palestinian_nationalism), shaping Pakistan's foreign policy to support Palestinian rights within the broader framework of Muslim solidarity. During the [1967 Arab-Israel war](/wiki/Six-Day_War), Pakistan supported the Arab states and played a key role in securing Iran's backing for the Arab cause both within the U.N. and beyond.

Pakistan's relations with Iran have been strained by sectarian tensions, with both Iran and Saudi Arabia using Pakistan as a battleground for their proxy sectarian war. Since the early days of the [Iran–Iraq war](/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_war), President [Zia-ul-Haq](/wiki/Zia-ul-Haq) played an important mediatory role, with Pakistan actively engaging in efforts to end the conflict. Pakistan provided support to Saudi Arabia during the [Gulf War](/wiki/Gulf_War). Pakistan chose to remain neutral during [Operation Decisive Storm](/wiki/Operation_Decisive_Storm), refraining from sending military support to Saudi Arabia in its offensive against [Yemen](/wiki/Yemen). Instead, Pakistan aimed to play a proactive diplomatic role in resolving the crisis, which led to tensions between the two countries. In 2016, Pakistan mediated between Saudi Arabia and Iran following the execution of Shia cleric [Nimr al-Nimr](/wiki/Nimr_al-Nimr), with visits to both countries by then Prime Minister [Nawaz Sharif](/wiki/Nawaz_Sharif) and the Chief of Army Staff, [Raheel Sharif](/wiki/Raheel_Sharif).

Pakistan provided refuge to millions of displaced Afghans after the [Soviet invasion](/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War) and supported the [Afghan mujahideen](/wiki/Afghan_mujahideen) in their efforts to expel Soviet forces from Afghanistan. After the Soviets withdrew, [infighting erupted among Mujahideen factions](/wiki/Afghan_Civil_War_(1989%E2%80%931992)) over control of Afghanistan. Pakistan facilitated [peace talks](/wiki/Peshawar_Accord) to help end the conflict. After four years of unresolved conflict between rival Mujahideen groups, Pakistan helped establish the Taliban as a stabilising force. Pakistan's support for the Sunni Taliban in Afghanistan challenged Shia-led Iran, which opposed a Taliban-controlled Afghanistan.

Pakistan vigorously advocated for self-determination among Muslims globally. Its efforts in supporting independence movements in countries like Indonesia, Algeria, Tunisia, Morocco, and [Eritrea](/wiki/Eritrea) fostered strong ties. Due to its support for [Azerbaijan](/wiki/Azerbaijan) in the [Nagorno-Karabakh conflict](/wiki/Nagorno-Karabakh_conflict), Pakistan has not established [diplomatic relations](/wiki/Armenia%E2%80%93Pakistan_relations) with [Armenia](/wiki/Armenia).

Pakistan and Bangladesh have experienced strained relations, particularly under the [Awami League](/wiki/Awami_League) governments led by [Sheikh Hasina](/wiki/Sheikh_Hasina), driven by her pro-India stance and historical grievances.

Pakistan, a prominent member of the [Organisation of Islamic Cooperation](/wiki/Organisation_of_Islamic_Cooperation) (OIC), prioritises maintaining cultural, political, social, and economic relations with Arab and other Muslim-majority nations in its foreign policy.

In September 2025, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia signed a [Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement](/wiki/Strategic_Mutual_Defence_Agreement) under which any aggression against one country is considered an aggression against both, formalising a defensive commitment that also extends Pakistan's nuclear umbrella to Saudi Arabia.

### Kashmir conflict

Main article: [Kashmir conflict](/wiki/Kashmir_conflict)

The areas shown in green are the Pakistani-controlled areas.

[Kashmir](/wiki/Kashmir), a Himalayan region at the northern tip of the [Indian subcontinent](/wiki/Indian_subcontinent), was governed as the autonomous [princely state](/wiki/Princely_state) of [Jammu and Kashmir](/wiki/Jammu_and_Kashmir_(princely_state)) during the [British Raj](/wiki/British_Raj) before the [Partition of India](/wiki/Partition_of_India) in August 1947. This sparked a [major territorial dispute](/wiki/Kashmir_conflict) between India and Pakistan, resulting in [several conflicts](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_wars_and_conflicts) over the region. India controls about 45.1% of Kashmir, including [Jammu and Kashmir](/wiki/Jammu_and_Kashmir_(union_territory)) and [Ladakh](/wiki/Ladakh), while Pakistan controls roughly 38.2%, comprising [Azad Jammu and Kashmir](/wiki/Azad_Kashmir) and [Gilgit−Baltistan](/wiki/Gilgit-Baltistan). Additionally, about 20% of the region, known as [Aksai Chin](/wiki/Aksai_Chin) and the [Shaksgam Valley](/wiki/Trans-Karakoram_Tract), is under Chinese control. India claims the entire Kashmir region based on the [Instrument of Accession](/wiki/Instrument_of_Accession_(Jammu_and_Kashmir)) signed by the princely state's ruler, Maharaja Hari Singh, while Pakistan argues for its Muslim-majority population, and geographical proximity to Pakistan. The United Nations was involved in resolving the conflict, leading to a ceasefire in 1949 and the establishment of the [Line of Control](/wiki/Line_of_Control) (LoC) as a *de facto* border. India, fearing Kashmir's secession, did not hold the promised plebiscite, as it believed Kashmiris would vote to join Pakistan.

Pakistan claims that its position is for the right of the [Kashmiri people](/wiki/Kashmiris) to determine their future through impartial elections as mandated by the United Nations, while India has stated that Kashmir is an "[integral part](/wiki/Akhand_Bharat)" of India, referring to the [1972 Simla Agreement](/wiki/Simla_Agreement) and to the fact that [regional elections](/wiki/Elections_in_Jammu_and_Kashmir) take place regularly. Certain Kashmiri independence groups believe that Kashmir should be independent of both India and Pakistan.

### Military

Main article: [Pakistan Armed Forces](/wiki/Pakistan_Armed_Forces)

[Pakistan Air Force](/wiki/Pakistan_Air_Force)'s [JF-17 Thunder](/wiki/JF-17_Thunder) flying in front of the 8,130-metre-high (26,660-foot) [Nanga Parbat](/wiki/Nanga_Parbat)

Pakistan is considered a [middle power](/wiki/Middle_power) nation, with the world's [seventh-largest standing armed forces](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_military_and_paramilitary_personnel) in terms of personnel size, comprising approximately 660,000 active-duty troops and 291,000 paramilitary personnel as of 2024. [Established in 1947](/wiki/Military_history_of_Pakistan), the [armed forces of Pakistan](/wiki/Armed_forces_of_Pakistan) wielded significant influence over [national politics](/wiki/Politics_of_Pakistan). The main branches include the [Army](/wiki/Pakistan_Army), [Navy](/wiki/Pakistan_Navy), and [Air Force](/wiki/Pakistan_Air_Force), supported by numerous [paramilitaries](/wiki/Civil_Armed_Forces).

The [Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee](/wiki/Chairman_Joint_Chiefs_of_Staff_Committee) (CJCSC) is the highest-ranking military officer, advising the civilian government. However, they lack direct command over the branches and serve as intermediaries, ensuring communication between the military and civilian leadership. Overseeing the [Joint Staff Headquarters](/wiki/Joint_Staff_Headquarters_(Pakistan)), they coordinate inter-service cooperation and joint military missions.

[Command and control](/wiki/Nuclear_command_and_control) over Pakistan's [strategic arsenal](/wiki/Pakistan%27s_nuclear_testing_series) development and employment is vested in the [National Command Authority](/wiki/National_Command_Authority_(Pakistan)), overseeing work on [nuclear doctrine](/wiki/Nuclear_Doctrine_of_Pakistan) to maintain [Full spectrum deterrence](/wiki/Full_spectrum_deterrence).

The United States, Turkey, and China maintain close military relations with [Pakistan Armed Forces](/wiki/Pakistan_Armed_Forces), regularly exporting military equipment and [technology transfer](/wiki/Technology_transfer). Pakistan was the 5th-largest recipient and importer of arms between 2019 and 2023.

#### Military history

Main article: [Military history of Pakistan](/wiki/Military_history_of_Pakistan)

Since 1947, Pakistan has been involved in [four](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_wars_and_conflicts) [conventional wars](/wiki/Conventional_warfare) with India. The [first conflict](/wiki/Indo-Pak_war_of_1947) took place in Kashmir and ended in a United Nations-mediated ceasefire, with Pakistan gaining control of one-third of the region. Territorial disputes led to [another war in 1965](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1965). In 1971, India and Pakistan fought [another war](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1971) over [East Pakistan](/wiki/East_Pakistan), with Indian forces aiding its independence, leading to the creation of [Bangladesh](/wiki/Bangladesh). Tensions in Kargil brought the two countries to the [brink of war](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1999).

Pakistan's primary intelligence agency, the Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI), was established within a year of Pakistan's independence in 1947. During the [Soviet–Afghan War](/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War), Pakistan's intelligence community, mostly the [ISI](/wiki/Inter-Services_Intelligence), [coordinated](/wiki/Operation_Cyclone) [US resources](/wiki/United_States_involvement_in_regime_change) to support [Afghan mujahideen](/wiki/Afghan_mujahideen) and [foreign fighters](/wiki/Afghan_Arabs) against Soviet presence. The [PAF](/wiki/Pakistan_Air_Force) engaged with [Soviet](/wiki/Soviet_Air_Forces) and [Afghan Air Forces](/wiki/Afghan_Air_Force) during the [conflict](/wiki/Russian_war_in_afghanistan). Pakistan has been an active [participant in UN peacekeeping missions](/wiki/United_Nations_peacekeeping_missions_involving_Pakistan), playing a major role in operations like the rescue mission in [Mogadishu](/wiki/Mogadishu), Somalia, in 1993. According to a 2023 UN report, the Pakistani military was the fifth largest troop contributor to UN peacekeeping missions.

Pakistan has [deployed its military](/wiki/Pakistan_Armed_Forces_deployments) in some Arab countries, providing defense, training, and advisory roles. The [PAF](/wiki/Pakistan_Air_Force)'s fighter pilots participated in missions against Israel during the [Six-Day War](/wiki/Six-Day_War) and the [Yom Kippur War](/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War). Pakistani [special forces](/wiki/Special_Service_Group) assisted [Saudi forces](/wiki/Saudi_forces) in [Mecca](/wiki/Mecca) during the [Grand Mosque Seizure](/wiki/Grand_Mosque_Seizure). Pakistan also sent 5,000 troops as part of a US-led coalition for the defense of Saudi Arabia during the [Gulf War](/wiki/Gulf_War).

Despite the UN arms embargo on [Bosnia](/wiki/Bosnia), the ISI under General [Javed Nasir](/wiki/Javed_Nasir) airlifted anti-tank weapons and missiles to Bosnian mujahideen, shifting the tide in favour of Bosnian Muslims. ISI, under Nasir's leadership, supported Chinese Muslims in [Xinjiang](/wiki/Xinjiang), rebel groups in the [Philippines](/wiki/Philippines), and religious groups in Central Asia.

Since 2001, the Pakistan military has been engaged in [counterinsurgency and internal security operations](/wiki/War_in_North-West_Pakistan) in the [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](/wiki/Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) province, primarily targeting [Tehrik-e-Taliban Pakistan](/wiki/Tehrik-e-Taliban_Pakistan) and associated militant groups. Major military operations conducted during this period include [Operation Enduring Freedom](/wiki/Operation_Enduring_Freedom), [Operation al-Mizan](/wiki/Operation_al-Mizan), [Operation Zalzala](/wiki/Operation_Zalzala), [Operation Sherdil](/wiki/Operation_Sherdil), [Operation Rah-e-Haq](/wiki/Operation_Rah-e-Haq), [Operation Rah-e-Rast](/wiki/Operation_Rah-e-Rast), and [Operation Rah-e-Nijat](/wiki/Operation_Rah-e-Nijat).

### Law enforcement

Main article: [Law enforcement in Pakistan](/wiki/Law_enforcement_in_Pakistan)

See also: [Pakistani Intelligence Community](/wiki/Pakistani_Intelligence_Community)

[Law enforcement in Pakistan](/wiki/Law_enforcement_in_Pakistan) consists of federal and provincial police agencies. Each of the [four provinces](/wiki/Administrative_units_of_Pakistan) ([Punjab](/wiki/Punjab,_Pakistan), [Sindh](/wiki/Sindh), [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](/wiki/Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa), and [Balochistan](/wiki/Balochistan,_Pakistan)) has its own police force, while the [Islamabad Capital Territory (ICT)](/wiki/Islamabad_Capital_Territory) has the Islamabad Police. Provincial police forces are led by an Inspector-General of Police (IGP), who is appointed from the federally recruited and trained [Police Service of Pakistan](/wiki/Police_Service_of_Pakistan) (PSP) through a consultative process between the federal and provincial governments. All positions above the Assistant Superintendent level are filled from the PSP, ensuring national standards across provincial forces.

**Specialised Units:**

- [National Highways & Motorway Police](/wiki/National_Highways_%26_Motorway_Police) (NHMP): Enforces traffic laws and ensures safety on Pakistan's inter-provincial motorway network.
- Specialised Rapid Response Units: Specialised counter-terrorism units, such as the [Punjab Elite Police Force](/wiki/Elite_Police), have been trained by army commandos and exist in every province to respond to hostage situations and neutralise armed groups.

The [Civil Armed Forces](/wiki/Civil_Armed_Forces) (CAF) assist local law enforcement agencies and participate in border security and internal security operations, particularly in conflict-affected regions.

In 2021, the [National Intelligence Coordination Committee](/wiki/National_Intelligence_Coordination_Committee_(Pakistan)) was established to improve coordination among Pakistan's intelligence agencies. The inaugural meeting was attended by the heads of the [ISI](/wiki/Inter-Services_Intelligence), [IB](/wiki/Intelligence_Bureau_(Pakistan)), and [FIA](/wiki/Federal_Investigation_Agency).

### Human rights

Main articles: [Human rights in Pakistan](/wiki/Human_rights_in_Pakistan) and [LGBT rights in Pakistan](/wiki/LGBT_rights_in_Pakistan)

Pakistan scored 27 out of 100 on the [Corruption Perceptions Index](/wiki/Corruption_Perceptions_Index) in 2024, ranking 135th out of 180 countries in the assessment of perceived public-sector corruption. In 2025, Pakistan ranked 158 out of 180 countries in the [Press Freedom Index](/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index) by [Reporters Without Borders](/wiki/Reporters_Without_Borders), highlighting restrictions on [freedom of the press](/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press). According to the journalist [Raza Rumi](/wiki/Raza_Rumi), in 2023, TV channels in Pakistan faced suspensions and legal threats for airing content critical of the government or military, while online platforms also experienced temporary takedowns. According to a 2025 report, some newspapers have faced financial pressure—such as withdrawal of government advertisements—for publishing content critical of government policies. Both military and civilian governments have historically used such tactics.

In Pakistan, all sexual activity outside of marriage is illegal. The punishment for sex outside marriage (*[zina](/wiki/Zina)*) ranges from up to five years' imprisonment for minors to 100 lashes for unmarried adults and [stoning to death](/wiki/Stoning_to_death) for married adults, depending on marital status, age, sanity, and whether strict evidentiary requirements for a *[hadd](/wiki/Hadd)* punishment—such as four adult male Muslim witnesses or a confession—are met; however, no one has been stoned to death under the law to date. Male [homosexuality](/wiki/Homosexuality) is illegal in Pakistan and is punishable by up to ten years in prison, [corporal punishment](/wiki/Corporal_punishment) such as [whipping](/wiki/Whipping), and, under [Islamic law](/wiki/Islamic_law) since 1990, potentially even stoning.

[Honour killings](/wiki/Honour_killings), locally referred to as *[karo-kari](/wiki/Karo-kari)* in parts of Pakistan, involve the killing of women and, less frequently, men by family or community members over alleged extramarital relationships or perceived violations of honour. Human rights organisations report that hundreds of women are killed each year, with many cases unreported. Pakistan also has a high number of reported [acid attacks](/wiki/Acid_attacks), with about 200 incidents annually as of 2023, most victims being women. According to [Masarrat Misbah](/wiki/Masarrat_Misbah), founder of the [Depilex Smileagain Foundation](/wiki/Depilex_Smileagain_Foundation), women have been targeted for rejecting marriage proposals, giving birth to daughters instead of sons, or insufficient dowry. The 2011 Acid and Burn Crime Bill criminalised acid attacks, making them punishable by lengthy imprisonment and fines.

In parts of Pakistan, girls are given in marriage to settle disputes or crimes between families in a customary practice called *[Vani](/wiki/Vani_(custom))*. Girls married through this practice are often treated as compensation for a dispute, which places them in a vulnerable social position within the family they marry into. In 2021, the [Federal Shariat Court](/wiki/Federal_Shariat_Court) declared the practice un-Islamic and unconstitutional, reaffirming earlier Supreme Court jurisprudence.

[UN Women](/wiki/UN_Women) reported in 2024 that around one in six girls in Pakistan are married during childhood, with the country home to nearly 19 million child brides. In 2025, Pakistan enacted a law setting the minimum legal age for marriage at 18 and introducing penalties for arranging underage marriages; the law is limited to the [Islamabad Capital Territory](/wiki/Islamabad_Capital_Territory) and does not apply nationwide.

Pakistan's economy [ranks 27th](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(PPP)) globally by [purchasing power parity](/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity) (PPP) and [42nd by nominal GDP](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)). Historically, Pakistan was part of the wealthiest [region](/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) in the first millennium CE, but lost ground to regions like China and Western Europe by the 18th century. Pakistan is a [developing country](/wiki/Developing_country), and part of the [Next Eleven](/wiki/Next_Eleven), poised to become one of the world's largest economies in the 21st century, alongside the [BRIC](/wiki/BRIC_(economics_term)) countries. The semi-industrialised economy is heavily dependent on agriculture, and industrial growth in Pakistan benefits significantly from agricultural expansion. In 2023, Pakistan was the 66th-largest export economy, with a trade deficit of US$21.3 billion.

Rising skyline of [Karachi](/wiki/Karachi), with several under construction skyscrapers

As of 2025, Pakistan's [nominal GDP](/wiki/Nominal_GDP) stood at US$407.79 billion, while GDP ([PPP](/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity)) was US$1.8 trillion in 2026. The nominal per capita GDP was US$1,700, and the [GDP (PPP)/capita](/wiki/GDP_(PPP)_per_capita) amounted to US$6,950 ([international dollars](/wiki/International_dollar)). Around 44.7% of the population live below the international poverty line of US$4.20 a day, 16.5% live in extreme poverty on less than US$3 a day, 8% of the total labour force is unemployed, and [CPI](/wiki/Consumer_price_index) inflation in 2025 was 4.5%. Pakistan ranks near the bottom globally on the [Global Gender Gap Index](/wiki/Global_Gender_Gap_Index)'s economic participation and opportunity sub-index for women, reflecting very low female labour force participation and limited representation of women in senior economic roles. Pakistan's middle class is estimated to be around 40–50 million people, representing 15–20 per cent of the population. Among [South Asian](/wiki/South_Asia) countries, Pakistan's GDP growth rate of 2.5% in 2024 was the second lowest after Afghanistan.

Pakistan has strong potential to produce diverse food commodities due to its vast natural resource base across varied ecological and climatic zones. It has the world's 7th-largest [labour force](/wiki/Labour_in_Pakistan), and its [diaspora](/wiki/Pakistani_diaspora) of around 10 million, sent US$38.3 billion in remittances in fiscal year 2024–25, as reported by the [Special Investment Facilitation Council](/wiki/Special_Investment_Facilitation_Council). Pakistan's share of global exports averaged 0.14% between 1990 and 2023 and was 0.12% in 2023.

### Agriculture

Main article: [Agriculture in Pakistan](/wiki/Agriculture_in_Pakistan)

The Pakistani economy has shifted from [agriculture](/wiki/Agriculture_in_Pakistan) to services, with agriculture contributing only 20.9% of the GDP as of 2015. Despite this, Pakistan's wheat production in 2005 surpassed Africa's and nearly matched South America's, highlighting its agricultural significance. The sector employs 43.5% of the labour force and is a major source of foreign exchange.

Manufactured exports, heavily reliant on agricultural raw materials like cotton and hides, face inflationary pressures due to supply shortages and market disruptions. Pakistan ranks fifth in cotton production, self-sufficient in sugarcane, and the fourth-largest milk producer globally. Though land and water resources haven't increased proportionately, productivity gains, especially from the [Green Revolution](/wiki/Green_Revolution) in the late 1960s and 1970s, significantly boosted wheat and rice yields. Private tube wells and High Yielding Varieties (HYVs) further augmented crop yields. Meat industry accounts for 1.4 percent of overall GDP.

### Industry

Main article: [Industry of Pakistan](/wiki/Industry_of_Pakistan)

See also: [Textile industry in Pakistan](/wiki/Textile_industry_in_Pakistan)

Television assembly factory in [Lahore](/wiki/Lahore). Pakistan's industrial sector accounts for about 20% of the GDP, and is dominated by [small and medium-sized enterprises](/wiki/Small_and_medium-sized_enterprises).

Industry, constituting 19.74% of GDP and 24% of total employment, is the second-largest sector. Large-scale manufacturing (LSM) dominates, representing 12.2% of GDP, with cement production thriving due to demand from Afghanistan and the domestic real estate sector. In 2013, Pakistan exported 7,708,557 metric tons of cement, with an installed capacity of 44,768,250 metric tons. The [textile industry](/wiki/Textile_industry_in_Pakistan), a key player in Pakistan's manufacturing, contributes 9.5% to GDP and employs around 15 million people. As of 2022, Pakistan ranks seventh globally in cotton production, with substantial spinning capacity, making it a major exporter of textile products in Asia. China has been a significant buyer of Pakistani textiles, importing US$1.527 billion worth of textiles in 2012.

### Services

As of 2014–15, the services sector contributes 58.8% to GDP, serving as the main driver of economic growth in Pakistan, with a consumption-oriented society. The sector's growth rate surpasses that of agriculture and industry, accounting for 54% of GDP and over one-third of total employment. It has strong linkages with other sectors, providing essential inputs to agriculture and manufacturing. Pakistan's IT sector is one of the fastest-growing, ranked 110th for ICT development by the [World Economic Forum](/wiki/World_Economic_Forum). With around 82 million internet users as of May 2020, Pakistan [ranks](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_Internet_users) among top ten globally, and its ICT industry is projected to exceed $10 billion by 2020. With 12,000 employees, Pakistan is among the top five freelancing nations, and its export performance in telecom, computer, and information services has notably improved.

### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in Pakistan](/wiki/Tourism_in_Pakistan)

[Shangrila Lake](/wiki/Shangrila_Lake) and adjoining resort in [Skardu](/wiki/Skardu), [Gilgit-Baltistan](/wiki/Gilgit-Baltistan)

With its diverse cultures, landscapes, and attractions, Pakistan drew around 6.6 million foreign tourists in 2018. However, this was a decline from the peak of tourism in the 1970s driven by the popular [Hippie trail](/wiki/Hippie_trail). Pakistan boasts attractions from [mangroves](/wiki/Indus_River_Delta-Arabian_Sea_mangroves) in the south to Himalayan [hill stations](/wiki/List_of_hill_stations_of_Pakistan) in the northeast, including ancient Buddhist ruins of [Takht-i-Bahi](/wiki/Takht-i-Bahi) and [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila), the 5,000-year-old [Indus Valley civilisation](/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilisation) sites such as [Mohenjo-daro](/wiki/Mohenjo-daro) and [Harappa](/wiki/Harappa), and numerous [mountain peaks](/wiki/List_of_mountains_in_Pakistan) over 7,000 metres (23,000 feet). The northern part of Pakistan boasts numerous old fortresses, showcasing ancient architecture. It encompasses the [Hunza](/wiki/Hunza_Valley) and [Chitral valleys](/wiki/Chitral_valley), where the small pre-Islamic [Kalasha](/wiki/Kalash_people) community resides, claiming descent from Alexander the Great. Lahore, Pakistan's cultural capital, showcases numerous examples of [Mughal architecture](/wiki/Mughal_architecture), including the [Badshahi Masjid](/wiki/Badshahi_Masjid), the [Shalimar Gardens](/wiki/Shalimar_Gardens_(Lahore)), the [Tomb of Jahangir](/wiki/Tomb_of_Jahangir), and the [Lahore Fort](/wiki/Lahore_Fort). Following the [2005 Kashmir earthquake](/wiki/2005_Kashmir_earthquake), *[The Guardian](/wiki/The_Guardian)* highlighted "The top five tourist sites in Pakistan" to boost tourism, featuring destinations like [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila), [Lahore](/wiki/Lahore), the [Karakoram Highway](/wiki/Karakoram_Highway), [Karimabad](/wiki/Karimabad_(Hunza)), and [Lake Saiful Muluk](/wiki/Lake_Saiful_Muluk). Festivals and government initiatives aim to promote Pakistan's cultural heritage. In 2015, the [World Economic Forum](/wiki/World_Economic_Forum) ranked Pakistan 125th out of 141 countries in its Travel & Tourism Competitiveness Report.

### Natural resources

Main articles: [Fuel extraction in Pakistan](/wiki/Fuel_extraction_in_Pakistan) and [Mining in Pakistan](/wiki/Mining_in_Pakistan)

[Surface mining](/wiki/Surface_mining) in [Sindh](/wiki/Sindh)

Pakistan's untapped coal reserves have been estimated as the fifth largest in the world and equivalent to about 400 billion barrels of oil, exceeding the combined oil reserves of Saudi Arabia and Iran. [Daniel Fitzgerald Runde](/wiki/Daniel_Fitzgerald_Runde) wrote in [Forbes](/wiki/Forbes) in 2016 that Pakistan "could be the 'Saudi Arabia of Coal'."

The [Reko Diq Mine](/wiki/Reko_Diq_Mine) has one of the world's largest undeveloped [copper](/wiki/Copper) deposits, with an estimated 15 million tonnes in reserves, and is expected to become the world’s fifth largest copper mine upon completion; a feasibility study also estimates that it could produce approximately 17.9 million ounces of gold over its projected 37-year mine life, with total copper and gold reserves valued at over $60 billion at prevailing prices.

## Infrastructure

See also: [Water supply and sanitation in Pakistan](/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Pakistan)

Pakistan was the sixteenth most water-stressed country in the world in 2022.

Pakistan was lauded as the top nation for infrastructure development in South Asia during the 2016 annual meetings of the IMF and World Bank.

### Power and energy

Main articles: [Nuclear power in Pakistan](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Pakistan), [Energy in Pakistan](/wiki/Energy_in_Pakistan), and [Electricity sector in Pakistan](/wiki/Electricity_sector_in_Pakistan)

[Tarbela Dam](/wiki/Tarbela_Dam), the largest earth filled dam in the world, was constructed in 1968.

As of May 2021, Pakistan operates six licensed commercial [nuclear power plants](/wiki/Nuclear_power_plants). The [Pakistan Atomic Energy Commission](/wiki/Pakistan_Atomic_Energy_Commission) (PAEC) oversees these plants, while the [Pakistan Nuclear Regulatory Authority](/wiki/Pakistan_Nuclear_Regulatory_Authority) ensures their safe operation. These plants contribute approximately 5.8% to Pakistan's electricity supply, while fossil fuels (crude oil and natural gas) provide 64.2%, [hydroelectric power](/wiki/Hydroelectric_power) provides 29.9%, and [coal](/wiki/Coal_power_plant) contributes 0.1%. The [KANUPP-I](/wiki/Karachi_Nuclear_Power_Plant), Pakistan's first commercial nuclear power plant, was supplied by Canada in 1971. Sino-Pakistani nuclear cooperation began in the 1980s, leading to the establishment of CHASNUPP-I. In 2005, both countries proposed a joint energy security plan, aiming for a generation capacity exceeding 160,000 [MWe](/wiki/Megawatt) by 2030. Pakistan's Nuclear Energy Vision 2050 targets a capacity of 40,000 MWe, with 8,900 MWe expected by 2030.

In June 2008, the nuclear complex at [Chashma](/wiki/Chashma_Nuclear_Power_Plant) in Punjab Province expanded with the installation of [Chashma-III](/wiki/Chashma_Nuclear_Power_Plant) and [Chashma–IV](/wiki/Chashma_Nuclear_Power_Plant) reactors, each with 325–340 MWe, costing [₨](/wiki/Pakistani_rupee)129 billion, with ₨80 billion from international sources, mainly China. Another agreement for China's assistance was signed in October 2008, seen as a response to the [US–India agreement](/wiki/India%E2%80%93United_States_Civil_Nuclear_Agreement). The project's cost was then US$1.7 billion, with a foreign loan of US$1.07 billion. In 2013, Pakistan established a second [nuclear complex](/wiki/Karachi_Nuclear_Power_Complex) in Karachi with plans for additional reactors, similar to Chashma. [Electrical energy in Pakistan](/wiki/Electricity_in_Pakistan) is generated by [various corporations](/wiki/Electricity_distribution_companies_of_Pakistan) and distributed evenly among the [four provinces](/wiki/Four_Provinces_(Pakistan)) by the [National Electric Power Regulatory Authority](/wiki/National_Electric_Power_Regulatory_Authority) (NEPRA). However, Karachi-based [K-Electric](/wiki/K-Electric) and [Water and Power Development Authority](/wiki/Water_and_Power_Development_Authority) (WAPDA) generate much of the electricity used in Pakistan and collect revenue nationwide. In 2023, Pakistan's installed [electricity generation](/wiki/Electricity_generation) capacity was ~45,885 MWt. Pakistan produced 1,135 megawatts of [renewable energy](/wiki/Renewable_energy) for the month of October 2016. Pakistan expects to produce 10,000 megawatts of renewable energy by 2025.

### Transport

Main article: [Transport in Pakistan](/wiki/Transport_in_Pakistan)

The motorway passes through the [Salt Range](/wiki/Salt_Range) mountains.

[Karachi Cantonment railway station](/wiki/Karachi_Cantonment_railway_station)

Pakistan boasts 2567 km of motorways and approximately 263,942 km of highways, which handle 92% of passengers and 96% of freight traffic. Despite constituting only 4.6% of the total road length, these north–south links manage 85% of the nation's traffic. They connect southern seaports such as [Karachi port](/wiki/Port_of_Karachi) and [Port Qasim](/wiki/Port_Qasim) in Sindh, along with [Gwadar Port](/wiki/Gwadar_port) and [Port of Pasni](/wiki/Port_of_Pasni) in Balochistan, to populous provinces like Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa domestically, and neighbouring countries like Afghanistan, [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia), and China through the [China Pakistan Economic Corridor](/wiki/China_Pakistan_Economic_Corridor). According to the [WEF](/wiki/World_Economic_Forum)'s Global Competitiveness Report, Pakistan's port infrastructure quality ratings rose from 3.7 to 4.1 between 2007 and 2016. The railway's share of inland traffic is reduced to below 8% for passengers and 4% for freight. This shift led to a decrease in total rail track from 8,775 kilometres (5,453 miles) in 1990–91 to 7,791 kilometres (4,841 miles) in 2011.

[Karakoram Highway](/wiki/Karakoram_Highway), connecting Pakistan to China, is one of the highest paved roads in the world.

The transport landscape of Pakistan features various [modern transit](/wiki/Rapid_transit) systems. The [Orange Line Metro Train](/wiki/Orange_Line_(Lahore_Metro)) in Lahore, inaugurated in 2020, spans 27.1 km (16.8 mi), and includes both elevated and underground sections, accommodating over 250,000 passengers daily. Lahore also boasts the [Lahore Metrobus](/wiki/Lahore_Metrobus), the first of its kind in Pakistan, operational since February 2013. The [Rawalpindi-Islamabad Metrobus](/wiki/Rawalpindi-Islamabad_Metrobus), stretching 48.1 km, commenced its first phase in June 2015, with subsequent extensions, and employs e-ticketing and an Intelligent Transportation System. [Multan Metrobus](/wiki/Multan_Metrobus), inaugurated in January 2017, serves [Multan](/wiki/Multan) with its rapid transit services. [Peshawar's Bus Rapid Transit](/wiki/Peshawar_Metrobus), inaugurated in August 2020, marks the fourth BRT system in Pakistan. Karachi's [Green Line Metrobus](/wiki/Green_Line_-_Karachi_Metrobus), operational since December 2021, is part of a larger metrobus project financed by the Government of Pakistan and initiated in February 2016. Meanwhile, [Faisalabad](/wiki/Faisalabad) awaits its proposed rapid transit project, the [Faisalabad Metrobus](/wiki/Faisalabad_Metrobus). [Karachi Circular Railway](/wiki/Karachi_Circular_Railway), partially revived in November 2020, offers public transit services in the Karachi metropolitan area. Additionally, plans are underway to resurrect Karachi's [tramway service](/wiki/Mohamedali_Tramways_Company), which ceased operations in 1975, in collaboration with Austrian experts.

As of 2013, Pakistan boasts approximately 151 airports and airfields, encompassing both military and [civilian](/wiki/Pakistan_Civil_Aviation_Authority) installations. Despite [Jinnah International Airport](/wiki/Jinnah_International_Airport) serving as the primary international gateway, significant international traffic also flows through [Lahore](/wiki/Allama_Iqbal_International_Airport), [Islamabad](/wiki/Islamabad_International_Airport), [Peshawar](/wiki/Peshawar_International_Airport), [Quetta](/wiki/Quetta_International_Airport), [Faisalabad](/wiki/Faisalabad_International_Airport), [Sialkot](/wiki/Sialkot_International_Airport), and [Multan](/wiki/Multan_International_Airport) airports. The civil aviation industry, deregulated in 1993, operates with a blend of [public](/wiki/Nationalisation_in_Pakistan) and [private](/wiki/Privatisation_in_Pakistan) entities while [state-owned](/wiki/State_owned_enterprises) [Pakistan International Airlines](/wiki/Pakistan_International_Airlines) (PIA) dominates, carrying 73% of domestic passengers and all domestic freight.

### Science and technology

Main articles: [Science and technology in Pakistan](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_Pakistan) and [List of Pakistani inventions and discoveries](/wiki/List_of_Pakistani_inventions_and_discoveries)

[Abdus Salam](/wiki/Abdus_Salam) won the 1979 [Nobel Prize in Physics](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics) for his contribution to [electroweak interaction](/wiki/Electroweak_interaction). He was the first Muslim to win a Nobel prize in science.

[Atta-ur-Rahman](/wiki/Atta_ur_Rahman_(scientist)) won the [UNESCO Science Prize](/wiki/UNESCO_Science_Prize) for pioneering contributions in chemistry in 1999, the first Muslim to win it.

Developments in [science and technology](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_Pakistan) have played a significant role in Pakistan's infrastructure, linking the nation to the global community. Each year, the [Pakistan Academy of Sciences](/wiki/Pakistan_Academy_of_Sciences) and the government invite scientists worldwide to the [International Nathiagali Summer College on Physics](/wiki/International_Nathiagali_Summer_College_on_Physics). In 2005, Pakistan hosted an international seminar on "Physics in Developing Countries" for the International Year of Physics. Pakistani theoretical physicist [Abdus Salam](/wiki/Abdus_Salam) won a [Nobel Prize in Physics](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics) for his work on the [electroweak interaction](/wiki/Electroweak_interaction). Pakistani scientists have made notable contributions in mathematics, biology, economics, [computer science](/wiki/Computer_science), and [genetics](/wiki/Genetics).

In [chemistry](/wiki/Chemistry), [Salimuzzaman Siddiqui](/wiki/Salimuzzaman_Siddiqui) identified the medicinal properties of the [neem](/wiki/Neem) tree's components. [Ayub K. Ommaya](/wiki/Ayub_K._Ommaya) developed the [Ommaya reservoir](/wiki/Ommaya_reservoir) for treating brain conditions. Scientific research is integral to [Pakistani universities](/wiki/Pakistani_universities), national laboratories, [science parks](/wiki/Science_park), and the [industry](/wiki/Industry_of_Pakistan). [Abdul Qadeer Khan](/wiki/Abdul_Qadeer_Khan) spearheaded Pakistan's [HEU](/wiki/Enriched_uranium)-based [gas-centrifuge](/wiki/Zippe-type_centrifuge) [uranium enrichment](/wiki/Uranium_enrichment) program for its [atomic bomb project](/wiki/Pakistan_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction). He established the [Kahuta Research Laboratories](/wiki/Kahuta_Research_Laboratories) (KRL) in 1976, serving as both its senior scientist and the Director-General until his retirement in 2001. Besides atomic bomb project, he made significant contributions in [molecular morphology](/wiki/Morphology_(biology)), physical [martensite](/wiki/Martensite), and their applications in [condensed](/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics) and [material physics](/wiki/Material_physics).

In 2023, Pakistan ranked 26th globally in published scientific papers. The influential [Pakistan Academy of Sciences](/wiki/Pakistan_Academy_of_Sciences) guides the government on science policies. Pakistan was ranked 99th in the [Global Innovation Index](/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) by 2025.

The 1960s marked the rise of Pakistan's space program, led by [SUPARCO](/wiki/Space_and_Upper_Atmosphere_Research_Commission), yielding advancements in [rocketry](/wiki/Rocket), [electronics](/wiki/Electronics_Engineering), and [aeronomy](/wiki/Aeronomy). Notably, Pakistan launched its [first rocket](/wiki/Rehbar-I) into space, pioneering South Asia's space exploration. In 1990, it successfully launched its [first satellite](/wiki/Badr-1), becoming the first Muslim nation and second in South Asia to achieve this milestone.

> Pakistan witnessed a fourfold increase in its scientific productivity in the past decade surging from approximately 2,000 articles per year in 2006 to more than 9,000 articles in 2015. Making Pakistan's cited article's higher than the [BRIC](/wiki/BRIC_(economics_term)) countries put together.

— [Thomson Reuters](/wiki/Thomson_Reuters)'s Another BRIC in the Wall 2016 report

Following the [1971 war with India](/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1971), Pakistan hastily developed [atomic weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon) to deter foreign intervention and entered the [atomic age](/wiki/Atomic_age). Tensions with India led to Pakistan's [1998 underground](/wiki/List_of_nuclear_weapons_tests_of_Pakistan) [nuclear tests](/wiki/Chagai-I), making it the [seventh country](/wiki/Nuclear_club) to possess such weapons.

Pakistan is the sole Muslim nation active in Antarctica research, maintaining its [Jinnah Antarctic Research Station](/wiki/Pakistan_Antarctic_Programme) since 1992. The government invests heavily in [information technology](/wiki/Information_technology_in_Pakistan) projects, focusing on e-government and infrastructure.

## Demographics

Main article: [Demographics of Pakistan](/wiki/Demographics_of_Pakistan)

Further information: [Overseas Pakistani](/wiki/Overseas_Pakistani)

Population Density per square kilometre of each Pakistani District as of the 2017 Pakistan Census

Population of each Pakistani District as of the 2017 Pakistan Census

This section is an excerpt from [Demographics of Pakistan](/wiki/Demographics_of_Pakistan).[(https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Demographics_of_Pakistan&action=edit)]

Pakistan had a population of 241,499,431 according to the final results of the [2023 census](/wiki/2023_Pakistani_census). This figure includes the country's [four provinces](/wiki/Administrative_units_of_Pakistan) e.g. Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Balochistan and the [Islamabad Capital Territory](/wiki/Islamabad_Capital_Territory). Azad Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan's census data is yet to be approved by CCI Council of Pakistan. Pakistan is the world's [fifth–most populous country](/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population).

Between 1951 and 2023, Pakistan's population expanded over sevenfold, going from 33.7 million to 241.5 million. The country has a relatively high, although declining, growth rate supported by high birth rates and low death rates. Between 1998 and 2017, the average annual population growth rate stood at +2.40%.

Dramatic social changes have led to urbanization and the emergence of two [megacities](/wiki/Megacity): [Karachi](/wiki/Karachi) and [Lahore](/wiki/Lahore). The country's urban population more than tripled between 1981 and 2017 (from 23.8 million to 75.7 million), as Pakistan's [urbanisation rate](/wiki/Urbanization) rose from 28.2% to 36.4%. Even with this, the nation's urbanisation rate remains one of [the lowest in the world](/wiki/Urbanization_by_sovereign_state), and in 2017, over 130 million Pakistanis (making up nearly 65% of the population) lived in rural areas.

Due to a high [fertility rate](/wiki/Total_fertility_rate), which was estimated at 3.5 in 2022, Pakistan has one of the world's [youngest populations](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_median_age). The 2017 census recorded that 40.3% of the country's population was under the age of 15, while only 3.7% of Pakistanis were aged 65 or more. The [median age](/wiki/Median_age) of the country was 19, while its [sex ratio](/wiki/Sex_ratio) was recorded to be 105 males per 100 females. Pakistan ranked last (148th of 148 countries) on the [Global Gender Gap Index](/wiki/Global_Gender_Gap_Report), with a gender parity score of 56.7%, reflecting one of the lowest levels of gender equality globally.

The [demographic history of Pakistan](/wiki/Demographic_history_of_Pakistan) from the ancient [Indus Valley civilization](/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation) to the modern era includes the arrival and settlement of many cultures and ethnic groups in the modern region of Pakistan from [Eurasia](/wiki/Eurasia) and the nearby [Middle East](/wiki/Middle_East). Because of this, Pakistan has a multicultural, multilinguistic, and multiethnic society. Pakistan is also thought to have the world's fourth-largest [refugee population](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_by_refugee_population), estimated at 1.4 million in mid-2021 by the [UNHCR](/wiki/United_Nations_High_Commissioner_for_Refugees).

### Urbanisation

Main article: [Urbanisation in Pakistan](/wiki/Urbanisation_in_Pakistan)

Since [independence](/wiki/Independence_of_Pakistan) due to the [partition of India](/wiki/Partition_of_India), [urbanisation](/wiki/Urbanisation_in_Pakistan) has surged for various reasons. In the south, [Karachi](/wiki/Karachi) stands as the most populous commercial hub along the [Indus River](/wiki/Indus_River). In the east, west, and north, a dense population arc spans cities like [Lahore](/wiki/Lahore), [Faisalabad](/wiki/Faisalabad), [Rawalpindi](/wiki/Rawalpindi), [Islamabad](/wiki/Islamabad), [Sargodha](/wiki/Sargodha), [Gujranwala](/wiki/Gujranwala), [Sialkot](/wiki/Sialkot), [Gujrat](/wiki/Gujrat_city), [Jhelum](/wiki/Jhelum), [Sheikhupura](/wiki/Sheikhupura), [Nowshera](/wiki/Nowshera,_Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa), [Mardan](/wiki/Mardan), and [Peshawar](/wiki/Peshawar). By 1990–2008, city dwellers constituted 36% of Pakistan's population, making it South Asia's most urbanised nation, with over 50% living in towns of 5,000+ inhabitants. [Immigration](/wiki/Immigration_to_Pakistan), both domestic and international, significantly fuels urban growth. Migration from India, especially to Karachi, the largest metropolis, and from nearby countries, accelerates urbanisation, posing new political and socio-economic challenges. Economic shifts like the green revolution and political developments also play crucial roles.

### Ethnicity and languages

Main articles: [Languages of Pakistan](/wiki/Languages_of_Pakistan) and [Ethnic groups of Pakistan](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_of_Pakistan)

See also: [Pakistanis](/wiki/Pakistanis)

Pakistan is a diverse society with estimates suggesting it has between 75 and 85 languages. Urdu and English serve as the official languages, with Urdu being the country's [lingua franca](/wiki/Lingua_franca) and a unifying force among over 75% of Pakistanis. According to the [2023 national census](/wiki/2023_Pakistani_census), the largest [ethnolinguistic groups](/wiki/Ethnolinguistic_group) include the [Punjabis](/wiki/Punjabis) (36.98%), [Pashtuns](/wiki/Pashtuns) (18.15%), [Sindhis](/wiki/Sindhis) (14.31%), [Saraikis](/wiki/Saraiki_people) (12%), [Urdu speaking people](/wiki/Urdu_speaking_people) (9.25%), [Balochs](/wiki/Baloch_people) (3.38%), [Hindkowans](/wiki/Hindkowans)/[Hazarewals](/wiki/Hazarewal) (2.32%), and [Brahuis](/wiki/Brahuis) (1.16%). The remaining population consists of various ethnic minorities such as [Kashmiris](/wiki/Kashmiris), [Paharis](/wiki/Pahari_people_(Kashmir)), [Chitralis](/wiki/Chitralis), various peoples of [Gilgit-Baltistan](/wiki/Gilgit-Baltistan), [Kohistanis](/wiki/Indus_Kohistani_people), [Torwalis](/wiki/Torwali_people), [Meos](/wiki/Meo_(ethnic_group)), [Hazaras](/wiki/Hazaras), [Kalash](/wiki/Kalash_people) and [Siddis](/wiki/Siddi). The Pakistani diaspora, numbering over seven million, is the sixth largest in the world.

### Immigration

Main article: [Immigration to Pakistan](/wiki/Immigration_to_Pakistan)

Afghan children near [Islamabad](/wiki/Islamabad) fetching water from water pump. (Pakistan once held the second largest refugee population globally after [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey).)

Even post-1947 partition, the Muslims from India kept migrating to Pakistan, especially Karachi and Sindh province. Wars in neighbouring Afghanistan in the 1980s and 1990s pushed millions of [Afghan refugees](/wiki/Afghan_refugees) into Pakistan, mainly in Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa and [tribal areas](/wiki/Federally_Administered_Tribal_Areas), with some in Karachi and Quetta. Pakistan hosts one of the world's largest refugee populations. Additionally, around 2 million [Bengalis](/wiki/Bengalis_in_Pakistan) and half a million undocumented individuals, purportedly from [Myanmar](/wiki/Myanmar), reside in Pakistan. Pakistan ordered the [mass expulsion of undocumented Afghans](/wiki/Deportation_of_undocumented_Afghans_from_Pakistan) in October 2023, citing national security risks.

Migration of Bengalis and [Rohingya](/wiki/Rohingya_people) to Pakistan started in the 1980s and continued till 1998. Karachi hosts a significant number of Bengali settlements, and large Rohingya migration made it one of their largest populations outside Myanmar. Karachi's [Burmese](/wiki/Burmese_people) community resides in various slums across the city.

According to [BBC](/wiki/BBC_World_Service), thousands of [Uyghur](/wiki/Uyghurs) Muslims live in Gilgit-Baltistan, some left Xinjiang, China and the thriving trading town of Kashgar in 1949, while others are later arrivals, claiming to escape political oppression. Since 1989, thousands of [Kashmiri](/wiki/Kashmiris) Muslim refugees fled to Pakistan, alleging rape and forced displacement by Indian soldiers.

### Diaspora

Main article: [Overseas Pakistani](/wiki/Overseas_Pakistani)

According to the UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Pakistan has the sixth-largest diaspora globally. Approximately 7 million Pakistanis reside abroad, mainly in the Middle East, Europe, and North America. Pakistan ranks 10th globally for remittances sent home. Saudi Arabia is the largest source of remittances, contributing $5.9 billion as of 2016. The term *[Overseas Pakistani](/wiki/Overseas_Pakistani)* is officially recognised by the Government of Pakistan, with the [Ministry of Overseas Pakistanis and Human Resource Development](/wiki/Ministry_of_Overseas_Pakistanis) addressing their needs, welfare, and issues. Overseas Pakistanis constitute the second-largest source of foreign exchange remittances to Pakistan, with remittances increasing by over 100% from US$8.9 billion in 2009–10 to US$19.9 billion in 2015–16.

[Islam](/wiki/Islam_in_Pakistan) is the state religion, with freedom of religion guaranteed by the [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan). The majority are Muslims (96.35%), followed by [Hindus](/wiki/Hinduism_in_Pakistan) (2.17%) and [Christians](/wiki/Christianity_in_Pakistan) (1.37%). Minorities include [Sikhs](/wiki/Sikhism_in_Pakistan), [Buddhists](/wiki/Buddhism_in_Pakistan), [Jains](/wiki/Jainism_in_Pakistan), [Zoroastrians](/wiki/Zoroastrianism) ([Parsi](/wiki/Parsis)), and the unique [Kalash people](/wiki/Kalash_people) who practice [animism](/wiki/Animism). In 2012, 2% of the population identified as atheist in a [Gallup](/wiki/Gallup_Pakistan) survey.

#### Islam

Main article: [Islam in Pakistan](/wiki/Islam_in_Pakistan)

[Faisal Mosque](/wiki/Faisal_Mosque), built in 1986 by Turkish architect [Vedat Dalokay](/wiki/Vedat_Dalokay) on behalf of [King](/wiki/House_of_Saud) [Faisal bin Abdul-Aziz](/wiki/Faisal_bin_Abdul-Aziz) of Saudi Arabia

Islam dominates in Pakistan, with about 96.35% of the population being Muslim. Pakistan ranks second globally in Muslim population, and is home to 10.5% of the world's Muslims. Karachi is the largest Muslim city in the world.

The majority follow [Sunni Islam](/wiki/Sunni_Islam), with a significant presence of [Sufism](/wiki/Sufism_in_Pakistan), while Shia Muslims constitute a minority. Shias represent between 5–25%. The Shia population in Pakistan was estimated at 42 million in 2019. As of 2012, 12% of Pakistani Muslims self-identify as [non-denominational Muslims](/wiki/Non-denominational_Muslims).

The [Ahmadis](/wiki/Ahmadiyya_in_Pakistan) are a minority, officially considered non-Muslims. Ahmadis face persecution, banned from calling themselves Muslims since 1974.

#### Hinduism

Main article: [Hinduism in Pakistan](/wiki/Hinduism_in_Pakistan)

Hindu proportion of each Pakistani District in 2017 according to the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics

[Hinduism](/wiki/Hinduism_in_Pakistan) is the second-largest religion, followed by 2.17% of the population according to the census in 2023. Pakistan had the fifth-largest Hindu population globally in 2010. In 2023, Hindus numbered 5,217,216. They reside across Pakistan but are concentrated in [Sindh](/wiki/Sindh), where they make up 8.81% of the population. [Umerkot district](/wiki/Umerkot_district) of the province is the only Hindu majority area. [Tharparkar district](/wiki/Tharparkar_district) hosts the largest Hindu population. Four districts – Umerkot, Tharparkar, [Mirpurkhas](/wiki/Mirpurkhas_District), and [Sanghar](/wiki/Sanghar_District) – have over half of Pakistan's Hindus.

At Pakistan's inception, the 'hostage theory' suggested fair treatment of Hindus to safeguard Muslims in India. However, some Pakistani Hindus felt marginalised, leading to emigration to India. They faced violence post the [Babri Masjid demolition](/wiki/Babri_Masjid_demolition).

#### Christianity and other religions

Main article: [Christianity in Pakistan](/wiki/Christianity_in_Pakistan)

Christian proportion of each Pakistani District in 2017 according to the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics

Christians are the next largest religious minority after Hindus, constituting 1.37% of the population. They are concentrated in [Lahore District](/wiki/Lahore_District) (5%) and [Islamabad Capital Territory](/wiki/Islamabad_Capital_Territory) (over 4%). Karachi hosts a historic [Roman Catholic](/wiki/Roman_Catholicism_in_Pakistan) community established by [Goan](/wiki/Goa) and [Tamil](/wiki/Tamil_people) migrants during British colonial rule.

Following Christianity, the [Bahá'í Faith](/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%27%C3%AD_Faith_in_Pakistan) had 30,000 followers in 2008, followed by Sikhism, Buddhism, and [Zoroastrianism](/wiki/Zoroastrianism), each with around 20,000 adherents in 2008, alongside a small [Jain community](/wiki/Jainism_in_Pakistan).

### Education

Main article: [Education in Pakistan](/wiki/Education_in_Pakistan)

See also: [Higher Education Commission (Pakistan)](/wiki/Higher_Education_Commission_(Pakistan)) and [Rankings of universities in Pakistan](/wiki/Rankings_of_universities_in_Pakistan)

[NUST](/wiki/NUST_School_of_Electrical_Engineering_and_Computer_Science) in Islamabad is a top ranked Engineering University.

Pakistan's [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan) mandates [free](/wiki/Free_education) primary and secondary education, with public universities established in each province, including [Punjab University](/wiki/University_of_the_Punjab), [Sindh University](/wiki/Sindh_University), [Peshawar University](/wiki/University_of_Peshawar), [Karachi University](/wiki/University_of_Karachi), and [Balochistan University](/wiki/University_of_Balochistan). The country's educational landscape encompasses both [public](/wiki/Public_university) and [private](/wiki/Private_university) universities, fostering collaboration to enhance research and [higher education](/wiki/Higher_education_in_Pakistan) opportunities, albeit with concerns regarding teaching quality in newer institutions. [Technical and vocational institutions](/wiki/Vocational_education) in Pakistan number approximately 3,193, complemented by *[madrassahs](/wiki/Madrassas_in_Pakistan)* providing free Islamic education to students, with government efforts to [regulate and monitor](/wiki/Education_reform) their quality amidst concerns over extremists recruitment. [Education](/wiki/Education_in_Pakistan) is divided into six main levels, including nursery, primary, [middle](/wiki/Middle_school), [matriculation](/wiki/High_school_(upper_secondary)), [intermediate](/wiki/Community_college), and university programs. Additionally, [private schools](/wiki/Schools_in_Pakistan) offer a parallel secondary education system based on the curriculum set by the [Cambridge International Examinations](/wiki/Cambridge_International_Examinations), with 439 international schools reported in Pakistan.

[Malala Yousafzai](/wiki/Malala_Yousafzai) was awarded the [Nobel Peace Prize](/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize) in 2014, alongside [Kailash Satyarthi](/wiki/Kailash_Satyarthi) of India, for her advocacy of educational initiatives, particularly girls' education worldwide.

Initiatives since 2007 made [English medium education](/wiki/English_medium_education) mandatory nationwide. Following a 2012 attack on activist [Malala Yousafzai](/wiki/Malala_Yousafzai) by the [Taliban](/wiki/Tehrik-i_Taliban_Pakistan), she became the youngest Nobel laureate for her education advocacy. Reforms in 2013 mandated Chinese language courses in Sindh, reflecting China's growing influence. As of 2018, Pakistan's literacy rate stands at 62.3%, with significant regional and gender disparities. Government initiatives, including computer literacy since 1995, aim to eradicate illiteracy, targeting 100% enrollment among primary school-age children and an ~86% literacy rate by 2015. Pakistan allocates 2.3% of its GDP to education, among the lowest in South Asia.

## Culture

Main articles: [Culture of Pakistan](/wiki/Culture_of_Pakistan), [British heritage of Pakistan](/wiki/British_heritage_of_Pakistan), and [Public holidays in Pakistan](/wiki/Public_holidays_in_Pakistan)

Artwork by [Sadequain](/wiki/Sadequain) on the ceiling of [Frere Hall](/wiki/Frere_Hall). Having painted around 15,000 paintings, Sadequain is considered one of the finest painters and calligraphers Pakistan has ever produced.

[Civil society](/wiki/Culture_of_Pakistan) in Pakistan is hierarchical, emphasising [local cultural etiquette](/wiki/Etiquette_in_Pakistan) and traditional Islamic values. The primary family unit is the [extended family](/wiki/Extended_family), but there's a rising trend towards [nuclear families](/wiki/Nuclear_families) due to socio-economic factors. Both men and women typically wear *[Shalwar Kameez](/wiki/Shalwar_Kameez)*; men also favour trousers, jeans, and shirts. The middle class has grown to about 35 million, with another 17 million in the upper and upper-middle classes, leading to a shift in power from rural landowners to urban elites. Festivals like *[Eid ul-Fitr](/wiki/Eid_ul-Fitr)*, *[Eid ul-Azha](/wiki/Eid_ul-Azha)*, [Ramadan](/wiki/Ramadan), [Christmas](/wiki/Christmas), [Easter](/wiki/Easter), [Holi](/wiki/Holi), and [Diwali](/wiki/Diwali) are primarily religious. Pakistan ranked 56th on the 2006 [A.T. Kearney](/wiki/A.T._Kearney)/FP [Globalization Index](/wiki/Globalization_Index) due to increasing globalisation.

### Architecture

Main articles: [Pakistani architecture](/wiki/Pakistani_architecture) and [Hindu, Jain and Buddhist architectural heritage of Pakistan](/wiki/Hindu,_Jain_and_Buddhist_architectural_heritage_of_Pakistan)

The [Lahore Fort](/wiki/Lahore_Fort), a landmark built during the Mughal era, is a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](/wiki/UNESCO_World_Heritage_Site)

Four periods define Pakistani architecture: [pre-Islamic](/wiki/History_of_Pakistan#Early_history), [Islamic](/wiki/History_of_Pakistan#Muslim_period), [colonial](/wiki/History_of_Pakistan#Colonial_era), and [post-colonial](/wiki/History_of_Pakistan#Independence). The onset of the [Indus civilisation](/wiki/Indus_civilisation) around the mid-3rd millennium BCE heralded an urban culture, evidenced by surviving large structures. Notable pre-Islamic settlements include [Mohenjo-daro](/wiki/Mohenjo-daro), [Harappa](/wiki/Harappa), and [Kot Diji](/wiki/Kot_Diji). The fusion of Buddhism and [Greek](/wiki/Indian_campaign_of_Alexander_the_Great) influences birthed a distinctive [Greco-Buddhist](/wiki/Greco-Buddhism) style from the 1st century CE, exemplified by the renowned [Gandhara style](/wiki/Gandara_art). Notable [Buddhist architectural](/wiki/Buddhist_architecture) remnants include the [Takht-i-Bahi](/wiki/Takht-i-Bahi) monastery in [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](/wiki/Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa).

The advent of Islam in present-day Pakistan marked the cessation of Buddhist architecture, ushering in [Islamic architecture](/wiki/Islamic_architecture). The notable [Indo-Islamic](/wiki/Indo-Islamic_architecture) structure, the [tomb of Shah Rukn-i-Alam](/wiki/Rukn-e-Alam) in Multan, remains significant. During the Mughal era, Persian-Islamic design merged with Hindustani art, seen in Lahore's architectural gems like the [Badshahi Mosque](/wiki/Badshahi_Mosque) and the [Lahore Fort](/wiki/Lahore_Fort) with the iconic [Alamgiri Gate](/wiki/Alamgiri_Gate). Lahore also boasts the vibrant [Wazir Khan Mosque](/wiki/Wazir_Khan_Mosque), and the lush [Shalimar Gardens](/wiki/Shalimar_Gardens_(Lahore)). In the British colonial period, Indo-European buildings emerged, blending European and Indian-Islamic styles. Post-colonial identity shines through modern landmarks like the [Faisal Mosque](/wiki/Faisal_Mosque), [Minar-e-Pakistan](/wiki/Minar-e-Pakistan), and [Mazar-e-Quaid](/wiki/Mazar-e-Quaid). [British architectural](/wiki/Architecture_of_the_United_Kingdom) influence persists in structures across Lahore, Peshawar, and Karachi.

### Clothing, arts, and fashion

Main articles: [Pakistani clothing](/wiki/Pakistani_clothing), [Shalwar kameez](/wiki/Shalwar_kameez), [Sherwani](/wiki/Sherwani), [Jinnah cap](/wiki/Jinnah_cap), [Peshawari chappal](/wiki/Peshawari_chappal), [Pakol](/wiki/Pakol), and [Sindhi topi](/wiki/Sindhi_topi)

A depiction of traditional clothing of women from [Sindh](/wiki/Sindh)

The *[Shalwar kameez](/wiki/Shalwar_kameez)* is Pakistan's [national dress](/wiki/National_dress), worn in all provinces: Punjab, Sindh, Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and Azad Kashmir. Each province has its own style. Pakistanis wear a variety of fabrics like silk, [chiffon](/wiki/Chiffon_(fabric)), and cotton. In addition to the national dress, men often wear domestically tailored suits and [neckties](/wiki/Necktie), especially in offices, schools, and social gatherings.

Pakistan's [fashion industry](/wiki/Pakistani_clothing) has thrived, blending traditional and modern styles to create a unique cultural identity. Regional and traditional dress remain significant symbols of native tradition, evolving into both modern and purer forms. Organisations like the Pakistan Fashion Design Council in [Lahore](/wiki/Lahore) and the Fashion Pakistan Council in [Karachi](/wiki/Karachi) host events like [PFDC Fashion Week](/wiki/PFDC_Sunsilk_Fashion_Week) and [Fashion Pakistan Week](/wiki/Fashion_Pakistan_Week). Pakistan's inaugural fashion week took place in November 2009.

### Literature and philosophy

Main articles: [Literature of Pakistan](/wiki/Literature_of_Pakistan), [Urdu poetry](/wiki/Urdu_poetry), and [Pakistani philosophy](/wiki/Pakistani_philosophy)

[Muhammad Iqbal](/wiki/Muhammad_Iqbal), Pakistan's national poet who conceived the [idea](/wiki/Conception_of_Pakistan) of Pakistan

Pakistan boasts literature in various languages including [Urdu](/wiki/Urdu_literature), [Sindhi](/wiki/Sindhi_literature), [Punjabi](/wiki/Punjabi_literature), [Pashto](/wiki/Pashto_literature_and_poetry), [Baluchi](/wiki/Balochi_Academy), [Persian](/wiki/Persian_literature), [English](/wiki/Pakistani_English_literature), and more. The [Pakistan Academy of Letters](/wiki/Pakistan_Academy_of_Letters) actively promotes literature and poetry both domestically and internationally. [National Library](/wiki/National_Library_of_Pakistan) contributes to literary dissemination. Historically, Pakistani literature consisted mainly of [lyric](/wiki/Lyric_poetry), [religious](/wiki/Sufi_poetry), and [folkloric](/wiki/Pakistani_folklore) works, later diversifying under colonial influence into prose fiction, now widely embraced.

The [national poet](/wiki/List_of_national_poets) of Pakistan, [Muhammad Iqbal](/wiki/Muhammad_Iqbal), wrote influential poetry in Urdu and Persian, advocating for Islamic civilisational revival. Notable figures in contemporary Urdu literature include [Josh Malihabadi](/wiki/Josh_Malihabadi), [Faiz Ahmed Faiz](/wiki/Faiz_Ahmed_Faiz), and [Saadat Hasan Manto](/wiki/Saadat_Hasan_Manto). Popular Sufi poets like [Shah Abdul Latif](/wiki/Shah_Abdul_Latif) and [Bulleh Shah](/wiki/Bulleh_Shah) are revered. [Mirza Kalich Beg](/wiki/Mirza_Kalich_Beg) is hailed as the father of modern Sindhi prose. Pakistani philosophy has been shaped by influences from British and American philosophy, with notable figures like [M. M. Sharif](/wiki/M._M._Sharif) contributing to its development. Post-1971, Marxist thought gained prominence in Pakistani philosophy through figures like [Jalaludin Abdur Rahim](/wiki/Jalaludin_Abdur_Rahim).

### Media and entertainment

Main articles: [Mass media in Pakistan](/wiki/Mass_media_in_Pakistan), [Cinema of Pakistan](/wiki/Cinema_of_Pakistan), [Music of Pakistan](/wiki/Music_of_Pakistan), [History of Pakistani pop music](/wiki/History_of_Pakistani_pop_music), [Theatre of Pakistan](/wiki/Theatre_of_Pakistan), and [Pakistani dramas](/wiki/Pakistani_dramas)

The private [print media](/wiki/List_of_newspapers_in_Pakistan), state-owned [Pakistan Television Corporation](/wiki/Pakistan_Television_Corporation) (PTV), and [Pakistan Broadcasting Corporation](/wiki/Pakistan_Broadcasting_Corporation) (PBC) dominated media until the 21st century. Pakistan now boasts a vast network of domestic, privately owned [24-hour electronic](/wiki/24-hour_news_cycle) [news media](/wiki/List_of_television_channels_in_Pakistan) and [cable](/wiki/Cable_television) [television channels](/wiki/Television_in_Pakistan). The [Reporters Without Borders](/wiki/Reporters_Without_Borders) has indicated pressure faced by Pakistani reporters, particularly when reporting against the military or government. The BBC describes Pakistani media as "among the most outspoken in South Asia". Pakistani media has been instrumental in exposing corruption.

The [Lollywood](/wiki/Lollywood), Punjabi, and [Pashto](/wiki/Pashto_cinema) film industry is centered in Karachi, Lahore, and Peshawar. Although Bollywood films were banned from public cinemas from 1965 to 2008, they remained influential in Pakistani popular culture. However, in 2019, the screening of Bollywood movies faced an indefinite ban. Despite challenges faced by the Pakistani film industry, Urdu [televised dramas](/wiki/Pakistani_dramas) and theatrical performances remain popular, frequently broadcast by many entertainment media outlets. Urdu dramas dominate the television entertainment industry, renowned for their quality since the 1990s. Pakistani music encompasses diverse forms, from provincial folk music and traditional styles like [Qawwali](/wiki/Qawwali) and [Ghazal](/wiki/Ghazal) Gayaki to modern fusions of traditional and western music. Pakistan boasts numerous renowned folk singers, and the arrival of Afghan refugees in western provinces has sparked interest in Pashto music, despite occasional intolerance.

### Cuisine

Main article: [Pakistani cuisine](/wiki/Pakistani_cuisine)

[Roti](/wiki/Roti), served with various side dishes, is considered a staple food in Pakistan

Pakistani cuisine, rooted in the royal kitchens of 16th-century Mughal emperors, blends influences from [British](/wiki/British_cuisine), [Indian](/wiki/Indian_cuisine), [Central Asian](/wiki/Central_Asian_cuisine), and [Middle Eastern](/wiki/Middle_Eastern_cuisine) culinary traditions. Unlike Middle Eastern fare, Pakistani dishes are heavily spiced with garlic, ginger, turmeric, [chilli](/wiki/Chili_powder), and [garam masala](/wiki/Garam_masala). [Roti](/wiki/Roti), a wheat-based flatbread, accompanies most meals, alongside [curry](/wiki/Curry), meat, vegetables, and lentils. Rice is also common, served plain, spiced, or in sweet dishes. [Lassi](/wiki/Lassi), a traditional drink from the [Punjab region](/wiki/Punjab_region), and [black tea with milk and sugar](/wiki/Pakistani_tea_culture) are popular beverages enjoyed nationwide. [Sohan halwa](/wiki/Sohan_halwa), a beloved sweet dish from southern Punjab, is savoured across Pakistan.

### Sports

Main article: [Sport in Pakistan](/wiki/Sport_in_Pakistan)

A cricket match between Pakistan and Australia at [Lord's](/wiki/Lord%27s).

The [Pakistan national hockey team](/wiki/Pakistan_men%27s_national_field_hockey_team) with the [1971 Hockey World Cup trophy](/wiki/1971_Men%27s_Hockey_World_Cup)

[Cricket](/wiki/Cricket) is the most popular sport in Pakistan, followed by [football](/wiki/Association_football). [Field hockey](/wiki/Field_hockey) is the national sport. Other sports like [squash](/wiki/Squash_(sport)), [polo](/wiki/Polo), and [traditional games](/wiki/Traditional_games_of_Pakistan) are also enjoyed.

In cricket, Pakistan boasts victories in all major [ICC](/wiki/International_Cricket_Council) tournaments, including the [ICC Cricket World Cup](/wiki/ICC_Cricket_World_Cup), [ICC World Twenty20](/wiki/ICC_World_Twenty20), and [ICC Champions Trophy](/wiki/ICC_Champions_Trophy). The [Pakistan Super League](/wiki/Pakistan_Super_League) ranks among the top [T20 leagues](/wiki/List_of_T20_cricket_competitions) globally.

In football, Pakistan established the [Pakistan Football Federation](/wiki/Pakistan_Football_Federation) soon after its creation, and it is known for producing [FIFA World Cup](/wiki/FIFA_World_Cup) balls.

In field hockey, Pakistan boasts four [Hockey World Cup](/wiki/Men%27s_FIH_Hockey_World_Cup) wins, eight [Asian Games](/wiki/Field_hockey_at_the_Asian_Games) gold medals, and three [Olympic](/wiki/Field_hockey_at_the_Summer_Olympics) gold medals. Squash player [Jahangir Khan](/wiki/Jahangir_Khan) holds the record for the longest winning streak in professional sport history, winning 555 consecutive matches. Pakistan has hosted various international events, including Cricket and Hockey World Cups and Asian Games.
