**Bradford City Association Football Club** is an English professional [football](/wiki/Association_football) club in [Bradford](/wiki/Bradford), [West Yorkshire](/wiki/West_Yorkshire). The club competes in [EFL League One](/wiki/EFL_League_One), the third tier of [English football](/wiki/English_football_league_system), and is managed by [Graham Alexander](/wiki/Graham_Alexander).

The club was founded in 1903 and immediately elected into the [Football League Second Division](/wiki/Football_League_Second_Division). Promotion to the top tier followed as they won the 1907–08 Second Division title and then they went on to win the [1911 FA Cup final](/wiki/1911_FA_Cup_final), which remains the club's only major honour. They were relegated in 1922 and again in 1927, before winning the [Third Division North](/wiki/Football_League_Third_Division_North) title in 1928–29. Another relegation in 1937 did allow the club to go on to win the [Third Division North Cup](/wiki/Football_League_Third_Division_North_Cup) in 1939, however a further relegation followed in 1962 to leave the club in the newly created [Fourth Division](/wiki/Football_League_Fourth_Division). They secured promotions back into the third tier in 1969 and 1977, but were relegated in 1972 and 1978. They found success in the 1980s under the stewardship of first [Roy McFarland](/wiki/Roy_McFarland) and then [Trevor Cherry](/wiki/Trevor_Cherry), winning promotion in 1981–82 and following this up with the [Third Division](/wiki/Football_League_Third_Division) title in 1984–85, though they were relegated out of the Second Division in 1990.

Bradford were promoted back into the second tier via the play-offs in [1996](/wiki/1996_Football_League_Second_Division_play-off_final), before securing another promotion in 1998–99 to reach the [Premier League](/wiki/Premier_League), marking a return to the top-flight after a 77-year absence. They entered Europe and reached the semi-finals of the [UEFA Intertoto Cup](/wiki/UEFA_Intertoto_Cup) in 2000–01 but ended the campaign with relegation from the Premier League. A succession of financial crises followed as the club entered [administration](/wiki/Administration_(British_football)) twice in two years and further relegations followed in 2004 and 2007 to leave the club back in the fourth tier. They found success under the management of [Phil Parkinson](/wiki/Phil_Parkinson) by reaching the [2013 League Cup final](/wiki/2013_Football_League_Cup_final) and then going on to win that year's [League Two play-off final](/wiki/2013_Football_League_Two_play-off_final) but were relegated from [League One](/wiki/EFL_League_One) in 2019.

The club's home ground is the 24,840-capacity [Valley Parade](/wiki/Valley_Parade), which was the site of the [Bradford City stadium fire](/wiki/Bradford_City_stadium_fire) on 11 May 1985, which took the lives of 56 supporters. They are the only professional football club in England to wear [claret](/wiki/Wine_(color)) and [amber](/wiki/Amber_(color)), and have worn these colours throughout their history. They have though been known by various nicknames, with the "[Bantams](/wiki/Bantam_(poultry))" being the most commonly used nickname as it appears on the current club crest. Supporters hold [West Yorkshire derby](/wiki/West_Yorkshire_derby) rivalries with [Huddersfield Town](/wiki/Huddersfield_Town_A.F.C.) and [Leeds United](/wiki/Leeds_United_F.C.), as well as a historic [Bradford derby](/wiki/Bradford_derby) rivalry with the now non-league side [Bradford (Park Avenue)](/wiki/Bradford_(Park_Avenue)_A.F.C.).

## History

Main article: [History of Bradford City A.F.C.](/wiki/History_of_Bradford_City_A.F.C.)

See also: [List of Bradford City A.F.C. seasons](/wiki/List_of_Bradford_City_A.F.C._seasons)

The Bradford City team which won the [1911 FA Cup](/wiki/1911_FA_Cup_Final)

Bradford City were formed in 1903 as a result of a series of meetings called by James Whyte, a sub-editor of the *Bradford Observer*, with [Football Association](/wiki/The_Football_Association) representatives and officials at [Manningham F.C.](/wiki/Manningham_F.C.), a [rugby league](/wiki/Rugby_league) side. The [Football League](/wiki/English_Football_League) saw the invitation as a chance to promote association football in the rugby league-dominated county of the [West Riding of Yorkshire](/wiki/West_Riding_of_Yorkshire). It duly elected the new club into the [Second Division](/wiki/Football_League_Second_Division), in place of [Doncaster Rovers](/wiki/Doncaster_Rovers_F.C.). Four days later, at the 23rd annual meeting of Manningham FC, the committee decided to change codes from rugby league to association football. Bradford City Association Football Club were formed without having played a game, taking over Manningham's colours of claret and amber, and their [Valley Parade](/wiki/Valley_Parade) ground.

[Robert Campbell](/wiki/Robert_Campbell_(football_manager)) was appointed the club's first manager and with the help of the new committee, he assembled a playing squad at the cost of [£](/wiki/Pound_sterling)917 10[s](/wiki/Shilling) 0[d](/wiki/Penny). City's first game was a 2–0 defeat at [Grimsby Town](/wiki/Grimsby_Town_F.C.) on 1 September 1903, six days before their first home game attracted 11,000 fans. The club finished 10th in their first season. [Peter O'Rourke](/wiki/Peter_O%27Rourke_(footballer)) took over as manager in November 1905, and he led City to the Second Division title in [1907–08](/wiki/1907%E2%80%9308_in_English_football) and with it [promotion](/wiki/Promotion_and_relegation) to the [First Division](/wiki/Football_League_First_Division). Having narrowly avoided relegation in their first season in the top flight, City recorded their highest finish of 5th in [1910–11](/wiki/1910%E2%80%9311_in_English_football). The same season they won the [FA Cup](/wiki/FA_Cup), when a goal from captain [Jimmy Speirs](/wiki/Jimmy_Speirs) won the [final](/wiki/1911_FA_Cup_final) replay against [Newcastle United](/wiki/Newcastle_United_F.C.). City's defence of the cup, which included the first [Bradford derby](/wiki/Bradford_derby) against [Bradford Park Avenue](/wiki/Bradford_Park_Avenue_A.F.C.), was stopped by [Barnsley](/wiki/Barnsley_F.C.) after a run of 12 consecutive clean sheets.

A graph showing Bradford City's league history

City remained in the top flight in the period up to the First World War and for three seasons afterwards, but were relegated in [1921–22](/wiki/1921%E2%80%9322_in_English_football) along with [Manchester United](/wiki/Manchester_United_F.C.). Back in the Second Division, attendances dropped and City struggled for form, with five consecutive finishes in the bottom half of the table. They suffered a second relegation to the [Third Division (North)](/wiki/Football_League_Third_Division_North) in [1926–27](/wiki/1926%E2%80%9327_in_English_football). Two seasons later, O'Rourke, who had initially retired in 1921 following the death of his son, returned and guided City to promotion with a record haul of 128 goals. O'Rourke left for a second time after one more season, and although City spent a total of eight seasons back in the Second Division, they rarely looked like earning promotion back to the top flight. Instead in [1936–37](/wiki/1936%E2%80%9337_in_English_football), the club were relegated back to the Third Division (North). City won their third piece of silverware two seasons later, when they lifted the [Third Division North Challenge Cup](/wiki/Football_League_Third_Division_North_Cup), but they were unable to defend the trophy because competitive football was suspended for the Second World War.

After the war, City went through two managers in the first two seasons, and were consistently in the bottom half of the Third Division (North) table until [1955–56](/wiki/1955%E2%80%9356_in_English_football). After three successive top half finishes, City were placed in the new national [Third Division](/wiki/Football_League_Third_Division) in [1958–59](/wiki/1958%E2%80%9359_in_English_football). Bradford spent just three seasons in the Third Division, but during their relegation season in [1960–61](/wiki/1960%E2%80%9361_in_English_football), they upset First Division side Manchester United in the inaugural season of the [League Cup](/wiki/Football_League_Cup). With 34 goals from [David Layne](/wiki/David_Layne), City nearly earned promotion the following season [1961–62](/wiki/1961%E2%80%9362_in_English_football), but did also suffer a record 9–1 defeat to [Colchester United](/wiki/Colchester_United_F.C.). Layne left for [Sheffield Wednesday](/wiki/Sheffield_Wednesday_F.C.), and without him City finished second from bottom of the league and had to apply for re-election. Bradford City just failed to win promotion in [1963–64](/wiki/1963%E2%80%9364_in_English_football), winning more games than any other team in the division that season, twenty five, with [Rodney Green](/wiki/Rodney_Green_(footballer)) top scoring with 29 league goals. There followed three difficult seasons during which time manager [Grenville Hair](/wiki/Grenville_Hair) died following a heart attack in training, City returned to the Third Division after getting promoted in [1968–69](/wiki/1968%E2%80%9369_in_English_football). City's stay in the Third Division lasted just three years, when they finished bottom in [1971–72](/wiki/1971%E2%80%9372_in_English_football). Promotion via fourth spot was won again in [1976–77](/wiki/1976%E2%80%9377_in_English_football) but it was instantly followed by a relegation season.

A memorial, erected on the club's new main stand at [Valley Parade](/wiki/Valley_Parade), to the victims of the [fire](/wiki/Bradford_City_stadium_fire) in 1985

City failed to win promotion for three successive seasons, until the board appointed former [England](/wiki/England_national_football_team) [centre back](/wiki/Centre_back) [Roy McFarland](/wiki/Roy_McFarland) as manager in May 1981. McFarland won promotion in his first season, but was poached by his former club [Derby County](/wiki/Derby_County_F.C.) just six months later. City won compensation from Derby and installed another England international [Trevor Cherry](/wiki/Trevor_Cherry) as McFarland's replacement. Cherry, with former teammate [Terry Yorath](/wiki/Terry_Yorath) as his assistant manager, failed to win for two months, but eventually the pair guided City to safety from relegation. During the summer, however, the club chairman Bob Martin had to call in the [official receivers](/wiki/Official_receiver). The club was saved by former chairman [Stafford Heginbotham](/wiki/Stafford_Heginbotham) and former board member Jack Tordoff, but to ensure the club could start the new season, prize asset, striker [Bobby Campbell](/wiki/Bobby_Campbell_(Northern_Irish_footballer)) was sold to Derby. City struggled but so did Campbell, and when he returned, the club went on a record run of ten successive victories. Although they missed out on promotion, City won the league the following season [1984–85](/wiki/1984%E2%80%9385_in_English_football), to return to the second tier of the Football League. However, City's triumph was overshadowed when the main stand at Valley Parade [caught fire](/wiki/Bradford_City_stadium_fire) during the final game of the season, killing 56 people.

City played games away from Valley Parade for 19 months. But just ten days after the new £2.6 million ground was opened, Cherry was sacked. His replacement, [Terry Dolan](/wiki/Terry_Dolan_(footballer)), steered City away from possible relegation, before he mounted a promotion challenge the following season. City went top of the table in September 1987, but fell away during Christmas and missed out on promotion on the final day of the season. Instead, they entered the [play-offs](/wiki/Football_League_Championship_play-offs) but were defeated in the semi-finals by [Middlesbrough](/wiki/Middlesbrough_F.C.). Two years later City were relegated back to the Third Division. For three seasons, City finished mid-table in the third tier, which was renamed Division Two following the formation of the [Premier League](/wiki/Premier_League) in 1992.

Bradford City against [Fulham](/wiki/Fulham_F.C.) at [Valley Parade](/wiki/Valley_Parade) during the early 1990s

In January 1994, [Geoffrey Richmond](/wiki/Geoffrey_Richmond) came from [Scarborough](/wiki/Scarborough_F.C.) to take over as chairman, and promised to guide City to the Premier League within five years. He cleared the debts and after four months sacked manager [Frank Stapleton](/wiki/Frank_Stapleton) to appoint his own manager, [Lennie Lawrence](/wiki/Lennie_Lawrence). Lawrence left after little more than a year to join [Luton Town](/wiki/Luton_Town_F.C.) but his successor, [Chris Kamara](/wiki/Chris_Kamara), took City to the [play-offs](/wiki/Football_League_One_play-offs) and their first game at [Wembley Stadium](/wiki/Wembley_Stadium_(1923)). They defeated [Notts County](/wiki/Notts_County_F.C.) 2–0 in the [final](/wiki/1996_Football_League_Second_Division_play-off_final) to earn promotion to Division One. City avoided relegation the following season by winning their last two league games, 1–0 against [Charlton Athletic](/wiki/Charlton_Athletic_F.C.) and then beating [Queens Park Rangers](/wiki/Queens_Park_Rangers_F.C.) 3–0 on the final day of the season, but Kamara was sacked in January 1998. [Paul Jewell](/wiki/Paul_Jewell) took over, initially on a temporary basis, before he was given a permanent contract. He bought the club's first £1 million signings and guided the club to the Premier League — the first time they had been in the top flight for 77 years — with a second-place finish. The following season, Jewell continued to defy the critics, who labelled his team *[Dad's Army](/wiki/Dad%27s_Army)*, by avoiding relegation again on the last day with a 1–0 victory over [Liverpool](/wiki/Liverpool_F.C.), with a goal from [David Wetherall](/wiki/David_Wetherall).

However, Jewell left shortly afterwards. His assistant [Chris Hutchings](/wiki/Chris_Hutchings) was promoted to the manager's position, and despite a series of new expensive signings, he was sacked by November 2000, with City second from bottom of the league. [Jim Jefferies](/wiki/Jim_Jefferies_(footballer)) took over but could not save the club from relegation. At the end of the first season back in Division One, City were placed in [administration](/wiki/Administration_(insolvency)) with debts of nearly £13 million. Two years later, the club suffered a second spell in administration and a second relegation. Two top-half finishes followed, but the club were relegated for a third time in seven seasons in [2006–07](/wiki/2006%E2%80%9307_in_English_football) meaning the following season would be their first in the bottom tier for 26 seasons. Former player [Stuart McCall](/wiki/Stuart_McCall) was appointed the new manager, and although he said anything less than promotion would be a failure, he finally led the team to a 10th-place finish. McCall eventually left Bradford City on 8 February 2010 following a board meeting after a run of poor results.

In September 2011, the club became linked with American amateur side [SC United Bantams](/wiki/SC_United_Bantams).

In January 2013, City became the first club from the fourth tier of English football since [Rochdale](/wiki/Rochdale_A.F.C.) in [1962](/wiki/1961%E2%80%9362_Football_League_Cup) to reach the [League Cup](/wiki/EFL_Cup) final, and the first fourth tier club ever to reach a major Wembley Cup final. They defeated three [Premier League](/wiki/Premier_League) sides en route to the final – [Wigan Athletic](/wiki/Wigan_Athletic_F.C.) 4–2 on penalties in the fourth round, [Arsenal](/wiki/Arsenal_F.C.) 3–2 on penalties in the quarter-finals and [Aston Villa](/wiki/Aston_Villa_F.C.) 4–3 on aggregate over the two legs of the semi-final. They met Premier League side [Swansea City](/wiki/Swansea_City_A.F.C.) in the [final](/wiki/2013_Football_League_Cup_final) at [Wembley](/wiki/Wembley_Stadium) but lost 5–0.
The run to the final is thought to have been worth at least £1.3 million to the club, with joint chairman Mark Lawn stating that the final itself could be worth an additional £1 million, taking the club's total earnings to £2.3 million during their [cup campaign](/wiki/2012%E2%80%9313_Football_League_Cup). On 18 May 2013, the club returned to [Wembley](/wiki/Wembley_Stadium) where they defeated [Northampton Town](/wiki/Northampton_Town_F.C.) 3–0 in the League Two [play-off final](/wiki/2013_Football_League_Two_play-off_final) to secure a place in [League One](/wiki/EFL_League_One) for 2013–14.

On 24 January 2015, Bradford City caused an upset by beating Premiership leaders [Chelsea](/wiki/Chelsea_F.C.) 4–2 away in the FA Cup. The victory sent Bradford through to the fifth round for the first time in eighteen years. They beat [Sunderland](/wiki/Sunderland_A.F.C.), another Premier League club, 2–0 at home in the next round on 15 February 2015. In the quarter-finals, the Bantams faced [Reading](/wiki/Reading_F.C.) at home, in a game that ended in a goalless draw. The replay was played on 16 March 2015 at the Madejski Stadium, where Reading won 3–0.

The club was relegated to League Two at the end of the 2018–19 season.

In December 2021, the club was approached by American investors known as WAGMI United (who use cryptocurrency and NFTs) about a possible buyout. The offer was rejected.

On 24 February 2022, [Mark Hughes](/wiki/Mark_Hughes) was appointed manager of the club on a contract until the summer of 2024. He was sacked on 4 October 2023, with player [Kevin McDonald](/wiki/Kevin_McDonald_(footballer,_born_1988)) becoming player-caretaker manager. Later that month, assistant manager [Mark Trueman](/wiki/Mark_Trueman) replaced McDonald as caretaker manager. On 6 November, [Graham Alexander](/wiki/Graham_Alexander) was signed as manager until the end of the 2026–27 season, with [Chris Lucketti](/wiki/Chris_Lucketti) as assistant manager.

Bradford City were promoted to [League One](/wiki/EFL_League_One) on the last day of the [2024–25 EFL season](/wiki/2024%E2%80%9325_EFL_League_Two) after their victory over [Fleetwood Town](/wiki/Fleetwood_Town_F.C.) which secured the third-place promotion place.

## Colours and club crest

Bradford City is the only professional football club in England to wear claret and amber. The club colours were inherited from [Manningham FC](/wiki/Manningham_FC), when the club converted to football upon Bradford City's foundation in 1903. However, whereas Manningham played in hoops, the new football club adopted claret and amber stripes. Manningham RFC adopted the colours in 1884 before the move to Valley Parade in 1886. Having originally worn black shirts with white shorts, the club's first game in claret and amber was against Hull on 20 September 1884, at Carlisle Road.

The reason Manningham chose claret and amber is not documented but the colours were the same as those of [The Prince of Wales's Own West Yorkshire Regiment](/wiki/West_Yorkshire_Regiment), which was based at Belle Vue Barracks on nearby Manningham Lane. Both Manningham, from 1886, and Bradford City, from 1903 to 1908, used the barracks as changing and club rooms.

Bradford City has worn claret and amber, with either white or black, since it was founded. Since the fire in 1985, the club has used black on the kit as a memorial to the 56 supporters who died. The club's away shirt has traditionally been white and to a lesser extent also blue, but there has been a profusion of other colours and designs particularly in more recent years. The away kit for the 2008–09 season was all white. For the 2009–10 season, the away kit was all black with a thin claret and amber stripe down the centre-left.

City scarves have also sold in large numbers in recent years to fans of *[Harry Potter](/wiki/Harry_Potter)*, because the colours are the same as Harry's house scarf at [Hogwarts School](/wiki/Hogwarts).

A number of other clubs across the world wear claret and amber. They include Scottish club [Motherwell](/wiki/Motherwell_F.C.), who originally wore blue and white until they wore claret and amber for the first time on 23 August 1913, against Celtic. It is erroneously believed that Motherwell chose the claret and amber colours because they were the racing colours of Lord Hamilton; it is more likely that Motherwell were influenced by Bradford City's English FA cup win in 1911.

The club's crest combines a series of logos from over the years. In 1974, City adopted a contemporary style crest incorporating the club's initials, with a B-C logo. At the time, the new logo maintained the previous nickname of the Paraders. By December 1981, the club relaunched the Bantams as the official identity with a bantam on the new crest. The crest maintains the club colours and also includes the words The Bantams.

## Nickname

Bradford City have had a number of nicknames during their history. In their early years, they were referred to as the "Robins" or "Wasps", taking over the nickname of Manningham FC, as a result of Manningham's claret and amber hoops. Other nicknames have been the "Citizens" or "Paraders", but the club is better known as the "Bantams".

The "Bantams" nickname is thought to have become popular during the First World War, when the club's stadium Valley Parade was used as a recruiting station for the West Yorkshire Regiment which was raising Pals Battalions, with some of them called "Bantams Battalions" due to the short height of many of the recruits being, between 5 ft (1.5m) tall and no more than 5 ft 3in (1.6m).The Bradford Pals had Bantam soldiers within its ranks.

## Stadium

Main article: [Valley Parade](/wiki/Valley_Parade)

See also: [Bradford City stadium fire](/wiki/Bradford_City_stadium_fire)

Valley Parade

Valley Parade

[Valley Parade](/wiki/Valley_Parade) was the site of a [quarry](/wiki/Quarry) on the hillside below [Manningham, Bradford](/wiki/Manningham,_Bradford), owned by Midland Railway Company, in 1886, when Manningham RFC bought one-third of the land and leased the remainder, because they had been forced to find a new home. They spent [£](/wiki/Pound_sterling)1,400 erecting a ground with a capacity of 20,000, club facilities and levelling the land. When Bradford City were formed in 1903, they took over the ground at Valley Parade, which was also at this time the headquarters of [The 2nd West Riding Brigade Royal Field Artillery (Territorial Force)](/wiki/West_Riding_Artillery), playing their first home game on 5 September 1903 against [Gainsborough Trinity](/wiki/Gainsborough_Trinity_F.C.), drawing a crowd of 11,000. Five years later, the club won promotion to the [First Division](/wiki/Football_League_First_Division), and so commissioned football architect [Archibald Leitch](/wiki/Archibald_Leitch) to redevelop the ground. The capacity was increased to 40,000 by December 1908 with a 5,300-seater main stand, a [terraced](/wiki/Terrace_(stadium)) paddock in front, a [Spion Kop](/wiki/Spion_Kop_(stadia)), and an 8,000-capacity Midland Road stand. Its first game against [Bristol City](/wiki/Bristol_City_F.C.) on Christmas Day attracted a crowd of 36,000. On 11 March 1911, Valley Parade attracted its highest attendance of 39,146, for an [FA Cup](/wiki/FA_Cup) game between Bradford City and [Burnley](/wiki/Burnley_F.C.) during Bradford's [FA Cup winning run](/wiki/1911_FA_Cup_Final).

Until 1952, by which time Bradford City had bought the remaining two-thirds of the ground to own it outright, the ground remained virtually unchanged. However, twice during the next decade, the club's Midland Road stand had to be demolished. Club officials first closed part of the stand in 1952, as a result of the [Burnden Park disaster](/wiki/Burnden_Park_disaster) six years earlier. Its frame was sold to [Berwick Rangers](/wiki/Berwick_Rangers_F.C.) and a replacement stand built in 1954. Six years later, the new stand was itself demolished, and Valley Parade remained a three-sided ground until 1966, when the pitch was moved, and a new stand built.

The Bradford End of [Valley Parade](/wiki/Valley_Parade), which was the first to be redeveloped after the ground reopened in 1986

On 11 May 1985, Valley Parade was the scene of a [fatal fire](/wiki/Bradford_City_stadium_fire), during which 56 supporters were killed and at least 265 were injured. The game was the final match of the [1984–85](/wiki/1984%E2%80%9385_in_English_football) season, before which City were presented with the [Third Division](/wiki/Football_League_Third_Division) championship trophy. The fire destroyed the wooden main stand in just nine minutes. The club played its home games at [Odsal Stadium](/wiki/Odsal_Stadium), a rugby league ground in Bradford, [Elland Road](/wiki/Elland_Road), Leeds, and [Leeds Road](/wiki/Leeds_Road,_Huddersfield), the former home of Huddersfield Town, until December 1986, while Valley Parade was redeveloped. The club spent £2.6 million building a new main stand and improving the Kop and reopened the new ground on 14 December 1986 for an exhibition match against an England international XI.

In 1991, the Bradford end of the ground was the next to be redeveloped and was converted into a two-tier stand with a scoreboard. In 1996, following City's promotion to [Division One](/wiki/Football_League_First_Division), club chairman [Geoffrey Richmond](/wiki/Geoffrey_Richmond) announced the construction of a 4,500 seater stand on the Midland Road side. Ahead of promotion to the [Premiership](/wiki/Premier_League) in 1999, Richmond spent another £6.5 million to convert the Kop into a two-tier 7,500-seat capacity stand. A corner stand between the Kop and main stand was opened in December 2000, taking the capacity to 20,000 for the first time since 1970. The following summer, the main stand was also converted into a two-tier stand, taking the capacity to 24,840. Further projects were planned until the club went into [administration](/wiki/Administration_(insolvency)) in May 2002 so none have taken place.

In August 2003, Valley Parade was sold to Gibb's pension fund for £5 million, with the club's offices, the shop and car park sold to London-based Development Securities for £2.5 million, but these (club offices, shop and car park) were bought back by the club's joint chairmen in the summer of 2011. The club's annual rent and maintenance costs to Gibb's pension fund is £1.2m, and so as of February 2009, the club is considering a return to Odsal. The club and Bradford Bulls would share the new £50m complex, which would also feature [cricket](/wiki/Cricket), cycling and [athletics](/wiki/Sport_of_athletics) facilities. Valley Parade has had several other names under sponsorship naming deals. In July 2016 it became the Northern Commercials Stadium, and in July 2019 it became the [Utilita Energy](/wiki/Utilita_Energy) Stadium. This partnership came to an end in July 2022. The [University of Bradford](/wiki/University_of_Bradford) subsequently became title sponsor of Valley Parade.

## Supporters

The club spearheaded an initiative in 2007 to slash the price of watching professional football for the [2007–08](/wiki/2007%E2%80%9308_in_English_football) season. As a result, season tickets to watch Bradford City were the cheapest in England at £138, the equivalent of £6 per match. When the offer finished, the club confirmed the amount of season tickets sold was 12,019. The scheme enabled the club to top the average league attendances for [Football League Two](/wiki/Football_League_Two) during the 2007–08 season, attracting more than three times more than any other club. The club won the Perform Best Fan Marketing campaign category in [The Football League Awards](/wiki/The_Football_League_Awards) for the scheme and earned them an invitation to the [Houses of Parliament](/wiki/Houses_of_Parliament). The club aimed to attract 20,000 fans for the [2008–09](/wiki/2008%E2%80%9309_in_English_football) by offering a free season ticket to anyone buying a season ticket as long as 9,000 adults sign up, but they fell 704 short of the target.

For the [2015–16](/wiki/2015%E2%80%9316_in_English_football) season, the club announced its latest season ticket scheme aimed at continuing to make football affordable for fans. Season ticket prices were set at £149 for adults, senior citizens and students, while admission for under-11s was free when purchased with an adult ticket. An initial campaign target of 15,000 was set. On 6 July, the club announced a record-breaking 18,000 tickets had been sold following a successful campaign. The campaign was repeated for the [2016–17 season](/wiki/2016-17_Bradford_City_A.F.C._season), where the club sold in excess of 17,000 tickets.

Bradford City announced 'Own The Moment' 2022–23 season ticket sales of 14,190 in September 2022. The figure was a League Two record for the club. It surpassed the previous fourth-tier season-ticket sales record of 13,614 in 2019–20.

On 4 March 2023, Bradford City set a new attendance record for Football League Two at a 2–0 victory against [Colchester United](/wiki/Colchester_United_F.C.), with an attendance of 20,383, including 345 away fans. The Bantams then broke this record again in a home fixture on 8 May 2023 against [Leyton Orient](/wiki/Leyton_Orient_F.C.), with 22,576 supporters in attendance, including 1,902 Leyton Orient fans.

## Rivalry

See also: [Bradford derby](/wiki/Bradford_derby) and [West Yorkshire derby](/wiki/West_Yorkshire_derby)

Bradford City have participated in the [Bradford derby](/wiki/Bradford_derby) with city rivals [Bradford Park Avenue](/wiki/Bradford_Park_Avenue_A.F.C.). The [West Yorkshire derby](/wiki/West_Yorkshire_derby) is held between City and local rivals [Leeds United](/wiki/Leeds_United_F.C.) and [Huddersfield Town](/wiki/Huddersfield_Town_A.F.C.). A "friendly" rivalry also existed with now-defunct club [Halifax Town](/wiki/Halifax_Town_A.F.C.).

According to a survey conducted in August 2019, Bradford City fans also see [Burnley](/wiki/Burnley_F.C.), [Barnsley](/wiki/Barnsley_F.C.) and [Oldham Athletic](/wiki/Oldham_Athletic_A.F.C.) as rivals.

- Record League Cup victory: **7–2** v [Darlington](/wiki/Darlington_F.C.), Second Round Second Leg, 25 September 2000
- Record league defeat: **0–8** v [Manchester City](/wiki/Manchester_City_F.C.), Second Division, 7 May 1927 / **1–9** v [Colchester United](/wiki/Colchester_United_F.C.), Fourth Division, 30 December 1961

- Record home attendance: **39,146** v [Burnley](/wiki/Burnley_F.C.), FA Cup fourth round, 11 March 1911
- Record gate receipts: **£300,000** v [Arsenal](/wiki/Arsenal_F.C.), Football League Cup quarter-final, 11 December 2012
- Longest unbeaten run : **21** 1968 to 1969
- Longest run of wins: **10** 1983 to 1984
- Most appearances : **574** [Ces Podd](/wiki/Ces_Podd)
- Most league appearances: **502** – [Ces Podd](/wiki/Ces_Podd)
- Most goals scored : **143** – [Bobby Campbell](/wiki/Bobby_Campbell_(Northern_Irish_footballer))
- Most league goals: **121** – [Bobby Campbell](/wiki/Bobby_Campbell_(Northern_Irish_footballer))
- Most goals in a season: **36** – [David Layne](/wiki/David_Layne), [1961–62](/wiki/1961%E2%80%9362_in_English_football)
- Most goals scored in a match: **7** – [Albert Whitehurst](/wiki/Albert_Whitehurst) v [Tranmere Rovers](/wiki/Tranmere_Rovers_F.C.), Third Division (North), 6 March 1929
- Highest transfer fee paid: **£2.5 million** – [David Hopkin](/wiki/David_Hopkin), from [Leeds United](/wiki/Leeds_United_F.C.), July 2000
- Highest transfer fee received: **£2 million** – [Des Hamilton](/wiki/Des_Hamilton), to [Newcastle United](/wiki/Newcastle_United_F.C.), March 1997 / [Andy O'Brien](/wiki/Andy_O%27Brien_(footballer)), to [Newcastle United](/wiki/Newcastle_United_F.C.), March 2001
- Most team league goals in a season: **128** – Third Division (North), [1928–29](/wiki/1928%E2%80%9329_in_English_football)

- Most points (two points for a win): **63** – Third Division (North), [1928–29](/wiki/1928%E2%80%9329_in_English_football)
