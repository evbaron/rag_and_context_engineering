# Calcium oxide

"Quicklime" redirects here; not to be confused with [Quickline](/wiki/Quickline).

**Calcium oxide** ([formula](/wiki/Chemical_formula): [Ca](/wiki/Calcium)[O](/wiki/Oxygen)), commonly known as **quicklime** or **burnt lime**, is a widely used [chemical compound](/wiki/Chemical_compound). It is a white, [caustic](/wiki/Corrosive_substance), [alkaline](/wiki/Alkali), [crystalline](/wiki/Crystal) solid at [room temperature](/wiki/Room_temperature). The broadly used term *[lime](/wiki/Lime_(material))* connotes calcium-containing [inorganic compounds](/wiki/Inorganic_compound), in which [carbonates](/wiki/Carbonate), [oxides](/wiki/Oxide), and [hydroxides](/wiki/Hydroxide) of [calcium](/wiki/Calcium), [silicon](/wiki/Silicon), [magnesium](/wiki/Magnesium), [aluminium](/wiki/Aluminium), and [iron](/wiki/Iron) predominate. By contrast, *quicklime* specifically applies to the single compound calcium oxide. Calcium oxide that survives processing without reacting in [building products](/wiki/Building_material), such as [cement](/wiki/Cement), is called **free lime**.

Quicklime is relatively inexpensive. Both it and the chemical derivative [calcium hydroxide](/wiki/Calcium_hydroxide) (of which quicklime is the [base anhydride](/wiki/Base_anhydride)) are important commodity chemicals.

## Preparation

Calcium oxide is usually made by the [thermal decomposition](/wiki/Thermal_decomposition) of materials, such as [limestone](/wiki/Limestone) or [seashells](/wiki/Seashell), that contain [calcium carbonate](/wiki/Calcium_carbonate) (CaCO3; mineral [calcite](/wiki/Calcite)) in a [lime kiln](/wiki/Lime_kiln). This is accomplished by heating the material to above 825 °C (1,517 °F), a process called [calcination](/wiki/Calcination) or *lime-burning*, to liberate a molecule of [carbon dioxide](/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) (CO2), leaving quicklime behind. This is also one of the few chemical reactions known in [prehistoric](/wiki/Prehistoric) times.

:   CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)

The quicklime is not stable and, when cooled, will [spontaneously react](/wiki/Spontaneous_process) with CO2 from the air until, after enough time, it will be completely converted back to calcium carbonate unless [slaked](/wiki/Slaking_(geology)) with water to set as [lime plaster](/wiki/Lime_plaster) or [lime mortar](/wiki/Lime_mortar).

Annual worldwide production of quicklime is around 283 million tonnes. China is by far the world's largest producer, with a total of around 170 million tonnes per year. The United States is the next largest, with around 20 million tonnes per year.

A demonstration of slaking of quicklime as a strongly exothermic reaction. Drops of water are added to pieces of quicklime. After a while, a pronounced [exothermic](/wiki/Exothermic) reaction occurs ("slaking of lime"). The temperature can reach up to some 300 °C (572 °F).

- Heat: Quicklime releases [thermal](/wiki/Heat) [energy](/wiki/Energy) by the formation of the hydrate, [calcium hydroxide](/wiki/Calcium_hydroxide), by the following equation:

:   :   CaO (s) + H2O (l) ⇌ Ca(OH)2 (aq) (ΔHr = −63.7 kJ/mol of CaO)
:   As it hydrates, an exothermic reaction results and the solid puffs up. The hydrate can be reconverted to quicklime by removing the water by heating it to redness to reverse the hydration reaction. One litre of water combines with approximately 3.1 kilograms (6.8 lb) of quicklime to give calcium hydroxide plus 3.54 [MJ](/wiki/Joule) of energy. This process can be used to provide a convenient portable source of heat, as for on-the-spot food warming in a [self-heating can](/wiki/Self-heating_can), cooking, and heating water without open flames. Several companies sell cooking kits using this heating method.

- It is a [food additive](/wiki/Food_additive) used as an acidity regulator, a flour treatment agent and a leavener. It has [E number](/wiki/E_number) **E529**.
- Light: When quicklime is heated to 2,400 °C (4,350 °F), it emits an intense glow. This form of illumination is known as a [limelight](/wiki/Limelight), and was used broadly in theatrical productions before the invention of electric lighting.
- Cement: Calcium oxide is a key ingredient for the process of making [cement](/wiki/Cement).
- As a cheap and widely available alkali.
- Petroleum industry: Water detection pastes contain a mix of calcium oxide and [phenolphthalein](/wiki/Phenolphthalein). Should this paste come into contact with water in a fuel storage tank, the CaO reacts with the water to form calcium hydroxide. Calcium hydroxide has a high enough pH to turn the phenolphthalein a vivid purplish-pink color, thus indicating the presence of water.
- [Chemical pulping](/wiki/Chemical_pulping): Calcium oxide is used to make [calcium hydroxide](/wiki/Calcium_hydroxide), which is used to regenerate [sodium hydroxide](/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide) from [sodium carbonate](/wiki/Sodium_carbonate) in the chemical recovery at [kraft pulp](/wiki/Kraft_pulp) mills.
- Plaster: There is archeological evidence that [Pre-Pottery Neolithic B](/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic_B) humans used limestone-based [plaster](/wiki/Plaster) for flooring and other uses. Such [Lime-ash floor](/wiki/Lime-ash_floor) remained in use until the late nineteenth century.
- Chemical or power production: Solid sprays or slurries of calcium oxide can be used to remove [sulfur dioxide](/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide) from exhaust streams in a process called [flue-gas desulfurization](/wiki/Flue-gas_desulfurization).
- [Carbon capture and storage](/wiki/Carbon_capture_and_storage): Calcium oxide can be used to capture carbon dioxide from flue gases in a process called [calcium looping](/wiki/Calcium_looping).
- Mining: *Compressed lime cartridges* exploit the exothermic properties of quicklime to break rock. A [shot hole](/wiki/Drilling_and_blasting#Procedure) is drilled into the rock in the usual way and a sealed cartridge of quicklime is placed within and [tamped](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tamp). A quantity of water is then injected into the cartridge and the resulting release of steam, together with the greater volume of the residual hydrated solid, breaks the rock apart. The method does not work if the rock is particularly hard.
- Disposal of corpses: Historically, it was mistakenly thought that quicklime was efficacious in accelerating the decomposition of corpses. The application of quicklime can, in fact, promote preservation. Quicklime can aid in eradicating the stench of decomposition, which may have led people to the erroneous conclusion.
- It has been determined that the durability of ancient Roman concrete is attributed in part to the use of quicklime as an ingredient. Combined with hot mixing, the quicklime creates macro-sized lime clasts with a characteristically brittle nano-particle architecture. As cracks form in the concrete, they preferentially pass through the structurally weaker lime clasts, fracturing them. When water enters these cracks it creates a calcium-saturated solution which can recrystallize as calcium carbonate, quickly filling the crack.
- The thermochemical heat storage mechanism is greatly impacted by the [sintering](/wiki/Sintering) of CaO and CaCO3. It demonstrates that the storage materials become less reactive and denser at increasing temperatures. It also pinpoints particular sintering processes and variables influencing the efficiency of these materials in heat storage.

### Weapon

Quicklime is also thought to have been a component of [Greek fire](/wiki/Greek_fire). Upon contact with water, quicklime would increase its temperature above 150 °C (302 °F) and ignite the fuel.

[David Hume](/wiki/David_Hume), in his *[History of England](/wiki/The_History_of_England_(Hume))*, recounts that early in the reign of [Henry III](/wiki/Henry_III_of_England), the English Navy destroyed an invading French fleet by blinding the enemy fleet with quicklime. Quicklime may have been used in medieval naval warfare – up to the use of "lime-mortars" to throw it at the enemy ships.

### Substitutes

Limestone is a substitute for lime in many applications, which include agriculture, fluxing, and sulfur removal. Limestone, which contains less reactive material, is slower to react and may have other disadvantages compared with lime, depending on the application; however, limestone is considerably less expensive than lime. Calcined gypsum is an alternative material in industrial plasters and mortars. Cement, cement kiln dust, fly ash, and lime kiln dust are potential substitutes for some construction uses of lime. Magnesium hydroxide is a substitute for lime in pH control, and magnesium oxide is a substitute for dolomitic lime as a flux in steelmaking.

## Safety

Because of vigorous reaction of quicklime with water, quicklime causes severe irritation when inhaled or placed in contact with moist skin or eyes. Inhalation may cause coughing, sneezing, and labored breathing. It may then evolve into burns with perforation of the nasal septum, abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting. Although quicklime is not considered a fire hazard, its reaction with water can release enough heat to ignite combustible materials.

## Mineral

Calcium oxide is also a separate mineral species (with the unit formula CaO), named 'Lime'. It has an [isometric crystal system](/wiki/Cubic_crystal_system), and can form a [solid solution series](/wiki/Solid_solution) with [monteponite](/wiki/Cadmium_oxide). The crystal is brittle, pyrometamorphic, and is unstable in moist air, quickly turning into [portlandite](/wiki/Portlandite) (Ca(OH)2).
