**Grenoble** ([/ɡrəˈnoʊbəl/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [*grə-NOH-bəl*](/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key); French: [[ɡʁənɔbl]](/wiki/Help:IPA/French) [ⓘ](/wiki/File:LL-Q150_(fra)-Ltrlg-Grenoble.wav); [Arpitan](/wiki/Arpitan_language): *Grenoblo* or *Grainóvol*; [Occitan](/wiki/Occitan_language): *Graçanòbol* or *Grenòble*) is the [prefecture](/wiki/Prefectures_in_France) and [largest city](/wiki/List_of_communes_in_France_with_over_20,000_inhabitants) of the [Isère](/wiki/Is%C3%A8re) [department](/wiki/Departments_of_France) in the [Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes](/wiki/Auvergne-Rh%C3%B4ne-Alpes) [region](/wiki/Regions_of_France) of southeastern France. It was the capital of the [Dauphiné](/wiki/Dauphin%C3%A9) [historical province](/wiki/Provinces_of_France) and lies where the river [Drac](/wiki/Drac_(river)) flows into the [Isère](/wiki/Is%C3%A8re_(river)) at the foot of the [French Alps](/wiki/French_Alps).

The population of the [commune](/wiki/Communes_of_France) of Grenoble was 156,140 as of 2023, while the population of the [Grenoble metropolitan area](/wiki/Grenoble_metropolitan_area) (French: *[aire d'attraction](/wiki/Functional_area_(France)) de Grenoble* or *agglomération grenobloise*) was 724,742 in 2022, which makes it the largest metropolis in the Alps, ahead of [Innsbruck](/wiki/Innsbruck) and [Bolzano](/wiki/Bolzano). A significant European scientific centre, the city advertises itself as the "Capital of the Alps", due to its size and its proximity to the mountains. The many suburban communes that make up the rest of the metropolitan area include four with populations exceeding 20,000: [Saint-Martin-d'Hères](/wiki/Saint-Martin-d%27H%C3%A8res), [Échirolles](/wiki/%C3%89chirolles), [Fontaine](/wiki/Fontaine,_Is%C3%A8re) and [Voiron](/wiki/Voiron).

Grenoble's history goes back over 2,000 years, to a time when it was a village of the [Allobroges](/wiki/Allobroges) [Gallic](/wiki/Gaul) tribe. It became the capital of the [Dauphiné](/wiki/Dauphin%C3%A9) in the 11th century. This status, consolidated by the annexation to [France](/wiki/France), allowed it to develop its economy. Grenoble then became a parliamentary and military city, close to the border with [Savoy](/wiki/Savoy), which at the time was part of the [Holy Roman Empire](/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire). Industrial development increased the prominence of Grenoble through several periods of economic expansion over the last three centuries. This started with a booming [glove](/wiki/Glove) industry in the 18th and 19th centuries, continued with the development of a strong [hydropower](/wiki/Hydropower) industry in the late 19th to early 20th centuries, and ended with a post-[World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) economic boom symbolized by the holding of the [X Olympic Winter Games](/wiki/1968_Winter_Olympics) in 1968.

The city has grown to be one of Europe's most important research, technology and innovation centres, with one in five inhabitants working directly in these fields. Grenoble was classified as a [global city](/wiki/Global_city) with the ranking of "sufficiency" by the [Globalization and World Cities Research Network](/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network) until 2024. The city held the title of European Green Capital in 2022.

## History

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of Grenoble](/wiki/Timeline_of_Grenoble).

### Antiquity

Remnants of the 3rd century AD Roman walls

The first references to what is now Grenoble date back to 43 BC. [Cularo](/wiki/Cularo) was at that time a village of the [Allobroges](/wiki/Allobroges) [Gallic](/wiki/Gallia) tribe, near a bridge across the Isère. Three centuries later and with insecurity rising in the late [Roman empire](/wiki/Roman_empire), a strong [wall](/wiki/Defensive_wall) was built around the small town in 286 AD.

The [Emperor](/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire) [Gratian](/wiki/Gratian) visited Cularo and, touched by the people's welcome, made the village a Roman city. In honour of this, Cularo was renamed **Gratianopolis** ("city of Gratian") in 381. Through regular historical sound shifts, this later became *Graignovol* during the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages), and this then became *Grenoble*.

Christianity spread to the region during the 4th century, and the [diocese](/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Grenoble-Vienne) of Grenoble was founded in 377 AD. From that time on, the bishops exercised significant political power over the city. Until the [French Revolution](/wiki/French_Revolution), they styled themselves the "bishops and princes of Grenoble".

### Middle Ages

After the collapse of the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire), the city became part of the first [Burgundian kingdom](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Burgundy) in the 5th century and of the later [Kingdom of Burgundy](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Burgundy-Arles) until 1032, when it was integrated into the [Holy Roman Empire](/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire). The Burgundian rule was interrupted between 942 and 970 by Arab rule based in [Fraxinet](/wiki/Fraxinet).

Grenoble grew significantly in the 11th century when the [Counts of Albon](/wiki/List_of_Counts_of_Albon_and_Dauphins_of_Viennois) chose the city as the capital of their territories. Their possessions at the time were a patchwork of several territories sprawled across the region, and the central position of Grenoble allowed the Counts to strengthen their authority. When they later adopted the title of "[Dauphins](/wiki/Dauphin_of_France)", Grenoble became the capital of the State of [Dauphiné](/wiki/Dauphin%C3%A9).

Despite their status, the Counts had to share authority over the city with the Bishop of Grenoble. One of the most famous of those was [Saint Hugh](/wiki/Hugh_of_Ch%C3%A2teauneuf). Under his rule, the city's bridge was rebuilt, and a regular and [leper](/wiki/Leper) hospital was built.

Coat of arms of the Dauphiné after becoming a province of France

The inhabitants of Grenoble took advantage of the conflicts between the Counts and the bishops and obtained the recognition of a Charter of Customs that guaranteed their rights. That charter was confirmed by Kings [Louis XI](/wiki/Louis_XI) in 1447 and [Francis I](/wiki/Francis_I_of_France) in 1541.

In 1336, the last Dauphin [Humbert II](/wiki/Humbert_II_of_Viennois) founded a court of justice, the Conseil delphinal, which settled at Grenoble in 1340. He also established the [University of Grenoble](/wiki/University_of_Grenoble) in 1339. Without an heir and deep into debt, Humbert sold his state to France in 1349, on the condition that the heir to the [French crown](/wiki/List_of_French_monarchs) used the title of *[Dauphin](/wiki/Dauphin_of_France)*. The first one, the future [Charles V](/wiki/Charles_V_of_France), spent nine months in Grenoble. The city remained the capital of the Dauphiné, henceforth a [province of France](/wiki/Provinces_of_France), and the [Estates of Dauphiné](/wiki/States_Provincial_(France)) were created.

The only Dauphin who governed his province was the future [Louis XI](/wiki/Louis_XI), whose "reign" lasted from 1447 to 1456. It was only under his rule that Dauphiné properly joined the Kingdom of France. The Old Conseil Delphinal became a [Parlement](/wiki/Parlement) (the third in France after the Parliaments of Paris and Toulouse), strengthening the status of Grenoble as a Provincial capital. He also ordered the construction of the Palais du Parlement (finished under [Francis I](/wiki/Francis_I_of_France)) and ensured that the Bishop pledged allegiance, thus unifying the political control of the city.

At that time, Grenoble was a crossroads between [Vienne](/wiki/Vienne,_Is%C3%A8re), Geneva, Italy, and [Savoy](/wiki/Savoy). It was the industrial centre of the Dauphiné and the province's biggest city, but a rather small one.

### Renaissance

François de Bonne, duc de Lesdiguières

Owing to Grenoble's geographical situation, French troops were garrisoned in the city and its region during the [Italian Wars](/wiki/Italian_Wars). [Charles VIII](/wiki/Charles_VIII_of_France), [Louis XII](/wiki/Louis_XII), and [Francis I](/wiki/Francis_I_of_France) went several times to Grenoble. Its people consequently had to suffer from the exactions of the soldiers.

The nobility of the region took part in various battles ([Marignano](/wiki/Battle_of_Marignano), [Pavia](/wiki/Battle_of_Pavia)) and in doing so gained significant prestige. The best-known of its members was [Bayard](/wiki/Pierre_Terrail,_seigneur_de_Bayard), "the knight without fear and beyond reproach".

Grenoble suffered as a result of the [French Wars of Religion](/wiki/French_Wars_of_Religion). The Dauphiné was indeed an important settlement for Protestants and therefore experienced several conflicts. The [baron des Adrets](/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_de_Beaumont,_baron_des_Adrets), the leader of the [Huguenots](/wiki/Huguenots), pillaged the [Cathedral of Grenoble](/wiki/Grenoble_Cathedral) and destroyed the tombs of the former Dauphins.

In August 1575, [Lesdiguières](/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_de_Bonne,_duc_de_Lesdigui%C3%A8res) became the new leader of the Protestants and, thanks to the accession of [Henry IV](/wiki/Henry_IV_of_France) to the throne of France, allied himself with the governor and the lieutenant general of the Dauphiné. But this alliance did not bring an end to the conflicts. Indeed, a Catholic movement, the *Ligue*, which took Grenoble in December 1590, refused to make peace. After months of assaults, Lesdiguières defeated the Ligue and took back Grenoble. He became the leader of the entire province.

Lesdiguières became the lieutenant-general of the Dauphiné and administered the Province from 1591 to 1626. He began the construction of the [Bastille](/wiki/Bastille_(Grenoble)) to protect the city and ordered the construction of new walls, increasing the city's size. He also constructed the Hôtel Lesdiguières, built new fountains, and dug sewers.

In 1689, the bishop [Étienne Le Camus](/wiki/%C3%89tienne_Le_Camus) launched the construction of [Saint-Louis Church](/wiki/Saint-Louis_Church,_Grenoble).

### From Louis XIV to the French Revolution

Grenoble [plan-relief](/wiki/Plan-relief) (1848)

*[Day of the Tiles](/wiki/Day_of_the_Tiles)*, 1890 painting by [Alexandre Debelle](/wiki/Alexandre_Debelle),
 ([Musée de la Révolution française](/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_de_la_R%C3%A9volution_fran%C3%A7aise))

The revocation of the [Edict of Nantes](/wiki/Edict_of_Nantes) by [Louis XIV](/wiki/Louis_XIV) caused the departure of 2,000 Protestants from Grenoble, weakening the city's economy. However, it also weakened the competing glove industry of [Grasse](/wiki/Grasse), leaving the glove factories of Grenoble without any competition. This allowed a stronger economic development for the city during the 18th century. At the beginning of that century, only 12 glovers made 15,000 dozen gloves each year; by 1787, 64 glovers made 160,000 dozen gloves each year.

The city gained some notoriety on 7 June 1788 when the townspeople assaulted troops of [Louis XVI](/wiki/Louis_XVI) in the "[Day of the Tiles](/wiki/Day_of_the_Tiles)". The people attacked the royal troops to prevent an expulsion of the notables of the city, which would have seriously endangered the economic prosperity of Grenoble. Following these events, the [Assembly of Vizille](/wiki/Assembly_of_Vizille) took place. Its members organized the meeting of the old [Estates General](/wiki/Estates-General_of_1789), thus beginning the [French Revolution](/wiki/French_Revolution). During the Revolution, Grenoble was represented in Paris by two illustrious notables, [Jean Joseph Mounier](/wiki/Jean_Joseph_Mounier) and [Antoine Barnave](/wiki/Antoine_Barnave).

In 1790, the Dauphiné was divided into three [departments](/wiki/Departments_of_France), and Grenoble became the *chef-lieu* of the [Isère department](/wiki/Is%C3%A8re). Only two [refractory priests](/wiki/Refractory_clergy) were executed at Grenoble during the [Reign of Terror](/wiki/Reign_of_Terror). [Pope](/wiki/Pope) [Pius VI](/wiki/Pius_VI), prisoner of France, spent two days at Grenoble in 1799 before going to [Valence](/wiki/Valence,_Dr%C3%B4me) where he died at 81 six weeks after his arrival.

### 19th century

Ramparts close to the Porte Saint Laurent

The establishment of the [Empire](/wiki/First_French_Empire) was overwhelmingly approved (in Isère, the results showed 82,084 yes and only 12 no). Grenoble held for the second time a prisoner Pope in 1809, when [Pius VII](/wiki/Pius_VII) spent 10 days in the city en route to his exile in [Fontainebleau](/wiki/Fontainebleau).

In 1813, Grenoble was under threat from the Austrian army, which invaded Switzerland and [Savoy](/wiki/Savoy). The well-defended city contained the Austrian attacks, and the French army defeated the Austrians, forcing them to withdraw at [Geneva](/wiki/Geneva). However, the later [invasion of France](/wiki/Six_Days_Campaign) in 1814 resulted in the capitulation of the troops and the occupation of the city.

*[Napoleon's Return from Elba](/wiki/Napoleon%27s_Return_from_Elba_(painting))* by [Charles de Steuben](/wiki/Charles_de_Steuben), 1818

During his [return](/wiki/Hundred_Days) from the island of [Elba](/wiki/Elba) in 1815, Napoleon took a [road](/wiki/Route_Napoleon) that led him near Grenoble at [Laffrey](/wiki/Laffrey). There he met the Royalist Régiment d'Angoulême (former 5th) of [Louis XVIII's](/wiki/Louis_XVIII) [Royal Army](/wiki/French_Royal_Army). Napoleon stepped toward the soldiers and said these famous words: "If there is among you a soldier who wants to kill his Emperor, here I am." The soldiers all joined his cause. After that, Napoleon was acclaimed at Grenoble and General [Jean Gabriel Marchand](/wiki/Jean_Gabriel_Marchand) could not prevent Napoleon from entering the city through the Bonne gate. He said later: "From [Cannes](/wiki/Cannes) to Grenoble, I still was an adventurer; in that last city, I came back a sovereign". But after the defeat of [Waterloo](/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo), the region suffered from a new invasion of Austrian and Sardinian troops.

Fountain of the Three Orders (1897)

The 19th century saw significant industrial development of Grenoble. The glove factories reached their Golden Age, and their products were exported to the [United States](/wiki/United_States), the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom), and [Russia](/wiki/Russia).

[General Haxo](/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Nicolas_Beno%C3%AEt,_Baron_Haxo) transformed the [Bastille](/wiki/Bastille_(Grenoble)) fortress, which took on its present aspect between 1824 and 1848. The [Second Empire](/wiki/Second_French_Empire) saw the construction of the French railway network, and the first trains arrived at Grenoble in 1858. Shortly thereafter Grenoble experienced widespread destruction by [extensive flooding](/wiki/Grenoble_Flood_1859) in 1859.

In 1869, engineer Aristide Bergès played a major role in industrializing hydroelectricity production. With the development of his paper mills, he accelerated the economic development of the Grésivaudan valley and Grenoble.

On 4 August 1897, a stone and bronze fountain was inaugurated in Grenoble to commemorate the pre-revolutionary events of June 1788. Built by the sculptor [Henri Ding](/wiki/Henri_Ding), the Fountain of the Three Orders, which represents three characters, is located on Place Notre-Dame. People in Grenoble interpret these characters as follows: "Is it raining?" inquires the [third estate](/wiki/The_third_estate); "Please heaven it had rained", lament the [clergy](/wiki/Clergy); and "It will rain", proclaims the [nobility](/wiki/Nobility).

### 20th century

[World War I](/wiki/World_War_I) accelerated Grenoble's economic development. To sustain the war effort, new hydroelectric industries developed along the various rivers of the region, and several existing companies moved into the armaments industry (for example in [Livet-et-Gavet](/wiki/Livet-et-Gavet)). Electrochemical factories were also established in the area surrounding Grenoble, initially to produce chemical weapons. This development resulted in significant immigration to Grenoble, particularly from Italian workers who settled in the Saint-Laurent neighborhood.

Gate of the exposition in 1925

The economic development of the city was highlighted by the organization of the [International Exhibition of Hydropower and Tourism](/wiki/International_Exhibition_of_Hydropower_and_Tourism) in 1925, which was visited by more than 1 million people. The organization of this exhibition forced the military to remove the old city walls and allowed the expansion of the city to the south. This exhibition also highlighted the city's hydropower industry and the region's tourist attractions.

The site of the exhibition became an urban park in 1926, named [Parc Paul Mistral](/wiki/Parc_Paul_Mistral) after the death of the mayor in 1932. The only building of this exhibition remaining in the park is the crumbling [Tour Perret](/wiki/Perret_tower_(Grenoble)), which has been closed to the public since 1960 due to its very poor state of maintenance.

#### World War II

During World War II, at the [Battle of France](/wiki/Battle_of_France), the German invasion was stopped near Grenoble at [Voreppe](/wiki/Voreppe) by the forces of General Cartier. The French forces resisted until the armistice, after which Grenoble was part of the [French State](/wiki/French_State) before an Italian occupation from 1942 to 1943. The relative tolerance of the Italian occupiers towards the Jewish populations resulted in a significant number moving to the region from the German-occupied parts of France.

Grenoble was extremely active in the [Résistance](/wiki/French_Resistance) against the occupation. Its action was symbolized by figures such as [Eugène Chavant](/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Chavant), Léon Martin, and [Marie Reynoard](/wiki/Marie_Reynoard). The University of Grenoble supported the clandestine operations and provided false documentation for young people to prevent them from being assigned to [STO](/wiki/Service_du_Travail_Obligatoire).

In September 1943, German troops occupied Grenoble, escalating the conflict with the clandestine movements. On 11 November 1943 (the anniversary of the [armistice of 1918](/wiki/World_War_I)), massive strikes and demonstrations took place in front of the local collaboration offices. In response, the occupiers arrested 400 demonstrators in the streets. On 13 November, the resistance blew up the artillery at the Polygon, which was a psychological shock for an enemy who then intensified the repression. On 25 November, the occupiers killed 11 members of the Résistance organizations of Grenoble. This violent crackdown was nicknamed "[Grenoble's Saint-Bartholomew](/wiki/Grenoble%27s_Saint-Bartholomew)". From these events, Grenoble was styled by the [Free French Forces](/wiki/Free_French_Forces) the title of *Capital of the [Maquis](/wiki/Maquis_(World_War_II))* on the antennas of the [BBC](/wiki/British_Broadcasting_Corporation).

This event only intensified the activities of Grenoble's resistance movements. The Germans could not prevent the destruction of their new arsenal on 2 December at the Bonne Barracks. After the [Normandy landing](/wiki/Invasion_of_Normandy), resistance operations reached their peak, with numerous attacks considerably hampering the activity of German troops. With the [landing in Provence](/wiki/Operation_Dragoon), German troops evacuated the city on 22 August 1944. On 5 November 1944, General [Charles de Gaulle](/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle) came to Grenoble and bestowed on the city the *[Compagnon de la Libération](/wiki/Ordre_de_la_Lib%C3%A9ration)* to recognise "a heroic city at the peak of the French resistance and combat for the liberation".

#### Post-war

The [Hôtel de Ville](/wiki/H%C3%B4tel_de_Ville,_Grenoble)

In 1955, future [physics Nobel Prize](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics) laureate [Louis Néel](/wiki/Louis_N%C3%A9el) created the Grenoble Center for Nuclear Studies (CENG), resulting in the birth of the Grenoble model, a combination of research and industry. The first stone was laid in December 1956.

In 1968, Grenoble hosted the [X Olympic Winter Games](/wiki/1968_Winter_Olympics). This event helped modernize the city with the development of infrastructure such as an airport, motorways, the new [Hôtel de Ville](/wiki/H%C3%B4tel_de_Ville,_Grenoble) (town hall), and a new train station. It also helped the development of ski resorts like [Chamrousse](/wiki/Chamrousse), [Les Deux Alpes](/wiki/Les_Deux_Alpes), and [Villard-de-Lans](/wiki/Villard-de-Lans).

Since 2017, anarchist movements have been behind a wave of arson attacks in Grenoble. During the last week of August 2023, a police operation was carried out in the Saint-Bruno district of Grenoble, after the city experienced a new series of gangland killings during the summer. Numerous recurring security problems were observed in the immediate vicinity of Place Saint-Bruno and Cours Berriat, particularly related to drug trafficking and arms trafficking.

## Geography

Grenoble with the [Dauphiné Alps](/wiki/Dauphin%C3%A9_Alps) in the background.

Grenoble is surrounded by mountains. To the north lies the [Chartreuse](/wiki/Chartreuse_Mountains), to the south and west the [Vercors](/wiki/Vercors_Plateau), and to the east the [Belledonne range](/wiki/Belledonne). Grenoble is regarded as the capital of the French Alps. It is the centre of the [Grenoble urban unit](/wiki/Grenoble_urban_unit) (agglomeration).

Except for a few dozen houses on the slopes of the [Bastille](/wiki/Bastille_(Grenoble)) hill of Chartreuse, Grenoble is exclusively built on the alluvial plain of the rivers [Isère](/wiki/Is%C3%A8re_(river)) and [Drac](/wiki/Drac_(river)) at an altitude of 214 metres (702 ft). As a result, the city itself is extremely flat. Mountain sports are an important tourist attraction in summer and winter. Twenty large and small ski resorts surround the city, the nearest being [Le Sappey-en-Chartreuse](/wiki/Le_Sappey-en-Chartreuse), which is about 15 minutes away by car.

Historically, Grenoble and the surrounding areas were heavy industry and mining sites. Abandoned mills and factories can be found in small towns and villages, and a few have been converted to tourist attractions, such as the coal mine at [La Mure](/wiki/La_Mure).

### Climate

The climate in Grenoble depends on the data from the chosen weather station. [Grenoble Airport](/wiki/Grenoble_Airport), located 40 km northwest of the city has a range from [temperate continental climate](/wiki/Temperate_continental_climate) to [oceanic climate](/wiki/Oceanic_climate) ([Köppen](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): Cfb, [Trewartha](/wiki/Trewartha_climate_classification): Dc, Do) depending on the chosen classifications. The area contains significant seasonal differences between warm to hot summers and cool to cold winters. Both temperatures above 30 °C (86 °F) for the summer months and winter [air frosts](/wiki/Air_frost) are common.

In addition, the climate is much gloomier than in the Mediterranean region, although less so than in Northern France. Rainfall is quite heavy by French standards, although the number of rainy days is relatively moderate.

As a result of winter lows averaging below freezing, snowfall also occurs, although the Grenoble Airport area itself is too mild to sustain a snowpack all winter, unlike the surrounding mountains. The record low of −27.1 °C (−16.8 °F) decisively indicates the continental influence, being colder than records in typical maritime climates. Winter nights are also colder than in all other French lowland areas.

The Grenoble metropolitan area experiences two different microclimates: one more windy and cold to the west, the other, on the contrary, not very windy and warmer to the east.

However, the city of Grenoble features a [humid subtropical climate](/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) (Köppen: Cfa) with no dry season. Although the record is incomplete, the newer station will meet the humid subtropical classification if maintained for the required 30-year period.

## Urbanism and architecture

The Bouchayer-Viallet site is a powerful symbol of Grenoble's industrial past. This former factory is now converted into a dual-purpose area more closely linked to the Berriat neighbourhood. Innovative business activities as [Apple Inc.](/wiki/Apple_Inc.) co-exist with housing, sporting facilities, contemporary music venue and arts centres as [Le Magasin](/wiki/Le_Magasin). At the entrance to the Bouchayer-Viallet site, Square des Fusillés has been redeveloped and extended taking over an old car park, to facilitate access from the tramway stop and Cours Berriat.

Redevelopment of the former De Bonne barracks was an important step in the drive to launch [sustainable housing](/wiki/Green_building) in France. In 2009, the site of De Bonne was distinguished as the best eco-neighborhood in France. A shopping mall contains 53 shops arranged around an inner concourse, with one side opening onto the park and the other connecting to the town.

## Main sights

The [Bastille](/wiki/Bastille_(Grenoble)) from downtown, with the Memorial at the back, on the top of the hill

### La Bastille

The [Bastille](/wiki/Bastille_(Grenoble)), an ancient series of fortifications on the mountainside, overlooks Grenoble on the northern side and is visible from many points in the city. The Bastille is one of Grenoble's most visited tourist attractions and provides a good vantage point over both the town below and the surrounding mountains.

"Les Bulles": the cable cars

The Bastille fort was begun in the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages), and later centuries saw extensive additions, including a semi-underground defense network. The Bastille has been credited as the most extensive example of early 18th-century fortifications in all of France. It then held an important strategic point on the French [Alpine](/wiki/Alps) frontier with the [Kingdom of Sardinia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Sardinia_(1720%E2%80%931861)).

The first cable transport system, installed on the Bastille in 1875, was built by the Porte de France Cement Company for freight. This cable transport system connected a quarry on Mount Jalla, just over the Bastille, and Grenoble. It was abandoned in the early 20th century.

Since 1934, the Bastille has been the destination of the "[Grenoble-Bastille cable car](/wiki/Grenoble-Bastille_cable_car)". This system of mostly transparent egg-shaped [cable cars](/wiki/Aerial_tramway) known to locals as "Les Bulles" (the bubbles) provides the occupants with an excellent view over the [Isère](/wiki/Is%C3%A8re_(river)). At the top are two restaurants and installed in the casemates of the fort itself since June 2006, the Bastille Art Centre allows visitors to see contemporary art exhibitions. There is also a small military museum on mountain troops (*Musée des troupes de montagne*) and, since 2000, a memorial to the mountain troops (*Mémorial national des troupes de montagne*) further along the road, on top of the hill.

### Palace of the Parliament of Dauphiné

Palace of the [Parliament](/wiki/Parliament) of [Dauphiné](/wiki/Dauphin%C3%A9)

This renaissance palace was constructed at the Place Saint André around 1500 and extended in 1539. It was the location of the [Parlement](/wiki/Parlement) of [Dauphiné](/wiki/Dauphin%C3%A9) until the [French Revolution](/wiki/French_Revolution). It then became the Grenoble courthouse, until the courts were moved to a modern building in 2002. The left wing of the palace was extended in 1897. The front of the former seat of the nearby Dauphiné Parlement combines elements from a gothic chapel and a Renaissance façade.

The building now belongs to the Isère Council (Conseil Général de l'Isère). An ongoing renovation project will give this building a new life whilst preserving its patrimonial character and adding a modern touch.

### Museum of Grenoble

The city's most prized museum, the [Museum of Grenoble](/wiki/Museum_of_Grenoble) (*Musée de Grenoble*), welcomes 200,000 visitors a year. It is primarily renowned for its extensive paintings collection, which covers Western paintings from the Middle Ages to the 21st century. In the early 20th century, the Museum of Grenoble became the first French museum to open its collections to modern art, and its collection of modern and contemporary art has grown to become one of the largest in Europe. The painting holdings include works by painters such as [Veronese](/wiki/Paolo_Veronese), [Rubens](/wiki/Rubens), [Zurbarán](/wiki/Zurbar%C3%A1n), [Ingres](/wiki/Ingres), [Delacroix](/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Delacroix), [Renoir](/wiki/Pierre-Auguste_Renoir), [Gauguin](/wiki/Paul_Gauguin), [Signac](/wiki/Paul_Signac), [Monet](/wiki/Claude_Monet), [Matisse](/wiki/Henri_Matisse), [Picasso](/wiki/Picasso), [Kandinsky](/wiki/Wassily_Kandinsky), [Joan Miró](/wiki/Joan_Mir%C3%B3), [Paul Klee](/wiki/Paul_Klee), [Giorgio de Chirico](/wiki/Giorgio_de_Chirico) and [Andy Warhol](/wiki/Andy_Warhol). The museum also presents a few Egyptian antiquities as well as Greek and Roman artifacts. The Sculpture collection features works by [Auguste Rodin](/wiki/Auguste_Rodin), [Matisse](/wiki/Matisse), [Alberto Giacometti](/wiki/Alberto_Giacometti) and [Alexander Calder](/wiki/Alexander_Calder). In April 2010, the *prophetess of Antinoe*, a 6th-century mummy discovered in 1907 in the [Coptic](/wiki/Copts) [necropolis](/wiki/Necropolis) of [Antinoe](/wiki/Antinopolis) in Middle Egypt, returned to the Museum of Grenoble, after more than fifty years of absence and extensive restoration.

### Archaeological museums

[Archaeological](/wiki/Grenoble_Archaeological_Museum) museum with the vestiges protected by a new cover of glass and metal (Place Saint-Laurent)

Situated on the right bank of the Isère, on Place Saint-Laurent, the [Grenoble Archaeological Museum](/wiki/Grenoble_Archaeological_Museum) presents the archaeological excavations done on its location. The vestiges date back to the 3rd century AD and provide a timeline of the history of Christianity in the region. The museum is situated below a 12th-century Benedictine church, under which [Jacques Joseph Champollion-Figeac](/wiki/Jacques_Joseph_Champollion-Figeac), brother of famed egyptologist [Jean-François Champollion](/wiki/Jean-Fran%C3%A7ois_Champollion), discovered a 6th-century AD church in 1803. It was one of the first classified monuments in France thanks to the intervention of [Prosper Mérimée](/wiki/Prosper_M%C3%A9rim%C3%A9e), historic monument inspector. Systematic excavations were conducted from 1978 to 2011, as part of a regional research program on the evolution of churches during the Middle Ages. After eight years of work, the museum reopened on 6 May 2011.

The [Musée de l'Ancien Évêché](/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_de_l%27Ancien_%C3%89v%C3%AAch%C3%A9) is the second archaeological museum in the city and is located near the [Grenoble Cathedral](/wiki/Grenoble_Cathedral). Installed in 1998, it houses the first baptistery of the city.

The Grenoble town hall hosts a bust of Stendhal by sculptor [Pierre Charles Lenoir](/wiki/Pierre_Charles_Lenoir).

## Education and science

### Secondary level

The large community of both foreign students and foreign researchers prompted the creation of an international school. The [Cité Scolaire Internationale Europole](/wiki/Cit%C3%A9_Scolaire_Internationale_de_Grenoble) (CSI Europole) was formerly housed within the *[Lycée Stendhal](/wiki/Lyc%C3%A9e_Stendhal)* across from the *[Maison du Tourisme](/wiki/Maison_du_Tourisme)*, but later moved to its own building in the Europole district. In the centre of the city, two high schools have provided education to the isérois for more than three centuries. The oldest one, the [Lycée Stendhal](/wiki/Lyc%C3%A9e_Stendhal), was founded in 1651 as a Jesuit College. An astronomical and astrological sundial created in the college's main building in 1673 can still be visited today. The second-oldest higher education establishment in Grenoble is the [Lycée Champollion](/wiki/Lyc%C3%A9e_Champollion), completed in 1887 to offer an excellent education to both high school students and students of preparatory classes.

### Higher education

Campus of the [Université Grenoble Alpes](/wiki/Universit%C3%A9_Grenoble_Alpes)

The city is an important university centre with over 54,000 students in 2013, of whom 16% arrive from abroad.

In a 1339 [pontificial bull](/wiki/Papal_bull), Pope [Benedict XII](/wiki/Benedict_XII) commissioned the establishment of the [University of Grenoble](/wiki/Universit%C3%A9_Grenoble_Alpes).

In 1965, the university mostly relocated from downtown to a suburban main campus outside of the city in [Saint Martin d'Hères](/wiki/Saint-Martin-d%27H%C3%A8res) (with some parts in [Gières](/wiki/Gi%C3%A8res)). However, smaller campuses remain both downtown and in the northwestern part of the city known as the *[Polygone Scientifique](/wiki/Polygone_Scientifique)* ("Scientific Polygon").

From 1970 to 2015, the university was divided into four separate institutions sharing the campus grounds, some buildings and laboratories, and even part of their administration:

- Grenoble I – [Joseph Fourier University](/wiki/Joseph_Fourier_University) (sciences, health, technologies)
- Grenoble II – [Pierre Mendès-France University](/wiki/Pierre_Mend%C3%A8s-France_University) (social sciences)
  - which includes the [Institute of political studies](/wiki/Institut_d%27%C3%A9tudes_politiques_de_Grenoble)
- Grenoble III – [Stendhal University](/wiki/Stendhal_University) (humanities)
- [Grenoble Institute of Technology](/wiki/Grenoble_Institute_of_Technology) (INPG or Grenoble-INP) is a federation of engineering colleges.

The first three of those merged back on 1 January 2016 to form the [Université Grenoble Alpes](/wiki/Universit%C3%A9_Grenoble_Alpes), and the last one joined them on 1 January 2020.

Grenoble Campus of the [ENAC](/wiki/%C3%89cole_nationale_de_l%27aviation_civile)

Campuses are also located in Grenoble for:

- [École nationale de l'aviation civile](/wiki/%C3%89cole_nationale_de_l%27aviation_civile) (French civil aviation university),
- École d'Architecture de Grenoble (School of Architecture of Grenoble) and
- [Grenoble École de Management](/wiki/Grenoble_%C3%89cole_de_Management) (Grenoble School of Management) triple accredited [AMBA](/wiki/Association_of_MBAs)-[EQUIS](/wiki/EFMD_Quality_Improvement_System)-[AACSB](/wiki/Association_to_Advance_Collegiate_Schools_of_Business) Business School.

#### Science and engineering

Site of [European Synchrotron Radiation Facility](/wiki/European_Synchrotron_Radiation_Facility), [Institut Laue-Langevin](/wiki/Institut_Laue-Langevin) and [European Molecular Biology Laboratory](/wiki/European_Molecular_Biology_Laboratory) at the Western end of the *[Polygone Scientifique](/wiki/Polygone_Scientifique)*

Grenoble is a major scientific centre, especially in the fields of [physics](/wiki/Physics), [computer science](/wiki/Computer_science), and [applied mathematics](/wiki/Applied_mathematics): [Universite Joseph Fourier](/wiki/Joseph_Fourier_University) (UJF) is one of the leading French scientific universities while the Grenoble Institute of Technology trains more than 5,000 engineers every year in key technology disciplines. Grenoble's high-tech expertise is organized mainly around three domains: information technology, biotechnologies, and new technologies of energy.

Many fundamental and applied scientific research laboratories are conjointly managed by Joseph Fourier University, Grenoble Institute of Technology, and the French *[National Centre for Scientific Research](/wiki/French_National_Centre_for_Scientific_Research)* (CNRS). Numerous other scientific laboratories are managed independently or in collaboration with the CNRS and the French *[National Institute for Research in Computer Science and Control](/wiki/Institut_national_de_recherche_en_informatique_et_en_automatique)* (INRIA).

Other research centres in or near Grenoble include the [European Synchrotron Radiation Facility](/wiki/European_Synchrotron_Radiation_Facility) (ESRF), the [Institut Laue-Langevin](/wiki/Institut_Laue-Langevin) (ILL), the [European Molecular Biology Laboratory](/wiki/European_Molecular_Biology_Laboratory) (EMBL), the [Institut de radioastronomie millimétrique](/wiki/Institut_de_radioastronomie_millim%C3%A9trique), one of the main research facilities of the [Commissariat à l'Énergie Atomique](/wiki/Commissariat_%C3%A0_l%27%C3%A9nergie_atomique) (Nuclear Energy Commission, CEA), the [LNCMI](/wiki/LNCMI) and the European branch of [Xerox Research](/wiki/Xerox) (whose most notable center was [PARC](/wiki/PARC_(company))). [Leti](/wiki/CEA-Leti:_Laboratoire_d%27%C3%A9lectronique_des_technologies_de_l%27information) and the recent development of [Minatec](/wiki/Minatec), a centre for innovation in micro- and nano-technology, only increases Grenoble's position as a European scientific centre. Biotechnologies are also well represented in the Grenoble region with the molecular biology research center [BioMérieux](/wiki/BioM%C3%A9rieux), the [Clinatec](/wiki/Clinatec) center, the regional center NanoBio and many ramifications of the global competitiveness cluster Lyonbiopôle.

Meanwhile, Grenoble has large laboratories related to space and to the understanding and observation of the universe as the [Institut de radioastronomie millimétrique](/wiki/Institut_de_radioastronomie_millim%C3%A9trique), the Institut de planétologie et d'astrophysique de Grenoble, the Laboratoire de physique subatomique et de cosmologie de Grenoble, the [Institut Néel](/wiki/Institut_N%C3%A9el) but also to a lesser extent the Institut des sciences de la Terre (part of the [Observatoire des Sciences de l'Univers de Grenoble](/wiki/Observatoire_des_Sciences_de_l%27Univers_de_Grenoble)).

In order to foster this technological cluster university institutions and research organizations united to create the [GIANT](https://www.giant-grenoble.org/en/presentation/) (Grenoble Innovation for Advanced New Technologies) Campus with the aim at becoming one of the world's top campuses in research (CEA, CNRS), higher education (INP-UGA, Grenoble Ecole de Management), and high tech.

The city benefits from the highest concentration of strategic jobs in France after Paris, with 14% of the employments, 35,186 jobs, 45% of which specialized in design and research. Grenoble is also the largest research center in France after Paris with 22,800 jobs (11,800 in public research, 7,500 in private research and 3,500 PhD students).

Grenoble is also renowned for the excellence of its academic research in humanities and political sciences. Its universities, alongside public scientific institutions, host some of the largest research centres in France (in fields such as political science, urban planning or the [sociology of organizations](/wiki/Sociology_of_organizations)).

### Knowledge and innovation community

Grenoble is one of the co-location centres of the [European Institute of Innovation and Technology](/wiki/European_Institute_of_Innovation_and_Technology)'s Knowledge and Innovation Communities for [sustainable energy](/wiki/Sustainable_energy).

## Economy

Industry occupies a large part of the local economy. High-tech industries have a significant presence, especially in the field of semiconductors, electronics, and [biotechnology](/wiki/Biotechnology). [STMicroelectronics](/wiki/STMicroelectronics), [Schneider Electric](/wiki/Schneider_Electric) and [Soitec](/wiki/Soitec) have major manufacturing and R&D facilities. Traditional industries in fields such as heavy equipment manufacturing and chemistry are still present and include [Caterpillar](/wiki/Caterpillar_Inc.), [GE Renewable Energy](/wiki/GE_Renewable_Energy), and [Arkema](/wiki/Arkema).

The town was once famous for [glove](/wiki/Glove) manufacturing, for which Xavier Jouvin introduced an innovative technique in the 19th century. A few small companies continue to produce gloves for a very high-end market.

The presence of companies such as HP or Caterpillar in the area has drawn many American and British workers to Grenoble, especially in the surrounding mountain villages. The region has the second largest English-speaking community in France, after Paris. That community has an English-speaking Church and supports the International School. Many of these Americans, British, Australians etc. go to Grenoble with the intention of returning home after some time but the mountains and general lifestyle often keep them there. Some choose to put their children in the international school "cité internationale", while the "American School of Grenoble" is the alternative for those who prefer to have the core curriculum in English. With numerous associations like Open House, this large English-speaking population organizes family events making life in Grenoble harder to turn away from.

Publisher [Glénat](/wiki/Gl%C3%A9nat_(publisher)) has its head office in Grenoble. [Inovallée](/wiki/Inovall%C3%A9e) is a [science park](/wiki/Science_park) with about 12,000 jobs located at [Meylan](/wiki/Meylan) and [Montbonnot-Saint-Martin](/wiki/Montbonnot-Saint-Martin) near Grenoble.

## Sport

[Stade des Alpes](/wiki/Stade_des_Alpes)

Grenoble hosted the [1968 Winter Olympics](/wiki/1968_Winter_Olympics). The city is surrounded by ski resorts nestled in the surrounding mountains. [Stade Lesdiguières](/wiki/Stade_Lesdigui%C3%A8res) is located in Grenoble and has been the venue for international [rugby league](/wiki/Rugby_league) and [rugby union](/wiki/Rugby_union) games.

Grenoble is the home of first [rugby union](/wiki/Rugby_union), [FC Grenoble](/wiki/FC_Grenoble), and [ice hockey](/wiki/Ice_hockey) teams, [Brûleurs de loups](/wiki/Br%C3%BBleurs_de_loups), and a second-tier football team, [Grenoble Foot 38](/wiki/Grenoble_Foot_38).

- [Six-Days of Grenoble](/wiki/Six-Days_of_Grenoble), a six-day track cycling race held since 1971
- The [via ferrata](/wiki/Via_ferrata) Grenoble is a climbing route located on the hill of the Bastille in Grenoble.

The abundance of natural sites around Grenoble as well as the particular influence of mountaineering practices and history make many Grenoble inhabitants very fond of sports and outdoor activities (e.g., hiking, mountain biking, [backcountry skiing](/wiki/Backcountry_skiing), [rock climbing](/wiki/Rock_climbing), and [paragliding](/wiki/Paragliding)). The [Tour de France](/wiki/Tour_de_France) cycling race regularly passes through the city.

## Transport

See also: [Grenoble tramway](/wiki/Grenoble_tramway)

The railway station and a tram (light rail)

A comprehensive bus and tram service operates 26 bus routes and five [tram](/wiki/Tram) lines. It serves much of greater Grenoble, while a new cable car system known as the Métrocâble is considered for construction. Being essentially flat, Grenoble is also a bicycle-friendly city.

The [Gare de Grenoble](/wiki/Gare_de_Grenoble) is served by the [TGV](/wiki/TGV) rail network, with frequent high-speed services to and from [Paris-Gare de Lyon](/wiki/Paris-Gare_de_Lyon), taking 3 hours, usually with a stop at [Lyon Saint-Exupéry Airport](/wiki/Lyon-Saint_Exup%C3%A9ry_Airport). While Grenoble is not directly on any high-speed line, TGVs can run at reduced speeds on the classic network and enable such connections. [Local rail](/wiki/Transport_express_r%C3%A9gional) services connect Grenoble with [Lyon](/wiki/Lyon), and less frequently to [Geneva](/wiki/Geneva), to [Valence](/wiki/Valence,_Dr%C3%B4me), and to destinations to the south. [Valence](/wiki/Valence,_Dr%C3%B4me) and Lyon to the west provides connections with TGV services along the Rhône Valley. Rail and road connections to the south are less developed.

Grenoble can be accessed by air from [Grenoble-Isère Airport](/wiki/Grenoble-Is%C3%A8re_Airport), [Lyon Saint-Exupéry Airport](/wiki/Lyon-Saint_Exup%C3%A9ry_Airport) and [Geneva International Airport](/wiki/Geneva_International_Airport), with the airport bus connections being most frequent to Lyon Saint-Exupéry.

[I-Road](/wiki/Toyota_concept_vehicles,_2010%E2%80%9319#i-Road) in Grenoble

Highways link Grenoble to the other major cities in the area including the [A48 autoroute](/wiki/A48_autoroute) to the northwest toward [Lyon](/wiki/Lyon), the [A49](/wiki/A49_autoroute) to the southwest toward the Rhone valley via [Valence](/wiki/Valence,_Dr%C3%B4me), the [A41](/wiki/A41_autoroute) to the northeast toward [Chambéry](/wiki/Chamb%C3%A9ry), the Alps, and Italy and Switzerland.

A partial ring road around the south of the city, the Rocade Sud, connects the motorway arriving from the northwest (A48) with that arriving from the northeast (A41). A project to complete the ring road, with a tunnel under the Bastille as part of the likely routes, was rejected after its environmental impact studies.

From 2014 to 2017, the city of Grenoble tested the rental of seventy [I-Road](/wiki/Toyota_concept_vehicles,_2010%E2%80%9319#i-Road) electric vehicles.

In 2016, the speed limit was lowered to 30 km/h (19 mph) on 80% of the streets of Grenoble and forty-two neighboring municipalities, to both improve safety and reduce pollution levels. The limit, however, remains 50 km/h (31 mph) on the main arteries.

## Culture

Le Magasin contemporary art centre

Grenoble hosts several festivals: the Détours de Babel in March, the Open Air Short Film Festival in early July, and the *Cabaret Frappé* music festival at the end of July.

The [Summum](/wiki/Summum_(Grenoble)) is the biggest concert hall in Grenoble, and the most famous artists produce there. Another big hall, *Le grand angle*, is located nearby in [Voiron](/wiki/Voiron). Smaller halls in the city include the *Salle Olivier Messiaen* in the [Minim Monastery](/wiki/Couvent_des_Minimes_de_Grenoble).

The main cultural center of the city is called [MC2](/wiki/Maison_de_la_Culture_de_Grenoble) (for *Maison de la culture, version 2*), which hosts music, theater, and dance performances. The [Conservatory of Grenoble](/wiki/Conservatory_of_Grenoble) is founded in 1935.

There are several theaters in Grenoble, the main one being Grenoble Municipal Theatre (*Théatre de Grenoble*). Others are the *Théâtre de Création*, the *Théâtre Prémol*, and the *Théâtre 145*. Grenoble also hosts Upstage Productions, which performs once a year through an exclusively English speaking troupe.

There are two main art centres in Grenoble: the *Centre national d'Art contemporain* (also called *[Le Magasin](/wiki/Le_Magasin)*) and the *Centre d'art Bastille*.

Grenoble is known for its [walnuts](/wiki/Walnut), Noix de Grenoble which enjoy an [*appellation* of controlled origin](/wiki/Appellation_d%27origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e).

## Crime

[Crime](/wiki/Crime) has been a concern in Grenoble for decades, namely [drug trafficking](/wiki/Drug_trafficking), and more recently [racketeering](/wiki/Racket_(crime)), especially in the construction field.

Crime in Grenoble and its area has grown significantly in the 2020s:

### 2020

Seven [shootings](/wiki/Shootings), according to authorities, all in the same summer.

### 2022

In 2022, nine different [shootings](/wiki/Shootings) are recorded in less than two months, according to local [authorities](/wiki/Authorities).

### 2024

In March, a new [shooting](/wiki/Shooting) happens in the suburb of Saint-Bruno, which is currently one of the primary "deal-points" of rival gangs. Just few months after, in the summer, seven [shootings](/wiki/Shootings) are recorded. [Mayor](/wiki/Mayor) [Éric Piolle](/wiki/%C3%89ric_Piolle) comes under criticism.

### 2025

On 12 February, a [grenade](/wiki/Grenade) is thrown out into a [pub](/wiki/Pub). Just the following week, on 19 February, a [ram-raid](/wiki/Ram-raid) car is used to start [arson](/wiki/Arson) in a [public library](/wiki/Public_library) that was opened just two months prior.

In March a former [druglord](/wiki/Druglord) is [assassinated](/wiki/Assassination) on the A41 [highway](/wiki/Highway), in plain sight. Investigations show that the drug lord was "very worried about his life".

During the night of the 2nd to 3rd of December, a 33-year-old man is killed. A week later, on night of the 6th to 7th of December, two killings occur: a 22-year-old man is killed on 8 May 1945 Avenue, at [Échirolles](/wiki/%C3%89chirolles); and an 18-year-old man is killed, nearby, at La Bruyère park.
