Ubiquitous [gardens](/wiki/Garden) exemplified by [landscaping](/wiki/Landscaping) at [Rutgers University](/wiki/Rutgers_University-Camden)-[Camden](/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey) lend New Jersey its eponymous nickname as the Garden State.

**New Jersey** (sometimes colloquially shortened **Jersey**) is a [state](/wiki/U.S._state) located in both the [Mid-Atlantic](/wiki/Mid-Atlantic_States) and [Northeastern](/wiki/Northeastern_United_States) regions of the United States. Located at the geographic hub of the [heavily urbanized](/wiki/Urban_area) [Northeast megalopolis](/wiki/Northeast_megalopolis), it is bordered to the northwest, north, and northeast by [New York State](/wiki/New_York_(state)); on its east, southeast, and south by the [Atlantic Ocean](/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean); on its west by the [Delaware River](/wiki/Delaware_River) and [Pennsylvania](/wiki/Pennsylvania); and on its southwest by [Delaware Bay](/wiki/Delaware_Bay) and [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware). At 7,354 square miles (19,050 km2), New Jersey is the [fifth-smallest state in land area](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_area). According to a 2025 [U.S. Census Bureau](/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau) estimate, it is the [11th-most populous state](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_population), with over 9.5 million residents, its highest estimated count ever. The state capital is [Trenton](/wiki/Trenton,_New_Jersey), and the state's most populous city is [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey). New Jersey is the only U.S. state in which every [county](/wiki/List_of_counties_in_New_Jersey) is deemed [urban](/wiki/Urban_area) by the U.S. Census Bureau. It is the [most densely populated](/wiki/List_of_states_and_territories_of_the_United_States_by_population_density) U.S. state.

New Jersey was first inhabited by [Paleo-Indians](/wiki/Paleo-Indians) as early as 13,000 BC. The [Lenape](/wiki/Lenape) were the dominant [Indigenous group](/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) when Europeans arrived in the early 17th century, and they were subdivided into dialectal groups such as the Munsee, in the north, and the Unami and the Unalachtigo, elsewhere. [Dutch](/wiki/Dutch_Republic) and [Swedish](/wiki/Swedish_Empire) colonists founded the first European settlements in the state, with the [British](/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) later seizing control of the region and establishing the [Province of New Jersey](/wiki/Province_of_New_Jersey), named after the [Channel Island](/wiki/Channel_Islands) of [Jersey](/wiki/Jersey). The colony's fertile lands and relative [religious tolerance](/wiki/Toleration) drew a large and diverse population. New Jersey was among the [Thirteen Colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) that supported the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution), hosting several [pivotal battles and military commands](/wiki/New_Jersey_in_the_American_Revolution) in the [American Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) earning the title "Crossroads of the American Revolution". New Jersey remained in the [Union](/wiki/Union_(American_Civil_War)) during the [American Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War) and [provided](/wiki/New_Jersey_in_the_American_Civil_War) troops, resources, and military leaders in support of the [Union Army](/wiki/Union_Army). After the war, the state emerged as a major [manufacturing](/wiki/Manufacturing) center and a leading destination for immigrants, helping drive the [Industrial Revolution](/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) in the U.S. New Jersey was the site of many industrial, technological, and commercial [innovations](/wiki/Innovation). [Many prominent Americans associated with New Jersey](/wiki/List_of_people_from_New_Jersey) have proven influential nationally and globally, including in [academia](/wiki/Academic_institutions), social advocacy, business, entertainment, government, military, [non-profit leadership](/wiki/Non-profit_organization), and other fields.

New Jersey's central location in the Northeast megalopolis helped fuel its rapid growth and [suburbanization](/wiki/Suburbanization) in the second half of the 20th century. Since the beginning of the 21st century, the state's economy has become highly diversified, with major sectors, including New Jersey's role as the world's largest [pharmaceutical industry](/wiki/Pharmaceuticals) hub, and is widely known as the "Medicine Chest of the World". New Jersey excels in [biotechnology](/wiki/Biotechnology), [information technology](/wiki/Information_technology), [finance](/wiki/Wall_Street_West), [digital media](/wiki/Digital_media), [filmmaking](/wiki/Filmmaking), and [tourism](/wiki/Tourism), and it has become an [Atlantic seaboard](/wiki/East_Coast_of_the_United_States) epicenter for [logistics](/wiki/Logistics) and [distribution](/wiki/Distribution_(marketing)). New Jersey is a major destination for immigrants and is home to one of the world's most [multicultural populations](/wiki/Ethnic_enclave). Since the onset of the 21st century, re-urbanization is taking place in New Jersey, particularly near [New Jersey Transit](/wiki/New_Jersey_Transit) [rail hubs](/wiki/Commuter_rail). New Jersey is the fastest-growing state in the [U.S. Northeast](/wiki/U.S._Northeast) and one of the fastest-growing in the United States overall, attributed to its resilient economy and aggressive [homebuilding](/wiki/Homebuilding) policies.

New Jersey is one of the most developed states in the U.S. New Jersey had a [median household income](/wiki/Median_income) of $99,781 as of 2023, the second-highest of any U.S. state behind [Massachusetts](/wiki/Massachusetts). Almost one-tenth of all households in the state, or over 323,000, are [millionaires](/wiki/Millionaire), the highest representation of millionaires among all states and the highest density per square mile. Despite not being a U.S. border state, one-quarter of New Jersey's population is [foreign born](/wiki/Foreign_born), the second-highest proportion after [California](/wiki/California). The state ranks near the top on both the [American Human Development Index](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_American_Human_Development_Index) and the [standard Human Development Index](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_Human_Development_Index). New Jersey is also ranked as one of the most expensive states for residents to live in.

According to [climatology](/wiki/Climatology) research by the U.S. [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration](/wiki/National_Oceanic_and_Atmospheric_Administration), New Jersey has been the fastest-warming state by average [air temperature](/wiki/Air_temperature) over a 100-year period beginning in the early 20th century, which has been attributed to significant warming of the [North Atlantic Ocean](/wiki/North_Atlantic_Ocean).

## History

Main article: [History of New Jersey](/wiki/History_of_New_Jersey)

### Prehistoric era

See also: [Paleontology in New Jersey](/wiki/Paleontology_in_New_Jersey)

The pressure of collision between [North America](/wiki/North_America) and [Africa](/wiki/Africa) gave rise to the [Appalachian Mountains](/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains). Around 18,000 years ago, the [Ice Age](/wiki/Wisconsin_glaciation) resulted in [glaciers](/wiki/Glacier) that reached New Jersey. As glaciers retreated, they left behind [Lake Passaic](/wiki/Lake_Passaic) along with rivers, [meadows](/wiki/Meadow), swamps, and [gorges](/wiki/Gorge).

Since the [6th millennium BC](/wiki/6th_millennium_BC), [Native American](/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) people have inhabited New Jersey, beginning with the [Lenape tribe](/wiki/Lenape). **Scheyichbi** is the [Lenape](/wiki/Unami_language) name for the land that represents present-day New Jersey. The Lenape were several [autonomous](/wiki/Autonomy) groups that practiced [maize](/wiki/Maize) agriculture in order to supplement their hunting and gathering in the region surrounding the [Delaware River](/wiki/Delaware_River), the lower [Hudson River](/wiki/Hudson_River), and western [Long Island Sound](/wiki/Long_Island_Sound). The Lenape were divided into [matrilineal](/wiki/Matrilineality) clans that were based upon common female ancestors. Clans were organized into three distinct [phratries](/wiki/Phratry) identified by their animal sign: [Turtle](/wiki/Turtle), [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey_(bird)), and [Wolf](/wiki/Wolf). They first encountered the [Dutch](/wiki/Dutch_colonization_of_the_Americas) in the early 17th century, and their primary relationship with the Dutch and later European settlers was through [fur trade](/wiki/Fur_trade).

### Colonial era

Main articles: [Colonial history of New Jersey](/wiki/Colonial_history_of_New_Jersey), [New Netherland](/wiki/New_Netherland), [New Sweden](/wiki/New_Sweden), [Province of New Jersey](/wiki/Province_of_New_Jersey), [East Jersey](/wiki/East_Jersey), [West Jersey](/wiki/West_Jersey), and [Dominion of New England](/wiki/Dominion_of_New_England)

A map of [New Netherland](/wiki/New_Netherland) and [New Sweden](/wiki/New_Sweden) in present-day New Jersey during the [colonial era](/wiki/Colonial_history_of_New_Jersey)

The [Dutch](/wiki/Netherlands) were the first [Europeans](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Europe) to lay claim to geographic territory in New Jersey. The Dutch colony of [New Netherland](/wiki/New_Netherland) consisted of parts of the modern [Mid-Atlantic](/wiki/Mid-Atlantic_(United_States)) states. Although the European principle of [land ownership](/wiki/Land_ownership) was not recognized by the [Lenape](/wiki/Lenape), [Dutch West India Company](/wiki/Dutch_West_India_Company) policy required its colonists to purchase land that they settled. The first to do so was [Michiel Pauw](/wiki/Michiel_Reyniersz_Pauw), who established a [patroonship](/wiki/Patroon) called [Pavonia](/wiki/Pavonia,_New_Netherland) in 1630 along [North River](/wiki/North_River_(Hudson_River)), that eventually became [Bergen](/wiki/Bergen,_New_Netherland), the first permanent settlement, local civil government and municipality in the state. [Peter Minuit](/wiki/Peter_Minuit)'s purchase of lands along the [Delaware River](/wiki/Delaware_River) established the colony of [New Sweden](/wiki/New_Sweden), that lasted until the Dutch conquered it in 1655. Then the entire region became a territory of [England](/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) on June 24, 1664, after an English fleet under command of Colonel [Richard Nicolls](/wiki/Richard_Nicolls) sailed into what is now [New York Harbor](/wiki/New_York_Harbor) and took control of [Fort Amsterdam](/wiki/Fort_Amsterdam), annexing the entire province.

During the [English Civil War](/wiki/English_Civil_War), the [Channel Island](/wiki/Channel_Islands) of [Jersey](/wiki/Jersey) remained loyal to the [British Crown](/wiki/The_Crown) and gave sanctuary to the [King](/wiki/King_of_England). In the Royal Square in [St Helier](/wiki/St_Helier), [Charles II of England](/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) was proclaimed King in 1649, following the execution of his father, [Charles I](/wiki/Charles_I_of_England). North American lands were divided by [Charles II](/wiki/Charles_II_of_England), who gave his brother, the Duke of York (later [King James II](/wiki/James_II_of_England)), the region between [New England](/wiki/New_England) and [Maryland](/wiki/Maryland) as a [proprietary colony](/wiki/Proprietary_colony) (as opposed to a [royal colony](/wiki/Royal_colony)). James then granted land between the [Hudson River](/wiki/Hudson_River) and the [Delaware River](/wiki/Delaware_River) (the land that would become New Jersey) to two friends who had remained loyal through the [English Civil War](/wiki/English_Civil_War): [Sir George Carteret](/wiki/George_Carteret) and [Lord Berkeley of Stratton](/wiki/John_Berkeley,_1st_Baron_Berkeley_of_Stratton). The area was named the [Province of New Jersey](/wiki/Province_of_New_Jersey).

Since its inception, New Jersey has been characterized by [ethnic and religious diversity](/wiki/Multiculturalism). New England [Congregationalists](/wiki/Congregationalists) settled alongside [Scots Presbyterians](/wiki/Scots%27_Church,_Melbourne) and [Dutch Reformed](/wiki/Dutch_Reformed) migrants. While the majority of residents lived in towns with individual [landholdings](/wiki/Landowner) of 100 acres (40 ha), a few rich proprietors owned vast estates. English [Quakers](/wiki/Quakers) and [Anglicans](/wiki/Anglicans) owned large landholdings. Unlike [Plymouth Colony](/wiki/Plymouth_Colony), [Jamestown](/wiki/Jamestown,_Virginia) and other colonies, New Jersey was populated by a secondary wave of immigrants who came from other colonies instead of those who migrated directly from Europe. New Jersey remained agrarian and rural throughout the colonial era, and [commercial farming](/wiki/Commercial_agriculture) developed sporadically. Some townships, such as [Burlington](/wiki/Burlington,_New_Jersey) on the Delaware River and [Perth Amboy](/wiki/Perth_Amboy), emerged as important ports for shipping to New York City and Philadelphia. The colony's fertile lands and [tolerant religious policy](/wiki/Toleration) drew more settlers, and New Jersey's population had increased to 120,000 by 1775.

Settlement for the first ten years of English rule took place along the [Hackensack River](/wiki/Hackensack_River) and [Arthur Kill](/wiki/Arthur_Kill). Settlers came primarily from New York and New England. On March 18, 1673, Berkeley sold his half of the colony to [Quakers](/wiki/Religious_Society_of_Friends) in England, who settled the [Delaware Valley](/wiki/Delaware_Valley) region as a Quaker colony, with [William Penn](/wiki/William_Penn) acting as [trustee](/wiki/Trustee) for the lands for a time. New Jersey was governed as two distinct provinces, [East](/wiki/East_Jersey) and [West Jersey](/wiki/West_Jersey), for 28 years between 1674 and 1702, which were part of the [Dominion of New England](/wiki/Dominion_of_New_England) from 1686 to 1689.

In 1702, the two provinces were reunited under a [royal governor](/wiki/Royal_Governor_of_New_Jersey) rather than a [proprietary one](/wiki/Proprietary_governor). [Edward Hyde](/wiki/Edward_Hyde,_3rd_Earl_of_Clarendon), titled Lord Cornbury, became the first governor of the royal colony. Britain believed that he was an ineffective and corrupt ruler, taking bribes and speculating on land. In 1708, he was recalled to England. New Jersey was then ruled by the governors of New York, but this infuriated the settlers of New Jersey, who accused these governors of favoritism to New York. [Judge Lewis Morris](/wiki/Lewis_Morris_(governor)) led the case for a separate governor, and was appointed governor by [King George II](/wiki/George_II_of_Great_Britain) in 1738.

### Revolutionary War era

Main articles: [American Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), [Northern theater of the American Revolutionary War](/wiki/Northern_theater_of_the_American_Revolutionary_War), [New Jersey in the American Revolution](/wiki/New_Jersey_in_the_American_Revolution), [Lee Resolution](/wiki/Lee_Resolution), [United States Declaration of Independence](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence), [Philadelphia campaign](/wiki/Philadelphia_campaign), [Articles of Confederation § Ratification](/wiki/Articles_of_Confederation#Ratification), [Treaty of Paris (1783)](/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1783)), [Constitutional Convention (United States)](/wiki/Constitutional_Convention_(United_States)), [Admission to the Union](/wiki/Admission_to_the_Union), and [List of U.S. states by date of admission to the Union](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_date_of_admission_to_the_Union)

*[Washington Crossing the Delaware](/wiki/Washington_Crossing_the_Delaware_(1851_painting))*, an 1851 portrait by [Emanuel Leutze](/wiki/Emanuel_Leutze) depicting [Washington's covert crossing the Delaware River](/wiki/George_Washington%27s_crossing_of_the_Delaware_River) from [Bucks County, Pennsylvania](/wiki/Bucks_County,_Pennsylvania) to [Mercer County](/wiki/Mercer_County,_New_Jersey) on December 25, 1776, prior to the [Battle of Trenton](/wiki/Battle_of_Trenton)

*Washington Rallying the Americans at the Battle of Princeton*, a portrait by [William Ranney](/wiki/William_Ranney) depicting [George Washington](/wiki/George_Washington) rallying [Continental Army](/wiki/Continental_Army) troops at the [Battle of Princeton](/wiki/Battle_of_Princeton) in January 1777

New Jersey was one of the [Thirteen Colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) that revolted against British rule in the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution). The [New Jersey Constitution of 1776](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Constitution_of_1776) was passed July 2, 1776, just two days before the [Second Continental Congress](/wiki/Second_Continental_Congress) declared American Independence from [Great Britain](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain). It was an act of the [Provincial Congress](/wiki/Provincial_Congress_of_New_Jersey), which made itself into the [State Legislature](/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature). To reassure neutrals, it provided that the legislature would disband if New Jersey reached reconciliation with Great Britain. Among the 56 [Founding Fathers](/wiki/Founding_Fathers) who signed the [Declaration of Independence](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence), five were New Jersey representatives: [Richard Stockton](/wiki/Richard_Stockton_(Continental_Congressman)), [John Witherspoon](/wiki/John_Witherspoon), [Francis Hopkinson](/wiki/Francis_Hopkinson), [John Hart](/wiki/John_Hart_(New_Jersey_politician)), and [Abraham Clark](/wiki/Abraham_Clark).

During the [American Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), British and American armies crossed New Jersey numerous times with several pivotal battles taking place in the state. In total, there were 296 engagements that occurred within New Jersey, more clashes than occurred in any other of the 13 colonies during the war. Because of this, New Jersey today is sometimes referred to as "The Crossroads of the American Revolution". The winter quarters of the [Continental Army](/wiki/Continental_Army) were established in New Jersey twice by General [George Washington](/wiki/George_Washington) in [Morristown](/wiki/Morristown,_New_Jersey), which has been called "The Military Capital of the American Revolution."

On the night of December 25–26, 1776, the Continental Army under [George Washington crossed the Delaware River](/wiki/Washington%27s_crossing_of_the_Delaware_River). After the crossing, they surprised and defeated the [Hessian troops](/wiki/Hessian_(soldiers)) in the [Battle of Trenton](/wiki/Battle_of_Trenton). Slightly more than a week after victory at [Trenton](/wiki/Trenton,_New_Jersey), Continental Army forces gained an important victory by stopping [General Cornwallis's](/wiki/Charles_Cornwallis,_1st_Marquess_Cornwallis) charges at the [Second Battle of Trenton](/wiki/Second_Battle_of_Trenton). Evading Cornwallis's army, the Continental Army launched a surprise attack on [Princeton](/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey) and defeated the British on January 3, 1777. [Emanuel Leutze](/wiki/Emanuel_Leutze)'s painting of *[Washington Crossing the Delaware](/wiki/Washington_Crossing_the_Delaware_(1851_painting))* became an icon of the Revolution.

Continental Army forces under Washington's command met British forces under General [Henry Clinton](/wiki/Henry_Clinton_(American_War_of_Independence)) at the [Battle of Monmouth](/wiki/Battle_of_Monmouth) in an indecisive engagement in June 1778. Washington's forces attempted to take the British column by surprise. When the British army attempted to flank the Americans, the Continental Army retreated in disorder. Their ranks were later reorganized and withstood British charges.

In the summer of 1783, the [Continental Congress](/wiki/Continental_Congress) met in [Nassau Hall](/wiki/Nassau_Hall) at [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University), making [Princeton](/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey) the nation's capital for four months. It was there that the Continental Congress learned of the signing of the [Treaty of Paris](/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1783)), which ended the war.

On December 18, 1787, New Jersey became the third state to ratify the [United States Constitution](/wiki/United_States_Constitution), which was overwhelmingly popular in New Jersey since it prevented New York and Pennsylvania from charging [tariffs](/wiki/Tariff) on goods imported from Europe. On November 20, 1789, New Jersey became the first in the newly formed Union to ratify the [Bill of Rights](/wiki/United_States_Bill_of_Rights).

The 1776 [New Jersey State Constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_New_Jersey) gave the vote to all inhabitants who had a certain level of wealth. This included women and Black people, but not married women because they were not legally permitted to own property separately from their husbands. Both sides, in several elections, claimed that the other side had had unqualified women vote and mocked them for use of petticoat electors, whether entitled to vote or not; on the other hand, both parties passed Voting Rights Acts. In 1807, legislature passed a bill interpreting the constitution to mean universal white male [suffrage](/wiki/Suffrage), excluding paupers; the constitution was itself an act of the legislature and not enshrined as the modern constitution.

### 19th century

Main articles: [New Jersey in the 19th century](/wiki/New_Jersey_in_the_19th_century) and [New Jersey in the American Civil War](/wiki/New_Jersey_in_the_American_Civil_War)

Map of the 107-mile-long (172 km) [Morris Canal](/wiki/Morris_Canal), which crosses the state

On February 15, 1804, New Jersey became the last northern state to [abolish](/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_States) new [slavery](/wiki/Slavery) and enacted legislation that slowly phased out existing slavery. This led to a gradual decrease of the slave population. By the [American Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War)'s end, about a dozen African Americans in New Jersey were still held in bondage. New Jersey voters eventually ratified the constitutional amendments banning slavery and granting rights to the United States' black population.

Industrialization accelerated in the present-day [North Jersey](/wiki/North_Jersey) region of the state following completion of the [Morris Canal](/wiki/Morris_Canal) in 1831. The canal allowed for [anthracite coal](/wiki/Anthracite) to be transported from eastern [Pennsylvania](/wiki/Pennsylvania)'s [Lehigh Valley](/wiki/Lehigh_Valley) to North Jersey's growing industries in [Paterson](/wiki/Paterson,_New_Jersey), [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey), and [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey).

In 1844, the second [state constitution](/wiki/New_Jersey_State_Constitution) was ratified and brought into effect. Counties thereby became districts for the state senate, and some realignment of boundaries (including the creation of [Mercer County](/wiki/Mercer_County,_New_Jersey)) immediately followed. This provision was retained in the 1947 Constitution, but was overturned by the [Supreme Court of the United States](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) in 1962, by the decision *[Baker v. Carr](/wiki/Baker_v._Carr)*. While the Governorship was stronger than under the 1776 constitution, the constitution of 1844 created many offices that were not responsible to him, or to the people, and it gave him a three-year term, but he could not succeed himself.

New Jersey was one of the few Union states (the others being [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware) and [Kentucky](/wiki/Kentucky)) to select a candidate other than [Abraham Lincoln](/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln) twice in national elections, and sided with [Stephen A. Douglas](/wiki/Stephen_A._Douglas) (1860) and [George B. McClellan](/wiki/George_B._McClellan) (1864) during their campaigns. McClellan, a native Philadelphian, had New Jersey ties and formally resided in New Jersey at the time; he later became Governor of New Jersey (1878–81). (In New Jersey, the factions of the Democratic party managed an effective coalition in 1860.) During the [American Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War), the state was led first by Republican governor [Charles Smith Olden](/wiki/Charles_Smith_Olden), then by Democrat [Joel Parker](/wiki/Joel_Parker_(politician)). During the war, 65,000–80,000 state soldiers enlisted in the Union army; unlike many other states, it saw no battles.

In the [Industrial Revolution](/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), cities like [Paterson](/wiki/Paterson,_New_Jersey) grew and prospered. Previously, the economy had been largely agrarian, which was problematically subject to crop failures and poor soil. This caused a shift to a more [industrialized](/wiki/Industrialization) economy, one based on manufactured commodities such as textiles and silk. [Inventor](/wiki/List_of_inventors) [Thomas Edison](/wiki/Thomas_Edison) also became an important figure of the Industrial Revolution, having been [granted 1,093 patents](/wiki/List_of_Edison_patents), many of which for inventions he developed while working in New Jersey. Edison's facilities, first at [Menlo Park](/wiki/Menlo_Park,_New_Jersey) and then in [West Orange](/wiki/West_Orange,_New_Jersey), are considered perhaps the first [research centers](/wiki/Research_center) in the United States. Christie Street in Menlo Park was the first thoroughfare in the world to have electric lighting. Transportation was greatly improved as [locomotion](/wiki/Locomotive) and [steamboats](/wiki/Steamboat) were introduced to New Jersey.

[Iron](/wiki/Iron_ore) mining was also a leading industry during the middle to late 19th century. [Bog iron](/wiki/Bog_iron) pits in the [New Jersey Pine Barrens](/wiki/New_Jersey_Pine_Barrens) were among the first sources of iron for the new nation. Mines such as Mt. Hope, Mine Hill, and the Rockaway Valley Mines created a thriving industry. Mining generated the impetus for new towns and was one of the driving forces behind the need for the [Morris Canal](/wiki/Morris_Canal). [Zinc](/wiki/Zinc) mines were also a major industry, especially the [Sterling Hill Mine](/wiki/Sterling_Hill_Mine).

### 20th century

Main article: [New Jersey in the 20th century](/wiki/New_Jersey_in_the_20th_century)

New Jersey prospered through the [Roaring Twenties](/wiki/Roaring_Twenties). The first [Miss America Pageant](/wiki/Miss_America_Pageant) was held in 1921 in [Atlantic City](/wiki/Atlantic_City); the [Holland Tunnel](/wiki/Holland_Tunnel) connecting [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey) to [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan) opened in 1927; and the first [drive-in movie](/wiki/Drive-in_movie) was shown in 1933 in [Camden](/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey). During the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression) of the 1930s, the state offered begging licenses to unemployed residents, the zeppelin airship [Hindenburg](/wiki/Hindenburg_disaster) crashed in flames over [Lakehurst](/wiki/Lakehurst,_New_Jersey), and the [SS *Morro Castle*](/wiki/SS_Morro_Castle_(1930)) beached itself near [Asbury Park](/wiki/Asbury_Park,_New_Jersey) after going up in flames while at sea.

Through both [World Wars](/wiki/World_War), New Jersey was a center for war production, especially naval construction. The [Federal Shipbuilding and Drydock Company](/wiki/Federal_Shipbuilding_and_Drydock_Company) yards in Kearny and Newark and the [New York Shipbuilding Corporation](/wiki/New_York_Shipbuilding_Corporation) yard in Camden produced [aircraft carriers](/wiki/Aircraft_carrier), battleships, cruisers, and destroyers. New Jersey manufactured 6.8 percent of total United States military armaments produced during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), ranking fifth among the 48 states. In addition, [Fort Dix](/wiki/Fort_Dix,_New_Jersey) (1917) (originally called "Camp Dix"), [Camp Merritt](/wiki/Camp_Merritt_(New_Jersey)) (1917), and [Camp Kilmer](/wiki/Camp_Kilmer) (1941) were all constructed to house and train American soldiers through both World Wars. New Jersey also became a principal location for defense in the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War). Fourteen [Nike missile](/wiki/Project_Nike) stations were constructed for the defense of the New York City and [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia) areas. *[PT-109](/wiki/Motor_Torpedo_Boat_PT-109)*, a motor torpedo boat commanded by Lt. (j.g.) [John F. Kennedy](/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) in World War II, was built at the Elco Boatworks in [Bayonne](/wiki/Bayonne,_New_Jersey). The aircraft carrier [USS *Enterprise* (CV-6)](/wiki/USS_Enterprise_(CV-6)) was briefly docked at the Military Ocean Terminal in Bayonne in the 1950s before she was sent to Kearney to be scrapped. In 1962, the world's first nuclear-powered cargo ship, the [NS Savannah](/wiki/NS_Savannah), was launched at Camden.

In 1951, the [New Jersey Turnpike](/wiki/New_Jersey_Turnpike) opened, facilitating efficient travel by car and truck between [North Jersey](/wiki/North_Jersey) and the [New York metropolitan area](/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area), and [South Jersey](/wiki/South_Jersey) and the [Philadelphia metropolitan area](/wiki/Philadelphia_metropolitan_area). Subsequently, in 1957, the [Garden State Parkway](/wiki/Garden_State_Parkway) was completed, serving as a diagonal counterpart to the Turnpike, and opening up highway travel along New Jersey's coastal flank between [Bergen County](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey) in the northeast and the [Cape May County](/wiki/Cape_May_County,_New_Jersey) [peninsula](/wiki/Cape_May_peninsula) at the southeastern tip of New Jersey; in doing so, the [Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore) became readily accessible to millions of residents in the New York metropolitan area. In 1959, Air Defense Command deployed the [CIM-10 Bomarc](/wiki/CIM-10_Bomarc) [surface-to-air missile](/wiki/Surface-to-air_missile) to [McGuire Air Force Base](/wiki/McGuire_Air_Force_Base). On June 7, 1960, an [explosion](/wiki/BOMARC_Missile_Accident_Site) in a CIM-10 Bomarc missile fuel tank caused an accident and subsequent plutonium contamination.

In the 1960s, [race riots](/wiki/Race_riot) erupted in many of the industrial cities of [North Jersey](/wiki/North_Jersey). The first race riots in New Jersey occurred in Jersey City on August 2, 1964. Several others ensued in 1967, in [Newark](/wiki/1967_Newark_riots) and [Plainfield](/wiki/1967_Plainfield_riots). [Other riots](/wiki/King_assassination_riots) followed the [assassination of Martin Luther King Jr.](/wiki/Assassination_of_Martin_Luther_King_Jr.) in April 1968, just as in the rest of the country. A [riot](/wiki/Camden_Riot_of_1971) occurred in [Camden](/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey) in 1971. As a result of an order from the [New Jersey Supreme Court](/wiki/New_Jersey_Supreme_Court) to fund schools equitably, the New Jersey legislature passed an income tax bill in 1976. Prior to this bill, the state had no income tax.

### 21st century

Main article: [New Jersey in the 21st century](/wiki/New_Jersey_in_the_21st_century)

In the early part of the 2000s, two [light rail](/wiki/Light_rail) systems were opened: the [Hudson–Bergen Light Rail](/wiki/Hudson%E2%80%93Bergen_Light_Rail) (HBLR) in [Hudson County](/wiki/Hudson_County) and the [River Line](/wiki/River_Line_(NJ_Transit)) between [Camden](/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey) and [Trenton](/wiki/Trenton,_New_Jersey). The intent of these projects was to encourage [transit-oriented development](/wiki/Transit-oriented_development) in [North Jersey](/wiki/North_Jersey) and [South Jersey](/wiki/South_Jersey), respectively. The HBLR was credited with a revitalization of [Hudson County](/wiki/Hudson_County,_New_Jersey) and [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey). Urban revitalization has continued in North Jersey in the 21st century. In 2014, Jersey City's Census-estimated population was 262,146, with the largest population increase of any municipality in New Jersey since 2010, representing an increase of 5.9% from the [2010 U.S. census](/wiki/2010_United_States_census), when the city's population was enumerated at 247,597. In 2017, Jersey City's tax base grew by US$136 million, giving Jersey City the largest municipal tax base in New Jersey. Between [2000](/wiki/2000_United_States_census) and [2010](/wiki/2010_United_States_census) Newark experienced its first population increase since the 1950s, and by [2020](/wiki/2020_United_States_census) had rebounded to 311,549.

## Geography

Interactive map of New Jersey

Main articles: [Geography of New Jersey](/wiki/Geography_of_New_Jersey) and [Climate of New Jersey](/wiki/Climate_of_New_Jersey)

See also: [List of counties in New Jersey](/wiki/List_of_counties_in_New_Jersey) and [Metropolitan Statistical Areas of New Jersey](/wiki/Metropolitan_Statistical_Areas_of_New_Jersey)

Sunrise on the [Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore) at [Spring Lake](/wiki/Spring_Lake,_New_Jersey) in [Monmouth County](/wiki/Monmouth_County,_New_Jersey) (top) and sunset at [Sunset Beach](/wiki/Sunset_Beach,_New_Jersey) in [Cape May County](/wiki/Cape_May_County,_New_Jersey) (bottom)

New Jersey is at the center of the [Northeast megalopolis](/wiki/Northeast_megalopolis), the most populated American [urban agglomeration](/wiki/Urban_agglomeration). It is bordered on the north and northeast by [New York](/wiki/New_York_(state)) (parts of which are across the [Hudson River](/wiki/Hudson_River), [Upper New York Bay](/wiki/Upper_New_York_Bay), the [Kill Van Kull](/wiki/Kill_Van_Kull), [Newark Bay](/wiki/Newark_Bay), and the [Arthur Kill](/wiki/Arthur_Kill)); on the east by the Atlantic Ocean; on the southwest by [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware) across [Delaware Bay](/wiki/Delaware_Bay); and on the west by [Pennsylvania](/wiki/Pennsylvania) across the [Delaware River](/wiki/Delaware_River).

New Jersey is broadly divided into the [North](/wiki/North_Jersey), [Central](/wiki/Central_Jersey), and [South Jersey](/wiki/South_Jersey) geographic regions, although some residents do not consider Central Jersey a region in its own right. Across the regions are five distinct areas divided by natural geography and population concentration. Northeastern New Jersey, often referred to as the [Gateway Region](/wiki/Gateway_Region), lies closest to [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan) in New York City, and up to a million residents commute daily into the city for work, many via public transportation. The [Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore), along the Atlantic Coast in Central and South Jersey, has its own unique natural, residential, and cultural characteristics owing to its location by the ocean. South Jersey represents the southernmost geographical region of the [northeastern United States](/wiki/Northeastern_United_States). The [Philadelphia metropolitan area](/wiki/Philadelphia_metropolitan_area) includes the southwestern counties of the state, which reside within the Delaware Valley surrounding [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia).

Despite the state's heavily urban character and a long history of [industrialization](/wiki/Industrialization), forests cover roughly 45 percent of New Jersey's land area, or approximately 2.1 million acres (8,500 km2), [ranking 31st](/wiki/Forest_cover_by_state_and_territory_in_the_United_States) among the 50 U.S. states and six territories. Northwestern New Jersey, often referred to as the [Skylands Region](/wiki/Skylands_Region), is more wooded, rural, and mountainous. The chief tree of the northern forests is the [oak](/wiki/Oak). The [New Jersey Pine Barrens](/wiki/New_Jersey_Pine_Barrens) is situated in the southern interior of New Jersey and covered extensively by mixed [pine](/wiki/Pine) and [oak](/wiki/Oak) forest; its population density is lower than most of the state.

[High Point](/wiki/High_Point_(New_Jersey)) in [Montague Township](/wiki/Montague_Township,_New_Jersey), [Sussex County](/wiki/Sussex_County,_New_Jersey) is the state's highest elevation at 1,803 feet (550 m) above sea level. The state's highest [prominence](/wiki/Prominence_(mountains)) is [Kitty Ann Mountain](/wiki/Kitty_Ann_Mountain) in [Morris County](/wiki/Morris_County,_New_Jersey), rising 892 feet (272 m). The [Palisades](/wiki/The_Palisades_(Hudson_River)) are a line of steep cliffs on the west side of the [Hudson River](/wiki/Hudson_River) in [Bergen](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey) and [Hudson Counties](/wiki/Hudson_County,_New_Jersey). Major [New Jersey rivers](/wiki/List_of_New_Jersey_rivers) include the [Hudson](/wiki/Hudson_River), [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware_River), [Raritan](/wiki/Raritan_River), [Passaic](/wiki/Passaic_River), [Hackensack](/wiki/Hackensack_River), [Rahway](/wiki/Rahway_River), [Musconetcong](/wiki/Musconetcong_River), [Mullica](/wiki/Mullica_River), [Rancocas](/wiki/Rancocas_River), [Manasquan](/wiki/Manasquan_River), [Maurice](/wiki/Maurice_River), and [Toms](/wiki/Toms_River) rivers. Due to New Jersey's [peninsular](/wiki/Peninsula) geography, both sunrise and sunset are visible over water from different points on the Jersey Shore.

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of New Jersey](/wiki/Climate_of_New_Jersey)

The state consists of two climate zones; most of the state has a [humid subtropical climate](/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) (Cfa), while the northwest has a [humid continental climate](/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) (Dfa). New Jersey receives between 2,400 and 2,800 hours of [sunshine](/wiki/Sunshine) annually.

Summers are typically hot and humid, with statewide average high temperatures of 82–87 °F (28–31 °C) and lows of 60–69 °F (16–21 °C); however, temperatures exceed 90 °F (32 °C) on average 25 days each summer, exceeding 100 °F (38 °C) in some years. Winters are usually cold, with average high temperatures of 34–43 °F (1–6 °C) and lows of 16 to 28 °F (−9 to −2 °C) for most of the state, but temperatures can, for brief periods, fall below 10 °F (−12 °C) and sometimes rise above 50 °F (10 °C). Northwestern parts of the state have significantly colder winters with sub-0 °F (−18 °C) being an almost annual occurrence. Spring and autumn may feature wide temperature variations, with lower humidity than summer. The [USDA Plant Hardiness Zone](/wiki/Hardiness_zone) classification ranges from 6 in the northwest of the state, to 7B near Cape May. All-time temperature extremes recorded in New Jersey include 110 °F (43 °C) on July 10, 1936, in [Runyon](/wiki/Runyon,_New_Jersey), Middlesex County and −34 °F (−37 °C) on January 5, 1904, in [River Vale](/wiki/River_Vale,_New_Jersey), Bergen County.

Average annual precipitation ranges from 43 to 51 inches (1,100 to 1,300 mm), spread uniformly throughout the year. Average snowfall per winter season ranges from 10–15 inches (25–38 cm) in the south and near the seacoast, 15–30 inches (38–76 cm) in the northeast and central part of the state, to about 40–50 inches (1.0–1.3 m) in the northwestern highlands, but this often varies considerably from year to year. Precipitation falls on an average of 120 days a year, with 25 to 30 thunderstorms, most of which occur during the summer.

During winter and early spring, New Jersey can experience [nor'easters](/wiki/Nor%27easter), which are capable of causing [blizzards](/wiki/Blizzards) or flooding throughout the northeastern United States. [Hurricanes](/wiki/Hurricane) and [tropical storms](/wiki/Tropical_storm), [tornadoes](/wiki/Tornado), and earthquakes are rare; the state was impacted by a hurricane in [1903](/wiki/1903_New_Jersey_hurricane), Tropical Storm [Floyd](/wiki/Hurricane_Floyd) in [1999](/wiki/1999_Atlantic_hurricane_season), and [Hurricane Sandy](/wiki/Hurricane_Sandy) in [2012](/wiki/2012_Atlantic_hurricane_season), which made landfall in the state with top winds of 90 mph (145 km/h).

#### Climate change

[Climate change is affecting New Jersey](/wiki/Climate_change_in_New_Jersey) faster than much of the rest of the United States. [Climatologists](/wiki/Climatologist) at the U.S. [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration](/wiki/National_Oceanic_and_Atmospheric_Administration) have concluded that New Jersey has been the fastest-warming state by average air temperature over a 100-year period beginning in the early 20th century.

#### Municipalities

For its overall population and nation-leading population density, New Jersey is primarily [suburban](/wiki/Suburban) and has a relative paucity of classically large cities. This [paradox](/wiki/Paradox) is most pronounced in [Bergen County](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey), the state's most populous county, whose 955,732 residents at the 2020 census inhabited 70 municipalities, of which the most populous is [Hackensack](/wiki/Hackensack,_New_Jersey), with 46,030 residents. Many urban areas extend far beyond the limits of a single large city, as New Jersey municipalities tend to be geographically small; three of the four largest cities in New Jersey by population have under 20 square miles (52 km2) of land area, and eight of the top ten, including all the top five, have a land area under 30 square miles (78 km2). As of the [2010 United States census](/wiki/2010_United_States_census), only four municipalities had over 100,000 residents (although Edison and Woodbridge Township came very close); this number increased to seven by the 2020 census.

Residents of New Jersey are most commonly referred to as New Jerseyans or, less commonly, as New Jerseyites. According to the [2020 U.S. census](/wiki/2020_United_States_census), the state had a population of 9,288,994, a 5.7% increase since the [2010 U.S. census](/wiki/2010_United_States_census), which counted 8,791,894 residents. The state ranked eleventh in the country by total population and first in population density, with 1,185 residents per square mile (458 per km2). Historically, New Jersey has experienced one of the fastest growth rates in the country, with its population increasing by double digits almost every decade until 1980; growth has since slowed but remained relatively robust until recently. In 2022, the Census Bureau estimated there were 6,262 fewer residents than in 2020, a decline of 0.3% from 2020, related to the [COVID-19 pandemic](/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic). In 2024, the Census Bureau reported that the state population rebounded by 2.3% or 211,837 residents from April 2020. New Jersey lead the [Northeast](/wiki/Northeastern_United_States) with a 1.3% growth rate or 121,209 residents from 2023 placing it among the top ten states nationwide for population growth.

New Jersey is the only state where every county is deemed [urban](/wiki/Urban_area) as defined by the [Census Bureau](/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau). Most residents live in the counties surrounding [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City), the nation's largest city, [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia), the nation's sixth-largest city, or along the eastern [Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore); the extreme southern and northwestern counties are relatively less dense overall. Since the 2000 census, the [United States Census Bureau](/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau) calculated that New Jersey's [center of population](/wiki/Center_of_population) was located in [East Brunswick](/wiki/East_Brunswick,_New_Jersey). The state is located in the middle of the [Northeast megalopolis](/wiki/Northeast_megalopolis), which has more than 50 million residents.

As of 2023, New Jersey had a [median household income](/wiki/Median_income) of $99,781, the [second-highest](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_income) of any U.S. state behind [Massachusetts](/wiki/Massachusetts). Conversely, New Jersey's poverty rate of 9.4% was slightly lower than the national average of 11.4%, and the [sixth lowest of the fifty states, Washington, D.C., and Puerto Rico](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_poverty_rate). This is attributed to several factors, including the state's proximity to the major economic centers of New York City and [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia), its hosting the highest number of [millionaires](/wiki/Millionaire) both per capita and per square mile in the U.S., and the fact that it has the most [scientists](/wiki/Scientist) and [engineers](/wiki/Engineer) per square mile in the world.

According to the [U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Housing_and_Urban_Development)'s 2022 [Annual Homeless Assessment Report](/wiki/Annual_Homeless_Assessment_Report_to_Congress), there were an estimated 8,752 [homeless](/wiki/Homelessness) people in New Jersey. The top countries of origin for New Jersey's immigrants in 2018 were India, [Dominican Republic](/wiki/Dominican_Republic), [Mexico](/wiki/Mexico), [Ecuador](/wiki/Ecuador), and the [Philippines](/wiki/Filipinos_in_New_Jersey).

Compared to the U.S. as a whole, the state is more racially and ethnically diverse and has a higher proportion of immigrants.

New Jersey is one of the most ethnically and religiously [diverse](/wiki/Polyethnicity) states in the United States. As of 2024, a quarter of New Jerseyans (25%) were [foreign born](/wiki/Foreign_born), the second most after [California](/wiki/California), compared to the national average of 14.8%. As of 2011, 56.4% of New Jersey's children under the age of one belonged to racial or ethnic minority groups, meaning that they had at least one parent who was not non-Hispanic white. The 2019 Vintage Year Census estimated that the state's ethnic makeup was as follows: 71.9% White alone, 15.1% Black or African American alone, 10.0% Asian alone, 0.6% American Indian and Alaska Native alone, 0.1% Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander alone, and 2.3% Two or more races. Hispanic or Latino accounted for 20.9%, while White alone (non-Hispanic or Latino) accounted for 54.6% of the population. Many of the municipalities in [Bergen County, New Jersey](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey), the state's largest county, have a sizeable minority population of Hispanics and Asians. There are also three [state-recognized tribes](/wiki/State-recognized_tribes_in_the_United_States), and in 2020, 51,186 identified as being Native American alone, while 96,691 did in combination with one or more other races.

New Jersey hosts some of the nation's largest communities of religious and ethnic minorities in proportional or absolute terms. It has the second-largest [Jewish](/wiki/Jewish_American) population by percentage (after New York); the largest [Hindu](/wiki/Hindu) and [Muslim](/wiki/Islam_in_the_United_States) populations by percentage; the largest population of [Peruvians](/wiki/Peruvian_American) in the U.S.; the largest population of [Cubans](/wiki/Cuban_Americans) outside [Florida](/wiki/Florida); the third-highest [Asian](/wiki/Asia) population by percentage; and the second-highest [Italian](/wiki/Italian_Americans_in_New_York_City) population, according to the [2000 census](/wiki/2000_United_States_census). African Americans, [Hispanics](/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans) ([Puerto Ricans](/wiki/Puerto_Rican_American) and [Dominicans](/wiki/Dominican_American)), [West Indians](/wiki/West_Indian_Americans), [Arabs](/wiki/Arab_American), and [Brazilian](/wiki/Brazilian_people) and [Portuguese Americans](/wiki/Portuguese_Americans) are also high in number. New Jersey also has the fourth-largest [Filipino](/wiki/Filipino_American) population, and fourth-largest [Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_American) population, per the 2010 U.S. Census.

New Jersey has the-third highest [Indian](/wiki/Indian_American#List_of_U.S._States_by_population_of_Asian_Indians) population of any state by absolute numbers and the [highest by percentage](/wiki/Indians_in_the_New_York_metropolitan_area), with [India Square](/wiki/India_Square) in [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey), [Hudson County](/wiki/Hudson_County,_New_Jersey) hosting the highest concentration of Asian Indians in the [Western Hemisphere](/wiki/Western_Hemisphere). A study by the [Pew Research Center](/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) found that in 2013, New Jersey was the only U.S. state in which immigrants born in [India](/wiki/India) constituted the largest [foreign-born](/wiki/Foreign_born) nationality, representing roughly 10% of all foreign-born residents in the state. [Central New Jersey](/wiki/Central_New_Jersey), particularly [Edison](/wiki/Edison_Township,_New_Jersey) and surrounding [Middlesex County](/wiki/Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey), has the highest concentration of Indians, at nearly 20% in 2020; [Little India](/wiki/Little_India_(Edison/Iselin)) is the largest and most diverse [South Asian](/wiki/South_Asian) cultural hub in the United States. The area includes a sprawling [Chinatown](/wiki/Chinese_in_New_York_City) and [Koreatown](/wiki/Korean_Americans_in_New_York_City) running along [New Jersey Route 27](/wiki/New_Jersey_Route_27). [Monroe Township](/wiki/Monroe_Township,_Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey) in Middlesex County has experienced a particularly rapid growth rate in its [Indian American](/wiki/Indians_in_the_New_York_City_metropolitan_region) population with an estimated 5,943 (13.6%) as of 2017, which was 23 times the 256 (0.9%) counted at the 2000 Census; [Diwali](/wiki/Diwali) is celebrated by the township as a [Hindu](/wiki/Hinduism) holiday. In Middlesex County, election [ballots](/wiki/Ballot) are printed in English, [Spanish](/wiki/Spanish_language), [Gujarati](/wiki/Gujarati_language), [Hindi](/wiki/Hindi), and [Punjabi](/wiki/Punjabi_language). [Robbinsville](/wiki/Robbinsville,_New_Jersey), in neighboring Mercer County, hosts [the world's largest](/wiki/Swaminarayan_Akshardham_(North_America)) [Hindu temple](/wiki/Hindu_temple) outside Asia. [Carteret](/wiki/Carteret,_New_Jersey)'s [Punjabi](/wiki/Punjabi_people) [Sikh](/wiki/Sikh) community, variously estimated at upwards of 3,000, is the largest concentration of Sikhs in the state. [Bergen County](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey) is home to America's largest [Malayali](/wiki/Malayali) community.

New Jersey also has the third-largest [Korean](/wiki/Korean_American) population, with Bergen County home to the highest Korean concentration per capita of any U.S. county (6.9% in 2011). It is a growing hub and home to [all of the nation's top ten municipalities by percentage of Korean population](/wiki/List_of_U.S._cities_with_significant_Korean-American_populations#Top_ten_municipalities_as_ranked_by_Korean-American_percentage_of_overall_population_in_2010), led by [Palisades Park (벼랑 공원)](/wiki/Koreatown,_Palisades_Park), the municipality with the highest [density](/wiki/List_of_U.S._cities_with_significant_Korean-American_populations#Municipalities_with_density_of_at_least_500_Korean_Americans_per_square_mile_in_2010) of ethnic [Koreans](/wiki/Koreans) in the [Western Hemisphere](/wiki/Western_Hemisphere). Displaying ubiquitous [Hangul (한글)](/wiki/Hangul) signage and known as the *Korean village*, Palisades Park uniquely comprises a Korean majority (52% in 2010) of its population, with both the [highest Korean-American density and percentage](/wiki/List_of_U.S._cities_with_significant_Korean-American_populations#Municipalities_with_density_of_at_least_500_Korean_Americans_per_square_mile_in_2010) of any municipality in the United States.

### Sexual orientation and gender identity

Further information: [LGBT culture in New York City](/wiki/LGBT_culture_in_New_York_City) and [LGBT culture in Philadelphia](/wiki/LGBT_culture_in_Philadelphia)

New Jersey is widely regarded as an [LGBTQ+ friendly](/wiki/Gay-friendly) state and is now home to more [gay villages](/wiki/List_of_gay_villages#United_States) per square mile than any other U.S. state. [Same-sex marriage in New Jersey](/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_New_Jersey) has been legally recognized since October 21, 2013, the effective date of a trial court ruling invalidating New Jersey's restriction of [marriage](/wiki/Marriage) to persons of different sexes at the time. In September 2013, Mary C. Jacobson, Assignment Judge of the [Mercer Vicinage](/wiki/Mercer_County,_New_Jersey) of the [Superior Court](/wiki/New_Jersey_Superior_Court), ruled that as a result of the [U.S. Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States)'s June 2013 decision in *[United States v. Windsor](/wiki/United_States_v._Windsor)*, the [Constitution of New Jersey](/wiki/Constitution_of_New_Jersey) requires the state to recognize [same-sex marriages](/wiki/Same-sex_marriage).

Numerous [gayborhoods](/wiki/Gayborhood) have emerged in New Jersey, most prominently in [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City), [Asbury Park](/wiki/Asbury_Park,_New_Jersey), [Maplewood](/wiki/Maplewood,_New_Jersey), [Montclair](/wiki/Montclair,_New_Jersey), and [Lambertville](/wiki/Lambertville,_New_Jersey). [Trenton](/wiki/Trenton,_New_Jersey), the [state capital](/wiki/List_of_U.S._state_capitals) of New Jersey, elected [Reed Gusciora](/wiki/Reed_Gusciora), its first openly gay mayor, in 2018, and Jennifer Williams, New Jersey's first openly [transgender](/wiki/Transgender) city councilmember, in 2022. In June 2018, Maplewood, [Essex County](/wiki/Essex_County,_New_Jersey) unveiled permanent [rainbow-colored crosswalks](/wiki/Rainbow_crossing) to celebrate LGBTQ pride. [Rahway](/wiki/Rahway,_New_Jersey), [Union County](/wiki/Union_County,_New_Jersey), also unveiled its own rainbow-colored crosswalks in June 2019. In January 2019, [New Jersey Governor](/wiki/Governor_of_New_Jersey) [Phil Murphy](/wiki/Phil_Murphy) signed legislation mandating LGBTQ+ inclusive [educational curriculum](/wiki/Curriculum) in schools. In February 2019, New Jersey began allowing a [neutral or non-binary gender](/wiki/Gender_neutrality) choice on [birth certificates](/wiki/Birth_certificate).

## Education

See also: [New Jersey Department of Education](/wiki/New_Jersey_Department_of_Education), [Post-secondary education in New Jersey](/wiki/Post-secondary_education_in_New_Jersey), [List of high schools in New Jersey](/wiki/List_of_high_schools_in_New_Jersey), and [List of school districts in New Jersey](/wiki/List_of_school_districts_in_New_Jersey)

[Old Queens](/wiki/Old_Queens) at [Rutgers University](/wiki/Rutgers_University), the largest state university system in New Jersey

[Cleveland Tower](/wiki/Cleveland_Tower) at [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University), an [Ivy League](/wiki/Ivy_League) university and one of the world's most prominent research institutions, served briefly as the U.S. Capitol in the 18th century.

As of the 2020–2021 school year, there were 686 operating [districts in the state](/wiki/List_of_school_districts_in_New_Jersey). Of these, 599 were [traditional public school](/wiki/Education_in_the_United_States) districts and 87 were [charter school](/wiki/Charter_schools_in_the_United_States) districts. The [NJDOE](/wiki/New_Jersey_Department_of_Education) reported a total district enrollment of 1,362,400 students, the lowest total enrollment since the early 2000s, though these figures do not consider [homeschooled](/wiki/Homeschooling) students or those attending [out-of-state schools](/wiki/Boarding_school). New Jersey public schools emphasize [STEM](/wiki/STEM) subjects, and New Jersey is home to more scientists and engineers per square mile than anywhere else in the world.

### Educational standards

New Jersey is known for the high quality of its education. In 2024, New Jersey spent the second-most per public school student among all states, behind [New York](/wiki/New_York_(state)), amounting to $26,600 spent per pupil. In 2018, over 50% of the expenditure was allocated to student instruction.

New Jersey is home to several prestigious private universities, including [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University), one of the world's most prominent [research universities](/wiki/Research_university), which was ranked first for the tenth consecutive year among all national universities by *[U.S. News & World Report](/wiki/U.S._News_%26_World_Report)* in 2025 and has ranked first in comparable lists published by *[Forbes](/wiki/Forbes)* and *[The Wall Street Journal](/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal)*, and public universities such as [Rutgers University](/wiki/Rutgers_University), headquartered in [New Brunswick](/wiki/New_Brunswick,_New_Jersey), [Middlesex County](/wiki/Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey), the state's flagship institution of higher education.

In 2019, 2020, and 2021, *Education Week* also ranked New Jersey public schools the best of all U.S. states.

In 2016, nine New Jersey-based [high schools](/wiki/Secondary_education_in_the_United_States) were ranked among the top 25 in the nation, more than any other state, in "America's Top High Schools 2016", published by *[Newsweek](/wiki/Newsweek)*.

In November 2023, New Jersey Governor [Phil Murphy](/wiki/Phil_Murphy) signed into law legislation eliminating testing for prospective teachers in reading, writing, and math, replacing it with an alternative certification process.

## Economy

See also: [New Jersey locations by per capita income](/wiki/New_Jersey_locations_by_per_capita_income)

The [U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis](/wiki/U.S._Bureau_of_Economic_Analysis) estimates that New Jersey's [gross state product](/wiki/Gross_state_product) in the third quarter of 2022 was $753 billion.

### Affluence

A heat map showing median income distribution in New Jersey by county

New Jersey's [per capita gross state product](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_GDP_per_capita_(nominal)) routinely ranks among the highest in the nation. In 2020, New Jersey had more [millionaires](/wiki/Millionaire) both per capita and per square mile, representing roughly 9.76% of all New Jersey household, than any state in the nation.

The state is ranked second in the nation by the number of places with per capita incomes exceeding the national average with 76.4%. Three of New Jersey's counties are among the 20 highest income U.S. counties in the nation as of 2023.

As of May 2025, the state's [unemployment](/wiki/Unemployment) rate was 4.8%.

### Fiscal policy

New Jersey has seven [tax brackets](/wiki/Tax_bracket) that determine [state income tax](/wiki/State_income_tax) rates, which range from 1.4% (for income below $20,000) to 10.75% (for income above $1,000,000).

The standard [sales tax](/wiki/Sales_tax) rate as of January 1, 2018, is 6.625%, applicable to all retail sales unless specifically exempt by law. This rate, which is comparably lower than that of New York City, often attracts numerous shoppers from New York City, often to suburban [Paramus](/wiki/Paramus), New Jersey, which has five malls, one of which (the [Garden State Plaza](/wiki/Garden_State_Plaza)) has over 2 million square feet (200,000 m2) of retail space. [Tax exemptions](/wiki/Tax_exemption) include most food items for at-home preparation, medications, most clothing, footwear and disposable paper products for use in the home. There are 27 [Urban Enterprise Zone](/wiki/Urban_Enterprise_Zone) statewide, including sections of [Paterson](/wiki/Paterson,_New_Jersey), [Elizabeth](/wiki/Elizabeth,_New_Jersey), and [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey). In addition to other benefits to encourage employment within the zone, shoppers can take advantage of a reduced 3.3125% [sales tax](/wiki/Sales_tax) rate (half the rate charged statewide) at eligible merchants.

New Jersey has the highest cumulative tax rate of all 50 states with residents paying a total of $68 billion in state and local taxes annually with a per capita burden of $7,816 at a rate of 12.9% of income. All [real property](/wiki/Real_property) located in the state is subject to [property tax](/wiki/Property_tax) unless specifically exempted by statute. New Jersey does not assess an intangible personal property tax or an [estate tax](/wiki/Estate_tax_in_the_United_States), but it does impose an [inheritance tax](/wiki/Inheritance_tax) (which is levied only on heirs who are not direct descendants). In 2023, Governor [Phil Murphy](/wiki/Phil_Murphy) signed into law a new tax-relief program known as StayNJ that will provide for an annual property-tax cut of 50% for those aged 65 and over with incomes below $500,000; the cut will go into effect in January 2026 and be capped at $6,500, but with this cap rising as indexed to the increase in New Jersey's overall property taxes.

### Federal taxation disparity

New Jersey consistently ranks as having one of the highest proportional levels of disparity of any state in the U.S., based upon what it receives from the [federal government](/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States) relative to what it pays. In 2015, [WalletHub](/wiki/WalletHub) ranked New Jersey the state that was least dependent on federal government aid overall and the state with the fourth-lowest return on taxpayer investment from the federal government, at 48 cents per dollar. In 2023, New Jersey sent approximately $183 billion to [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.) in taxes but only received approximately $113 billion in federal investment resulting in each state resident sending the federal government approximately $7,500 more than they get back. In 2025, WalletHub again ranked New Jersey as the least dependent on federal government aid overall with now the second-lowest return on taxpayer spending from the federal government, at 48 cents per dollar.

New Jersey has one of the highest tax burdens of any state the nation. Factors for this include the large federal tax liability, which is not adjusted for New Jersey's higher cost of living and [Medicaid](/wiki/Medicaid) funding formulas.

### Industries

Further information: [Biotech and pharmaceutical companies in New Jersey](/wiki/Biotech_and_pharmaceutical_companies_in_New_Jersey)

[Cranberry](/wiki/Cranberry) harvest

New Jersey's economy is multifaceted, featuring high levels of both [productivity](/wiki/Productivity) and [retail consumption](/wiki/Retail); the Garden State's economy comprises the [pharmaceutical industry](/wiki/Pharmaceutical_industry), [biotechnology](/wiki/Biotechnology), [information technology](/wiki/Information_technology), the [financial industry](/wiki/Financial_industry), [tourism](/wiki/Tourism), [filmmaking](/wiki/Filmmaking), [telecommunications](/wiki/Telecommunications), [gambling](/wiki/Gambling), food processing, electrical equipment manufacturing, printing, and publishing. New Jersey's agricultural outputs are nursery stock, horses, vegetables, fruits and nuts, seafood, and dairy products. New Jersey ranks second among states in [blueberry](/wiki/Blueberry) production, third in [cranberries](/wiki/Cranberry) and [spinach](/wiki/Spinach), and fourth in [bell peppers](/wiki/Bell_peppers), [peaches](/wiki/Peach), and [head lettuce](/wiki/Lettuce). The state harvests the fourth-largest number of acres planted with [asparagus](/wiki/Asparagus). [South Jersey](/wiki/South_Jersey) has become an [East Coast epicenter](/wiki/East_Coast_of_the_United_States) for [logistics](/wiki/Logistics) and [warehouse](/wiki/Warehouse) construction. In 2025, 99.7% of businesses in New Jersey were [small businesses](/wiki/Small_business), employing 48.8% of the state's workforce.

#### Scientific economy

New Jersey has a strong [scientific economy](/wiki/Scientist) and is home to major pharmaceutical and telecommunications firms, drawing on the state's large and well-educated labor pool, including one of the highest concentrations of [engineers](/wiki/Engineer) and other scientists in the world. There is also a robust service economy in retail sales, education, and real estate, serving residents who work in New York City or Philadelphia. [Thomas Edison](/wiki/Thomas_Edison) invented the first [electric light bulb](/wiki/Electric_light_bulb) at his home in [Menlo Park, Edison](/wiki/Menlo_Park,_New_Jersey) in 1879. New Jersey is also a key participant in the [renewable wind industry](/wiki/Wind_industry). New Jersey has more scientists and engineers per square mile than anywhere in the world, and is a global leader in [pharmaceuticals](/wiki/Pharmaceuticals), [biotechnology](/wiki/Biotechnology), [life sciences](/wiki/Life_sciences), and [technology](/wiki/Technology).

#### Corporate and retail

New Jersey hosts numerous business headquarters, including twenty-four [Fortune 500](/wiki/Fortune_500) companies. [Paramus](/wiki/Paramus,_New_Jersey) in [Bergen County](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey) has become the top retail [ZIP code](/wiki/ZIP_code) (07652) in the United States, with the municipality generating over US$6 billion in annual retail sales. Several New Jersey counties, including [Somerset](/wiki/Somerset_County,_New_Jersey) (7), [Morris](/wiki/Morris_County,_New_Jersey) (10), [Hunterdon](/wiki/Hunterdon_County,_New_Jersey) (13), Bergen (21), and [Monmouth](/wiki/Monmouth_County,_New_Jersey) (42), have been ranked among the [highest-income counties in the United States](/wiki/Highest-income_counties_in_the_United_States).

#### Shipping, manufacturing, and logistics

The [Port Newark-Elizabeth Marine Terminal](/wiki/Port_Newark-Elizabeth_Marine_Terminal) was the world's first [container seaport](/wiki/Container_port) and remains one of the world's largest and busiest.

Shipping is a key industry in New Jersey because of the state's strategic geographic location, the [Port of New York and New Jersey](/wiki/Port_of_New_York_and_New_Jersey) being the busiest [seaport](/wiki/Seaport) on the [U.S. East Coast](/wiki/U.S._East_Coast). The [Port Newark-Elizabeth Marine Terminal](/wiki/Port_Newark-Elizabeth_Marine_Terminal) was the world's first [container port](/wiki/Container_port) and today is one of the world's largest and busiest. New Jersey's location at the center of the [Eastern North American](/wiki/Eastern_North_America) population belt has made the state a prime hub for the logistics, warehousing, and [supply chain](/wiki/Supply_chain) management industries. The [manufacturing](/wiki/Manufacturing) economy in New Jersey had declined for several decades in the post-[Industrial Revolution](/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) era but has since resumed growth.

#### Tourism

[Sunset](/wiki/Sunset) on the beach at [Atlantic City](/wiki/Atlantic_City,_New_Jersey), an [oceanfront resort](/wiki/Seaside_resort) and the nexus of New Jersey's [gambling industry](/wiki/Casino)

New Jersey's location at the center of the [Northeast megalopolis](/wiki/Northeast_megalopolis) and its extensive transportation system have put over one-third of all United States residents and many Canadian residents within overnight distance by land. This accessibility to consumer revenue has enabled [seaside resorts](/wiki/Seaside_resort) such as [Atlantic City](/wiki/Atlantic_City,_New_Jersey) and the remainder of the [Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore), as well as the state's other natural and cultural attractions, to contribute significantly to the record 111 million tourist visits to New Jersey in 2018, providing US$44.7 billion in tourism revenue, directly supporting 333,860 jobs, sustaining more than 531,000 jobs overall including peripheral impacts, and generating US$5 billion in state and local tax revenue.

#### Gambling

Main article: [Gambling in New Jersey](/wiki/Gambling_in_New_Jersey)

In 1976, a [referendum](/wiki/Referendum) by Jersey voters approved [casino](/wiki/Casino) gambling in Atlantic City, where the first legalized casino opened in 1978. At that time, [Las Vegas](/wiki/Las_Vegas_Valley) was the only other casino resort in the country. Today, several casinos lie along the [Atlantic City Boardwalk](/wiki/Atlantic_City_Boardwalk), the oldest and longest boardwalk in the world, at 5+1⁄2 miles (8.9 km) in length. Atlantic City experienced a dramatic contraction in its stature as a gambling destination after 2010, including the closure of multiple casinos since 2014, spurred by competition from the advent of legalized gambling in other northeastern U.S. states.

On February 26, 2013, Governor Chris Christie signed [online gambling](/wiki/Online_gambling) into law. [Sports betting](/wiki/Sports_betting) has become a growing source of gambling revenue in New Jersey, with sportsbooks bringing in almost $12 billion in bets, making over $1 billion in revenue in 2023. Since being legalized across the nation by the [U.S. Supreme Court](/wiki/U.S._Supreme_Court) on May 14, 2018, New Jersey led all states in sports betting handle until New York passed them. In September 2022, the lifetime revenue from [online casinos](/wiki/Online_casinos) operating in New Jersey for the nine years since the industry's launch had surpassed $5 billion.

##### Television and film production

Main article: [Television and film of New Jersey](/wiki/Television_and_film_of_New_Jersey)

New Jersey is a growing center for [filmmaking](/wiki/Filmmaking) and [television production](/wiki/Television_production), with [media companies](/wiki/Digital_media), enticed by its proximity to [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan), in conjunction with [tax incentives](/wiki/Tax_incentive), collectively spending billions of dollars to develop large new [studio facilities](/wiki/Film_studio) and [sound stage](/wiki/Sound_stage) complexes. Motion picture technology was developed by [Thomas Edison](/wiki/Thomas_Edison), with much of his early work done at his [West Orange](/wiki/West_Orange,_New_Jersey) laboratory. [Edison's Black Maria](/wiki/Edison%27s_Black_Maria) was the first motion picture studio. [America's first motion picture industry](/wiki/America%27s_first_motion_picture_industry) started in 1907 in [Fort Lee](/wiki/Fort_Lee,_New_Jersey) and the first studio was constructed there in 1909. [DuMont Laboratories](/wiki/DuMont_Laboratories) in [Passaic](/wiki/Passaic,_New_Jersey) developed early sets and made the first broadcast to the private home.

A number of television shows and films have been filmed in New Jersey. Since 1978, the state has maintained a Motion Picture and Television Commission to encourage filming in-state. New Jersey has long offered tax credits to television producers. Governor Chris Christie suspended the credits in 2010, but the [New Jersey State Legislature](/wiki/New_Jersey_State_Legislature) in 2011 approved the restoration and expansion of the tax credit program. Under bills passed by both the state Senate and Assembly, the program offers 20 percent tax credits (22% in [urban enterprise zones](/wiki/Urban_enterprise_zone)) to television and film productions that shoot in the state and meet set standards for hiring and local spending. When Governor Phil Murphy took office, he instated the New Jersey Film & Digital Media Tax Credit Program in 2018 and expanded it in 2020. The benefits include a 30% tax credit on film projects and a 40% subsidy for studio developments.

#### Finance as Wall Street West

The developing waterfront skyline of [Downtown Jersey City](/wiki/Downtown_Jersey_City), dubbed [Wall Street West](/wiki/Wall_Street_West)

[Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City)'s [Hudson River waterfront](/wiki/Downtown_Jersey_City), from [Exchange Place](/wiki/Exchange_Place_(Jersey_City)) to [Newport](/wiki/Newport,_Jersey_City), is known as [Wall Street West](/wiki/Wall_Street_West) and has over 13 million square feet of [Class A office space](/wiki/Class_A_office). One third of the [private sector](/wiki/Private_sector) jobs in the city are in the [financial services](/wiki/Finance) sector: more than 60% are in the [securities industry](/wiki/Security_(finance)), 20% are in banking and 8% in [insurance](/wiki/Insurance). Jersey City is home to the headquarters of [Verisk Analytics](/wiki/Verisk_Analytics) and [Lord Abbett](/wiki/Lord_Abbett), a privately held money management firm. Companies such as [Computershare](/wiki/Computershare), [ADP](/wiki/Automatic_Data_Processing), [IPC Systems](/wiki/IPC_Systems), and [Fidelity Investments](/wiki/Fidelity_Investments) also conduct operations in the city. In 2014, *[Forbes](/wiki/Forbes)* magazine moved its headquarters to the district, having been awarded a $27 million tax grant in exchange for bringing 350 jobs to the city over a ten-year period. By the early 2020s, the construction of residential [skyscrapers](/wiki/Skyscraper) Downtown made median rental rates in Jersey City amongst the highest of any city in the United States.

#### Natural resources and energy

See also: [List of power stations in New Jersey](/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_New_Jersey)

Limited mining activity of [zinc](/wiki/Zinc), iron, and [manganese](/wiki/Manganese) still takes place in the area in and around the [Franklin Furnace](/wiki/Franklin_Furnace) in [Sussex County](/wiki/Sussex_County,_New_Jersey).

Although New Jersey is home to many energy-intensive industries, its energy consumption is only 2.7% of the U.S. total, and its [carbon dioxide emissions](/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_emissions) are 0.8% of the U.S. total. New Jersey's electricity comes primarily from natural gas and nuclear power. New Jersey is seventh in the nation in [solar power](/wiki/Solar_power_in_New_Jersey) installations, enabled by one of the country's most favorable [net metering](/wiki/Net_metering) policies and [renewable portfolio standard](/wiki/Renewable_portfolio_standards_in_the_United_States). The state has more than 140,000 solar installations.

## Environment

Due to past industrial activity, New Jersey has more [Superfund](/wiki/Superfund) toxic waste sites than any other state in the union despite its small geographic size. By 2024, only 35 of New Jersey's Superfund sites (out of about 150 that have been on the EPA's list since the Superfund law was passed in 1980) have been cleaned up.

In late 2023, a concern became public about [PFAs](/wiki/Per-_and_polyfluoroalkyl_substances) (so-called "forever chemicals") existing in the state's water supplies.

## Transportation

Main article: [Transportation in New Jersey](/wiki/Transportation_in_New_Jersey)

New Jersey's population density and location at the geographic center of the [Northeast Megalopolis](/wiki/Northeast_Megalopolis) have rendered it a vital transportation for hub for both passengers and industry.

### Roadways

See also: [State highways in New Jersey](/wiki/State_highways_in_New_Jersey)

Map of New Jersey's major transportation networks and cities

The [George Washington Bridge](/wiki/George_Washington_Bridge), connecting [Fort Lee](/wiki/Fort_Lee,_New_Jersey) (foreground) in [Bergen County](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey) across the [Hudson River](/wiki/Hudson_River) to [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City) (background), is the world's busiest motor vehicle bridge.

The [New Jersey Turnpike](/wiki/New_Jersey_Turnpike) is one of the most prominent and heavily trafficked roadways in the United States. This [toll road](/wiki/Toll_road), which overlaps with [Interstate 95](/wiki/Interstate_95_in_New_Jersey) for much of its length, carries traffic between [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware) and New York, and up and down the [East Coast](/wiki/East_Coast_of_the_United_States) in general. Commonly referred to as simply "the Turnpike", it is known for its numerous [rest areas](/wiki/Rest_area) named after prominent New Jerseyans.

The [Garden State Parkway](/wiki/Garden_State_Parkway), or simply "the Parkway", carries relatively more in-state traffic than interstate traffic and runs from New Jersey's northern border to its southernmost tip at [Cape May](/wiki/Cape_May,_New_Jersey). It is the main route that connects the [New York metropolitan area](/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area) to the [Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore). With a total of fifteen travel and six shoulder lanes, the [Driscoll Bridge](/wiki/Driscoll_Bridge) on the Parkway, spanning the [Raritan River](/wiki/Raritan_River) in [Middlesex County](/wiki/Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey), is the widest motor vehicle bridge in the world by number of lanes as well as one of the busiest.

New Jersey is connected to New York City via various key bridges and tunnels. The double-decked [George Washington Bridge](/wiki/George_Washington_Bridge) carries the heaviest load of motor vehicle traffic of any bridge in the world, at 102 million vehicles per year, across fourteen lanes. It connects [Fort Lee, New Jersey](/wiki/Fort_Lee,_New_Jersey) to the [Washington Heights](/wiki/Washington_Heights,_Manhattan) neighborhood of [Upper Manhattan](/wiki/Upper_Manhattan), and carries [Interstate 95](/wiki/Interstate_95) and [U.S. Route 1/9](/wiki/U.S._Route_1/9) across the [Hudson River](/wiki/Hudson_River). The [Lincoln Tunnel](/wiki/Lincoln_Tunnel) connects to [Midtown Manhattan](/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan) carrying [New Jersey Route 495](/wiki/New_Jersey_Route_495), and the [Holland Tunnel](/wiki/Holland_Tunnel) connects to [Lower Manhattan](/wiki/Lower_Manhattan) carrying [Interstate 78](/wiki/Interstate_78_in_New_Jersey). New Jersey is also connected to [Staten Island](/wiki/Staten_Island) by three bridges—from north to south, the [Bayonne Bridge](/wiki/Bayonne_Bridge), the [Goethals Bridge](/wiki/Goethals_Bridge), and the [Outerbridge Crossing](/wiki/Outerbridge_Crossing).

New Jersey has [interstate compacts](/wiki/Interstate_compact) with all three of its neighboring states. The [Port Authority of New York and New Jersey](/wiki/Port_Authority_of_New_York_and_New_Jersey), the [Delaware River Port Authority](/wiki/Delaware_River_Port_Authority) (with [Pennsylvania](/wiki/Pennsylvania)), the [Delaware River Joint Toll Bridge Commission](/wiki/Delaware_River_Joint_Toll_Bridge_Commission) (with Pennsylvania), and the [Delaware River and Bay Authority](/wiki/Delaware_River_and_Bay_Authority) (with [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware)) operate most of the major transportation routes in and out of the state. Bridge tolls are collected only from traffic exiting the state, with the exception of the private [Dingman's Ferry Bridge](/wiki/Dingman%27s_Ferry_Bridge) over the Delaware River, which charges a toll in both directions.

It is unlawful for a customer to serve themselves gasoline in New Jersey. It became the last remaining U.S. state where all [gas stations](/wiki/Filling_station) are required to sell full-service gasoline to customers at all times in 2016, after [Oregon](/wiki/Oregon)'s introduction of restricted self-service gasoline availability took effect.

### Airports

[Newark Liberty International Airport](/wiki/Newark_Liberty_International_Airport) (EWR) is one of the [busiest airports](/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_the_United_States) in the United States. Operated by the [Port Authority of New York and New Jersey](/wiki/Port_Authority_of_New_York_and_New_Jersey), it is one of the three main airports serving the [New York metropolitan area](/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area), along with [John F. Kennedy International Airport](/wiki/John_F._Kennedy_International_Airport) and [LaGuardia Airport](/wiki/LaGuardia_Airport), which are both in Queens, New York. [United Airlines](/wiki/United_Airlines) is the airport's largest tenant, operating an entire terminal (Terminal C) there, which it uses as one of its primary [hubs](/wiki/Airline_hub). [FedEx Express](/wiki/FedEx_Express) operates a large [cargo terminal](/wiki/Cargo_terminal) at EWR as well. The adjacent [Newark Airport railroad station](/wiki/Newark_Liberty_International_Airport_(NJT_station)) provides access to [Amtrak](/wiki/Amtrak) and [NJ Transit](/wiki/NJ_Transit) trains along the [Northeast Corridor Line](/wiki/Northeast_Corridor_Line).

Two smaller commercial airports, [Atlantic City International Airport](/wiki/Atlantic_City_International_Airport) and rapidly growing [Trenton-Mercer Airport](/wiki/Trenton-Mercer_Airport), also operate in other parts of the state. [Teterboro Airport](/wiki/Teterboro_Airport) in [Bergen County](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey) and [Millville Municipal Airport](/wiki/Millville_Municipal_Airport) in [Cumberland County](/wiki/Cumberland_County,_New_Jersey) are [general aviation](/wiki/General_aviation) airports popular with [private](/wiki/Private_aviation) and [corporate aircraft](/wiki/Corporate_aviation) due to their proximity to New York City and the [Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore), respectively.

### Rail and bus

Main article: [NJ Transit](/wiki/NJ_Transit)

Further information: [New Jersey Transit Bus Operations](/wiki/New_Jersey_Transit_Bus_Operations), [New Jersey Transit Rail Operations](/wiki/New_Jersey_Transit_Rail_Operations), [Port Authority Trans-Hudson](/wiki/Port_Authority_Trans-Hudson), [PATCO Speedline](/wiki/PATCO_Speedline), and [List of New Jersey railroads](/wiki/List_of_New_Jersey_railroads)

Two [Hudson–Bergen Light Rail](/wiki/Hudson%E2%80%93Bergen_Light_Rail) trains in [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey)

A [NJ Transit](/wiki/NJ_Transit) train on the [Northeast Corridor](/wiki/Northeast_Corridor) in [Rahway](/wiki/Rahway,_New_Jersey)

[NJ Transit](/wiki/NJ_Transit) operates extensive rail and bus service throughout the state. A state-run corporation, it began with the consolidation of several private bus companies in [North Jersey](/wiki/North_Jersey) in 1979. In the early 1980s, it acquired [Conrail](/wiki/Conrail)'s commuter train operations that connected suburban towns to New York City. NJ Transit has [12 rail lines](/wiki/NJ_Transit_Rail_Operations) that run through different parts of the state and 165 stations statewide. Most of the lines end at either [New York Penn Station](/wiki/New_York_Penn_Station) in [Midtown Manhattan](/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan) or [Hoboken Terminal](/wiki/Hoboken_Terminal) in [Hoboken](/wiki/Hoboken,_New_Jersey), although some lines provide service to both terminal stations. The [Atlantic City Line](/wiki/Atlantic_City_Line) provides service between [Atlantic City](/wiki/Atlantic_City,_New_Jersey) and [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia).

NJ Transit also operates three [light rail](/wiki/Light_rail) systems in the state. The [Hudson-Bergen Light Rail](/wiki/Hudson-Bergen_Light_Rail) connects [Bayonne](/wiki/Bayonne,_New_Jersey) to [North Bergen](/wiki/North_Bergen,_New_Jersey), through [Hoboken](/wiki/Hoboken,_New_Jersey) and [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey). The [Newark Light Rail](/wiki/Newark_Light_Rail) is partially underground, and connects downtown [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey) with other parts of the city and its suburbs, [Belleville](/wiki/Belleville,_New_Jersey) and [Bloomfield](/wiki/Bloomfield,_New_Jersey). The [River Line](/wiki/River_Line_(New_Jersey_Transit)) connects [Trenton](/wiki/Trenton,_New_Jersey) and [Camden](/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey).

The [PATH](/wiki/Port_Authority_Trans-Hudson) is a [rapid transit](/wiki/Rapid_transit) system consisting of four lines operated by the [Port Authority of New York and New Jersey](/wiki/Port_Authority_of_New_York_and_New_Jersey). It links [Hoboken](/wiki/Hoboken,_New_Jersey), [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey), [Harrison](/wiki/Harrison,_New_Jersey), and [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey) with New York City. The [PATCO Speedline](/wiki/PATCO_Speedline) is a rapid transit system that links [Camden County](/wiki/Camden_County,_New_Jersey) to Philadelphia. Both the PATCO and the PATH are two of only five rapid transit systems in the United States to operate 24 hours a day.

[Amtrak](/wiki/Amtrak) operates numerous long-distance passenger trains in New Jersey, both to and from neighboring states and around the country. In addition to the Newark Airport connection, other major Amtrak railway stations include [Trenton Transit Center](/wiki/Trenton_(Amtrak_station)), [Metropark](/wiki/Metropark_(NJT_station)), and the historic [Newark Penn Station](/wiki/Newark_Penn_Station).

The [Southeastern Pennsylvania Transportation Authority](/wiki/SEPTA), or SEPTA, has two commuter rail lines that operate into New Jersey. The [Trenton Line](/wiki/Trenton_Line) terminates at the [Trenton Transit Center](/wiki/Trenton_Transit_Center), and the [West Trenton Line](/wiki/West_Trenton_Line_(SEPTA)) terminates at the [West Trenton Rail Station](/wiki/West_Trenton_Rail_Station) in [Ewing](/wiki/Ewing,_New_Jersey).

[AirTrain Newark](/wiki/AirTrain_Newark) is a [monorail](/wiki/Monorail) connecting the Amtrak/NJ Transit station on the Northeast Corridor to the airport's terminals and parking lots.

Some private bus carriers still remain in New Jersey. Most of these carriers operate with state funding to offset losses and state owned buses are provided to these carriers, of which [Coach USA](/wiki/Coach_USA) companies make up the bulk. Other carriers include private charter and tour bus operators that take gamblers from other parts of New Jersey, New York City, Philadelphia, and [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware) to the casino resorts of Atlantic City.

### Ferries

[Cape May–Lewes Ferry](/wiki/Cape_May%E2%80%93Lewes_Ferry) connects New Jersey and [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware) across [Delaware Bay](/wiki/Delaware_Bay).

[New York Waterway](/wiki/NY_Waterway) has ferry terminals at [Belford](/wiki/Belford,_New_Jersey), [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City), Hoboken, [Weehawken](/wiki/Weehawken), and [Edgewater](/wiki/Edgewater,_New_Jersey), with service to different parts of Manhattan. [Liberty Water Taxi](/wiki/Liberty_Water_Taxi) in [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City) has ferries from [Paulus Hook](/wiki/Paulus_Hook) and [Liberty State Park](/wiki/Liberty_State_Park) to [Battery Park City](/wiki/Battery_Park_City) in Manhattan. [Statue Cruises](/wiki/Statue_Cruises) offers service from Liberty State Park to the [Statue of Liberty National Monument](/wiki/Statue_of_Liberty_National_Monument), including [Ellis Island](/wiki/Ellis_Island). [SeaStreak](/wiki/SeaStreak) offers services from the [Raritan Bayshore](/wiki/Raritan_Bayshore) to Manhattan, [Martha's Vineyard](/wiki/Martha%27s_Vineyard), and [Nantucket](/wiki/Nantucket).

## Culture

[New Brunswick](/wiki/New_Brunswick,_New_Jersey), nicknamed the *Hub city* and the *Healthcare City*, is a focus city for academia, [healthcare](/wiki/Healthcare), and culture in New Jersey, and has many [high-rise](/wiki/Tower_block) buildings.

### General

New Jersey has continued to play a prominent role as a U.S. cultural nexus. The state has its own [cuisines](/wiki/Cuisine_of_New_Jersey), religious communities, [museums](/wiki/List_of_museums_in_New_Jersey), and [halls of fame](/wiki/Category:Halls_of_fame_in_New_Jersey).

New Jersey is the birthplace of many modern inventions, including [FM radio](/wiki/FM_radio), the motion picture camera, the [lithium battery](/wiki/Lithium_battery), the [light bulb](/wiki/Light_bulb), [transistors](/wiki/Transistor), and the electric train. Other New Jersey creations include: the drive-in movie, the cultivated [blueberry](/wiki/Blueberry), [cranberry sauce](/wiki/Cranberry_sauce), the [boardwalk](/wiki/Boardwalk), the [zipper](/wiki/Zipper), the [phonograph](/wiki/Phonograph), [saltwater taffy](/wiki/Saltwater_taffy), the [dirigible](/wiki/Dirigible), the [seedless watermelon](/wiki/Watermelon), the [first use](/wiki/David_Bushnell_(inventor)) of a [submarine](/wiki/Submarine) in warfare, and the [ice cream cone](/wiki/Ice_cream_cone).

[Diners](/wiki/Diner) are iconic to New Jersey. The state is home to many diner manufacturers and has over 600 diners, more than any other place in the world.

New Jersey is the only state to have never had a [state song](/wiki/List_of_U.S._state_songs); as of 2021, it is one of only two states (the other being [Maryland](/wiki/Maryland)) that are currently without a state song. "I'm From New Jersey" is incorrectly listed on many websites as being the New Jersey state song, but it was not even a contender when the New Jersey Arts Council submitted state song suggestions to the [New Jersey Legislature](/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature) in 1996.

New Jersey is frequently the target of jokes in American culture, especially from New York City-based television shows, such as *[Saturday Night Live](/wiki/Saturday_Night_Live)*. Academic [Michael Aaron Rockland](/wiki/Michael_Aaron_Rockland) attributes this to New Yorkers' view that New Jersey is the beginning of [Middle America](/wiki/Middle_America_(United_States)). The [New Jersey Turnpike](/wiki/New_Jersey_Turnpike), which runs between two major East Coast cities, New York City and Philadelphia, is also cited as a reason, as people who traverse through the state may only see its industrial zones. Reality television shows like *[Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore_(TV_series))* and *[The Real Housewives of New Jersey](/wiki/The_Real_Housewives_of_New_Jersey)* have reinforced stereotypical views of New Jersey culture, but Rockland cited *[The Sopranos](/wiki/The_Sopranos)* and the music of [Bruce Springsteen](/wiki/Bruce_Springsteen) as exporting a more positive image.

The "New" in "New Jersey" is often omitted in casual conversation.

### Cuisine

Main article: [Cuisine of New Jersey](/wiki/Cuisine_of_New_Jersey)

New Jersey is known for several foods developed within the region, including [Taylor Ham](/wiki/Taylor_Pork_Roll) (also known as [pork roll](/wiki/Pork_roll)), [sloppy joe sandwiches](/wiki/Sloppy_joe_(New_Jersey)), [tomato pies](/wiki/Trenton_tomato_pie), [salt water taffy](/wiki/Taffy_(candy)), and [Texas wieners](/wiki/Chili_dog). Just as [New York City's cuisine](/wiki/Cuisine_of_New_York_City) has an influence on [North Jersey](/wiki/North_Jersey), [Philadelphia's cuisine](/wiki/Cuisine_of_Philadelphia) influences [South Jersey](/wiki/South_Jersey).

New Jersey's third-largest industry is food and agriculture just behind pharmaceuticals and tourism. New Jersey is one of the top 10 producers of [blueberries](/wiki/Blueberry), [cranberries](/wiki/Cranberry), [peaches](/wiki/Peach), [tomatoes](/wiki/Tomato), [bell peppers](/wiki/Bell_pepper), [eggplant](/wiki/Eggplant), [cucumbers](/wiki/Cucumber), [apples](/wiki/Apple), [spinach](/wiki/Spinach), [squash](/wiki/Squash_(plant)), and [asparagus](/wiki/Asparagus) in the United States. Many restaurants in the state offer locally grown ingredients because of this.

[Campbell's Soup Company](/wiki/Campbell%27s_Soup_Company) has been headquartered in [Camden](/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey) since 1869. [Goya Foods](/wiki/Goya_Foods), the largest [Hispanic](/wiki/Hispanic)-owned food company in the United States, operates a corporate headquarters in [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey). [Mars Wrigley Confectionery](/wiki/Wrigley_Company)'s US headquarters has been based in [Hackettstown](/wiki/Hackettstown,_New_Jersey) and [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey) since 2007.

Several states with substantial Italian American populations take credit for the development of [submarine sandwiches](/wiki/Submarine_sandwich), including New Jersey.

### Music

Main article: [Music of New Jersey](/wiki/Music_of_New_Jersey)

New Jersey has long been an important origin for both [rock](/wiki/Rock_and_roll) and [rap](/wiki/Hip_hop_music) music. Prominent musicians from or with significant connections to New Jersey include:

- Singer [Frank Sinatra](/wiki/Frank_Sinatra) was born in [Hoboken](/wiki/Hoboken,_New_Jersey). He sang with a neighborhood vocal group, the Hoboken Four, and appeared in neighborhood theater amateur shows before he became an [Academy Award](/wiki/Academy_Awards)-winning actor.
- Singer [Whitney Houston](/wiki/Whitney_Houston) was born in [Newark, New Jersey](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey). Houston began singing at [New Hope Baptist Church](/wiki/New_Hope_Baptist_Church_(Newark)) in Newark as a child and became a background vocalist while in high school. She then became the [best-selling New Jersey musician ever](/wiki/List_of_best-selling_music_artists), having sold over 220 million records worldwide.
- [Bruce Springsteen](/wiki/Bruce_Springsteen), who has sung of New Jersey life on most of his albums, is from [Freehold](/wiki/Freehold_Borough,_New_Jersey). Some of his songs that represent New Jersey life are "[Born to Run](/wiki/Born_to_Run_(song))", "Spirit in the Night", "[Rosalita (Come Out Tonight)](/wiki/Rosalita_(Come_Out_Tonight))", "[Thunder Road](/wiki/Thunder_Road_(song))", "[Atlantic City](/wiki/Atlantic_City_(song))", and "[Jungleland](/wiki/Jungleland)".
- [Irvington](/wiki/Irvington,_New_Jersey)'s [Queen Latifah](/wiki/Queen_Latifah) was one of the first female rappers to succeed in music, film, and television.
- [Southside Johnny](/wiki/Southside_Johnny), eponymous leader of Southside Johnny and the Asbury Jukes was raised in [Ocean Grove](/wiki/Ocean_Grove,_NJ). He is considered the "Grandfather of the New Jersey Sound" and is cited by Jersey-born [Jon Bon Jovi](/wiki/Jon_Bon_Jovi) as his reason for singing.
- [Jon Bon Jovi](/wiki/Jon_Bon_Jovi), from [Sayreville](/wiki/Sayreville,_New_Jersey), reached fame in the 1980s with hard rock outfit [Bon Jovi](/wiki/Bon_Jovi). The band has also written many songs about life in New Jersey, including "Livin' On A Prayer", and named [one of their albums](/wiki/New_Jersey_(album)) after the state.
- Grammy-winning jazz vocalist [Sarah Vaughan](/wiki/Sarah_Vaughan) was born and raised in [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey). After singing in her church's choir as a child, she was sneaking into Newark nightclubs to sing and play piano by the time she was a teenager.
- In 1964, [the Isley Brothers](/wiki/The_Isley_Brothers) founded the record label [T-Neck Records](/wiki/T-Neck_Records), named after [Teaneck](/wiki/Teaneck,_New_Jersey), their home at the time.
- The Broadway musical *[Jersey Boys](/wiki/Jersey_Boys)* is based on the lives of the members of the [Four Seasons](/wiki/The_Four_Seasons_(group)), three of whose members were born in New Jersey ([Tommy DeVito](/wiki/Tommy_DeVito_(musician)), [Frankie Valli](/wiki/Frankie_Valli), and [Nick Massi](/wiki/Nick_Massi)) while a fourth, [Bob Gaudio](/wiki/Bob_Gaudio), was born in [the Bronx](/wiki/The_Bronx) but raised in [Bergenfield](/wiki/Bergenfield,_New_Jersey).

## Sports

Main article: [Sports in New Jersey](/wiki/Sports_in_New_Jersey)

[MetLife Stadium](/wiki/MetLife_Stadium) in [East Rutherford](/wiki/East_Rutherford,_New_Jersey), one of only two [NFL](/wiki/National_Football_League) stadiums shared by two teams, is home to the [New York Giants](/wiki/New_York_Giants) and [New York Jets](/wiki/New_York_Jets).

New Jersey currently has six teams from [major professional sports leagues](/wiki/Major_professional_sports_leagues_in_the_United_States_and_Canada) playing in the state with only one team identifying themselves as solely being from New Jersey. The remaining five teams identify themselves as being from the [New York metropolitan area](/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area) with the [National Women's Soccer League](/wiki/National_Women%27s_Soccer_League) team having a team name that represents both New Jersey and New York. The National Hockey League and [National Basketball Association](/wiki/National_Basketball_Association) teams representing [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia) have their training facilities in [South Jersey](/wiki/South_Jersey).

### Professional sports

[Prudential Center](/wiki/Prudential_Center) in [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey), home of the [NHL](/wiki/National_Hockey_League)'s [New Jersey Devils](/wiki/New_Jersey_Devils)

The [National Hockey League](/wiki/National_Hockey_League)'s [New Jersey Devils](/wiki/New_Jersey_Devils), based in [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey) at the [Prudential Center](/wiki/Prudential_Center), is the only major league [sports franchise](/wiki/Sports_franchise) to bear the state's name. Founded in 1974 in [Kansas City, Missouri](/wiki/Kansas_City,_Missouri), as the [Kansas City Scouts](/wiki/Kansas_City_Scouts), the team played in [Denver, Colorado](/wiki/Denver,_Colorado), as the [Colorado Rockies](/wiki/Colorado_Rockies_(NHL)) from 1976 until the spring of 1982 when naval architect, businessman, and [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City) native [John J. McMullen](/wiki/John_J._McMullen) purchased, renamed, and moved the franchise to [Brendan Byrne Arena](/wiki/Brendan_Byrne_Arena) in [East Rutherford's](/wiki/East_Rutherford,_New_Jersey) [Meadowlands Sports Complex](/wiki/Meadowlands_Sports_Complex). While the team was poor to mediocre in Kansas City, Denver, and its first years in New Jersey, qualifying for the playoffs once in the 13 seasons from 1974 to 1987, the Devils ultimately established themselves in late 1980s and early 1990s during the tenure of Hall of Fame president and general manager [Lou Lamoriello](/wiki/Lou_Lamoriello). As of 2023, the Devils have appeared in 23 postseasons in 40 seasons in New Jersey, reaching five [Stanley Cup](/wiki/Stanley_Cup) Finals (most recently in [2012](/wiki/2012_Stanley_Cup_playoffs)) and winning it in [1995](/wiki/1995_Stanley_Cup_playoffs), [2000](/wiki/2000_Stanley_Cup_playoffs), and [2003](/wiki/2003_Stanley_Cup_playoffs). The organization is the youngest of the nine "Big Four" major league teams based in New York metropolitan area, ultimately establishing its core following throughout the northern and central portions of the state and carving a place in a media market once dominated by the [New York Rangers](/wiki/New_York_Rangers) and [Islanders](/wiki/New_York_Islanders) which has the distinction of being the only metropolitan area in the country with three major league professional sports teams participating in the same sport.

In 2018, the [Philadelphia Flyers](/wiki/Philadelphia_Flyers) renovated and expanded their training facility, the Virtua Center Flyers Skate Zone, in [Voorhees Township](/wiki/Voorhees_Township,_New_Jersey) in the southern portion of the state.

In 2024, the [New York Sirens](/wiki/New_York_Sirens) of the [Professional Women's Hockey League](/wiki/Professional_Women%27s_Hockey_League) (PWHL) made the Prudential Center their home rink and moved their practice facility to the [Richard J. Codey Arena](/wiki/Richard_J._Codey_Arena) in [West Orange](/wiki/West_Orange,_New_Jersey), the former Devil's practice facility from 1986 to 2007.

The [New York metropolitan area](/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area)'s two [National Football League](/wiki/National_Football_League) teams, the [New York Giants](/wiki/New_York_Giants) and the [New York Jets](/wiki/New_York_Jets), play at [MetLife Stadium](/wiki/MetLife_Stadium) in [East Rutherford's](/wiki/East_Rutherford,_New_Jersey) [Meadowlands Sports Complex](/wiki/Meadowlands_Sports_Complex). Built for about $1.6 billion, the venue is one of the most expensive stadiums ever built. On February 2, 2014, MetLife Stadium hosted [Super Bowl XLVIII](/wiki/Super_Bowl_XLVIII).

The [New York Red Bulls](/wiki/New_York_Red_Bulls) of [Major League Soccer](/wiki/Major_League_Soccer) play in [Sports Illustrated Stadium](/wiki/Sports_Illustrated_Stadium), a soccer-specific stadium in [Harrison](/wiki/Harrison,_New_Jersey) across the [Passaic River](/wiki/Passaic_River) from downtown [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey). On July 27, 2011, Red Bull Arena hosted the [2011 MLS All-Star Game](/wiki/2011_MLS_All-Star_Game). [Gotham FC](/wiki/Gotham_FC) of the [National Women's Soccer League](/wiki/National_Women%27s_Soccer_League) (NWSL), founded in 2008 as Sky Blue FC, moved it's home field from [Yurcak Field](/wiki/Yurcak_Field) at [Rutgers University](/wiki/Rutgers_University) to Sports Illustrated Stadium in 2020.

New Jersey hosted matches during the [1994 FIFA World Cup](/wiki/1994_FIFA_World_Cup) at [Giants Stadium](/wiki/Giants_Stadium) and will be one of 16 cities to host matches during the [2026 FIFA World Cup](/wiki/2026_FIFA_World_Cup), at [MetLife Stadium](/wiki/MetLife_Stadium), which will also host the [tournament final](/wiki/2026_FIFA_World_Cup_final).

From 1977 to 2012, New Jersey had a [National Basketball Association](/wiki/National_Basketball_Association) team, the [New Jersey Nets](/wiki/New_Jersey_Nets). [WNBA](/wiki/WNBA)'s [New York Liberty](/wiki/New_York_Liberty) played in New Jersey from 2011 to 2013 while their primary home arena, [Madison Square Garden](/wiki/Madison_Square_Garden) was undergoing renovations. In 2016, the [Philadelphia 76ers](/wiki/Philadelphia_76ers) of the NBA opened their new headquarters and training facility, the Philadelphia 76ers Training Complex, in [Camden](/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey).

The Meadowlands Sports Complex is home to the [Meadowlands Racetrack](/wiki/Meadowlands_Racetrack), the major [harness racing](/wiki/Harness_racing) track in the state. [Monmouth Park Racetrack](/wiki/Monmouth_Park_Racetrack) in Oceanport is a popular spot for [thoroughbred racing](/wiki/Thoroughbred_racing) in New Jersey and the northeast. It hosted the [Breeders' Cup](/wiki/Breeders%27_Cup) in 2007, and its turf course was renovated in preparation.

### College sports

See also: [List of college athletic programs in New Jersey](/wiki/List_of_college_athletic_programs_in_New_Jersey)

#### Major schools

New Jerseyans' collegiate allegiances are predominantly split among the three major [NCAA Division I](/wiki/NCAA_Division_I) programs in the state: the [Rutgers University](/wiki/Rutgers_University%E2%80%93New_Brunswick) (New Jersey's flagship state university) [Scarlet Knights](/wiki/Rutgers_Scarlet_Knights), members of the [Big Ten Conference](/wiki/Big_Ten_Conference); the [Seton Hall University](/wiki/Seton_Hall_University) (the state's largest [Catholic](/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_the_United_States) university) [Pirates](/wiki/Seton_Hall_Pirates), members of the [Big East Conference](/wiki/Big_East_Conference); and the [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University) (the state's [Ivy League](/wiki/Ivy_League) university) [Tigers](/wiki/Princeton_Tigers).

The intense rivalry between Rutgers and Princeton athletics began with the [first intercollegiate football game](/wiki/1869_college_football_season) in 1869. The schools have not met on the football field since 1980, but they continue to play each other annually in all other sports offered by the two universities.

Rutgers, which fields 24 teams in various sports, is nationally known for its football program, with a 6–4 all-time [bowl](/wiki/Bowl_game) record; and its women's basketball programs, which appeared in a [National Final](/wiki/2007_NCAA_Division_I_women%27s_basketball_tournament) in 2007. In 2008 and 2009, Rutgers expanded their football home, [Rutgers Stadium](/wiki/SHI_Stadium), now called SHI Stadium, on the [Busch Campus](/wiki/Busch_Campus_of_Rutgers_University). The basketball teams play at the [Rutgers Athletic Center](/wiki/Rutgers_Athletic_Center) on [Livingston Campus](/wiki/Livingston_Campus_(Rutgers_University)). Both venues and campuses are in [Piscataway](/wiki/Piscataway_Township,_New_Jersey), across the [Raritan River](/wiki/Raritan_River) from [New Brunswick](/wiki/New_Brunswick,_New_Jersey). The university also fields men's basketball and baseball programs. Rutgers' fans live mostly in the western parts of the state and [Middlesex County](/wiki/Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey); its alumni base is the largest in the state.

Rutgers' satellite campuses in Camden and Newark each field their own athletic programs—the [Rutgers–Camden Scarlet Raptors](/wiki/Rutgers%E2%80%93Camden_Scarlet_Raptors) and the [Rutgers–Newark Scarlet Raiders](/wiki/Rutgers%E2%80%93Newark_Scarlet_Raiders)—which both compete in NCAA [Division III](/wiki/Division_III_(NCAA)).

Seton Hall fields no football team, but its men's basketball team is one of the [Big East](/wiki/Big_East_Conference)'s storied programs. No New Jersey team has won more games in the [NCAA Division I men's basketball tournament](/wiki/NCAA_Division_I_men%27s_basketball_tournament), and it is the state's only men's basketball program to reach a modern [National Final](/wiki/1989_NCAA_Division_I_men%27s_basketball_tournament). The Pirates play their home games at [Prudential Center](/wiki/Prudential_Center) in downtown [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey), about 4 miles (6 km) from the university's [South Orange](/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey) campus. Their fans hail largely from the predominantly Roman Catholic areas of the northern part of the state and the [Jersey Shore](/wiki/Jersey_Shore). The annual inter-conference rivalry game between Seton Hall and Rutgers, whose venue alternates between Newark and Piscataway, the Garden State Hardwood Classic, is planned through 2026.

#### Other schools

The state's other Division I schools include the [Monmouth University Hawks](/wiki/Monmouth_Hawks) (West Long Branch), the [New Jersey Institute of Technology (NJIT) Highlanders](/wiki/NJIT_Mens_Soccer) (Newark), the [Rider University Broncs](/wiki/Rider_University#Athletics) (Lawrenceville), and the [Saint Peter's University Peacocks](/wiki/Saint_Peter%27s_College,_New_Jersey#Athletics) (Jersey City).

[Fairleigh Dickinson University](/wiki/Fairleigh_Dickinson_Knights) competes in both Division I and Division III. It has two campuses, each with its own sports teams. The teams at the Metropolitan Campus are known as the FDU Knights, and compete in the [Northeast Conference](/wiki/Northeast_Conference) and NCAA Division I. The college at Florham (FDU-Florham) teams are known as the FDU-Florham Devils and compete in the [Middle Atlantic Conferences' Freedom Conference](/wiki/Middle_Atlantic_Conferences#Freedom_Conference) and NCAA Division III.

Among the various Division III schools in the state, the [Stevens Institute of Technology](/wiki/Stevens_Tech_Ducks_football) Ducks have fielded the longest continuously running collegiate men's lacrosse program in the country. 2009 marked the 125th season.

## Government and politics

Main article: [Government of New Jersey](/wiki/Government_of_New_Jersey)

### Executive

Further information: [Governor of New Jersey](/wiki/Governor_of_New_Jersey) and [Lieutenant Governor of New Jersey](/wiki/Lieutenant_Governor_of_New_Jersey)

[Mikie Sherrill](/wiki/Mikie_Sherrill) [(D)](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States))
[57th](/wiki/List_of_Governors_of_New_Jersey) [Governor](/wiki/Governor_of_New_Jersey)
since January 20, 2026

[Dale Caldwell](/wiki/Dale_Caldwell) (D)
[4th](/wiki/List_of_Lieutenant_Governors_of_New_Jersey) [Lt. Governor](/wiki/Lieutenant_Governor_of_New_Jersey)
since January 20, 2026

The position of Governor of New Jersey is one of the most powerful in the nation. The governor is elected on a ticket with their lieutenant governor as the only statewide elected executive officials in the state; the governor appoints the entire executive cabinet and judges of the Supreme and Superior Courts. Mikie Sherrill (D) is the governor. The governor's mansion is [Drumthwacket](/wiki/Drumthwacket), located in [Princeton](/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey), [Mercer County](/wiki/Mercer_County,_New_Jersey).

Before 2010, New Jersey was one of the few states without a [lieutenant governor](/wiki/Lieutenant_governor_(United_States)). [Republican](/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States)) [Kim Guadagno](/wiki/Kim_Guadagno) was elected the first [lieutenant governor of New Jersey](/wiki/Lieutenant_Governor_of_New_Jersey) on the Republican ticket with Governor [Chris Christie](/wiki/Chris_Christie) and took office on January 19, 2010. The position was created as the result of a [Constitutional amendment](/wiki/Constitutional_amendment) to the [New Jersey State Constitution](/wiki/New_Jersey_State_Constitution) passed by the voters in 2005. Previously a gubernatorial vacancy would be filled by the president of the [New Jersey State Senate](/wiki/New_Jersey_State_Senate) as acting governor, thus directing half of the legislative and all of the executive process. The current lieutenant governor is Dale Caldwell (D).

### Legislative

Main article: [New Jersey Legislature](/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature)

The design of the golden-domed [New Jersey State House](/wiki/New_Jersey_State_House) in [Trenton](/wiki/Trenton,_New_Jersey) differs from most other U.S. state houses in not resembling the [U.S. Capitol](/wiki/United_States_Capitol).

The current version of the [New Jersey State Constitution](/wiki/New_Jersey_State_Constitution) was adopted in 1947. It provides for a [bicameral](/wiki/Bicameral) [New Jersey Legislature](/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature), consisting of an [upper house](/wiki/Upper_house) [Senate](/wiki/New_Jersey_Senate) of 40 members and a [lower house](/wiki/Lower_house) [General Assembly](/wiki/New_Jersey_General_Assembly) of 80 members. Each of the 40 legislative districts elects one state senator and two Assembly members. Assembly members are elected for a two-year term in all odd-numbered years; state senators are elected in years ending in 1, 3, and 7 and thus serve either four- or two-year terms.

New Jersey is one of only five states that elects its state officials in odd-numbered years (the others are [Kentucky](/wiki/Kentucky), [Louisiana](/wiki/Louisiana), [Mississippi](/wiki/Mississippi), and [Virginia](/wiki/Virginia)). New Jersey holds elections for these offices every four years, in the year following each federal Presidential election year.

### Judicial

Main article: [Judiciary of New Jersey](/wiki/Judiciary_of_New_Jersey)

The [New Jersey Supreme Court](/wiki/New_Jersey_Supreme_Court) consists of a chief justice and six associate justices. All are appointed by the governor with the [advice and consent](/wiki/Advice_and_consent) of a majority of the membership of the state senate. Justices serve an initial seven-year term, after which they can be reappointed to serve until age 70.

Most of the day-to-day work in the New Jersey courts is carried out in the Municipal Court, where simple traffic tickets, minor criminal offenses, and small civil matters are heard.

More serious criminal and civil cases are handled by the [Superior Court](/wiki/New_Jersey_Superior_Court) for each county. All Superior Court judges are appointed by the governor with the [advice and consent](/wiki/Advice_and_consent) of a majority of the membership of the state senate. Each judge serves an initial seven-year term and can be reappointed to serve until age 70. New Jersey's judiciary is unusual in that it still has separate courts of law and [equity](/wiki/Equity_(law)), like its neighbor [Delaware](/wiki/Delaware) but unlike most other U.S. states. The [New Jersey Superior Court](/wiki/New_Jersey_Superior_Court) is divided into Law and Chancery Divisions at the trial level; the Law Division hears both criminal cases and civil lawsuits where the plaintiff's primary remedy is [damages](/wiki/Damages), while the Chancery Division hears family cases, civil suits where the plaintiff's primary remedy is equitable relief, and probate trials.

The Superior Court also has an [Appellate Division](/wiki/New_Jersey_Superior_Court,_Appellate_Division), which functions as the state's intermediate [appellate court](/wiki/Appellate_court). Superior Court judges are assigned to the Appellate Division by the Chief Justice.

There is also a Tax Court, which is a court of limited jurisdiction. Tax Court judges hear appeals of tax decisions made by County Boards of Taxation. They also hear appeals on decisions made by the director of the Division of Taxation on such matters as state income, sales and business taxes, and homestead rebates. Appeals from Tax Court decisions are heard in the Appellate Division of Superior Court. Tax Court judges are appointed by the governor for initial terms of seven years, and upon reappointment are granted tenure until they reach the mandatory retirement age of 70. There are 12 Tax Court judgeships.

### Counties

Further information: [List of New Jersey counties](/wiki/List_of_New_Jersey_counties)

New Jersey is divided into 21 counties; 13 date from the colonial era. New Jersey was completely divided into counties by 1692; the present counties were created by dividing the existing ones; most recently [Union County](/wiki/Union_County,_New_Jersey) in 1857. New Jersey was formerly the only state in the nation where elected county officials were called freeholders. Elected county officials are now called county commissioners as of bill S855 signed by Governor Murphy on August 8, 2020. The county commissioners govern each county as part of its own [Board of Chosen County Commissioners](/wiki/Board_of_County_Commissioners_(New_Jersey)) The number of county commissioners in each county is determined by referendum, and must consist of three, five, seven or nine members.

Depending on the county, the executive and [legislative](/wiki/Legislative) functions may be performed by the [Board of County Commissioners](/wiki/Board_of_County_Commissioners_(New_Jersey)) or split into separate branches of government. In 16 counties, the County Commissioners perform both legislative and executive functions on a commission basis, with each commissioner assigned responsibility for a department or group of departments. In the other five counties ([Atlantic](/wiki/Atlantic_County,_New_Jersey), [Bergen](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey), [Essex](/wiki/Essex_County,_New_Jersey), [Hudson](/wiki/Hudson_County,_New_Jersey) and [Mercer](/wiki/Mercer_County,_New_Jersey)), there is a directly elected [County Executive](/wiki/County_Executive) who performs the executive functions while the commissioners retain a legislative and oversight role. In counties without an Executive, a County Administrator (or County Manager) may be hired to perform day-to-day administration of county functions.

### Municipalities

Further information: [List of municipalities in New Jersey](/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_New_Jersey)

New Jersey currently has 564 municipalities; the most recent dissolution of a municipality was when [Pine Valley](/wiki/Pine_Valley,_New_Jersey) merged into [Pine Hill](/wiki/Pine_Hill,_New_Jersey) on January 1, 2022. Unlike other states, all New Jersey land is part of a municipality. In 2008, Governor [Jon Corzine](/wiki/Jon_Corzine) proposed cutting state aid to all towns under 10,000 people, to encourage mergers to reduce administrative costs. In May 2009, the Local Unit Alignment Reorganization and Consolidation Commission began a study of about 40 small communities in South Jersey to decide which ones might be good candidates for consolidation.

#### Forms of municipal government

Starting in the 20th century, largely driven by reform-minded goals, a series of six modern forms of government was implemented. This began with the [Walsh Act](/wiki/Walsh_Act_(New_Jersey)), enacted in 1911 by the [New Jersey Legislature](/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature), which provided for a three- or five-member commission elected on a non-partisan basis. This was followed by the [1923 Municipal Manager Law](/wiki/1923_Municipal_Manager_Law), which offered a non-partisan council, provided for a weak mayor elected by and from the members of the council, and introduced a [Council-manager government](/wiki/Council-manager_government) structure with an appointed manager responsible for the day-to-day administration of municipal affairs.

The [Faulkner Act](/wiki/Faulkner_Act_(New_Jersey)), originally enacted in 1950 and substantially amended in 1981, offers four basic plans: [Mayor-Council](/wiki/Faulkner_Act_(Mayor-Council)), [Council-Manager](/wiki/Faulkner_Act_(Council-Manager)), [Small Municipality](/wiki/Faulkner_Act_(Small_Municipality)), and [Mayor-Council-Administrator](/wiki/Faulkner_Act_(Mayor-Council-Administrator)). The act provides many choices for communities with a preference for a strong executive and professional management of municipal affairs and offers great flexibility in allowing municipalities to select the characteristics of its government: the number of seats on the council; seats selected at-large, by wards, or through a combination of both; staggered or concurrent terms of office; and a mayor chosen by the council or elected directly by voters. Most large municipalities and a majority of New Jersey's residents are governed by municipalities with Faulkner Act charters. Municipalities can also formulate their own unique form of government and operate under a [Special Charter](/wiki/Special_Charter_(New_Jersey)) with the approval of the [New Jersey Legislature](/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature).

While municipalities retain their names derived from types of government, they may have changed to one of the modern forms of government, or further in the past to one of the other traditional forms, leading to municipalities with formal names quite baffling to the general public. For example, though there are four municipalities that are officially of the village type, none use the village form of government. [Loch Arbour](/wiki/Loch_Arbour,_New_Jersey) and [Ridgefield Park](/wiki/Ridgefield_Park,_New_Jersey) (now with a Walsh Act form), [Ridgewood](/wiki/Ridgewood,_New_Jersey) (now with a Faulkner Act Council-Manager charter) and [South Orange](/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey) (now operates under a [Special Charter](/wiki/Special_Charter_(New_Jersey))) all migrated to other non-village forms.

### Politics

Main article: [Politics of New Jersey](/wiki/Politics_of_New_Jersey)

#### Social attitudes and issues

See also: [LGBT rights in New Jersey](/wiki/LGBT_rights_in_New_Jersey) and [Gun laws in New Jersey](/wiki/Gun_laws_in_New_Jersey)

Socially, New Jersey is considered one of the more liberal states in the nation. Polls indicate that 60% of the population are self-described as [pro-choice](/wiki/Pro-choice), although a majority are opposed to late trimester and [intact dilation and extraction](/wiki/Intact_dilation_and_extraction) and public funding of abortion. As of 2022, all aspects of reproductive choice (including abortion) are protected by law.

In a 2009 [Quinnipiac University Polling Institute](/wiki/Quinnipiac_University_Polling_Institute) poll, a plurality supported [same-sex marriage](/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_New_Jersey) 49% to 43% opposed. On October 18, 2013, the [New Jersey Supreme Court](/wiki/New_Jersey_Supreme_Court) rendered a provisional, unanimous (7–0) order authorizing same-sex marriage in the state, pending a legal appeal by Governor Chris Christie, who then withdrew this appeal hours after the inaugural same-sex marriages took place on October 21, 2013.

New Jersey also has some of the most stringent [gun control](/wiki/Gun_control) laws in the U.S. These include bans on [assault firearms](/wiki/Assault_weapon), hollow-nose bullets and slingshots. No gun offense in New Jersey is graded less than a felony. [BB guns](/wiki/BB_guns) and black-powder guns are all treated as modern firearms. New Jersey does not recognize out-of-state gun licenses and aggressively enforces its own gun laws.

In 2020, the state's voting population passed a public question that amended the state constitution to legalize [marijuana](/wiki/Marijuana) and erase past legal convictions for possession. The measure passed by a two-thirds vote. At the time the measure was enacted, about a dozen other U.S. states had also legalized the sale and possession of marijuana. As of 2024, local governments and municipalities are still in the process of regulating marijuana-related businesses within their jurisdictions.

#### Elections

Main article: [Elections in New Jersey](/wiki/Elections_in_New_Jersey)

See also: [Political party strength in New Jersey](/wiki/Political_party_strength_in_New_Jersey)

New Jersey is a [Democratic](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)) stronghold. [New Jersey Democrats](/wiki/New_Jersey_Democratic_State_Committee) have majority control of both houses of the [New Jersey Legislature](/wiki/New_Jersey_Legislature) (Senate, 24–16, and Assembly, 46–34), 9–3 split of the state's twelve seats in the [U.S. House of Representatives](/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives), and both [U.S. Senate](/wiki/United_States_Senate) seats. There have been recent Republican governors, however: [Christine Todd Whitman](/wiki/Christine_Todd_Whitman) won election in 1993 and 1997 and [Chris Christie](/wiki/Chris_Christie) in 2009 and 2013.

In federal elections, the state leans heavily towards the [Democratic Party](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)), having last voted for a Republican for president in 1988. New Jersey was a crucial [swing state](/wiki/Swing_state) in the elections of [1960](/wiki/1960_United_States_presidential_election), [1968](/wiki/1968_United_States_presidential_election), and [1992](/wiki/1992_United_States_presidential_election). The last elected Republican to hold a Senate seat from New Jersey was [Clifford P. Case](/wiki/Clifford_P._Case) in 1979. Newark Mayor [Cory Booker](/wiki/Cory_Booker) was elected in October 2013 to join [Robert Menendez](/wiki/Bob_Menendez) to make New Jersey the first state with concurrently serving black and Latino U.S. senators.

The state's Democratic strongholds include [Camden County](/wiki/Camden_County,_New_Jersey), [Essex County](/wiki/Essex_County,_New_Jersey) (the state's most Democratic county—it includes [Newark](/wiki/Newark,_New_Jersey), the state's largest city), [Hudson County](/wiki/Hudson_County,_New_Jersey) (the second-strongest Democratic county, including [Jersey City](/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey), the state's second-largest city); [Mercer County](/wiki/Mercer_County,_New_Jersey) (especially around [Trenton](/wiki/Trenton,_New_Jersey) and [Princeton](/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey)), [Middlesex County](/wiki/Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey), and [Union County](/wiki/Union_County,_New_Jersey) (including [Elizabeth](/wiki/Elizabeth,_New_Jersey), the state's fourth-largest city). Other suburban counties, especially [Bergen County](/wiki/Bergen_County,_New_Jersey) and [Burlington County](/wiki/Burlington_County,_New_Jersey), had the majority of votes go to the [Democratic Party](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)).

The northwestern and southeastern counties of the state are reliably Republican: Republicans have support along the coast in [Ocean County](/wiki/Ocean_County,_New_Jersey) and [Cape May County](/wiki/Cape_May_County,_New_Jersey) as well as in the mountainous northwestern part of the state, especially in [Hunterdon County](/wiki/Hunterdon_County,_New_Jersey), [Sussex County](/wiki/Sussex_County,_New_Jersey), and [Warren County](/wiki/Warren_County,_New_Jersey).

To be eligible to vote in a U.S. election, all New Jerseyans are required to start their residency in the state 30 days prior to an election and register 21 days prior to election day.

#### Capital punishment

Main article: [Capital punishment in New Jersey](/wiki/Capital_punishment_in_New_Jersey)

On December 17, 2007, Governor Jon Corzine signed into law a bill that would eliminate the death penalty in New Jersey. New Jersey was the first state to pass such legislation since [Iowa](/wiki/Iowa) and [West Virginia](/wiki/West_Virginia) eliminated executions in 1965. Corzine also signed a bill that would downgrade the Death Row prisoners' sentences from "Death" to "Life in Prison with No Parole".
