# Zodiac

For the East Asian zodiac, see [Chinese zodiac](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_zodiac). For other uses, see [Zodiac (disambiguation)](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiac_(disambiguation)).

The [Earth's orbit](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_orbit) around the [Sun](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) causes the apparent motion of the latter along the [ecliptic](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic) (red). Earth is [axially tilted](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt) 23.4° relative to this plane; its equator, [extended to the stars](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_equator), is shown in light blue.

Astrophotos of the twelve zodiac constellations

The **zodiac** is a belt-shaped region of the sky that extends approximately 8° north and south [celestial latitude](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_latitude) of the [ecliptic](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic) – the [apparent path](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_path) of the [Sun](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) across the [celestial sphere](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_sphere) over the course of the year. Within this zodiac belt appear the [Moon](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) and the brightest [planets](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet), along their orbital planes. The zodiac is divided along the ecliptic into 12 equal parts, called "[signs](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrological_sign)", each occupying 30° of [celestial longitude](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_longitude). These signs roughly correspond to the [astronomical](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical) [constellations](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation) with the following modern names: [Aries](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aries_(astrology)), [Taurus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taurus_(astrology)), [Gemini](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_(astrology)), [Cancer](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_(astrology)), [Leo](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_(astrology)), [Virgo](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_(astrology)), [Libra](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libra_(astrology)), [Scorpio](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpio_(astrology)), [Sagittarius](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagittarius_(astrology)), [Capricorn](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capricorn_(astrology)), [Aquarius](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquarius_(astrology)), and [Pisces](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisces_(astrology)).

The signs have been used to determine the time of the year by identifying each sign with the days of the year the Sun is in the respective sign. In [Western astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_astrology), and formerly [astronomy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), the time of each sign is associated with different attributes. The zodiacal system and its angular measurement in 360 [sexagesimal](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagesimal) [degree (°)](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_(angle)) originated with [Babylonian astronomy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomy) during the [1st millennium BC](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_millennium_BC), probably during the [Achaemenid Empire](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire). It was communicated into [Greek astronomy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_astronomy) by the 2nd century BC, as well as into developing the [Hindu zodiac](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_astrology). Due to the [precession of the equinoxes](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precession_of_the_equinoxes), the time of year that the Sun is in a given constellation has changed since Babylonian times, and the point of [March equinox](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox) has moved from [Aries](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aries_(constellation)) into [Pisces](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisces_(constellation)).

The zodiac forms a [celestial coordinate system](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinate_system), or more specifically an [ecliptic coordinate system](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_coordinate_system), which takes the ecliptic as the origin of latitude and the [Sun's position](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_of_the_Sun) at [vernal equinox](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox_(celestial_coordinates)) as the origin of longitude. In modern astronomy, the ecliptic coordinate system is still used for tracking Solar System objects.

## Name

The English word *[zodiac](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/zodiac)* derives from *[zōdiacus](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/zodiacus#Latin)*, the [Latinized](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names) form of the [Ancient Greek](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language) *zōdiakòs* ([ζῳδιακός](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%B6%E1%BF%B3%CE%B4%CE%B9%CE%B1%CE%BA%CF%8C%CF%82#Ancient_Greek)), meaning "of or relating to little animals", *Zōdion* ([ζῴδιον](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%B6%E1%BF%B4%CE%B4%CE%B9%CE%BF%CE%BD#Ancient_Greek)) being the diminutive of *zōon* ([ζῷον](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%B6%E1%BF%B7%CE%BF%CE%BD#Ancient_Greek), "animal"). The name reflects the prominence of animals (and [mythological hybrids](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythological_hybrid)) among the twelve signs. In English, the term "zodiac" may also be used in reference to or translation for the similar twelve year cycle (also sometimes applied to other time units than years) of the East Asian-derived systems referred to as the [Chinese zodiac](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_zodiac) (see also, [Earthly Branches](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthly_Branches)): similarities include the use of animal or theriomorphic figures associated with a twelve year cycle used culturally to allege or describe personality traits, life events, and their interrelationships—thus this use in translation.

## Usage

Modern zodiac wheel showing the 12 signs used in [horoscopic astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horoscopic_astrology)

The zodiac was in use by the [Roman era](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_era), based on concepts inherited by [Hellenistic astronomy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_astronomy) from [Babylonian astronomy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomy) of the [Chaldean period](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian) (mid-1st millennium BC), which, in turn, derived from an earlier system of lists of stars along the ecliptic. The construction of the zodiac is described in [Ptolemy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy)'s comprehensive 2nd century AD work, the *[Almagest](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest)*.

Although the zodiac remains the basis of the [ecliptic coordinate system](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_coordinate_system) in use in [astronomy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) besides the [equatorial](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_coordinate_system) one, the term and the names of the twelve signs are today mostly associated with [horoscopic astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horoscopic_astrology). The term "zodiac" may also refer to the region of the [celestial sphere](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_sphere) encompassing the paths of the planets corresponding to the band of about 8 arc degrees above and below the ecliptic. The zodiac of a given planet is the band that contains the path of that particular body; e.g., the "zodiac of the Moon" is the band of 5° above and below the ecliptic. By extension, the "zodiac of the comets" may refer to the band encompassing most [short-period comets](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-period_comet).

## History

### Early history

As early as the 14th century BC a complete list of the 36 Egyptian [decans](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decan) was placed among the hieroglyphs adorning the tomb of [Seti I](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seti_I); they figured again in the temple of [Ramesses II](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramesses_II), and characterize every Egyptian astrological monument. Both the famous zodiacs of [Dendera](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendera) display their symbols, identified by [Karl Richard Lepsius](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Richard_Lepsius).

Roman Egyptian coin of [Antoninus Pius](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoninus_Pius) (dated year 8 of his reign or 145 AD) showing his portrait and a Zodiac wheel with the busts of [Helios](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helios) and [Selene](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selene) in the center

A sixth-century mosaic zodiac wheel in synagogue [Beth Alpha](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beth_Alpha) incorporating Greek-Byzantine elements, Israel

Zodiac circle with planets, c. 1000 – *NLW MS 735C*

The division of the ecliptic into the zodiacal signs originates in [Babylonian astronomy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomy) during the first half of the [1st millennium BC](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_millennium_BC). The zodiac draws on stars in earlier [Babylonian star catalogues](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_star_catalogues), such as the [MUL.APIN](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUL.APIN) catalogue, which was compiled around 1000 BC. Some constellations can be traced even further back, to Bronze Age ([Old Babylonian Empire](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Babylonian_Empire)) sources, including [Gemini](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_(constellation)) "The Twins", from [Sumerian](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumerian_language): 𒀯𒈦𒋰𒁀𒃲𒃲, romanized: *MULMAŠ.TAB.BA.GAL.GAL* "The Great Twins"; [Cancer](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_(constellation)) "The Crab", from [Sumerian](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumerian_language): 𒀯𒀠𒇻, romanized: *MULAL.LUL* "The Crayfish", among others.

Around the end of the fifth century BC, [Babylonian](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia) astronomers divided the ecliptic into 12 equal "signs", by analogy to 12 schematic months of 30 days each. Each sign contained 30° of [celestial longitude](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_longitude), thus creating the first known celestial coordinate system. According to calculations by modern astrophysics, the zodiac was introduced between 409 and 398 BC, during [Persian rule](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire), and probably within a very few years of 401 BC. Unlike modern astrologers, who place the beginning of the sign of [Aries](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aries_(astrology)) at the position of the Sun at the [vernal equinox](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox_(celestial_coordinates)) in the [Northern Hemisphere](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere) ([March equinox](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox)), Babylonian astronomers fixed the zodiac in relation to stars, placing the beginning of [Cancer](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_(astrology)) at the "Rear Twin Star" ([β Geminorum](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollux_(star))) and the beginning of [Aquarius](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquarius_(astrology)) at the "Rear Star of the Goat-Fish" ([δ Capricorni](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Capricorni)).

Due to the [precession of the equinoxes](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precession_of_the_equinoxes), the time of year the Sun is in a given constellation has changed since Babylonian times, as the point of March equinox has moved from [Aries](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aries_(constellation)) into [Pisces](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisces_(constellation)).

Because the divisions were made into equal arcs of 30° each, they constituted an ideal system of reference for making predictions about a planet's longitude. However, Babylonian techniques of observational measurements were in a rudimentary stage of evolution. They measured the position of a planet in reference to a set of "normal stars" close to the ecliptic (±9° of latitude). The normal stars were used as observational reference points to help position a planet within this ecliptic coordinate system.

In [Babylonian astronomical diaries](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomical_diaries), a planet position was generally given with respect to a zodiacal sign alone, though less often in specific degrees within a sign. When the degrees of longitude were given, they were expressed with reference to the 30° of the zodiacal sign, i.e., not with a reference to the continuous 360° ecliptic. In astronomical [ephemerides](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephemerides), the positions of significant astronomical phenomena were computed in [sexagesimal](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagesimal) fractions of a degree (equivalent to [minutes and seconds of arc](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_and_second_of_arc)). For daily ephemerides, the daily positions of a planet were not as important as the astrologically significant dates when the planet crossed from one zodiacal sign to the next.

### Hebrew astronomy and astrology

Knowledge of the Babylonian zodiac is said to be reflected in the [Hebrew Bible](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible); [E. W. Bullinger](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._W._Bullinger) interpreted the creatures that appear in the [book of Ezekiel](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Ezekiel) ([1:10](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezekiel_1#Verse_10)) as the middle signs of the four quarters of the zodiac, with the Lion as [Leo](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_(astrology)), the Bull as [Taurus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taurus_(astrology)), the Man as Aquarius and the Eagle as a higher aspect of Scorpio. Some authors have linked the signs of the zodiac with the [twelve tribes of Israel](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_tribes_of_Israel), and with the lunar [Hebrew calendar](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_calendar), which has twelve [lunar months](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_months) in a [lunar year](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_year). [Ernest L. Martin](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_L._Martin) and others have argued that the arrangement of the tribes around the [Tabernacle](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabernacle) (reported in the [Book of Numbers](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Numbers)) corresponded to the order of the zodiac, with [Judah](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe_of_Judah), [Reuben](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe_of_Reuben), [Ephraim](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe_of_Ephraim), and [Dan](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe_of_Dan) representing the middle signs of Leo, Aquarius, Taurus, and Scorpio, respectively. Such connections were taken up by [Thomas Mann](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann), who in his novel *[Joseph and His Brothers](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_and_His_Brothers)*, attributes characteristics of a sign of the zodiac to each tribe, in his rendition of the [Blessing of Jacob](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blessing_of_Jacob).

### Hellenistic and Roman era

The 1st century BC [Dendera zodiac](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendera_zodiac) (19th-century engraving)

The Babylonian star catalogs entered [Greek astronomy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_astronomy) in the 4th century BC, via [Eudoxus of Cnidus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudoxus_of_Cnidus).
Babylonia or [Chaldea](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaldea) in the Hellenistic world came to be so identified with astrology that "Chaldean wisdom" became among [Greeks](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks) and [Romans](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) the synonym of [divination](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination) through the [planets](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planets) and [stars](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star). [Hellenistic astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_astrology) derived in part from Babylonian and [Egyptian astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_astronomy). [Horoscopic astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horoscopic_astrology) first appeared in [Ptolemaic Egypt](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom) (305 BC–30 BC). The [Dendera zodiac](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendera_zodiac), a relief dating to c. 50 BC, is the first known depiction of the classical zodiac of twelve signs.

The earliest extant Greek text using the Babylonian division of the zodiac into 12 signs of 30 equal degrees each is the *Anaphoricus* of [Hypsicles of Alexandria](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypsicles_of_Alexandria) (fl. 190 BC). Particularly important in the development of Western horoscopic astrology was the astrologer and astronomer [Ptolemy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrabiblos), whose work *Tetrabiblos* laid the basis of the [Western astrological tradition](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_astrological_tradition). Under the Greeks, and Ptolemy in particular, the planets, Houses, and signs of the zodiac were rationalized and their function set down in a way that has changed little to the present day. Ptolemy lived in the 2nd century AD, three centuries after the discovery of the [precession](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precession) of the equinoxes by [Hipparchus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus) around 130 BC. Hipparchus' lost work on precession never circulated very widely until it was brought to prominence by Ptolemy, and there are few explanations of precession outside the work of Ptolemy until late Antiquity, by which time Ptolemy's influence was widely established. Ptolemy clearly explained the theoretical basis of the western zodiac as being a [tropical coordinate system](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_astrology), by which the zodiac is aligned to the equinoxes and solstices, rather than the visible constellations that bear the same names as the zodiac signs.

### Hindu zodiac

According to mathematician-historian [Montucla](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-%C3%89tienne_Montucla), the Hindu zodiac was adopted from the [Greek zodiac](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_astronomy) through communications between ancient India and the Greek empire of [Bactria](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bactria). The [Hindu zodiac](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_zodiac) uses the [sidereal coordinate system](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_astrology), which makes reference to the fixed stars. The tropical zodiac (of Mesopotamian origin) is divided by the intersections of the [ecliptic](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic) and [equator](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator), which shifts in relation to the backdrop of fixed stars at a rate of 1° every 72 years, creating the phenomenon known as precession of the equinoxes. The Hindu zodiac, being sidereal, does not maintain this seasonal alignment, but there are still similarities between the two systems. The Hindu zodiac signs and corresponding Greek signs sound very different, being in Sanskrit and Greek respectively, but their symbols are nearly identical. For example, *dhanu* means "bow" and corresponds to Sagittarius, the "archer", and *kumbha* means "water-pitcher" and corresponds to Aquarius, the "water-carrier".

### Middle Ages

Angers Cathedral South Rose Window of Christ (center) with elders (bottom half) and zodiac (top half). Medieval stained glass by Andre Robin after the fire of 1451

During the [Abbasid era](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate), Greek reference books were translated into [Arabic](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic), and [Islamic astronomers](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world) then did their own observations, correcting Ptolemy's Almagest. One such book was [Al-Sufi](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Rahman_al-Sufi)'s *[Book of Fixed Stars](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Fixed_Stars)*, which has pictorial depictions of 48 constellations. The book was divided into three sections: constellations of the zodiac, constellations north of the zodiac, and southern constellations. When Al-Sufi's book, and other works, were translated in the 11th century, there were mistakes made in the translations. As a result, some stars ended up with the names of the constellation they belong to (e.g. [Hamal](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamal) in Aries).

The High Middle Ages saw a revival of interest in [Greco-Roman magic](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_magic), first in [Kabbalism](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabbalism) and later continued in [Renaissance magic](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_magic). This included magical uses of the zodiac, as found, e.g., in the [Sefer Raziel HaMalakh](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sefer_Raziel_HaMalakh).

The zodiac is found in medieval [stained glass](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stained_glass) as at [Angers Cathedral](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angers_Cathedral), where the master glass maker, André Robin, made the ornate [rosettes](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosette_window) for the North and South transepts after the fire there in 1451.

### Medieval Islamic era

Ottoman-style sundial with folded gnomon and compass. The sundial features engraved toponyms in Arabic and zodiac symbols. [Debbane Palace](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debbane_Palace) museum, [Lebanon](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanon)

[Astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) emerged in the 8th century AD as a distinct discipline in Islam, with a mix of Indian, Hellenistic Iranian and other traditions blended with Greek and Islamic astronomical knowledge, for example Ptolemy's work and Al-Sufi's Book of Fixed Stars. A knowledge of the influence that the stars have on events on the earth was important in Islamic civilization. As a rule, it was believed that the signs of the zodiac and the planets control the destiny not only of people but also of nations, and that the zodiac has the ability to determine a person's physical characteristics as well as intelligence and personal traits.

The practice of astrology at this time could be divided into four broader categories: [Genethlialogy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natal_astrology), Catarchic Astrology, Interrogational Astrology and General Astrology. However the most common type of astrology was Genethlialogy, which examined all aspects of a person's life in relation to the planetary positions at their birth; more commonly known as our horoscope.

Astrology services were offered widely across the empire, mainly in bazaars, where people could pay for a reading. Astrology was valued in the royal courts, for example, the Abbasid Caliph Al-Mansur used astrology to determine the best date for founding the new capital of Baghdad. While horoscopes were generally widely accepted by society, many scholars condemned the use of astrology and divination, linking it to occult influences. Many theologians and scholars thought that it went against the tenets of Islam; as only God should be able to determine events rather than astrologers looking at the positions of the planets.

In order to calculate someone's horoscope, an astrologer would use three tools: an astrolabe, ephemeris and a takht. First, the astrologer would use an astrolabe to find the position of the sun, align the rule with the persons time of birth and then align the rete to establish the altitude of the sun on that date. Next, the astrologer would use an ephemeris, a table denoting the mean position of the planets and stars within the sky at any given time. Finally, the astrologer would add the altitude of the sun taken from the astrolabe, with the mean position of the planets on the person's birthday, and add them together on the takht (also known as the dustboard). The dust board was merely a tablet covered in sand; on which the calculations could be made and erased easily. Once this had been calculated, the astrologer was then able to interpret the horoscope. Most of these interpretations were based on the zodiac in literature. For example, there were several manuals on how to interpret each zodiac sign, the treatise relating to each individual sign and what the characteristics of these zodiacs were.

### Early modern

An example of the use of signs as astronomical coordinates may be found in the *Nautical Almanac and Astronomical Ephemeris for the year 1767*. The "Longitude of the Sun" columns show the sign (represented as a digit from 0 to and including 11), degrees from 0 to 29, minutes, and seconds.

[Mughal king](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire) [Jahangir](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahangir) issued an attractive series of coins in gold and silver depicting the twelve signs of the zodiac.

As each sign takes up exactly 30 degrees of the zodiac, the average duration of the solar stay in each sign is one twelfth of a [sidereal year](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_year), or 30.43 standard [days](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day). Due to Earth's slight [orbital eccentricity](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity), the duration of each sign varies appreciably, between about 29.4 days for Capricorn and about 31.4 days for Cancer (see [Equation of time](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_of_time)). In addition, because the Earth's axis is at an angle, some signs take longer to rise than others, and the farther away from the equator the observer is situated, the greater the difference. Thus, signs are spoken of as ["long" or "short" ascension](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascendant).

## Constellations

These two maps of the constellations, made two centuries apart, both show the zodiac constellations along a curved line representing the [ecliptic](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic).

18th-century [star chart](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart) illustrating the feet of [Ophiuchus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophiuchus) crossing the ecliptic

In [tropical astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_and_tropical_astrology), the [zodiacal signs](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrological_sign) are distinct from the [constellations](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation) associated with them, not only because of their drifting apart due to the precession of equinoxes but because the physical constellations take up varying widths of the ecliptic, so the Sun is not in each constellation for the same amount of time. Thus, [Virgo](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_(constellation)) takes up 5 times as much [ecliptic longitude](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_longitude) as [Scorpius](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpius_(constellation)). The zodiacal signs are an abstraction from the physical constellations, and each represent exactly 1⁄12th of the full circle, but the time spent by the Sun in each sign varies slightly due to the eccentricity of the [Earth's orbit](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_orbit).

[Sidereal astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_and_tropical_astrology) assigns the zodiac sign approximately to the corresponding constellation. This alignment needs recalibrating every so often to keep the alignment in place.

The ecliptic intersects with 13 constellations of [Ptolemy](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy)'s *[Almagest](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest)*, as well as of the more precisely delineated [IAU designated constellations](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IAU_designated_constellations). In addition to the twelve constellations after which the twelve zodiac signs are named, the ecliptic intersects [Ophiuchus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophiuchus), the bottom part of which interjects between Scorpius and Sagittarius. Occasionally this difference between the astronomical constellations and the [astrological signs](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrological_sign) is mistakenly reported in the popular press as a "change" to the list of traditional signs by some astronomical body like the IAU, [NASA](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA), or the [Royal Astronomical Society](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Astronomical_Society). This happened in a 1995 report of the *[BBC Nine O'Clock News](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Nine_O%27Clock_News)* and various reports in 2011 and 2016.

Some "parazodiacal" constellations are touched by the paths of the planets, leading to counts of up to 25 "constellations of the zodiac". The ancient Babylonian [MUL.APIN](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUL.APIN) catalog lists [Orion](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_(constellation)), [Perseus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perseus_(constellation)), [Auriga](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auriga_(constellation)), and [Andromeda](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_(constellation)). Modern astronomers have noted that planets pass through [Crater](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crater_(constellation)), [Sextans](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextans), [Cetus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetus_(constellation)), [Pegasus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pegasus_(constellation)), [Corvus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corvus_(constellation)), [Hydra](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydra_(constellation)), [Orion](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_(constellation)), and [Scutum](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scutum_(constellation)), with [Venus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus) very rarely passing through [Aquila](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquila_(constellation)), [Canis Minor](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canis_Minor), [Auriga](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auriga_(constellation)), and [Serpens](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpens).

Some other constellations are mythologically associated with the zodiacal ones: [Piscis Austrinus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piscis_Austrinus), The Southern Fish, is attached to Aquarius. In classical maps, it swallows the stream poured out of Aquarius' pitcher, but perhaps it formerly just swam in it. [Aquila](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquila_(constellation)), The Eagle, was possibly associated with the zodiac by virtue of its main star, [Altair](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altair). [Hydra](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydra_(constellation)) in the Early Bronze Age marked the [celestial equator](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_equator) and was associated with Leo, which is shown standing on the serpent on the [Dendera zodiac](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendera_zodiac).

## Precession of the equinoxes

Further information: [Axial precession](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_precession), [Epoch (astronomy)](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_(astronomy)), [Sidereal and tropical astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_and_tropical_astrology), [Astrological age](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrological_age), and [Ayanamsa](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayanamsa)

Path taken by the point of the [March equinox](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox) along the ecliptic over the past 6,000 years

The zodiac system was developed in [Babylonia](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian), some 2,500 years ago, during the "[Age of Aries](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Aries)". At the time, it is assumed, the precession of the equinoxes was unknown. Contemporary use of the coordinate system is presented with the choice of interpreting the system either as [sidereal](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_astrology), with the signs fixed to the [stellar background](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_stars), or as [tropical](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_astrology), with the signs fixed to the point ([vector](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector) of the Sun) at the [March equinox](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox).

[Western astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_astrology) takes the tropical approach, whereas [Hindu astrology](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_astrology) takes the sidereal one. This results in the originally unified zodiacal coordinate system drifting apart gradually, with a clockwise (westward) precession of 1.4 degrees per century.

For the tropical zodiac used in Western astronomy and astrology, this means that the tropical *sign* of Aries currently lies somewhere within the *constellation* Pisces ("[Age of Pisces](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Pisces)").

The sidereal coordinate system takes into account the [ayanamsa](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayanamsa), *ayan* meaning "transit" or "movement", and *amsa* meaning 'small part', i.e. movement of equinoxes in small parts. It is unclear when Indians became aware of the precession of the equinoxes, but [Bhāskara II](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bh%C4%81skara_II)'s 12th-century treatise *[Siddhanta Shiromani](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddhanta_Shiromani)* gives equations for measurement of precession of equinoxes, and says his equations are based on some lost equations of *[Suryasiddhanta](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suryasiddhanta)* plus the equation of Munjaala.

The discovery of precession is attributed to Hipparchus around 130 BC. Ptolemy quotes from Hipparchus' now-lost work entitled "On the Displacement of the Solstitial and Equinoctial Points" in the seventh book of his 2nd century astronomical text, *Almagest*, where he describes the phenomenon of precession and estimates its value. Ptolemy clarified that the convention of Greek mathematical astronomy was to commence the zodiac from the point of the vernal equinox and to always refer to this point as "the first degree" of Aries. This is known as the "tropical zodiac" (from the Greek word trópos, turn) because its starting point revolves through the circle of background constellations over time.

The principle of the vernal point acting as the first degree of the zodiac for Greek astronomers is described in the 1st century BC astronomical text of [Geminus](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geminus) of Rhodes. Geminus explains that Greek astronomers of his era associate the first degrees of the zodiac signs with the two solstices and the two equinoxes, in contrast to the older Chaldean (Babylonian) system, which placed these points within the zodiac signs. This illustrates that Ptolemy merely clarified the convention of Greek astronomers and did not originate the principle of the tropical zodiac, as is sometimes assumed.

Ptolemy demonstrates that the principle of the tropical zodiac was well known to his predecessors within his astrological text, the *[Tetrabiblos](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrabiblos)*, where he explains why it would be an error to associate the regularly spaced signs of the seasonally aligned zodiac with the irregular boundaries of the visible constellations:

> The beginnings of the signs, and likewise those of the terms, are to be taken from the equinoctial and tropical points. This rule is not only clearly stated by writers on the subject, but is especially evident by the demonstration constantly afforded, that their natures, influences and familiarities have no other origin than from the tropics and equinoxes, as has been already plainly shown. And, if other beginnings were allowed, it would either be necessary to exclude the natures of the signs from the theory of prognostication, or impossible to avoid error in then retaining and making use of them; as the regularity of their spaces and distances, upon which their influence depends, would then be invaded and broken in upon.

## In modern astronomy

Two false dawns, [gegenschein](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gegenschein) (middle) and the rest of the zodiacal band of the [zodiacal light](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiacal_light), with the zodiac marked (visually crossed by the [Milky Way](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way)), in this composite image of the night sky above the northern and southern hemisphere

Astronomically, the zodiac defines a belt of space extending 8° or 9° in celestial latitude to the north and south of the [ecliptic](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic), within which the orbits of the Moon and the principal planets remain. It is a feature of the ecliptic coordinate system – a [celestial coordinate system](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinate_system) centered upon the ecliptic (the plane of the Earth's orbit and the Sun's apparent path), by which [celestial longitude](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_longitude) is measured in degrees east of the vernal equinox (the ascending intersection of the ecliptic and equator). The zodiac is narrow in angular terms because most of the Sun's planets have orbits that have only a slight [inclination](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_inclination) to the [orbital plane](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_plane_(astronomy)) of the Earth. Stars within the zodiac are subject to [occultations](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultation) by the Moon and other Solar System bodies. These events can be useful, for example, to estimate the cross-sectional dimensions of a [minor planet](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_planet), or check a star for a close companion.

Zodiacal light viewed from [Cerro Paranal](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_Paranal)

The Sun's placement upon the vernal equinox, which occurs annually around 21 March, defines the starting point for measurement, the first degree of which is historically known as the "[first point of Aries](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Point_of_Aries)". The first 30° along the ecliptic is nominally designated as the zodiac sign Aries, which no longer falls within the proximity of the constellation Aries since the effect of precession is to move the vernal point through the backdrop of visible constellations. It is currently located near the end of the constellation Pisces, having been within that constellation since the 2nd century AD. The subsequent 30° of the ecliptic is nominally designated the zodiac sign Taurus, and so on through the twelve signs of the zodiac so that each occupies 1⁄12th (30°) of the zodiac's great circle. Zodiac signs have never been used to determine the boundaries of astronomical constellations that lie in the vicinity of the zodiac, which are, and always have been, irregular in their size and shape.

The convention of measuring celestial longitude within individual signs was still being used in the mid-19th century, but modern astronomy now numbers degrees of celestial longitude continuously from 0° to 360°, rather than 0° to 30° within each sign. This coordinate system is primary used by astronomers for observations of Solar System objects.

The use of the zodiac as a means to determine astronomical measurement remained the main method for defining celestial positions by Western astronomers until the Renaissance, at which time preference moved to the equatorial coordinate system, which measures astronomical positions by [right ascension](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_ascension) and [declination](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declination) rather than the ecliptic-based definitions of celestial longitude and [celestial latitude](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_latitude). The orientation of equatorial coordinates are aligned with the Earth's axis of rotation, rather than the plane of the planet's orbit around the Sun.

The word "zodiac" is used in reference to the [zodiacal cloud](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiacal_cloud) of dust grains that move among the planets, and the [zodiacal light](//en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiacal_light) that originates from their scattering of sunlight. While its name is derived from the zodiac, the zodiacal light covers the entire night sky, with enhancements in certain directions.
