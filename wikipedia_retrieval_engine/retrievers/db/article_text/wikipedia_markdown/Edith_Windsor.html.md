**Edith Windsor** (née **Schlain**; June 20, 1929 – September 12, 2017) was an American [LGBT rights](/wiki/LGBT_rights_in_the_United_States) activist and a technology manager at [IBM](/wiki/IBM). She was the lead plaintiff in the 2013 [Supreme Court of the United States](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) case *[United States v. Windsor](/wiki/United_States_v._Windsor)*, which overturned Section 3 of the [Defense of Marriage Act](/wiki/Defense_of_Marriage_Act) and was considered a landmark legal victory for the [same-sex marriage movement in the United States](/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_the_United_States). The [Obama administration](/wiki/Obama_administration) and federal agencies extended rights, privileges and benefits to married same-sex couples because of the decision.

## Early life and education

Windsor was born in [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia), [Pennsylvania](/wiki/Pennsylvania), on June 20, 1929, the youngest of three children of James and Celia Schlain, a [Russian Jewish](/wiki/Russian_Jew) immigrant family of modest means. During her childhood, her family suffered as a result of the [Great Depression](/wiki/Great_Depression), and her father lost both his candy-and-ice-cream store and their home above it. In school, she at times experienced [anti-Semitism](/wiki/Anti-Semitism). Throughout school, she dated boys her age, but said later she recalls having crushes on girls.

Windsor received her bachelor's degree from [Temple University](/wiki/Temple_University) in 1950. In 1955, she began pursuing a master's degree in mathematics, which she obtained from [New York University](/wiki/New_York_University) in 1957. She then joined [IBM](/wiki/IBM), where she worked for the next sixteen years. During this time, she spent two semesters studying applied mathematics at [Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_University) on an IBM fellowship.

## Career

While attending New York University, Windsor worked for the university's math department, entering data into its [UNIVAC](/wiki/UNIVAC). She also worked as a programmer at [Combustion Engineering](/wiki/Combustion_Engineering), Inc., where she worked with physicists and the UNIVAC.

After receiving her master's degree in mathematics in 1957 from New York University, Windsor began work in senior technical and management positions at [IBM](/wiki/IBM) in 1958. Her work at IBM was primarily related to [systems architecture](/wiki/Systems_architecture) and implementation of [operating systems](/wiki/Operating_systems) and [natural language processors](/wiki/Natural_language_processors). Windsor began her career at IBM as a mainframe programmer. In May 1968, she attained the title designating the highest level technical position at IBM, senior systems programmer. Windsor worked at IBM for 16 years and was well known around IBM for her "top-notch debugging skills". She received the first IBM PC delivered in New York City. However, the company rejected her insurance form naming her partner Thea Spyer as a beneficiary. Windsor also assisted the Atomic Energy Commission, and was at one point even investigated by the FBI. Windsor feared that it was because of her closeted homosexuality. This was during the time right after the [Lavender Scare](/wiki/Lavender_Scare). She later found out it was because of her sister's ties to the Teachers' Union.

In 1975, Windsor left IBM and became the founding president of PC Classics, a consulting firm specializing in software development projects. During this time consulting, Windsor helped many LGBTQ groups become "tech literate". She helped many LGBTQ organizations computerize their mail systems.

## Personal life

Saul Windsor was Edie's older brother's best friend, whom she had known for many years and respected. They went to college together and during their third year, Saul proposed marriage and Edie accepted. Their relationship ended at one time during the engagement when Edie fell in love with a female classmate. However, after Windsor decided she did not want to live life as a lesbian, they reconciled and got married after graduation, in May 1951. They divorced less than one year afterward, on March 3, 1952, and she confided in him that she longed to be with women. Shortly after her divorce, Windsor left Philadelphia for [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City).

Windsor met Thea Spyer, an Amsterdam-born [psychologist](/wiki/Psychologist), in 1963 at Portofino, a restaurant in [Greenwich Village](/wiki/Greenwich_Village). When they initially met, each was already in a relationship. They occasionally saw each other at events over the next two years, but it was not until a trip to the East End of [Long Island](/wiki/Long_Island) in the late spring of 1965 that they began dating each other. To help keep the relationship a secret from her co-workers, Windsor invented a relationship with Spyer's fictional brother Willy – who was actually a childhood doll belonging to Windsor – to explain Spyer's phone calls to the office. In 1967, Spyer asked Windsor to marry, although it was not yet legal anywhere in the United States. Fearing that a traditional [engagement ring](/wiki/Engagement_ring) might expose Windsor's [sexual orientation](/wiki/Sexual_orientation) to her coworkers, Spyer instead proposed with a circular diamond pin.

Six months after getting engaged, Windsor and Spyer moved into an apartment in Greenwich Village. In 1968, they purchased a small house on Long Island together, where they went on vacation for the following forty summers. The couple often took trips both in the United States and internationally. They also entertained at their home frequently, with Spyer preparing meals, including an annual [Memorial Day](/wiki/Memorial_Day) weekend celebration of their anniversary.

In 1977, Spyer was diagnosed with progressive [multiple sclerosis](/wiki/Multiple_sclerosis). The disease caused a gradual, but ever-increasing [paralysis](/wiki/Paralysis). Windsor used her early retirement to become a full-time caregiver for Spyer, and the couple continued to adjust their daily behavior to accommodate.

Windsor and Spyer entered a domestic partnership in New York City in 1993. Registering on the first available day, they were issued certificate number eighty.

Spyer suffered a [heart attack](/wiki/Heart_attack) in 2002 and was diagnosed with [aortic stenosis](/wiki/Aortic_stenosis). In 2007, her doctors told her she had less than a year to live. New York had not yet legalized same-sex marriage, so the couple opted to marry in [Toronto](/wiki/Toronto), [Canada](/wiki/Canada), on May 22, 2007, with Canada's first openly gay judge, Justice [Harvey Brownstone](/wiki/Harvey_Brownstone), presiding, and with the assistance of a filmmaker and same-sex marriage activist familiar with the laws in both countries. An announcement of their wedding was published in *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*. Spyer died from complications related to her heart condition on February 5, 2009. After Spyer's death, Windsor was hospitalized with [stress cardiomyopathy](/wiki/Stress_cardiomyopathy).

On September 26, 2016, Windsor married Judith Kasen at New York City Hall. At the time of the wedding, Windsor was age 87 and Kasen was age 51.

Windsor was also a member of the non-denominational [Congregation Beit Simchat Torah](/wiki/Congregation_Beit_Simchat_Torah) synagogue, which has been self-described as the world's largest [LGBTQ](/wiki/LGBTQ) synagogue.

In October 2019, Windsor's memoir *A Wild and Precious Life* was published by [St. Martin's Press](/wiki/St._Martin%27s_Press). The writing was begun before Windsor's death in 2017 and was completed by her co-author [Joshua Lyon](/wiki/Joshua_Lyon). It was also released as an [audiobook](/wiki/Audiobook), read by Donna Postel and Joshua Lyon.

## Activism

Windsor with [Carolyn Maloney](/wiki/Carolyn_Maloney) in 2016

In June 1969, Windsor and Spyer returned from a vacation in Italy to discover the [Stonewall Riots](/wiki/Stonewall_Riots) had begun the night before. In the following years, the couple publicly participated in LGBT marches and events. They also lent their [Cadillac](/wiki/Cadillac) [convertible](/wiki/Convertible) to LGBT rights organizations.

Following her departure from IBM in 1975, she increased her involvement with LGBT organizations. She volunteered for the [Gay & Lesbian Advocates & Defenders](/wiki/Gay_%26_Lesbian_Advocates_%26_Defenders), the East End Gay Organization, the [LGBT Community Center](/wiki/Lesbian,_Gay,_Bisexual_%26_Transgender_Community_Center), 1994 Gay Games New York, and helped found Old Queers Acting Up, an improv group utilizing skits to address social justice issues. She served on the board of [Services & Advocacy for GLBT Elders (SAGE)](/wiki/Services_%26_Advocacy_for_GLBT_Elders) from 1986 to 1988 and again from 2005 to 2007.

Windsor continued to be a public advocate for same-sex marriage in the years following *United States v. Windsor*. She helped Senator [Dianne Feinstein](/wiki/Dianne_Feinstein) and Representative [Jerrold Nadler](/wiki/Jerrold_Nadler) introduce the [Respect for Marriage Act](/wiki/Respect_for_Marriage_Act) at a press conference in [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.), in 2011. She was also a prominent supporter of Israeli LGBT rights group [A Wider Bridge](/wiki/A_Wider_Bridge). In 2013, *[Time](/wiki/Time_(magazine))* magazine named Windsor as a finalist for their [Person of the Year](/wiki/Time_Person_of_the_Year) award, losing out only to Pope Francis. Later in life, she became an ardent supporter of New York City's largest LGBTQ+ band, the [Lesbian & Gay Big Apple Corps](/wiki/Lesbian_%26_Gay_Big_Apple_Corps), calling them "her band". They performed a concert called *The Roaring Music of Women: A Tribute to the Iconic Edie Windsor* in her honor on Saturday, April 7, 2018.

## *United States v. Windsor*

Main article: [United States v. Windsor](/wiki/United_States_v._Windsor)

Upon Spyer's death on February 5, 2009, Windsor became the executor and sole beneficiary of Spyer's estate, via a [revocable trust](/wiki/Revocable_trust). Windsor was required to pay $363,053 in federal estate taxes on her inheritance of her wife's estate. Had federal law recognized the validity of their marriage, Windsor would have qualified for an unlimited spousal deduction and paid no federal estate taxes.

Windsor sought to claim the federal [estate tax](/wiki/Inheritance_tax) [exemption](/wiki/Tax_exemption) for [surviving spouses](/wiki/Widow). She was barred from doing so by Section 3 of the [Defense of Marriage Act (DOMA)](/wiki/Defense_of_Marriage_Act) (codified at [1 U.S.C.](/wiki/Title_1_of_the_United_States_Code) [§ 7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/1/7)), which provided that the term "spouse" only applied to marriages between a man and woman. The [Internal Revenue Service](/wiki/Internal_Revenue_Service) found that the exemption did not apply to [same-sex marriages](/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_the_United_States), denied Windsor's claim, and compelled her to pay $363,053 in estate taxes.

In 2010 Windsor filed a lawsuit against the federal government in the [U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York](/wiki/U.S._District_Court_for_the_Southern_District_of_New_York), seeking a refund because DOMA singled out legally married same-sex couples for "differential treatment compared to other similarly situated couples without justification." In 2012, Judge [Barbara S. Jones](/wiki/Barbara_S._Jones) ruled that Section 3 of DOMA was unconstitutional under the due process guarantees of the Fifth Amendment and ordered the federal government to issue the tax refund, including interest. The [U.S. Second Circuit Court of Appeals](/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Second_Circuit) affirmed in a 2–1 decision later in 2012.

The [U.S. Supreme Court](/wiki/U.S._Supreme_Court) heard oral arguments in the case in March 2013, and on June 26 of that year issued a 5–4 decision affirming that Section 3 of DOMA was unconstitutional "as a deprivation of the liberty of the person protected by the Fifth Amendment."

## Recognition

Sign thanking Windsor at a rally supporting same-sex marriage

Windsor was honored by the [National Computing Conference](/wiki/Joint_Computer_Conference) in 1987 as a "pioneer in operating systems".

On Windsor's 70th birthday in 1999, the Edie Windsor Fund for Old Lesbians was gifted to Windsor by Spyer and their friends. It is maintained and administered by Open Meadows Foundation, and provides grants to projects for and by older lesbians.

A 2009 documentary, *[Edie & Thea: A Very Long Engagement](/wiki/Edie_%26_Thea:_A_Very_Long_Engagement)*, by Susan Muska and Greta Olfsdottir, documents Windsor and Spyer's life and wedding. The DVD of the film contains a full-length interview with Justice Harvey Brownstone, the Canadian judge who officiated at the Windsor/Spyer wedding.

She was the [grand marshal](/wiki/Grand_marshal) of the 2013 [New York City LGBT Pride March](/wiki/LGBT_Pride_March_(New_York_City)).

She was a runner-up, to [Pope Francis](/wiki/Pope_Francis), for 2013 [*Time* Person of the Year](/wiki/Time_Person_of_the_Year).

Windsor was honored as [The New Jewish Home's](/wiki/The_New_Jewish_Home) Eight Over Eighty Gala 2014 honoree.

On May 22, 2014, she received an honorary [Doctorate of Humane Letters](/wiki/Doctorate_of_Humane_Letters) from [Johns Hopkins University](/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University).

In June 2014 Windsor traveled back to Toronto, the city where she married Thea Spyer, to receive an award at [WorldPride](/wiki/WorldPride). While in Toronto she appeared on the [CTV Television Network](/wiki/CTV_Television_Network)'s national morning show, *Canada AM*, with Harvey Brownstone, the Toronto judge who officiated at her wedding.

On June 26, 2014, Windsor was featured on [Logo TV](/wiki/Logo_TV)'s 2014 LOGO Trailblazers.

In 2016, Lesbians Who Tech initiated the Edie Windsor Coding Scholarship Fund.

In 2017, the Midtown SAGE Center for LGBT seniors in Manhattan was renamed as The Edie Windsor SAGE Center in New York City.

In 2018, a block of South 13th Street in Philadelphia was designated as Edie Windsor Way.

In June 2019, Windsor was one of the inaugural fifty American "pioneers, trailblazers, and heroes" inducted on the [National LGBTQ Wall of Honor](/wiki/National_LGBTQ_Wall_of_Honor) within the [Stonewall National Monument](/wiki/Stonewall_National_Monument) (SNM) in [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City)'s [Stonewall Inn](/wiki/Stonewall_Inn). The SNM is the first [U.S. national monument](/wiki/National_monument_(United_States)) dedicated to [LGBTQ rights](/wiki/LGBT_rights_in_the_United_States) and [history](/wiki/History_of_LGBT_people), while the Wall's unveiling was timed to take place during the [50th anniversary](/wiki/Stonewall_50_%E2%80%93_WorldPride_NYC_2019) of the [Stonewall riots](/wiki/Stonewall_riots).

## Death

On September 12, 2017, Windsor's wife Judith Kasen-Windsor confirmed that Windsor had died in Manhattan, but did not specify a cause. Former US President [Bill Clinton](/wiki/Bill_Clinton), New York Governor [Andrew Cuomo](/wiki/Andrew_Cuomo), California US Senator [Dianne Feinstein](/wiki/Dianne_Feinstein), and various politicians and celebrities posted words of tribute on their [Twitter](/wiki/Twitter) accounts. Former Secretary of State [Hillary Clinton](/wiki/Hillary_Clinton) spoke at her funeral.
