**Daniel D. Tompkins** (June 21, 1774 – June 11, 1825) was an American politician who served as the fifth [vice president of the United States](/wiki/Vice_president_of_the_United_States) from 1817 to 1825. He previously served as the fourth [governor of New York](/wiki/Governor_of_New_York) from 1807 to 1817.

Born in [Scarsdale, New York](/wiki/Scarsdale,_New_York), on June 21, 1774, Tompkins practiced law in [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City) after graduating from [Columbia College](/wiki/Columbia_University). He was a delegate to the [1801 New York constitutional convention](/wiki/New_York_Constitution#Constitutional_Convention_of_1801) and served on the [New York Supreme Court](/wiki/New_York_Supreme_Court) from 1804 to 1807. In 1807, he defeated incumbent [Morgan Lewis](/wiki/Morgan_Lewis_(governor)) to become the governor of New York. He held that office from 1807 to 1817, serving for the duration of the [War of 1812](/wiki/War_of_1812). During the war, he often spent his own money to equip and pay the militia when the legislature was not in session or would not approve the necessary funds.

Tompkins was the [Democratic-Republican Party](/wiki/Democratic-Republican_Party_(United_States))'s vice-presidential nominee in the [1816 presidential election](/wiki/1816_United_States_presidential_election). The ticket of [James Monroe](/wiki/James_Monroe) and Tompkins easily prevailed over limited [Federalist](/wiki/Federalist_Party) opposition. He served as vice president from 1817 to 1825, and was the only 19th-century vice president to serve two full terms. In 1820, he sought another term as Governor of New York, but was defeated by [DeWitt Clinton](/wiki/DeWitt_Clinton). After the War of 1812, Tompkins was in poor physical and financial health, the latter condition stemming largely from his spending for the military effort during the war. He fell into alcoholism and was unable to re-establish fiscal solvency despite winning partial reimbursement from the federal government in 1823. He died 99 days after completing a second term and leaving office on 11 June 1825 at the age of 50.

## Name

Tompkins was baptized **Daniel Tompkins**, but added the middle initial "D." either before or during his time as a student at [Columbia College](/wiki/Columbia_College). According to his granddaughter, Helen T. Tompkins, this was to distinguish himself from another Daniel Tompkins who was a student there, though records of Columbia College do not list another Daniel Tompkins studying at Columbia at the time. There is controversy as to what the middle initial stood for; some have suggested "Decius". The generally accepted conclusion is that it did not stand for anything and served only to distinguish him from another Daniel Tompkins who perhaps studied with him in primary or secondary school.

## Early life

The Daniel D. Tompkins Memorial in [Scarsdale, New York](/wiki/Scarsdale,_New_York)

Daniel D. Tompkins was born on June 21, 1774, in Scarsdale, [Westchester County, New York](/wiki/Westchester_County,_New_York), at his family's estate, Fox Meadow. His parents were Sarah Ann (Hyatt) and Jonathan Griffin Tompkins. His older brother, [Caleb Tompkins](/wiki/Caleb_Tompkins) was a [U.S. Representative](/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) from 1817 to 1821. Daniel Tompkins graduated from Columbia College in [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City) in 1795, and then studied law with [James Kent](/wiki/James_Kent_(jurist)) and [Peter Jay Munro](/wiki/Peter_Jay_Munro). He was admitted to the [bar](/wiki/Bar_(law)) in 1797, and practiced in New York City.

## Political career

### Early years

Despite the [Federalist](/wiki/Federalist_Party) leanings of Kent and Munro, Tompkins entered politics as a [Democratic-Republican](/wiki/Democratic-Republican_Party). He was a delegate to the [New York State Constitutional Convention](/wiki/New_York_State_Constitutional_Convention) in 1801, and a member of the [New York State Assembly](/wiki/New_York_State_Assembly) in 1804. In 1804, he was elected [U.S. Representative](/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives). He resigned before the beginning of the term to accept, at age 30, an appointment as associate justice of the [New York Supreme Court of Judicature](/wiki/New_York_Supreme_Court), in which capacity he served from 1804 to 1807.

### Governor

Coat of Arms of Daniel D. Tompkins

On April 30, 1807, Tompkins was elected [Governor of New York](/wiki/Governor_of_New_York), defeating incumbent [Morgan Lewis](/wiki/Morgan_Lewis_(governor)) – by 35,074 votes to 30,989. He was 33 when elected and is the youngest governor of New York. He was reelected three times: in 1810, defeating [Jonas Platt](/wiki/Jonas_Platt) by 43,094 votes to 36,484; in 1813, defeating [Stephen Van Rensselaer](/wiki/Stephen_Van_Rensselaer) by 43,324 votes to 39,718; and 1816, defeating [Rufus King](/wiki/Rufus_King) by 45,412 votes to 38,647. Tompkins was supported by New York mayor [DeWitt Clinton](/wiki/DeWitt_Clinton) in his first run for Governor. But when Clinton challenged President [James Madison](/wiki/James_Madison) in the [1812 election](/wiki/1812_United_States_presidential_election), Tompkins broke with Clinton, and supported Madison.

During the [War of 1812](/wiki/War_of_1812), Tompkins proved to be one of the most effective war governors. He played an important role in reorganizing the [state militia](/wiki/State_militia) and promoted the formation of a standing state military force based on select [conscription](/wiki/Conscription). He declined an appointment as [United States Secretary of State](/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_State) by President James Madison in 1814, instead accepting appointment as commander of the federal military district that included New York City. Tompkins even financed New York's war effort with money borrowed on his personal credit. But he did not carefully document these very substantial expenditures, and was denied reimbursement.

[The Gradual Manumission Act of July 4, 1799](/wiki/History_of_slavery_in_New_York_(state)) provided for the eventual manumission of slave children born in New York after that date. In 1817, at Tompkins' suggestion, New York enacted emancipation of all slaves, to take effect on July 4, 1827. The ["Fifth of July" celebration](/wiki/Fifth_of_July_(New_York)) in New York commemorates the final outcome.

### Vice presidency (1817–1825)

Many New York Democratic-Republicans supported Tompkins for President in the [1816 election](/wiki/1816_United_States_presidential_election), but James Monroe received the party's nomination. Tompkins was instead elected Vice President as Monroe's running mate. Tompkins [was re-elected in 1820](/wiki/1820_United_States_presidential_election). He served from March 4, 1817, to March 4, 1825.

When Tompkins became Vice President, he was in poor health, due to a fall from a horse on November 3, 1814. His finances were also quite poor due to his unreimbursed war expenses. He also slipped into [alcoholism](/wiki/Alcoholism).

With poor physical and financial health, Tompkins spent much of his vice presidency outside of [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.), and made for a poor presiding officer of the Senate while it debated the [Missouri Compromise](/wiki/Missouri_Compromise) in 1820.

In April 1820, while serving as Vice President, he ran for Governor of New York against incumbent DeWitt Clinton. Tompkins lost, 45,900 votes to 47,447. He was a delegate to the 1821 [New York State Constitutional Convention](/wiki/New_York_State_Constitutional_Convention), serving as its president.

In 1823, Tompkins finally won compensation from the federal government, but he continued to drink heavily and was unable to resolve his business affairs.

## Developer

in 1815, Tompkins purchased the Van Buskirk Farm in [New Brighton](/wiki/New_Brighton,_Staten_Island) and property on [Grymes Hill](/wiki/Grymes_Hill,_Staten_Island) on the northeastern shore of [Staten Island](/wiki/Staten_Island). There he established a settlement named [Tompkinsville](/wiki/Tompkinsville,_Staten_Island). His main residence was located on Fort Hill in Tompkinsville; it burned down in 1874. Minthorne Street Hannah Street, and Westervelt Avenue in Tompkinsville are named for his son, daughter, and son-in-law.

He built a dock in the neighborhood in 1817 and offered daily ferry service between Staten Island and [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan). In 1816 he acquired additional land from the Church of St. Andrew, but his financial troubles later led the church to foreclose. The Westervelts then bought the property, which they later divided into many lots to sell off.

## Personal life

On February 20, 1798, Daniel Tompkins, 23, married 16-year-old Hannah Minthorne, the daughter of Mangle Minthorne, an assistant alderman of New York City. The couple had eight children, including Arietta Minthorn Tompkins (born July 31, 1800), who married a son of [Smith Thompson](/wiki/Smith_Thompson) in 1818; Hannah, wife of Dr. John S. Westervelt; and [(Mangle) Minthorne Tompkins](/wiki/Minthorne_Tompkins) (December 26, 1807 – June 5, 1881), who was the [Free Soil Party](/wiki/Free_Soil_Party) candidate for Governor of New York in 1852. Hannah and Minthorne were named after their mother. The Tompkinses also fostered young orphan [Henry Brewerton](/wiki/Henry_Brewerton) (1801–1879). Brewerton attended the [United States Military Academy](/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy) at West Point, and served as an Army engineer officer from 1819 to 1867.

Hannah was ill in the year before her husband became Vice President, and did not attend his inauguration. She survived him by nearly four years in Tompkinsville.

### Freemasonry

Apart from his political career, Tompkins was an active [Freemason](/wiki/Freemason) throughout his life. He was a member of Hiram Lodge 72, [Mount Pleasant, New York](/wiki/Mount_Pleasant,_New_York) and became [Grand Master](/wiki/Grand_Master_(Masonic)) of the [Grand Lodge of New York](/wiki/Grand_Lodge_of_New_York) from 1820 to 1822. The Daniel D. Tompkins Memorial Chapel at the Masonic Home in [Utica, New York](/wiki/Utica,_New_York), was built in his honor in 1911. The Grand Lodge of New York celebrated the centennial of the chapel on June 25, 2011.

He also served as the first Sovereign Grand Commander of the [Northern Masonic Jurisdiction](/wiki/Supreme_Council,_Scottish_Rite,_Northern_Jurisdiction,_USA) [Scottish Rite](/wiki/Scottish_Rite), a branch of Freemasonry. Tompkins served in this capacity from 1813 to 1825, although he did not devote much time to the newly formed group.

## Death

Tompkins died in [Tompkinsville](/wiki/Tompkinsville,_Staten_Island) on [Staten Island](/wiki/Staten_Island), on June 11, 1825, 10 days before his 51st birthday. He was interred in the Minthorne vault in the west yard of [St. Mark's Church in-the-Bowery](/wiki/St._Mark%27s_Church_in-the-Bowery), New York City, as was his wife. His post-vice presidency lifespan is the shortest of any Vice President, and he also lived the shortest life of any now-deceased Vice President. He was the youngest Vice President until [John C. Breckinridge](/wiki/John_C._Breckinridge) in 1857 at 36. He was the only 19th-century Vice President to serve two terms under the same President, and two full terms at all.

## Legacy

The cover to the vault in which Tompkins' remains were interred

The [Tompkinsville neighborhood](/wiki/Tompkinsville,_Staten_Island) of [Staten Island](/wiki/Staten_Island) is named for Tompkins, and streets in that neighborhood are named for his children. Tompkins [Masonic Lodge](/wiki/Masonic_lodge) #471 in that same section of Staten Island is also named for him. Tompkins is credited with being one of the founding members of the [Brighton Heights Reformed Church](/wiki/Brighton_Heights_Reformed_Church) on Staten Island. The church was founded in 1823, during his term as vice president. Its first meeting place was in New York Marine Hospital (then known as the Quarantine), a predecessor of the immigration facility on [Ellis Island](/wiki/Ellis_Island).

Four forts in New York State in the [War of 1812](/wiki/War_of_1812) were named for Governor Tompkins, in [Staten Island](/wiki/Fort_Tompkins_(Staten_Island)), [Sackets Harbor](/wiki/Fort_Tompkins_(Sackets_Harbor,_New_York)), [Buffalo](/wiki/Fort_Tompkins_(Buffalo,_New_York)), and [Plattsburgh](/wiki/Fort_Tompkins_(Plattsburgh,_New_York)).

Tompkins Park in [Bedford Stuyvesant, Brooklyn](/wiki/Bedford_Stuyvesant,_Brooklyn), New York (now called [Herbert Von King Park](/wiki/Herbert_Von_King_Park)) was named after Tompkins. The nearby Tompkins Avenue and Tompkins Public Houses are likewise named.

[Tompkins County](/wiki/Tompkins_County,_New_York) in New York, [Tompkins Square Park](/wiki/Tompkins_Square_Park) in Manhattan, Public School 69 Daniel D. Tompkins School in Staten Island, and the [Town of Tompkins](/wiki/Tompkins,_New_York) are named after him, as is Tompkins Road, running between [Post Road (NY-22)](/wiki/New_York_State_Route_22) and Fenimore Road in Scarsdale, New York.

[Tompkinsville, Kentucky](/wiki/Tompkinsville,_Kentucky), is named for Tompkins. It is the county seat of [Monroe County, Kentucky](/wiki/Monroe_County,_Kentucky), which is named for the President under whom Tompkins served as Vice President.

Tompkins was mentioned by [Kris Kringle](/wiki/Santa_Claus) in the 1947 film *[Miracle on 34th Street](/wiki/Miracle_on_34th_Street)*. The screenplay was incorrect, however, in that Kringle mentions that Tompkins served as vice president under [John Quincy Adams](/wiki/John_Quincy_Adams) when Adams's vice president was actually [John C. Calhoun](/wiki/John_C._Calhoun). Tompkins was the sixth vice president and Adams was the sixth president, leading to confusion in the script.

American actor and producer [Richard Kollmar](/wiki/Richard_Kollmar), husband of columnist and TV personality [Dorothy Kilgallen](/wiki/Dorothy_Kilgallen), was a great-great-grandchild of Tompkins.
