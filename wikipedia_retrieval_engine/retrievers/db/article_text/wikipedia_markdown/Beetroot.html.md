**Beetroot** or **beet** is the [taproot](/wiki/Taproot) portion of a *[Beta vulgaris](/wiki/Beta_vulgaris)* subsp. *vulgaris* plant in the Conditiva [Group](/wiki/Cultivar_group). The plant is a [root vegetable](/wiki/Root_vegetable) also known as the **table beet**, **garden beet**, or dinner beet, or else categorized by color as **red beet** or golden beet. Its leaves constitute a [leaf vegetable](/wiki/Leaf_vegetable) called **beet greens**. It is native to the [Azores](/wiki/Azores), [Western Europe](/wiki/Western_Europe) to the [Mediterranean](/wiki/Mediterranean) and [India](/wiki/India).
Beetroot can be eaten raw, roasted, steamed, or boiled. Beetroot can also be canned, either whole or cut up, and often are pickled, spiced, or served in a sweet-and-sour sauce.

It is one of several cultivated varieties of *Beta vulgaris* subsp. *vulgaris* grown for their edible taproots or leaves, [classified](/wiki/Taxonomy_(biology)) as belonging to the Conditiva [Group](/wiki/Cultivar_group). Other cultivars of the same subspecies include the [sugar beet](/wiki/Sugar_beet), the leaf vegetable known as spinach beet (Swiss [chard](/wiki/Chard)), and the [fodder](/wiki/Fodder) crop [mangelwurzel](/wiki/Mangelwurzel).

## Etymology

*Beta* is the ancient Latin name for beetroot, possibly of [Celtic origin](/wiki/Celts), becoming *bete* in [Old English](/wiki/Old_English). *Root* derives from the late Old English *rōt*, itself from [Old Norse](/wiki/Old_Norse) *rót*.

## History

The domestication of beetroot can be traced to the emergence of an [allele](/wiki/Allele) that enables [biennial](/wiki/Biennial_plant) harvesting of leaves and taproot. Beetroot was domesticated in the ancient Middle East, primarily for their greens, and were grown by the Ancient Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans. By the Roman era, it is thought that they were also cultivated for their roots. From the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages), beetroot was used to treat various conditions, especially illnesses relating to digestion and the blood. [Bartolomeo Platina](/wiki/Bartolomeo_Platina) recommended taking beetroot with [garlic](/wiki/Garlic) to nullify the effects of "garlic-breath".

During the middle of the 17th century, [wine](/wiki/Wine) often was colored with beetroot juice.

Food shortages in Europe [following World War I](/wiki/Aftermath_of_World_War_I) caused great hardships, including cases of *[mangelwurzel](/wiki/Mangelwurzel)* disease, as relief workers called it. It was symptomatic of eating only beetroot.

Beetroot was grown in many [victory gardens](/wiki/Victory_garden) during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), in part because the species was seen as an indicator of [soil pH](/wiki/Soil_pH) with good growth a sign that soil acidity was not too strong.

## Archaeology

The archaeological record for beet is sparse. Identifying the recovered remains is a complicated endeavor given the morphological similarities between charred domestic and wild fruits. However, written records offer more clarity; some date back to the eighth century BC in Babylonia. By the first century BC, both Roman and Jewish texts suggest beetroots were present in the Mediterranean.
Physical specimens have been found in the Third-Dynasty Saqqara pyramid at Memphis in Egypt and in the Netherlands in a Neolithic site. Charred remains from Roman sites in Germany are more significant, since they provide strong physical evidence of evidence-based domestication.

## Culinary use

Usually, the deep purple roots of beetroot are eaten boiled, roasted, or raw, and either alone or combined with any salad vegetable. The green, leafy portion of the beetroot is also edible. The young leaves can be added raw to salads, while the mature leaves are most commonly served boiled or steamed, in which case they have a taste and texture similar to [spinach](/wiki/Spinach). Beetroot can be roasted, boiled or steamed, peeled, and then eaten warm with or without butter; cooked, pickled, and then eaten cold as a [condiment](/wiki/Condiment); or peeled, shredded raw, and then eaten as a salad. Pickled beetroot is a traditional food in many countries.

### Australia and New Zealand

In [Australia](/wiki/Australia) and [New Zealand](/wiki/New_Zealand), sliced pickled beetroot is a common ingredient in traditional [hamburgers](/wiki/Hamburger#Australia_and_New_Zealand).

### Eastern Europe

In [Eastern Europe](/wiki/Eastern_European_cuisine), beetroot soup, such as [borscht](/wiki/Borscht) [Ukrainian] and *barszcz czerwony* [Polish], is common. In [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukrainian_cuisine), a related dish called *shpundra* is also common; this hearty beetroot stew, often made with pork belly or ribs, is sometimes referred to as a thicker version of borscht. In [Poland](/wiki/Polish_cuisine) and Ukraine, beetroot is combined with [horseradish](/wiki/Horseradish) to form *ćwikła* or *бурячки* (buryachky), which is traditionally eaten with cold cuts and sandwiches, but often also added to a meal consisting of meat and potatoes.

Similarly, in [Serbia](/wiki/Serbian_cuisine), beetroot (referred to by the local name *cvekla*) is used as a winter salad, seasoned with salt and vinegar, with meat dishes.

As an addition to horseradish, it is also used to produce the "red" variety of *[chrain](/wiki/Chrain)*, a condiment in [Ashkenazi Jewish](/wiki/Jewish_cuisine), [Hungarian](/wiki/Hungarian_cuisine), Polish, [Lithuanian](/wiki/Lithuanian_cuisine), [Russian](/wiki/Russian_cuisine), and Ukrainian cuisine.

Cold beetroot soup called *ŝaltibarščiai* is often eaten in Lithuania. Traditionally it consists of kefir, boiled beetroot, cucumber, dill, spring onions and can be eaten with boiled eggs and potatoes.

[Botvinya](/wiki/Botvinya) is a traditional Russian [cold soup](/wiki/Cold_soup) made from leftover beet greens and chopped beetroots, typically with bread and [kvass](/wiki/Kvass) added. Botvinya got its name from the Russian *botva*, which means "root vegetable greens", referring to beet plant leaves.

Svekolnik, or [svyokolnik](/wiki/Russian_cuisine#Cold_soups), is yet another Russian beet-based soup, typically distinguished from borscht in that vegetables for svekolnik are cooked raw and not sautéed, while many types of borscht typically include sautéed carrots and other vegetables. Svekolnik got its name from *svyokla*, the Russian word for "beet." Sometimes, various types of cold borscht are also called "svekolnik".

### India

In [Indian cuisine](/wiki/Indian_cuisine), chopped, cooked, spiced beetroot is a common side dish. Yellow-colored beetroots are grown on a very small scale for home consumption.

### North America

Besides standard fruit and vegetable dishes, certain varieties of beets are sometimes used as a garnish to a [tart](/wiki/Tart).

### Northern Europe

A common dish in Sweden and elsewhere in the Nordic countries is [biff à la Lindström](/wiki/Biff_%C3%A0_la_Lindstr%C3%B6m), a variant of meatballs or burgers, with chopped or grated beetroot added to the [minced meat](/wiki/Ground_meat). Swedish [pyttipanna](/wiki/Pyttipanna) is also traditionally served with pickled beetroot.

In [Northern Germany](/wiki/Northern_Germany), beetroot is mashed with *[Labskaus](/wiki/Labskaus)* or added as its side dish.

## Industrial production and other uses

A large proportion of commercial production is processed into boiled and sterilized beetroot or [pickles](/wiki/Pickling).

[Betanin](/wiki/Betanin), obtained from the roots, is used industrially as red [food colorant](/wiki/Food_coloring) to enhance the color and flavor of [tomato paste](/wiki/Tomato_paste), sauces, desserts, [jams](/wiki/Jam) and jellies, [ice cream](/wiki/Ice_cream), candy, and [breakfast cereals](/wiki/Breakfast_cereal). When beetroot juice is used, it is most stable in foods with low water content, such as frozen novelties and fruit fillings.

Beetroot can be used to make wine.

### Nitrate

Beets tend to accumulate nitrates from soil. Excessive nitrates pose a risk to health because they can be converted into [nitrosamine](/wiki/Nitrosamine) in the human stomach. On the other hand, an appropriate amount of nitrates is responsible for the blood-pressure-lowering effect of beetroot juice (see above) and possible other health benefits. In any case, it would be desirable to have a way to remove excess nitrates from beetroot juice.

*[Paracoccus denitrificans](/wiki/Paracoccus_denitrificans)* (Pd) bacterium can break down the nitrates, but an intermediate of the breakdown is nitrite, which leads to a risk of nitrosamine formation during denitrification by Pd. Adding [ascorbic acid](/wiki/Ascorbic_acid) prevents nitrosamine formation in this bacterial process, ensuring safe removal of nitrates. Denitrifying concentrated beet juice requires [halophilic](/wiki/Halophilic) bacteria due to the higher [osmotic concentration](/wiki/Osmotic_concentration), with *Nesterenkonia halobia* (formerly *Micrococcus halobius*) being the most efficient out of three species tested.

## Pigment

The red color compound betanin is a [betalain](/wiki/Betalain) in the category of [betacyanins](/wiki/Betacyanins). It is not broken down in the body, but excreted in urine and stools.

In higher concentrations, this may temporarily cause urine or stools to assume a reddish color, in the case of urine a condition called [beeturia](/wiki/Beeturia). Although harmless, this effect may cause initial concern as a medical problem due to a visual similarity with [blood in the stool](/wiki/Blood_in_stool), blood passing through the anus ([hematochezia](/wiki/Hematochezia)), or blood in the urine ([hematuria](/wiki/Hematuria)).
