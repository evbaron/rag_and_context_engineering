# Benjamin Waterhouse Hawkins

**Benjamin Waterhouse Hawkins** (8 February 1807 – 27 January 1894) was an English [sculptor](/wiki/Sculpture) and [natural history](/wiki/Natural_history) artist renowned for his work on the life-size models of [dinosaurs in the Crystal Palace Park](/wiki/Crystal_Palace_Dinosaurs) in south London. The models, accurately made using the latest scientific knowledge, created a sensation at the time. Hawkins was also a noted lecturer on [zoological](/wiki/Zoological) topics.

## Education and early career

Benjamin Waterhouse Hawkins was born in [Bloomsbury](/wiki/Bloomsbury), London, on [Devonshire Street](/wiki/Devonshire_Street_(Westminster)) on 8 February 1807, the son of artist Thomas Hawkins and his wife Louisa Anna Waterhouse, the daughter of a plantation owner in Jamaica. He studied at St. Aloysius College, and learned sculpture from [William Behnes](/wiki/William_Behnes). At the age of 20, he began to study natural history and later geology. He contributed illustrations to *The Zoology of the Voyage of HMS Beagle*.

During the 1840s, he produced studies of living animals in [Knowsley](/wiki/Knowsley_Hall) Park, near [Liverpool](/wiki/Liverpool) for [Edward Stanley, 13th Earl of Derby](/wiki/Edward_Stanley,_13th_Earl_of_Derby). The park was one of the largest private menageries in Victorian England and Hawkins' work was later published with [John Edward Gray](/wiki/John_Edward_Gray)'s text as *"Gleanings from the Menagerie at Knowsley"* . Over the same period Hawkins exhibited four sculptures at the [Royal Academy](/wiki/Royal_Academy) between 1847 and 1849, and was elected a member of the [Society of Arts](/wiki/Society_of_Arts) in 1846 and a fellow of the [Linnean Society](/wiki/Linnean_Society) in 1847. Fellowship of the [Geological Society of London](/wiki/Geological_Society_of_London) followed in 1854.

## Great Exhibition

Further information: [Crystal Palace Dinosaurs](/wiki/Crystal_Palace_Dinosaurs)

Meanwhile, possibly due to Derby's connections, Hawkins was appointed assistant superintendent of the [Great Exhibition of 1851](/wiki/Great_Exhibition_of_1851) in London. The following year, he was appointed by the Crystal Palace company to create 33 [life-size concrete models of extinct dinosaurs](/wiki/Crystal_Palace_Dinosaurs) to be placed in the south London park to which the great glass exhibition hall was to be relocated. In this work, which took some three years, he collaborated with Sir [Richard Owen](/wiki/Richard_Owen) and other leading scientific figures of the time: Owen estimated the size and overall shape of the animals, leaving Hawkins to sculpt the models according to Owen's directions.

A dinner was held inside the mould used to make the *[Iguanodon](/wiki/Iguanodon)*. The dinner party, hosted by Owen on 31 December 1853, garnered attention in the press. Most of the sculptures are still on display in [Crystal Palace Park](/wiki/Crystal_Palace_Park).

## United States

Benjamin Waterhouse Hawkins' mounted *[Hadrosaurus](/wiki/Hadrosaurus)* in Philadelphia in 1868, making it the first mounted dinosaur skeleton in the world.

In 1868, he traveled to the [United States](/wiki/United_States) to deliver a series of lectures. Working with the scientist [Joseph Leidy](/wiki/Joseph_Leidy), Hawkins designed and cast an almost complete [skeleton](/wiki/Skeleton) of [Hadrosaurus](/wiki/Hadrosaurus) foulkii which was then displayed at the [Academy of Natural Sciences](/wiki/Academy_of_Natural_Sciences) in [Philadelphia](/wiki/Philadelphia). Supported on an iron framework in a lifelike pose, this was the world's first mounted dinosaur skeleton.

Hawkins was later commissioned to produce models for [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City)'s [Central Park](/wiki/Central_Park) museum similar to these he had created in Sydenham. He established a studio on the original site of the [American Museum of Natural History](/wiki/American_Museum_of_Natural_History) in [Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan), and planned to create a [Paleozoic Museum](/wiki/Paleozoic_Museum). During his ten years in America (1868–1878), Hawkins designed exhibit halls for the [Smithsonian Institution](/wiki/Smithsonian_Institution) in [Washington, D.C.](/wiki/Washington,_D.C.), and began to create an enormous paleontological museum for New York City. The museum was to have been in Central Park. His work was all destroyed in 1871 by [Henry Hilton](/wiki/Henry_Hilton), the corrupt and bizarre-acting Treasurer and VP of Central Park, but was for many decades thought to have been the work of Hilton's employer, William "Boss" Tweed, a corrupt politician who wasn't adequately compensated for his patronage. However, Tweed himself was fighting scandals regarding his corrupt dealings at the time, and was later proved innocent of the destruction of Hawkins' models in 2023, when the real culprit was revealed through reexamination of historical records and annual reports and minutes. Hilton's motivations towards the vandalism are largely unknown, but may have been personal, with Hilton being purported to have told Hawkins that he "should not bother with "dead animals", as there was enough to do among the living", and that Hilton had little understanding or appreciation for art or nature, with several instances being recorded of him whitewashing priceless relics, statues and artifacts in bizarre acts of vandalism. Furthermore, Hilton had been placed in charge of establishing the American Museum of Natural History, and it is possible he wanted to eliminate the planned Paleozoic Museum, which he saw as competition.

Following the tragic loss of his studio through destruction of all of his dinosaur models at the hands of Hilton's vandals, he returned to England in 1874, but almost immediately returned, doing dinosaur reconstructions at [Princeton University](/wiki/Princeton_University) (then called the College of New Jersey) in Princeton, New Jersey (where he also created paintings of dinosaurs). These paintings remain in the collection of the [Princeton University Art Museum](/wiki/Princeton_University_Art_Museum). Hawkins also worked at the Centennial Exhibition of 1876 in Philadelphia. He again returned to Britain in 1878.

## Personal life and death

Hawkins married Mary Selina Green on 4 July 1826. The couple had five children. On 23 April 1836, Hawkins bigamously married painter Frances Louisa Keenan, a daughter of artist John Keenan. On 17 May 1883, they remarried in a [civil ceremony](/wiki/Civil_ceremony). Hawkins kept in touch with Mary and her children, but lived with Louisa, having two additional daughters. On his 1874 return to England, he seems to have become estranged from Louisa. He was living with his son by Mary, amidst what he described a "climax of domestic troubles" thought to indicate that Louisa had finally learned that their 38-year marriage had been invalid, and this may have led to his precipitous return to America in 1875. After his second return to England, he moved to [West Brompton](/wiki/West_Brompton) to be near his first wife, Mary, who was ill. Mary died in 1880.

In 1883, Hawkins again married Louisa, although since they were not cohabitants at the time this was probably done for legalistic reasons (to legitimize their children), and they apparently never reconciled before her death the next year. Hawkins suffered a debilitating stroke in 1889, leading to erroneous reports of his death. He died in [Putney](/wiki/Putney) on 27 January 1894. There is a [blue plaque](/wiki/Blue_plaque) at 22 Belvedere Road ("Fossil Villa") in [Upper Norwood](/wiki/Upper_Norwood), commemorating where he lived between 1856 and 1872.

## Legacy

[Robert J. Sawyer](/wiki/Robert_J._Sawyer)'s 1994 novel *End of an Era* mentions the famous New Year's Eve 1853 dinner party inside the *Iguanodon*, citing both Hawkins and Sir Richard Owen by name. A biography of Hawkins titled *All In The Bones* was published.
