The [41st Canadian federal election](/wiki/2011_Canadian_federal_election) was held on May 2, 2011. It resulted in a [Conservative](/wiki/Conservative_Party_of_Canada) [majority government](/wiki/Majority_government) under the leadership of [Prime Minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Canada) [Stephen Harper](/wiki/Stephen_Harper). It was the third consecutive election win for Harper, and with 166 of 308 seats, giving them a majority government for the first time in their eight-year history. It was also the first right-of-centre majority government since the [Progressive Conservatives](/wiki/Progressive_Conservative_Party_of_Canada) won their last majority in 1988. The Conservative Party won 39.62% of the popular vote, an increase of 1.96%, and posted a net gain of 24 seats in the [House of Commons](/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_Canada).

The election resulted in significant upheaval within the opposition parties, as the [New Democratic Party](/wiki/New_Democratic_Party_(Canada)) (NDP) rode an "orange surge" in the polls during the campaign to 103 seats, becoming [Her Majesty's Loyal Opposition](/wiki/Official_Opposition_(Canada)) for the first time in party history. The total eclipsed the party's previous best of 43 seats in 1988. The Liberals, however, were reduced to third party status nationwide, returning only 34 MPs—less than half of what they had at dissolution. It was the first time in Canadian history that the Liberals were not one of the top two parties in the house. [Green Party](/wiki/Green_Party_of_Canada) leader [Elizabeth May](/wiki/Elizabeth_May) won in her riding, becoming the first Green Party candidate elected to a governmental body in Canada, and to a national body in North America.

Following their staggering defeats, including losing their own seats, Bloc Québécois leader [Gilles Duceppe](/wiki/Gilles_Duceppe) and Liberal leader [Michael Ignatieff](/wiki/Michael_Ignatieff) both announced their resignations as party leaders.

## Party summaries

### Conservatives

The Conservatives, who had been leading in the polls since the [writs were dropped](/wiki/Dropping_the_writ), won 166 seats - enough for the first Conservative [majority government](/wiki/Majority_government) since the Progressive Conservative-[Canadian Alliance](/wiki/Canadian_Alliance) merger that formed the party in 2003. Notably, the Tories made significant inroads in [Toronto](/wiki/Toronto), taking eight seats there. While the Tories had won a few seats in the Toronto suburbs since the PC-Canadian Alliance merger, this was the first time a right-of-centre party had won seats in the former [Metro Toronto](/wiki/Metro_Toronto) itself since the PC meltdown of 1993. Combined with their traditionally heavy support in the [west](/wiki/Western_Canada), this was enough to win a 14-seat majority with 39.62 percent of the national [popular vote](/wiki/Direct_election) - a result also notable for being the first time the modern Conservative party successfully polled a larger share of the vote than the combined tally of the PC and CA parties in [the election preceding their merger](/wiki/2000_Canadian_federal_election).

Despite winning a majority government, the Conservatives lost over half their seats in Quebec to the NDP, retaining only five seats in that province.

### New Democrats

The NDP had a major [windfall](/wiki/Windfall_gain), emerging as a truly national party for the first time in its [50-year history](/wiki/History_of_the_New_Democratic_Party). They won 103 seats—more than double their previous high (when they won 43 seats in [1988](/wiki/1988_Canadian_federal_election)). Much of this was due to a breakthrough in [Quebec](/wiki/Quebec), a province where they had been more or less nonexistent for the better part of their history. From only one seat at [dissolution](/wiki/Dissolution_of_parliament), the NDP took 59 of 75 seats there, dominating [Montreal](/wiki/Montreal) and sweeping [Quebec City](/wiki/Quebec_City) and the [Outaouais](/wiki/Outaouais_(region)). By comparison, the NDP had only won one other seat in Quebec in its entire history prior to 2011 (and had held only one other seat, via a floor-crossing). It had not even been fully organized in the province since 1990, when its Quebec wing seceded to preach [sovereigntism](/wiki/Quebec_sovereignty). The 59 seats won by the NDP in Quebec is the most won by any party in that province since the [Progressive Conservatives](/wiki/Progressive_Conservative_Party_of_Canada) won 63 seats there in 1988. In several cases, NDP candidates in Quebec won handily even though they didn't even actively campaign.

Among the new NDP MPs were several university students. Five members of the [McGill University](/wiki/McGill_University) NDP club—[Charmaine Borg](/wiki/Charmaine_Borg), [Matthew Dubé](/wiki/Matthew_Dub%C3%A9), [Mylène Freeman](/wiki/Myl%C3%A8ne_Freeman), [Laurin Liu](/wiki/Laurin_Liu), and [Jamie Nicholls](/wiki/Jamie_Nicholls_(politician))—were elected from Montreal-area ridings. Liu is the youngest woman ever elected to Parliament. Also elected was [Pierre-Luc Dusseault](/wiki/Pierre-Luc_Dusseault), a freshman at the [Université de Sherbrooke](/wiki/Universit%C3%A9_de_Sherbrooke); his victory in [Sherbrooke, Quebec](/wiki/Sherbrooke_(federal_electoral_district)) makes him the [youngest MP in Canadian history](/wiki/Electoral_firsts_in_Canada) (he only turned 20 two days before he was sworn in).

However, they were unable to make much of an impact in their former western heartland. They actually lost [Elmwood—Transcona](/wiki/Elmwood%E2%80%94Transcona), the former seat of longtime MP and former [deputy leader](/wiki/Deputy_leader) [Bill Blaikie](/wiki/Bill_Blaikie), by only 300 votes.

### Liberals

Winning only 34 seats, the Liberals suffered the worst result in their [history](/wiki/History_of_the_Liberal_Party_of_Canada). They sat as the third party in the [41st Parliament](/wiki/41st_Canadian_Parliament), the first since [Confederation](/wiki/Canadian_Confederation) where the Liberals did not form either the [Government](/wiki/Government_of_Canada) or the [Official Opposition](/wiki/Official_Opposition_(Canada)) in the House of Commons. This was the worst showing for an incumbent Official Opposition party in terms of seats, and the lowest percentage for a national Official Opposition party (the Bloc Québécois in [1997](/wiki/1997_Canadian_federal_election) won more seats with a smaller vote share on account of its being a regional party).

The Liberals' poor showing was largely due to a collapse of their support in [Montreal](/wiki/Montreal) and [Toronto](/wiki/Toronto), which had been the backbones of Liberal support for almost two decades. With few exceptions, their support in Toronto flowed to the Tories, while most of their base in Montreal switched to the NDP.

In 2008, they won 20 out of the 23 ridings fully or partially within Toronto. However, in 2011, they only won six, losing 6 to the NDP and 9 to the Conservatives. Additionally, after going into the election holding 30 of the 44 seats in the [Greater Toronto Area](/wiki/Greater_Toronto_Area), they only won seven in 2011.

In Montreal, the Liberals lost five of their 12 seats, and came close to losing several more. Most notably, they came within 2,500 votes of losing [Mount Royal](/wiki/Mount_Royal_(electoral_district)), long reckoned as the safest Liberal riding in the nation.

Liberal leader [Michael Ignatieff](/wiki/Michael_Ignatieff) lost his seat of [Etobicoke—Lakeshore](/wiki/Etobicoke%E2%80%94Lakeshore_(federal_electoral_district)) to first time challenger [Bernard Trottier](/wiki/Bernard_Trottier) by a margin of 5.27% of the total votes. Other famous MP's who also lost their seats are [Ken Dryden](/wiki/Ken_Dryden) ([York Centre](/wiki/York_Centre_(federal_electoral_district))), [Dan McTeague](/wiki/Dan_McTeague) ([Pickering—Scarborough East](/wiki/Pickering%E2%80%94Scarborough_East_(federal_electoral_district))), [Gerard Kennedy](/wiki/Gerard_Kennedy) ([Parkdale—High Park](/wiki/Parkdale%E2%80%94High_Park_(federal_electoral_district))), [Ujjal Dosanjh](/wiki/Ujjal_Dosanjh) ([Vancouver South](/wiki/Vancouver_South_(federal_electoral_district))) and [Joe Volpe](/wiki/Joe_Volpe) ([Eglinton—Lawrence](/wiki/Eglinton%E2%80%94Lawrence_(federal_electoral_district))).

All told, the Liberals only won 11 seats in Ontario (all but four in Toronto) and seven in Quebec (all in Montreal)—the fewest the party has ever won in either province. They went into the next Parliament holding only four seats west of Ontario ([Winnipeg North](/wiki/Winnipeg_North), [Wascana](/wiki/Wascana), [Vancouver Centre](/wiki/Vancouver_Centre) and [Vancouver Quadra](/wiki/Vancouver_Quadra)).

### Bloc Québécois

The Bloc was practically eliminated from the scene, losing 43 seats. This reduced them to a [rump](/wiki/Rump_party) of four seats, only a third of the number required for [official party status](/wiki/Official_party_status). In many cases, they lost seats they held since their [debut performance](/wiki/1993_Canadian_federal_election) in 1993. With few exceptions, their support bled over to the NDP. Notably, the Bloc lost all but one seat in the Montreal area. This included all of their seats in the eastern part of the city, the birthplace of the sovereigntist movement. The Bloc went into the election holding all but one seat in eastern Montreal, but lost all of them to the NDP. They also lost all or most of their seats in their longstanding strongholds in the rest of the province, such as Quebec City and central Quebec. Several Bloc MPs who had never had serious difficulty being reelected ended up losing their seats in landslides. Bloc leader [Gilles Duceppe](/wiki/Gilles_Duceppe), at the time the longest-tenured party leader in Canada, lost his seat in [Laurier—Sainte-Marie](/wiki/Laurier%E2%80%94Sainte-Marie) to NDP challenger [Hélène Laverdière](/wiki/H%C3%A9l%C3%A8ne_Laverdi%C3%A8re).

### Greens

Despite losing a significant share of the national vote compared to the 2008 election, Green Party leader [Elizabeth May](/wiki/Elizabeth_May) became the first Green Party member elected to the Canadian Parliament.

### Incumbents defeated

[Liberal Party](/wiki/Liberal_Party_of_Canada) leader [Michael Ignatieff](/wiki/Michael_Ignatieff) lost his riding of [Etobicoke—Lakeshore](/wiki/Etobicoke%E2%80%94Lakeshore_(federal_electoral_district)) to [Bernard Trottier](/wiki/Bernard_Trottier), a [Conservative](/wiki/Conservative_Party_of_Canada), and the following day he announced he would resign as Liberal leader. Gilles Duceppe, leader of the [Bloc Québécois](/wiki/Bloc_Qu%C3%A9b%C3%A9cois) and incumbent in [Laurier—Sainte-Marie](/wiki/Laurier%E2%80%94Sainte-Marie) was defeated by Hélène Laverdière of the [NDP](/wiki/New_Democratic_Party_(Canada)) and announced his intention to resign as leader of the Bloc.

Four Cabinet ministers, [Lawrence Cannon](/wiki/Lawrence_Cannon) ([Foreign Affairs](/wiki/Minister_of_Foreign_Affairs_(Canada))), [Gary Lunn](/wiki/Gary_Lunn) ([Sport](/wiki/Minister_of_State_(Sport))), [Jean-Pierre Blackburn](/wiki/Jean-Pierre_Blackburn) ([Veterans Affairs](/wiki/Minister_of_Veterans_Affairs_(Canada)) and Agriculture), and [Josée Verner](/wiki/Jos%C3%A9e_Verner) ([Intergovernmental Affairs](/wiki/Minister_of_Intergovernmental_Affairs_(Canada)) and [Francophonie](/wiki/Minister_responsible_for_the_Francophonie_(Canada))) lost their seats. Lunn lost to [Green Party](/wiki/Green_Party_of_Canada) leader [Elizabeth May](/wiki/Elizabeth_May), and the NDP won the other three seats.

### Open seats

The Bloc gained [Haute-Gaspésie—La Mitis—Matane—Matapédia](/wiki/Haute-Gasp%C3%A9sie%E2%80%94La_Mitis%E2%80%94Matane%E2%80%94Matap%C3%A9dia) by the largest margin of victory of their four seats won and vacated by a long-standing member of the Bloc Québécois.

The Conservatives gained [Calgary Centre-North](/wiki/Calgary_Centre-North) and [Prince George—Peace River](/wiki/Prince_George%E2%80%94Peace_River), both vacated by long-standing Conservatives.
