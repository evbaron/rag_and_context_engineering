The **1984 NBA World Championship Series** was the championship round of the [National Basketball Association](/wiki/National_Basketball_Association)'s (NBA) [1983–84 season](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_NBA_season), and the culmination of the [season's playoffs](/wiki/1984_NBA_playoffs). The [Eastern Conference](/wiki/Eastern_Conference_(NBA)) champion [Boston Celtics](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Boston_Celtics_season) defeated the [Western Conference](/wiki/Western_Conference_(NBA)) champion [Los Angeles Lakers](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Los_Angeles_Lakers_season) in seven games. Celtics forward [Larry Bird](/wiki/Larry_Bird) averaged 27 points and 14 rebounds a game during the series, earning the [NBA Finals Most Valuable Player](/wiki/Bill_Russell_NBA_Finals_Most_Valuable_Player_Award) (MVP). The 1984 NBA Finals has been often regarded as one of the greatest finals ever played, and is considered the "peak" of the 1980s Celtics-Lakers rivalry

This series was a rematch of the Los Angeles Lakers and Boston Celtics after their rivalry was revived in 1979 with the [Magic Johnson](/wiki/Magic_Johnson)–[Larry Bird](/wiki/Larry_Bird) pair entering the league. After alternating wins with the Lakers, the Celtics won Game 7 and the series with a score of 111–102.

This was the last NBA Finals to use the 2–2–1–1–1 format until [2014](/wiki/2014_NBA_Finals). [The following year](/wiki/1985_NBA_Finals), the NBA Finals format was changed to 2–3–2 after Celtics president [Red Auerbach](/wiki/Red_Auerbach) complained to NBA Commissioner [David Stern](/wiki/David_Stern) about the constant travel during the Finals (at the time, all NBA teams still traveled primarily by commercial aircraft, and players were sometimes forced to cram themselves into coach seats if first or business class seats were not available). Although the 2–2–1–1–1 format remained intact for the remainder of the [playoff rounds](/wiki/NBA_playoffs), the 2–3–2 format would be used up through [2013](/wiki/2013_NBA_Finals).

As of 2025, [MLB](/wiki/Major_League_Baseball) is the only major professional sports league to retain the 2-3-2 format for its playoff series.

## Background

The seeds of the 1984 Finals were first sown five years earlier, during the [1979 NCAA Men's Division I Basketball Tournament](/wiki/1979_NCAA_Men%27s_Division_I_Basketball_Tournament). In the final game of the tournament, Larry Bird and his erstwhile unbeaten [Indiana State Sycamores](/wiki/Indiana_State_Sycamores_men%27s_basketball) lost to Magic Johnson and his [Michigan State Spartans](/wiki/Michigan_State_Spartans_men%27s_basketball) by the score of 75–64. After the tournament, both entered the NBA in the [1979–80 season](/wiki/1979%E2%80%9380_NBA_season) with high expectations. Bird, who was selected 6th in the [1978 NBA draft](/wiki/1978_NBA_draft) but committed back to Indiana State for his senior season, was named [Rookie of the Year](/wiki/NBA_Rookie_of_the_Year_Award) after leading the Celtics to a 32-game turnaround from the previous year, going from 29 to 61 wins. The expected Celtics–Lakers finals, however, never happened. The [Philadelphia 76ers](/wiki/1979%E2%80%9380_Philadelphia_76ers_season) defeated the Celtics in the conference finals before losing to the Lakers in the [1980 NBA Finals](/wiki/1980_NBA_Finals), with Johnson earning [Finals MVP](/wiki/Bill_Russell_NBA_Finals_Most_Valuable_Player_Award) honors for his Game 6 performance. Since then Bird won a championship in [1981](/wiki/1981_NBA_Finals), then Magic led the Lakers to the finals in [1982](/wiki/1982_NBA_Finals) and [1983](/wiki/1983_NBA_Finals), winning in the former.

### Boston Celtics

Main article: [1983–84 Boston Celtics season](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Boston_Celtics_season)

In the [1983–84 season](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_NBA_season), the Celtics won 62 games to lead the league. The Celtics were led by Bird, who won his first [MVP award](/wiki/NBA_Most_Valuable_Player_Award), and was complemented by 1981 Finals MVP [Cedric Maxwell](/wiki/Cedric_Maxwell), first-time [all-star](/wiki/1984_NBA_All-Star_Game) and [Sixth Man Award](/wiki/NBA_Sixth_Man_of_the_Year_Award) winner [Kevin McHale](/wiki/Kevin_McHale_(basketball)), [Robert Parish](/wiki/Robert_Parish), [Gerald Henderson](/wiki/Gerald_Henderson) and [Danny Ainge](/wiki/Danny_Ainge). Boston's most crucial addition was [Dennis Johnson](/wiki/Dennis_Johnson), whom they acquired from the [Phoenix Suns](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Phoenix_Suns_season) in the offseason in hopes of addressing their porous back-court defense.

In the [playoffs](/wiki/1984_NBA_Playoffs), the Celtics defeated the [Washington Bullets](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Washington_Bullets_season) in four, the [New York Knicks](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_New_York_Knicks_season) in seven, and the [Milwaukee Bucks](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Milwaukee_Bucks_season) in five.

### Los Angeles Lakers

Main article: [1983–84 Los Angeles Lakers season](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Los_Angeles_Lakers_season)

The Lakers were coming off a four-game sweep by the [Philadelphia 76ers](/wiki/1982%E2%80%9383_Philadelphia_76ers_season) in the previous year's finals. Before the season began, the Lakers traded long-time guard [Norm Nixon](/wiki/Norm_Nixon) to the [San Diego Clippers](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_San_Diego_Clippers_season) in exchange for the draft rights to [Byron Scott](/wiki/Byron_Scott). The trade signaled a transition period, as some of the key players from the first two championships gave way to younger talent. Despite the changes, it did not stop the Lakers from finishing with the best record (54–28) in the Western Conference, powered by their one-two punch of [Kareem Abdul-Jabbar](/wiki/Kareem_Abdul-Jabbar) and [Magic Johnson](/wiki/Magic_Johnson).

During the playoffs, the Lakers defeated the [Kansas City Kings](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Kansas_City_Kings_season) in three, the [Dallas Mavericks](/wiki/1983%E2%80%9384_Dallas_Mavericks_season) in five, and the Phoenix Suns in six. However, the Lakers suffered a key injury when their 3rd leading scorer, [Jamaal Wilkes](/wiki/Jamaal_Wilkes) (17 PPG) was ruled out of the finals. This cost the Lakers valuable depth, as [James Worthy](/wiki/James_Worthy), a key contributor off the bench, would now have to start in Wilkes' place.

In Game 2, the Lakers led 113–111 with 18 seconds left when [Gerald Henderson](/wiki/Gerald_Henderson) stole a [James Worthy](/wiki/James_Worthy) pass to score a game tying [layup](/wiki/Layup). The Lakers then inbounded the ball and Magic Johnson inexplicably dribbled the clock out during regulation time. With the Celtics down by one near the end of overtime, [Scott Wedman](/wiki/Scott_Wedman) hit a shot from the baseline with 14 seconds remaining to put Boston ahead. The Lakers' chance to win on the ensuing possession was thwarted by a [Robert Parish](/wiki/Robert_Parish) steal, followed by [Larry Bird](/wiki/Larry_Bird) sinking a pair of free throws to secure the 124–121 victory for the Celtics.

In Game 4, the Lakers had a five-point game lead with less than a minute to play, but made several execution errors, including [Magic Johnson](/wiki/Magic_Johnson)'s bad pass to [Robert Parish](/wiki/Robert_Parish) late in the fourth quarter, and missing two crucial free throws in OT as the Celtics tied the game and then came away with a 129–125 victory in overtime. Johnson was called "Tragic Johnson" by Celtics fans due to the two crucial errors he committed in Game 4 (the Parish steal, followed by two botched free throws in OT). The Lakers took an early lead in overtime, but a controversial foul call on Kareem Abdul-Jabbar, with 16 seconds remaining in regulation, had been his 6th foul, and he was out of the game. The Laker momentum was stalled, and [Larry Bird](/wiki/Larry_Bird) came up with a crucial jumper over Magic Johnson with 16 seconds remaining in overtime, then [M.L. Carr](/wiki/M.L._Carr) stole [James Worthy](/wiki/James_Worthy)'s inbounds pass followed by a dunk to seal the win. The game was also marked by Celtic forward [Kevin McHale](/wiki/Kevin_McHale_(basketball))'s clothesline take-down of Laker forward [Kurt Rambis](/wiki/Kurt_Rambis) on a breakaway layup which triggered the physical aspect of the rivalry. [Larry Bird](/wiki/Larry_Bird) would go after [Kareem Abdul-Jabbar](/wiki/Kareem_Abdul-Jabbar) later on in the third quarter, and 1981 Finals MVP [Cedric Maxwell](/wiki/Cedric_Maxwell) further antagonized the Lakers by following a missed [James Worthy](/wiki/James_Worthy) free throw by crossing the lane with his hands around his own neck, symbolizing that Worthy was "choking" under pressure. Also, Bird pushed [Michael Cooper](/wiki/Michael_Cooper) to the baseline following the inbound play during the second quarter.

Game 4 of the 1984 Finals marked the last Finals game to go into overtime until [Game 2 of the 1990 NBA Finals](/wiki/1990_NBA_Finals#Game_2).

In Game 5, the Celtics took a 3–2 series lead with a 121–103 victory, as [Larry Bird](/wiki/Larry_Bird) scored 34 points and grabbed 17 rebounds. The game was known as the "Heat Game", as it was played under 97 °F (36 °C) heat, and without any air conditioning, at [Boston Garden](/wiki/Boston_Garden). The Celtics did not warm up with their sweat pants on because of extreme heat, and an [oxygen tank](/wiki/Oxygen_tank) was provided to give air to an aging Kareem Abdul-Jabbar. Referee [Hugh Evans](/wiki/Hugh_Evans_(basketball)) became dehydrated and fainted at one point in the first half. He worked the first half, but was replaced by [John Vanak](/wiki/John_Vanak) for the second half. It was also the last time that a team with home court advantage in the NBA finals played Game 5 on its own floor until [2014](/wiki/2014_NBA_Finals). The next year, the NBA Finals switched to the 2-3-2 format with Game 5 going to the team without home-court advantage, which continued through [2013](/wiki/2013_NBA_Finals).

In Game 6, the Lakers evened the series with a 119–108 victory. In the game the Lakers answered the Celtics' rough tactics when Laker forward [James Worthy](/wiki/James_Worthy) shoved [Cedric Maxwell](/wiki/Cedric_Maxwell) into a basket support. After the game a Laker fan threw a beer at Celtics guard [M.L. Carr](/wiki/M.L._Carr) as he left the floor, causing him to label the series "all-out-war."

In Game 7, the heat that was an issue in Game 5 was not as bad (indoor temperatures hovered around 91 °F (33 °C) during the game, due to additional fans being brought in to try to cool the air). The Celtics were led by Cedric Maxwell who had 24 points, eight rebounds and eight assists as they came away with a 111–102 victory. In the game, the Lakers rallied to cut a 14-point-deficit to three with one minute remaining when Cedric Maxwell knocked the ball away from Magic Johnson. [Dennis Johnson](/wiki/Dennis_Johnson) responded by sinking two free throws to seal the victory. [Larry Bird](/wiki/Larry_Bird) was named [MVP of the series](/wiki/NBA_Finals_MVP_Award).

The series was the eighth time in NBA history that the Celtics and Lakers met in the NBA finals, with Boston winning each time, and the first championship that the Celtics claimed at home since [1966](/wiki/1966_NBA_Finals).

## Television coverage

The 1984 championship series scored high TV [ratings](/wiki/Nielsen_ratings). All the playoff action was documented on the [1984 NBA Season](/wiki/1983-84_NBA_season) documentary *Pride and Passion*, narrated by [Dick Stockton](/wiki/Dick_Stockton). Stockton, the play-by-play announcer for the series, was joined by [Tom Heinsohn](/wiki/Tom_Heinsohn), and the duo would call the next four NBA Finals until [1987](/wiki/1987_NBA_Finals).

## Legacy and Aftermath

Further information: [1985 NBA Finals](/wiki/1985_NBA_Finals) and [1987 NBA Finals](/wiki/1987_NBA_Finals)

Reflecting back on the series, [Magic Johnson](/wiki/Magic_Johnson) said ". . . (the Lakers) learned a valuable lesson. Only the strong survive. . . talent just don't get it. That's the first time the (80's) Lakers ever encountered that, someone stronger minded." Johnson himself would receive tremendous criticism for his performance in the Finals by fans and media, after being thoroughly outplayed by rival [Larry Bird](/wiki/Larry_Bird). Following the Finals, he was jokingly named "Tragic Johnson" for his performance in the series.

Both teams met again in the Finals [the next year](/wiki/1985_NBA_Finals), although a bar fight involving Larry Bird outside a Boston bar (Chelsea's) during the 1985 Eastern Conference Finals impacted the Celtics in the series. Bird suffered a temporary finger injury which affected his play; he struggled in the last two games of the Eastern Conference Finals against the Sixers and throughout the 1985 Finals. In the series, the Lakers returned the favor and defeated the Celtics in six games, clinching the title at [Boston Garden](/wiki/Boston_Garden) after years of torment and bad misfortune at the arena. The Celtics would then win their next championship [two years later](/wiki/1986_NBA_Finals) over the [Hakeem Olajuwon](/wiki/Hakeem_Olajuwon)-led [Houston Rockets](/wiki/Houston_Rockets) in six games. The Celtics and Lakers met for the third time in the 1980s in [1987](/wiki/1987_NBA_Finals), which the Lakers also won in six games. Johnson, who by then had shaken and outgrown the "Tragic Johnson" nickname, would win his third [NBA Finals MVP](/wiki/NBA_Finals_MVP) in the series, highlighted by a "[baby sky hook](/wiki/1987_NBA_Finals#Game_4)" game winner in Game 4.

### In Popular Culture

The 1984 NBA Finals (as well as the 1985 and 1987 Finals) has been covered in-depth in many documentaries, including [ESPN](/wiki/ESPN)’s [30 for 30](/wiki/30_for_30) *[Celtics/Lakers: Best of Enemies](/wiki/Celtics/Lakers:_Best_of_Enemies)*, [HBO](/wiki/HBO)’s *[Celtics City](/wiki/Celtics_City)* and *[Magic & Bird: A Courtship of Rivals](/wiki/Magic_%26_Bird:_A_Courtship_of_Rivals).*

The 1984 Finals was dramatized in the Season 2 of [HBO's](/wiki/HBO) *[Winning Time: The Rise of the Lakers Dynasty](/wiki/Winning_Time:_The_Rise_of_the_Lakers_Dynasty)*, which was based on the book *Showtime: Magic, Kareem, Riley, and the Los Angeles Lakers Dynasty of the 1980s* by [Jeff Pearlman](/wiki/Jeff_Pearlman).
