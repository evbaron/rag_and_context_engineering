The **City of London** (informally known as **the City** or **the Square Mile**) is a [city](/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom), [ceremonial county](/wiki/Ceremonial_counties_of_England) and [local government district](/wiki/Districts_of_England) in [England](/wiki/England). Established by [the Romans](/wiki/Roman_Britain) around 47 AD as *[Londinium](/wiki/Londinium)*, it forms the [historic centre](/wiki/Old_town) of the wider [London](/wiki/London) metropolis. Surrounded by the modern ceremonial county of [Greater London](/wiki/Greater_London), from which it remains separate, the City is a unique local authority area governed by the [City of London Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation), which is led by the [Lord Mayor of London](/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_London), though it is included as part of the [region of England](/wiki/Regions_of_England) governed by the [Greater London Authority](/wiki/Greater_London_Authority) (which shares local government powers in the region with the councils of 32 [London boroughs](/wiki/London_boroughs) and the City of London Corporation).

Nicknamed *the Square Mile*, the City of London has an area of 1.12 sq mi (716.80 acres; 2.90 km2), making it the [smallest city in the United Kingdom by area](/wiki/List_of_smallest_cities_in_the_United_Kingdom). It had a population of 8,583 at the [2021 census](/wiki/2021_United_Kingdom_census), however over 500,000 people were employed in the area as of 2019.

Together with [Canary Wharf](/wiki/Canary_Wharf) and the [West End](/wiki/West_End_of_London), the City of London forms the primary [central business district](/wiki/Central_business_district) of London, which is one of the leading [financial centres](/wiki/Financial_centre) of the world. The [Bank of England](/wiki/Bank_of_England) and the [London Stock Exchange](/wiki/London_Stock_Exchange) are both based in the City. The [insurance](/wiki/Insurance) industry also has a major presence in the area, and the presence of the [Inns of Court](/wiki/Inns_of_Court) on the City's western boundary has made it a centre for the [legal profession](/wiki/Legal_profession).

The present City of London constituted the majority of London from its [settlement by the Romans](/wiki/Londinium) in the 1st century AD to the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages), with only areas within its boundaries being considered London until expansion. It contains several historic sites, including [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral), the [Royal Exchange](/wiki/Royal_Exchange,_London), [Mansion House](/wiki/Mansion_House,_London), [Guildhall](/wiki/Guildhall,_London), the [Old Bailey](/wiki/Old_Bailey), [Smithfield Market](/wiki/Smithfield_Market), the [Monument to the Great Fire of London](/wiki/Monument_to_the_Great_Fire_of_London), and the remains of the ancient [London Wall](/wiki/London_Wall).

## History

Main article: [History of London](/wiki/History_of_London)

### Origins

Main article: [Londinium](/wiki/Londinium)

The [Waterloo Helmet](/wiki/Waterloo_Helmet), c. 150–50 BC, found in the [River Thames](/wiki/River_Thames)

A surviving fragment of the London Wall, built around AD 200, close to [Tower Hill](/wiki/Tower_Hill)

The Roman legions established a settlement known as "Londinium" on the current site of the City of London around AD 43. Its bridge over the River Thames turned the city into a [road nexus](#Road) and major [port](#Port), serving as a major commercial centre in [Roman Britain](/wiki/Roman_Britain) until its abandonment during [the 5th century](#5th_century). Archaeologist Leslie Wallace notes that, because extensive archaeological excavation has not revealed any signs of a significant [pre-Roman](/wiki/Prehistoric_Britain#Late_pre-Roman_Iron_Age_(LPRIA)) presence, "arguments for a purely Roman foundation of London are now common and uncontroversial."

At its height, the Roman city had a population of approximately 45,000–60,000 inhabitants. Londinium was an ethnically diverse city, with inhabitants from across the Roman Empire, including natives of Britannia, [continental Europe](/wiki/Continental_Europe), the Middle East, and North Africa. The Romans built the London Wall some time between AD 190 and 225. The boundaries of the Roman city were similar to those of the City of London today, though the City extends further west than Londinium's [Ludgate](/wiki/Ludgate), and the Thames was undredged and thus wider than it is today, with Londinium's shoreline slightly north of the City's present shoreline. The Romans built a bridge across the river, as early as AD 50, near to today's London Bridge.

### Decline

By the time the London Wall was constructed, the City's fortunes were in decline, and it faced problems of plague and fire. The Roman Empire entered a long period of [instability and decline](/wiki/Decline_of_the_Roman_Empire), including the [Carausian Revolt](/wiki/Carausian_Revolt) in Britain. In the 3rd and 4th centuries, the City was under attack from [Picts](/wiki/Picts), Scots, and [Saxon](/wiki/Saxons) raiders. The decline continued, both for Londinium and the Empire, and in AD 410 the Romans withdrew entirely from Britain. Many of the Roman public buildings in Londinium by this time had fallen into decay and disuse, and gradually after the formal withdrawal the City became almost (if not, at times, entirely) uninhabited. The centre of trade and population moved away from the walled Londinium to [Lundenwic](/wiki/Lundenwic) ("London market"), a settlement to the west, roughly in the modern-day [Strand](/wiki/Strand,_London)/[Aldwych](/wiki/Aldwych)/[Covent Garden](/wiki/Covent_Garden) area.

### Anglo-Saxon restoration

Main article: [Anglo-Saxon London](/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_London)

During the Anglo-Saxon [Heptarchy](/wiki/Heptarchy), the London area came in turn under the Kingdoms of [Essex](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Essex), [Mercia](/wiki/Mercia), and later [Wessex](/wiki/Wessex), though from the mid 8th century it was frequently under threat from raids by different groups including the [Vikings](/wiki/Vikings).

[Bede](/wiki/Bede) records that in AD 604 [St Augustine](/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury) consecrated [Mellitus](/wiki/Mellitus) as the first bishop to the [Anglo-Saxon](/wiki/Anglo-Saxons) kingdom of the [East Saxons](/wiki/East_Saxons) and their king, [Sæberht](/wiki/S%C3%A6berht_of_Essex). Sæberht's uncle and overlord, [Æthelberht](/wiki/%C3%86thelberht_of_Kent), king of [Kent](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent), built a church dedicated to St Paul in London, as the seat of the new bishop. It is assumed, although unproven, that this first Anglo-Saxon cathedral stood on the same site as the later medieval and the present cathedrals.

[Alfred the Great](/wiki/Alfred_the_Great), [King of Wessex](/wiki/King_of_Wessex) occupied and began the resettlement of the old Roman walled area, in 886, and appointed his son-in-law [Earl Æthelred of Mercia](/wiki/Earl_Aethelred_of_Mercia) over it as part of their reconquest of the Viking occupied parts of England. The refortified Anglo-Saxon settlement was known as ("London Fort", a [borough](/wiki/Borough)). The historian Asser said that "Alfred, king of the Anglo-Saxons, restored the city of London splendidly ... and made it habitable once more." Alfred's "restoration" entailed reoccupying and refurbishing the nearly deserted Roman walled city, building quays along the Thames, and laying a new city street plan.

Alfred's taking of London and the rebuilding of the old Roman city was a turning point in history, not only as the permanent establishment of the City of London, but also as part of a unifying moment in early England, with Wessex becoming the dominant English kingdom and the repelling (to some degree) of the Viking occupation and raids. While London, and indeed England, were afterwards subjected to further periods of Viking and Danish raids and occupation, the establishment of the City of London and the [Kingdom of England](/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) prevailed.

In the 10th century, [Athelstan](/wiki/Athelstan) permitted eight [mints](/wiki/Royal_mint) to be established, compared with six in his capital, [Winchester](/wiki/Winchester,_Hampshire), indicating the wealth of the city. London Bridge, which had fallen into ruin following the Roman evacuation and abandonment of Londinium, was rebuilt by the Saxons, but was periodically destroyed by Viking raids and storms.

As the focus of trade and population was moved back to within the old Roman walls, the older Saxon settlement of Lundenwic was largely abandoned and gained the name of *Ealdwic* (the "old settlement"). The name survives today as Aldwych (the "old market-place"), a name of a street and an area of the City of Westminster between [Westminster](/wiki/Westminster) and the City of London.

### Medieval era

See also: [Norman and Medieval London](/wiki/Norman_and_Medieval_London)

Map of London in about 1300

The ["Woodcut" map of London](/wiki/Woodcut_map_of_London), dating from the 1560s

Following the [Battle of Hastings](/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings), [William the Conqueror](/wiki/William_the_Conqueror) marched on London, reaching as far as [Southwark](/wiki/Southwark), but failed to get across London Bridge or defeat the Londoners. He eventually crossed the River Thames at [Wallingford](/wiki/Wallingford,_Oxfordshire), pillaging the land as he went. Rather than continuing the war, [Edgar the Ætheling](/wiki/Edgar_the_%C3%86theling), [Edwin of Mercia](/wiki/Edwin,_Earl_of_Mercia) and [Morcar](/wiki/Morcar) of Northumbria surrendered at [Berkhamsted](/wiki/Berkhamsted). William granted the citizens of London a charter in 1075; the city was one of a few examples of the English retaining some authority. The city was not covered by the [Domesday Book](/wiki/Domesday_Book).

William built three castles around the city, to keep Londoners subdued:

- [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London), which is still a major establishment.
- [Baynard's Castle](/wiki/Baynard%27s_Castle), which no longer exists but gave its name to a city ward.
- [Montfichet's Tower](/wiki/Montfichet%27s_Tower) or Castle on [Ludgate Hill](/wiki/Ludgate_Hill), which was dismantled and sold off in the 13th century.

Around 1132 the City was given the right to appoint [its own sheriffs](/wiki/Sheriff_of_the_City_of_London) rather than having sheriffs appointed by the monarch. London's chosen sheriffs also served as the sheriffs for the county of [Middlesex](/wiki/Middlesex). This meant that the City and Middlesex were regarded as one administratively for addressing crime and keeping the peace (not that the county was a dependency of the city). London's sheriffs continued to serve Middlesex until the county was given its own sheriffs again following the [Local Government Act 1888](/wiki/Local_Government_Act_1888). By 1141 the whole body of the citizenry was considered to constitute a single community. This '[commune](/wiki/Medieval_commune)' was the origin of the [City of London Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation) and the citizens gained the right to appoint, with the king's consent, a mayor in 1189, and to directly elect the mayor from 1215.

From medieval times, the City has been composed of [25 ancient wards](/wiki/Wards_of_the_City_of_London), each headed by an [alderman](/wiki/Alderman), who chairs [Wardmotes](/wiki/Wardmote), which still take place at least annually. A [Folkmoot](/wiki/Folkmoot), for the whole of the City held at the outdoor cross of [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral), was formerly also held. Many of the medieval offices and traditions continue to the present day, demonstrating the unique nature of the City and its [Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation).

In 1381, the [Peasants' Revolt](/wiki/Peasants%27_Revolt) affected London. The rebels took the City and the Tower of London, but the rebellion ended after its leader, Wat Tyler, was killed during a confrontation that included Lord Mayor [William Walworth](/wiki/William_Walworth). In 1450, rebel forces again occupied the City during [Jack Cade's Rebellion](/wiki/Jack_Cade%27s_Rebellion) before being ousted by London citizens following a bloody battle on London Bridge. In 1550, the area south of London Bridge in [Southwark](/wiki/Southwark) came under the control of the City with the establishment of the ward of [Bridge Without](/wiki/Bridge_Without).

- A pivotal event during the [Peasants' Revolt](/wiki/Peasants%27_Revolt), 1381: their leader [Wat Tyler](/wiki/Wat_Tyler) is stabbed by [William Walworth](/wiki/William_Walworth), Lord Mayor.
- The 1666 [Great Fire](/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London) as depicted in a 17th-century painting: it shows [Old London Bridge](/wiki/Old_London_Bridge) at left, churches, houses, and the [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London) at right, as seen from a boat near Tower Wharf
- Map showing the extent of the Great Fire of London, which destroyed nearly 80% of the City

The city was burnt severely on a number of occasions, the worst being in 1123 and in the [Great Fire of London](/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London) in 1666. Both of these fires were referred to as *the* Great Fire. After the fire of 1666, a number of plans were drawn up to remodel the city and its street pattern into a [renaissance](/wiki/Renaissance)-style city with planned urban blocks, squares and boulevards. These plans were almost entirely not taken up, and the medieval street pattern re-emerged almost intact.

### Early modern period

In the 1630s the Crown sought to have the Corporation of the City of London extend its jurisdiction to surrounding areas. In what is sometimes called the "great refusal", the Corporation said no to the King, which in part accounts for its unique government structure to the present.

By the late 16th century, London increasingly became a major centre for banking, international trade and commerce. The [Royal Exchange](/wiki/Royal_Exchange,_London) was founded in 1565 by Sir [Thomas Gresham](/wiki/Thomas_Gresham) as a centre of commerce for London's merchants, and gained Royal patronage in 1571. Although no longer used for its original purpose, its location at the corner of [Cornhill](/wiki/Cornhill,_London) and [Threadneedle Street](/wiki/Threadneedle_Street) continues to be the geographical centre of the city's core of banking and financial services, with the [Bank of England](/wiki/Bank_of_England) moving to its present site in 1734, opposite the Royal Exchange. Immediately to the south of Cornhill, [Lombard Street](/wiki/Lombard_Street,_London) was the location from 1691 of [Lloyd's Coffee House](/wiki/Lloyd%27s_Coffee_House), which became the world-leading insurance market. London's insurance sector continues to be based in the area, particularly in [Lime Street](/wiki/Lime_Street_(ward)).

In 1708, [Christopher Wren](/wiki/Christopher_Wren)'s masterpiece, [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral), was completed on his birthday. The first service had been held on 2 December 1697, more than 10 years earlier. It replaced the original St Paul's, which had been completely destroyed in the Great Fire of London, and is considered to be one of the finest cathedrals in Britain and a fine example of [Baroque architecture](/wiki/Baroque_architecture).

### Growth of London

The 18th century was a period of rapid growth for London, reflecting an increasing national population, the early stirrings of the [Industrial Revolution](/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), and London's role at the centre of the evolving [British Empire](/wiki/British_Empire). The urban area expanded beyond the borders of the City of London, most notably during this period towards the [West End](/wiki/West_End_of_London) and Westminster.

Expansion continued and became more rapid by the beginning of the 19th century, with London growing in all directions. To the [East](/wiki/East_End_of_London) the [Port of London](/wiki/Port_of_London) grew rapidly during the century, with the construction of many docks, needed as the Thames at the City could not cope with the volume of trade. The arrival of the railways and the [Tube](/wiki/London_Underground) meant that London could expand over a much greater area. By the mid-19th century, with London still rapidly expanding in population and area, the City had already become only a small part of the wider metropolis.

### 19th and 20th centuries

An attempt was made in 1894 with the [Royal Commission on the Amalgamation of the City and County of London](/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_the_Amalgamation_of_the_City_and_County_of_London) to end the distinction between the city and the surrounding County of London, but a change of government at Westminster meant the option was not taken up. The City as a distinct [polity](/wiki/Polity) survived despite its position within the London conurbation and [numerous local government reforms](/wiki/History_of_local_government_in_London). Supporting this status, the city was a special [parliamentary borough](/wiki/Parliamentary_borough) that elected four members to the [unreformed House of Commons](/wiki/Unreformed_House_of_Commons), who were retained after the [Reform Act 1832](/wiki/Reform_Act_1832); reduced to two under the [Redistribution of Seats Act 1885](/wiki/Redistribution_of_Seats_Act_1885); and ceased to be a separate constituency under the [Representation of the People Act 1948](/wiki/Representation_of_the_People_Act_1948). Since then the city is a minority (in terms of population and area) of the [Cities of London and Westminster](/wiki/Cities_of_London_and_Westminster_(UK_Parliament_constituency)).

[St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral) (pictured 1896) dominated the skyline of the City for centuries. Its current structure by [Christopher Wren](/wiki/Christopher_Wren) was completed in 1706 after its medieval predecessor burned with much of the City in the Great Fire of 1666.

The City's population fell rapidly in the 19th century and through most of the 20th century, as people moved outwards in all directions to London's vast [suburbs](/wiki/Metro-land), and many residential buildings were demolished to make way for office blocks. Like many areas of London and other British cities, the City fell victim to large-scale and highly destructive aerial bombing during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), especially in [the Blitz](/wiki/The_Blitz). Whilst St Paul's Cathedral survived the onslaught, large swathes of the area did not and the particularly heavy raids of late December 1940 led to a [firestorm](/wiki/Firestorm) called the [Second Great Fire of London](/wiki/Second_Great_Fire_of_London).

There was a major rebuilding programme in the decades following the war, in some parts (such as at the Barbican) dramatically altering the urban landscape. But the destruction of the older historic fabric allowed the construction of modern and larger-scale developments, whereas in those parts not so badly affected by bomb damage the City retains its older character of smaller buildings. The street pattern, which is still largely medieval, was altered slightly in places, although there is a more recent trend of reversing some of the post-war modernist changes made, such as at [Paternoster Square](/wiki/Paternoster_Square).

The City suffered terrorist attacks including the [1993 Bishopsgate bombing](/wiki/1993_Bishopsgate_bombing) ([IRA](/wiki/Provisional_Irish_Republican_Army)) and the [7 July 2005 London bombings](/wiki/7_July_2005_London_bombings) ([Islamist](/wiki/Islamic_terrorism)). In response to the 1993 bombing, a system of road barriers, checkpoints and surveillance cameras referred to as the "[ring of steel](/wiki/Ring_of_steel_(London))" has been maintained to control entry points to the City.

The 1970s saw the construction of tall office buildings including the 600-foot (183 m), 47-storey [NatWest Tower](/wiki/NatWest_Tower), the first skyscraper in the UK. By the 2010s, office space development had intensified in the City, especially in the central, northern and eastern parts, with skyscrapers including [30 St. Mary Axe](/wiki/30_St_Mary_Axe) ("the Gherkin"'), [Leadenhall Building](/wiki/122_Leadenhall_Street) ("the Cheesegrater"), [20 Fenchurch Street](/wiki/20_Fenchurch_Street) ("the Walkie-Talkie"), the [Broadgate Tower](/wiki/Broadgate_Tower), the [Heron Tower](/wiki/Heron_Tower) and [22 Bishopsgate](/wiki/22_Bishopsgate).

The main residential section of the City today is the [Barbican Estate](/wiki/Barbican_Estate), constructed between 1965 and 1976. The [Museum of London](/wiki/Museum_of_London) was based there until December 2022 (it is due to reopen in West Smithfield in 2026), whilst a number of other services provided by the corporation are still maintained on the Barbican Estate.

## Governance

[Guildhall](/wiki/Guildhall,_London) is the ceremonial and administrative centre of the city.

[Mansion House](/wiki/Mansion_House,_London) is the official residence of the Lord Mayor.

[John Stuttard](/wiki/John_Stuttard), Lord Mayor of the City of London 2006–2007, during the Lord Mayor's Show of 2006

See also: [City of London Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation) and [History of local government in England § The City of London](/wiki/History_of_local_government_in_England#The_City_of_London)

The City has a unique political status, a legacy of its uninterrupted integrity as a [corporate city](/wiki/County_corporate) since the Anglo-Saxon period and its singular relationship with the [Crown](/wiki/British_monarchy). Historically its system of government was not unusual, but it was not reformed by the [Municipal Corporations Act 1835](/wiki/Municipal_Corporations_Act_1835) and little changed by later reforms, so that it is the only local government in the UK where elections are not run on the basis of one vote for every adult citizen.

It is administered by the [City of London Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation), headed by the [Lord Mayor of London](/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_London) (not to be confused with the separate [Mayor of London](/wiki/Mayor_of_London), an office created only in the year 2000), which is responsible for a number of functions and has interests in land beyond the city's boundaries. Unlike other English local authorities, the corporation has two council bodies: the (now largely ceremonial) [Court of Aldermen](/wiki/Court_of_Aldermen) and the [Court of Common Council](/wiki/Court_of_Common_Council). The [Court of Aldermen](/wiki/Court_of_Aldermen) represents the wards, with each ward (irrespective of size) returning one alderman. The chief executive of the Corporation holds the ancient office of [Town Clerk of London](/wiki/Town_Clerk_of_London).

The City is a ceremonial county which has a Commission of Lieutenancy headed by the Lord Mayor instead of a [Lord-Lieutenant](/wiki/Lord-Lieutenant) and has [two Sheriffs](/wiki/Sheriffs_of_the_City_of_London) instead of a [High Sheriff](/wiki/High_Sheriff) (see [list of Sheriffs of London](/wiki/List_of_Sheriffs_of_London)), quasi-judicial offices appointed by the [livery companies](/wiki/Livery_company), an ancient political system based on the representation and protection of trades ([craft guilds](/wiki/Craft_guilds)). Senior members of the livery companies are known as [liverymen](/wiki/Liveryman) and form the Common Hall, which chooses the lord mayor, the sheriffs and certain other officers.

### Wards

Main article: [Wards of the City of London](/wiki/Wards_of_the_City_of_London)

The City is made up of [25 wards](/wiki/Wards_of_the_City_of_London). They are survivors of the medieval government system that allowed a very local area to exist as a self-governing unit within the wider city. They can be described as electoral/political divisions; ceremonial, geographic and administrative entities; sub-divisions of the city. Each ward has an [Alderman](/wiki/Alderman), who until the mid-1960s held office for life but since put themselves up for re-election at least every six years, and are the only [directly elected](/wiki/Direct_elections) Aldermen in the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom). Wards continue to have a [Beadle](/wiki/Beadle), an ancient position which is now largely ceremonial and whose main remaining function is the running of an annual [Wardmote](/wiki/Wardmote) of electors, representatives and officials. At the Wardmote the ward's Alderman appoints at least one Deputy for the year ahead, and Wardmotes are also held during elections. Each ward also has a Ward Club, which is similar to a [residents' association](/wiki/Residents%27_association).

The wards are ancient and their number have changed three times:

- in 1394 [Farringdon](/wiki/Farringdon,_London) was divided into Farringdon Within and Farringdon Without
- in 1550 the ward of Bridge Without, [south of the river](/wiki/Southwark), was created, the ward of Bridge becoming Bridge Within;
- in 1978 these Bridge wards were merged as [Bridge](/wiki/Bridge_(City_of_London_ward)) ward.

A map of the wards as they were in the late 19th century

A map of the wards since 2003

Following boundary changes in 1994, and later reform of the business vote in the City, there was a major boundary and electoral representation revision of the wards in 2003, and they were reviewed again in 2010 for change in 2013, though not to such a dramatic extent. The review was conducted by senior officers of the corporation and senior judges of the [Old Bailey](/wiki/Old_Bailey); the wards are reviewed by this process to avoid [malapportionment](/wiki/Malapportionment). The procedure of review is unique in the United Kingdom as it is not conducted by the [Electoral Commission](/wiki/Electoral_Commission_(United_Kingdom)) or a local government boundary commission every eight to 12 years, which is the case for all other [wards in Great Britain](/wiki/Wards_and_electoral_divisions_of_the_United_Kingdom). Particular churches, [livery company](/wiki/Livery_company) halls and other historic buildings and structures are associated with a ward, such as St Paul's Cathedral with [Castle Baynard](/wiki/Castle_Baynard), and London Bridge with Bridge; boundary changes in 2003 removed some of these historic connections.

Each ward elects an [alderman](/wiki/Alderman) to the [Court of Aldermen](/wiki/Court_of_Aldermen), and [commoners](/wiki/Commoners) (the City equivalent of a [councillor](/wiki/Councillor)) to the [Court of Common Council](/wiki/Court_of_Common_Council) of the corporation. Only electors who are [Freemen of the City of London](/wiki/Freeman_of_the_City_of_London) are eligible to stand. The number of commoners a ward sends to the Common Council varies from two to ten, depending on the number of electors in each ward. Since the 2003 review it has been agreed that the four more residential wards: [Portsoken](/wiki/Portsoken), [Queenhithe](/wiki/Queenhithe), [Aldersgate](/wiki/Aldersgate) and [Cripplegate](/wiki/Cripplegate) together elect 20 of the 100 commoners, whereas the business-dominated remainder elect the remaining 80 commoners. Boundary changes in 2003 and 2013 have increased the residential emphasis of the mentioned four wards.

Census data provides eight nominal rather than 25 real wards, all of varying size and population. ### Elections

The City has a unique electoral system. Most of its voters are representatives of businesses and other bodies that occupy premises in the city. Its ancient wards have very unequal numbers of voters. In elections, both the businesses based in the city and the residents of the City vote.

The City of London Corporation was not reformed by the [Municipal Corporations Act 1835](/wiki/Municipal_Corporations_Act_1835), because it had a more extensive electoral franchise than any other borough or city; in fact, it widened this further with its own equivalent legislation allowing one to become a [freeman](/wiki/Freedom_of_the_City) without being a [liveryman](/wiki/Liveryman). In 1851, the city had a population of 127,869, but increasing development of the city as a central business district led to this falling to 26,923 in 1901 and to 5,234 in 1951. It has risen slightly to around 9,000 since, largely due to the development of the [Barbican Estate](/wiki/Barbican_Estate). In 2009, the business vote was about 24,000, greatly exceeding residential voters. As the City of London Corporation has not been affected by other municipal legislation over the period of time since then, its electoral practice has become increasingly anomalous. Uniquely for city or borough elections, its elections remain independent-dominated.

The business or "[non-residential vote](/wiki/Non-residential_vote)" was abolished in other UK local council elections by the [Representation of the People Act 1969](/wiki/Representation_of_the_People_Act_1969), but was preserved in the City of London. The principal reason given by successive UK governments for retaining this mechanism for giving businesses representation, is that the City is "primarily a place for doing business". About 330,000 non-residents constitute the day-time population and use most of its services, far outnumbering residents, who number around 7,000 (2011). By contrast, opponents of the retention of the business vote argue that it is a cause of institutional inertia.

The [City of London (Ward Elections) Act 2002](/wiki/City_of_London_(Ward_Elections)_Act_2002) (c. vi), a local act of Parliament, reformed the voting system and greatly increased the business franchise, allowing many more businesses to be represented. Under the new system, the number of non-resident voters has doubled from 16,000 to 32,000. Previously disenfranchised firms (and other organisations) are entitled to nominate voters, in addition to those already represented, and all such bodies are now required to choose their voters in a representative fashion. Bodies employing fewer than 10 people may appoint one voter; those employing 10 to 50 people one voter for every five employees; those employing more than 50 people 10 voters and one additional voter for each 50 employees beyond the first 50. The Act also changed other aspects of an earlier act relating to elections in the City, from 1957.

### The Temple

Main article: [Temple, London](/wiki/Temple,_London)

[Inner Temple](/wiki/Inner_Temple) and [Middle Temple](/wiki/Middle_Temple) (which neighbour each other) in the western ward of [Farringdon Without](/wiki/Farringdon_Without) are within the boundaries and liberties of the City, but can be thought of as independent [enclaves](/wiki/Enclave). They are two of the few remaining [liberties](/wiki/Liberty_(division)), an old name for a geographic division with special rights. They are [extra-parochial areas](/wiki/Extra-parochial_area), historically not governed by the [City of London Corporation](/wiki/City_of_London_Corporation) (and are today regarded as local authorities for most purposes) and equally outside the [ecclesiastical jurisdiction](/wiki/Ecclesiastical_jurisdiction) of the [Bishop of London](/wiki/Bishop_of_London).

### Other functions

[Leadenhall Market](/wiki/Leadenhall_Market) is a historic market nestled between [Gracechurch Street](/wiki/Gracechurch_Street) and [Lime Street](/wiki/Lime_Street,_London).

Within the City, the Corporation owns and runs both [Smithfield Market](/wiki/Smithfield_Market) and [Leadenhall Market](/wiki/Leadenhall_Market). It owns land beyond its boundaries, including [open spaces](/wiki/Corporation_of_London_open_spaces) (parks, forests and commons) in and around Greater London, including most of [Epping Forest](/wiki/Epping_Forest) and [Hampstead Heath](/wiki/Hampstead_Heath). The Corporation owns [Old Spitalfields Market](/wiki/Old_Spitalfields_Market) and [Billingsgate Fish Market](/wiki/Billingsgate_Fish_Market), in the neighbouring [London Borough of Tower Hamlets](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Tower_Hamlets). It owns and helps fund the [Old Bailey](/wiki/Old_Bailey), the Central Criminal Court for [England and Wales](/wiki/England_and_Wales), as a gift to the nation, having begun as the City and Middlesex Sessions. [The Honourable The Irish Society](/wiki/The_Honourable_The_Irish_Society), a body closely linked with the Corporation, also owns many public spaces in [Northern Ireland](/wiki/Northern_Ireland).

The City has its own independent police force, the [City of London Police](/wiki/City_of_London_Police), the Common Council (the main body of the corporation) is the [police authority](/wiki/Police_authority). The corporation also run the [Hampstead Heath Constabulary](/wiki/Hampstead_Heath_Constabulary), [Epping Forest Keepers](/wiki/Epping_Forest_Keepers) and the [City of London market constabularies](/wiki/City_of_London_market_constabularies) (whose members are no longer attested as constables but retain the historic title). The majority of Greater London is policed by the [Metropolitan Police Service](/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Service), based at [New Scotland Yard](/wiki/Scotland_Yard).

The City has one hospital, [St Bartholomew's Hospital](/wiki/St_Bartholomew%27s_Hospital), also known as 'Barts'. Founded in 1123, it is located at [Smithfield](/wiki/Smithfield,_London), and is undergoing a long-awaited regeneration after doubts as to its continuing use during the 1990s.

The City is the third largest UK patron of the arts. It oversees the [Barbican Centre](/wiki/Barbican_Centre) and subsidises several important performing arts companies.

The London Port Health Authority, which is the responsibility of the Corporation, is responsible for all port health functions on the [tidal part of the Thames](/wiki/Tideway), including the [Port of London](/wiki/Port_of_London) and related seaports, and [London City Airport](/wiki/London_City_Airport). The Corporation oversees the [Bridge House Estates](/wiki/Bridge_House_Estates), which maintains [Blackfriars Bridge](/wiki/Blackfriars_Bridge), [Millennium Bridge](/wiki/Millennium_Bridge_(London)), [Southwark Bridge](/wiki/Southwark_Bridge), London Bridge and [Tower Bridge](/wiki/Tower_Bridge). The [City's flag](/wiki/Flag_of_the_City_of_London) flies over Tower Bridge, although neither footing is in the City.

### The boundary of the City

[City of London boundary marker](/wiki/Dragon_boundary_mark) on approach to London Bridge

The size of the City was constrained by a defensive perimeter wall, known as London Wall, which was built by the Romans in the late 2nd century to protect their strategic port city. However the boundaries of the City of London no longer coincide with the old city wall, as the City expanded its jurisdiction slightly over time. During the [medieval](/wiki/Medieval) era, the City's jurisdiction expanded westwards, crossing the [River Fleet](/wiki/River_Fleet), the historic western border of the original settlement, which was the along [Fleet Street](/wiki/Fleet_Street) to [Temple Bar](/wiki/Temple_Bar,_London). The City also took in the other "City bars" which were situated just beyond the old walled area, such as at Holborn, Aldersgate, [West Smithfield](/wiki/Smithfield,_London#West_Smithfield_Bars), Bishopsgate and Aldgate. These were the important entrances to the City and their control was vital in maintaining the City's special privileges over certain trades.

Dragon statue on the [Temple Bar](/wiki/Temple_Bar,_London) monument, which marks the boundary between the City of London and City of Westminster

Most of the wall has disappeared, but several sections remain visible. A section near what later became the [Museum of London](/wiki/Museum_of_London) was revealed after the devastation of an air raid on 29 December 1940 at the height of the [Blitz](/wiki/The_Blitz). Other visible sections are at [St Alphage](/wiki/St_Alphage_London_Wall), and there are two sections near the [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London). The River Fleet was [canalised](/wiki/Canal) after the Great Fire of 1666 and then in stages was bricked up and has been since the 18th century one of London's "[lost rivers or streams](/wiki/Subterranean_rivers_of_London)", today underground as a [storm drain](/wiki/Storm_drain).

The boundary of the City was unchanged until minor boundary changes on 1 April 1994, when it expanded slightly to the west, north and east, taking small parcels of land from the [City of Westminster](/wiki/City_of_Westminster), the [Camden](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Camden), the [Islington](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Islington), the [Hackney](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Hackney) and the [Tower Hamlets](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Tower_Hamlets). The main purpose of these changes was to tidy up the boundary where it had been rendered obsolete by changes in the urban landscape. In this process the City also lost small parcels of land, though there was an overall net gain (the City grew from 1.05 to 1.12 square miles). Most notably, the changes placed the (then recently developed) [Broadgate](/wiki/Broadgate) estate entirely within the City.

[Southwark](/wiki/Southwark), to the south of the City on the other side of the Thames, was within the City between 1550 and 1899 as the Ward of [Bridge Without](/wiki/Bridge_Without), a situation connected with the [Guildable Manor](/wiki/Guildable_Manor). The City's administrative responsibility there had in practice disappeared by the mid-[Victorian](/wiki/Victorian_era) period as various aspects of metropolitan government were extended into the neighbouring areas. Today it is part of the [London Borough of Southwark](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Southwark). The [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London) has always been outside the City and comes under the [London Borough of Tower Hamlets](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Tower_Hamlets).

### Arms, motto and flag

Main articles: [Coat of arms of the City of London](/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_the_City_of_London) and [Flag of the City of London](/wiki/Flag_of_the_City_of_London)

City of London arms on a saddle blanket, as seen outside the [Royal Courts of Justice](/wiki/Royal_Courts_of_Justice) during the [Lord Mayor's Show](/wiki/Lord_Mayor%27s_Show), 2011

The Corporation of the City of London has a full [achievement](/wiki/Achievement_(heraldry)) of armorial bearings consisting of a shield on which the arms are displayed, a [crest](/wiki/Crest_(heraldry)) displayed on a helm above the shield, [supporters](/wiki/Supporters) on either side and a motto displayed on a scroll beneath the arms.

The [coat of arms](/wiki/Coat_of_arms) is "[anciently recorded](/wiki/Time_immemorial)" at the [College of Arms](/wiki/College_of_Arms). The arms consist of a silver shield bearing a red cross with a red upright sword in the first quarter. They combine the emblems of the patron saints of England and London: the [Cross of St George](/wiki/St_George%27s_Cross) with the symbol of the martyrdom of [Saint Paul](/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle). The sword is often erroneously supposed to commemorate the killing of [Peasants' Revolt](/wiki/Peasants%27_Revolt) leader [Wat Tyler](/wiki/Wat_Tyler) by [Lord Mayor of London](/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_London) [William Walworth](/wiki/William_Walworth). However, the arms were in use some months before Tyler's death, and the tradition that Walworth's dagger is depicted may date from the late 17th century.

The [Latin](/wiki/Latin) motto of the city is "*Domine dirige nos*", which translates as "Lord, direct us". It is thought to have been adopted in the 17th century, as the earliest record of it is in 1633.

A [banner of the arms](/wiki/Banner_of_arms) (the design on the shield) is flown as a [flag](/wiki/Flag_of_the_City_of_London).

## Geography

The City of London is the [smallest ceremonial county](/wiki/List_of_ceremonial_counties_of_England) of England by area and population, and the fourth most densely populated. Of the [326 English districts](/wiki/Districts_of_England), it is the second smallest [by population](/wiki/List_of_English_districts_by_population), after the [Isles of Scilly](/wiki/Isles_of_Scilly), and the smallest [by area](/wiki/List_of_English_districts_by_area). It is also [the smallest English city](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_the_United_Kingdom#England) by population (and in Britain, only two cities in Wales are smaller), and the [smallest in the UK by area](/wiki/List_of_smallest_cities_in_the_United_Kingdom).

The elevation of the City ranges from sea level at the Thames to 21.6 metres (71 ft) at the junction of High Holborn and Chancery Lane. Two small but notable hills are within the historic core, [Ludgate Hill](/wiki/Ludgate_Hill) to the west and [Cornhill](/wiki/Cornhill,_London) to the east. Between them ran the [Walbrook](/wiki/Walbrook), one of the many ["lost" rivers or streams of London](/wiki/Subterranean_rivers_of_London) (another is the [Fleet](/wiki/River_Fleet)).

### Boundary

Modern borders of the City of London, showing surrounding [London boroughs](/wiki/London_borough) and the pre-1994 boundary (where changed) in red. The area covered by the [Inner](/wiki/Inner_Temple) and [Middle Temple](/wiki/Middle_Temple) is marked in green.

Beginning in the west, where the City borders Westminster, the boundary crosses the [Victoria Embankment](/wiki/Victoria_Embankment) from the Thames, passes to the west of [Middle Temple](/wiki/Middle_Temple), then turns for a short distance along the Strand and near [Temple Bar](/wiki/Temple_Bar,_London) then north up [Chancery Lane](/wiki/Chancery_Lane), where it borders Camden. It turns east along [Holborn](/wiki/Holborn) to Holborn Circus and then goes northeast to [Charterhouse Street](/wiki/Charterhouse_Street). As it crosses [Farringdon Road](/wiki/Farringdon_Road) it becomes the boundary with Islington. It continues to [Aldersgate](/wiki/Aldersgate), goes north, and turns east into some back streets soon after Aldersgate becomes [Goswell Road](/wiki/Goswell_Road), since 1994 embracing all of the corporation's Golden Lane Estate. Here, at Baltic Street West, is the most northerly extent. The boundary includes all of the [Barbican Estate](/wiki/Barbican_Estate) and continues east along Ropemaker Street and its continuation on the other side of [Moorgate](/wiki/Moorgate), becomes South Place. It goes north, reaching the border with Hackney, then east, north, east on back streets, with Worship Street forming a northern boundary, so as to include the [Broadgate](/wiki/Broadgate) estate. The boundary then turns south at [Norton Folgate](/wiki/Norton_Folgate) and becomes the border with [Tower Hamlets](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Tower_Hamlets). It continues south into [Bishopsgate](/wiki/Bishopsgate), and takes some backstreets to Middlesex Street ([Petticoat Lane](/wiki/Petticoat_Lane)) where it continues south-east then south. It then turns south-west, crossing the [Minories](/wiki/Minories) so as to exclude the [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London), and then reaches the Thames.

The boundary then runs up the centre of the low-tide channel of the Thames, with the exception that [Blackfriars Bridge](/wiki/Blackfriars_Bridge) (including the river beneath and land at its south end) is entirely part of the City, making the City and [Borough of Richmond upon Thames](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Richmond_upon_Thames) the only London districts to span north and south of the river. The span and southern abutment of London Bridge is part of the city for some purposes (and as such is part of [Bridge](/wiki/Bridge_(City_of_London_ward)) ward).

The boundaries are marked by black bollards bearing the city's emblem, and by [dragon boundary marks](/wiki/Dragon_boundary_mark) at major entrances, such as Holborn and the south end of London Bridge. A more substantial monument marks the boundary at [Temple Bar](/wiki/Temple_Bar,_London) on Fleet Street.

In some places, the financial district extends slightly beyond the boundaries, notably to the north and east, into the London boroughs of Tower Hamlets, Hackney and Islington, and informally these locations are regarded as being part of the "Square Mile". Since the 1990s the eastern fringe, extending into Hackney and Tower Hamlets, has increasingly been a focus for large office developments due to the availability of large sites compared to within the city.

### Climate

The nearest weather station has historically been the London Weather Centre at [Kingsway](/wiki/Kingsway,_London)/ [Holborn](/wiki/Holborn), although observations ceased in 2010. Now St. James Park provides the nearest official readings.

The city has an [oceanic climate](/wiki/Maritime_Climate) ([Köppen](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) "Cfb") modified by the [urban heat island](/wiki/Urban_heat_island) in the centre of London. This generally causes higher night-time minima than outlying areas. For example, the August mean minimum of 14.7 °C (58.5 °F) compares to a figure of 13.3 °C (55.9 °F) for Greenwich and Heathrow whereas is 11.6 °C (52.9 °F) at Wisley in the middle of several square miles of [Metropolitan Green Belt](/wiki/Metropolitan_Green_Belt). All figures refer to the observation period 1971–2000.

Accordingly, the weather station holds the record for the UK's warmest overnight minimum temperature, 24.0 °C (75.2 °F), recorded on 4 August 1990. The maximum is 37.6 °C (99.7 °F), set on 10 August 2003. The absolute minimum for the weather station is a mere −8.2 °C (17.2 °F), compared to readings around −15.0 °C (5.0 °F) towards the edges of London. Unusually, this temperature was during a windy and snowy cold spell (mid-January 1987), rather than a cold clear night, cold air drainage is arrested due to the vast urban area surrounding the city.

The station holds the record for the highest British mean monthly temperature, 24.5 °C (76.1 °F) (mean maximum 29.2 °C (84.6 °F), mean minimum 19.7 °C (67.5 °F) during July 2006). However, in terms of daytime maximum temperatures, Cambridge NIAB and Botanical Gardens with a mean maximum of 29.1 °C (84.4 °F), and Heathrow with 29.0 °C (84.2 °F) all exceeded this.

## Public services

City of London coat of arms on the street

### Police and security

Further information: [Crime in London](/wiki/Crime_in_London)

A City of London Police vehicle on [Blackfriars Bridge](/wiki/Blackfriars_Bridge)

The city is a [police area](/wiki/Police_area) and has its own [police force](/wiki/Territorial_police_force), the [City of London Police](/wiki/City_of_London_Police), separate from the [Metropolitan Police Service](/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Service) covering the majority of Greater London. The City Police previously had three police stations, at [Snow Hill](/wiki/Snow_Hill,_London), [Wood Street](/wiki/Wood_Street,_London) and [Bishopsgate](/wiki/Bishopsgate). They now only retain Bishopsgate along with an administrative headquarters at Guildhall Yard East. The force comprises 735 police officers including 273 detectives. It is the smallest territorial police force in [England and Wales](/wiki/England_and_Wales), in both geographic area and the number of police officers.

Where the majority of British police forces have silver-coloured [badges](/wiki/Cap_badge), those of the City of London Police are black and gold featuring the City crest. The force has rare [red and white](/wiki/Sillitoe_tartan#United_Kingdom) chequered cap bands and unique red and white striped duty arm bands on the sleeves of the tunics of constables and sergeants (red and white being the colours of the city), which in most other British police forces are black and white. City police sergeants and constables wear crested [custodian helmets](/wiki/Custodian_helmet) whilst on foot patrol. These helmets do not feature either [St Edward's Crown](/wiki/St_Edward%27s_Crown) or the [Brunswick Star](/wiki/Brunswick_Star), which are used on most other police helmets in [England and Wales](/wiki/England_and_Wales).

The city's position as the United Kingdom's financial centre and a critical part of the country's economy, contributing about 2.5% of the UK's [gross national product](/wiki/Gross_national_product), has resulted in it becoming a target for political violence. The [Provisional IRA](/wiki/Provisional_IRA) exploded several [bombs](/wiki/Bomb) in the early 1990s, including the [1993 Bishopsgate bombing](/wiki/1993_Bishopsgate_bombing).

The area is also spoken of as a possible target for [al-Qaeda](/wiki/Al-Qaeda). For instance, when in May 2004 the BBC's *[Panorama](/wiki/Panorama_(TV_series))* programme examined the preparedness of Britain's emergency services for a terrorist attack on the scale of the [11 September 2001 attacks](/wiki/September_11_attacks), they simulated a chemical explosion on [Bishopsgate](/wiki/Bishopsgate) in the east of the city. The ["Ring of Steel"](/wiki/Ring_of_steel_(London)) was established in the wake of the IRA bombings to guard against terrorist threats.

### Fire brigade

Main article: [London Fire Brigade](/wiki/London_Fire_Brigade)

The city has fire risks in many historic buildings, including [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral), [Old Bailey](/wiki/Old_Bailey), [Mansion House](/wiki/Mansion_House,_London), [Smithfield Market](/wiki/Smithfield_Market), the [Guildhall](/wiki/Guildhall,_London), and also in numerous high-rise buildings. There is one London Fire Brigade station in the city, at [Dowgate](/wiki/Dowgate), with one [pumping appliance](/wiki/Fire_appliances_in_the_United_Kingdom). The City relies upon stations in the surrounding London boroughs to support it at some incidents. The first fire engine is in attendance in roughly five minutes on average, the second when required in a little over five and a half minutes. There were 1,814 incidents attended in the City in 2006/2007, the lowest in Greater London.

### Power

There is a [power station](/wiki/Power_station) located in [Charterhouse Street](/wiki/Charterhouse_Street) that also provides heat to some of the surrounding buildings.

The [Office for National Statistics](/wiki/Office_for_National_Statistics) recorded the population in 2011 as 7,375; slightly higher than in the [previous census](/wiki/United_Kingdom_Census_2001), 2001, and estimates the population as at mid-2016 to be 9,401. At the 2001 census the ethnic composition was 84.6% [White](/wiki/White_people), 6.8% [South Asian](/wiki/British_Asian), 2.6% [Black](/wiki/Black_British), 2.3% [Mixed](/wiki/British_Mixed-Race), 2.0% [Chinese](/wiki/British_Chinese) and 1.7% were listed as "[other](/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_the_United_Kingdom)". The population was between 120,000 and 140,000 in the first half of the 19th century, decreasing dramatically from 1851 to 1991, with a small increase between 1991 and 2001. The only notable boundary change since the first census in 1801 occurred in 1994.

The city's full-time working residents have much higher gross weekly pay than in London and Great Britain (England, Wales and Scotland): £773.30 compared to £598.60 and £491.00 respectively. There is a large inequality of income between genders (£1,085.90 in men compared to £653.50 in women), and this can be explained by job type and length of employment respectively. The 2001 Census showed the city as a unique district amongst 376 districts surveyed in [England](/wiki/List_of_English_districts) and [Wales](/wiki/Districts_of_Wales). The city had the highest proportional population increase, one-person households, people with qualifications at degree level or higher and the highest indications of overcrowding. It recorded the lowest proportion of households with cars or vans, people who travel to work by car, married couple households and the lowest average household size: just 1.58 people. It also ranked highest within the Greater London area for the percentage of people with no religion and people who are employed.

## Economy

See also: [Economy of London](/wiki/Economy_of_London)

The [Bank of England](/wiki/Bank_of_England), on [Threadneedle Street](/wiki/Threadneedle_Street), is the central bank of the United Kingdom.

[Paternoster Square](/wiki/Paternoster_Square), since 2004 the home of the [London Stock Exchange](/wiki/London_Stock_Exchange)

The City of London vies with New York City's [Lower Manhattan](/wiki/Lower_Manhattan) for the distinction of the world's pre-eminent financial centre. The [London Stock Exchange](/wiki/London_Stock_Exchange) ([shares](/wiki/Share_capital) and [bonds](/wiki/Bond_(finance))), [Lloyd's of London](/wiki/Lloyd%27s_of_London) (insurance) and the [Bank of England](/wiki/Bank_of_England) are all based in the city. Over 500 banks have offices in the city. The [Alternative Investment Market](/wiki/Alternative_Investment_Market), a market for trades in [equities](/wiki/Capital_stock) of smaller firms, is a recent development. In 2009, the City of London accounted for 2.4% of UK GDP.

London's foreign exchange market has been described by Reuters as 'the crown jewel of London's financial sector'. Of the $3.98 trillion daily global turnover, as measured in 2009, trading in London accounted for around $1.85 trillion, or 46.7% of the total. The [pound sterling](/wiki/Pound_sterling), the currency of the United Kingdom, is globally the fourth-most traded currency and the fourth most held [reserve currency](/wiki/Reserve_currency).

Canary Wharf, a few miles east of the City in Tower Hamlets, which houses many banks and other institutions formerly located in the Square Mile, has since 1991 become another centre for London's financial services industry. Although growth has continued in both locations, and there have been relocations in both directions, the Corporation has come to realise that its planning policies may have been causing financial firms to choose Canary Wharf as a location.

In 2022, 12.3% of City of London residents had been granted non-domicile status in order to avoid their paying tax in the UK.

### Headquarters

Many major global companies have their headquarters in the city, including [Aviva](/wiki/Aviva), [BT Group](/wiki/BT_Group), [Lloyds Banking Group](/wiki/Lloyds_Banking_Group), [Quilter](/wiki/Quilter_plc), [Prudential](/wiki/Prudential_plc), [Schroders](/wiki/Schroders_plc), [Standard Chartered](/wiki/Standard_Chartered), and [Unilever](/wiki/Unilever_PLC).

A number of the world's largest law firms are headquartered in the city, including four of the [Magic Circle](/wiki/Magic_Circle_(law_firms)) law firms ([Allen & Overy](/wiki/Allen_%26_Overy), [Freshfields Bruckhaus Deringer](/wiki/Freshfields_Bruckhaus_Deringer), [Linklaters](/wiki/Linklaters) and [Slaughter & May](/wiki/Slaughter_%26_May)), as well as other firms such as [Ashurst](/wiki/Ashurst_LLP), [DLA Piper](/wiki/DLA_Piper), [Eversheds Sutherland](/wiki/Eversheds_Sutherland), [Herbert Smith Freehills](/wiki/Herbert_Smith_Freehills) and [Hogan Lovells](/wiki/Hogan_Lovells).

### Other sectors

Barbican Centre

Whilst the financial sector, and related businesses and institutions, continue to dominate, the economy is not limited to that sector. The legal profession has a strong presence, especially in the west and north (i.e., towards the [Inns of Court](/wiki/Inns_of_Court)). Retail businesses were once important, but have gradually moved to the [West End of London](/wiki/West_End_of_London), though it is now Corporation policy to encourage retailing in some locations, for example at [Cheapside](/wiki/Cheapside) near St Paul's. The city has a number of visitor attractions, mainly based on its historic heritage as well as the [Barbican Centre](/wiki/Barbican_Centre) and adjacent [Museum of London](/wiki/Museum_of_London), though tourism is not at present a major contributor to the city's economy or character. The city has many pubs, bars and restaurants, and the "night-time" economy does feature in the [Bishopsgate](/wiki/Bishopsgate) area, towards [Shoreditch](/wiki/Shoreditch). The meat market [at Smithfield](/wiki/Smithfield_Market), wholly within the city, continues to be one of London's main markets (the only one remaining in central London) and the country's largest [meat market](/wiki/Butcher). In the east is [Leadenhall Market](/wiki/Leadenhall_Market), a fresh food market that is also a visitor attraction.

### Retail and residential

The trend for purely office development is beginning to reverse as the Corporation encourages residential use, albeit with development occurring when it arises on windfall sites. The city has a target of 90 additional dwellings per year. Some of the extra accommodation is in small pre-[World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) [listed buildings](/wiki/Listed_building), which are not suitable for occupation by the large companies which now provide much of the city's employment. Recent residential developments include "the Heron", a high-rise residential building on the Milton Court site adjacent to the Barbican, and the [Heron Plaza](/wiki/Heron_Tower#Heron_Plaza) development on Bishopsgate is also expected to include residential parts.

Since the 1990s, the City has diversified away from near exclusive office use in other ways. For example, several hotels and the first [department store](/wiki/Department_store) opened in the 2000s. A shopping centre was more recently opened at [One New Change](/wiki/One_New_Change), [Cheapside](/wiki/Cheapside) (near St Paul's Cathedral) in October 2010, which is open seven days a week. However, large sections remain quiet at weekends, especially in the eastern section, and it is quite common to find shops, [pubs](/wiki/Public_house) and cafes closed on these days.

## Landmarks

### Historic buildings

Fire, bombing and post-[World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) redevelopment have meant that the city, despite its history, has fewer intact historic structures than one might expect. Nonetheless, there remain many dozens of (mostly Victorian and Edwardian) fine buildings, typically in historicist and [neoclassical style](/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture). They include the [Monument to the Great Fire of London](/wiki/Monument_to_the_Great_Fire_of_London) ("the Monument"), [St Paul's Cathedral](/wiki/St_Paul%27s_Cathedral), [the Guildhall](/wiki/Guildhall,_London), the [Royal Exchange](/wiki/Royal_Exchange,_London), [Dr. Johnson's House](/wiki/Dr._Johnson%27s_House), [Mansion House](/wiki/Mansion_House,_London) and a [great many churches](/wiki/Category:Churches_in_the_City_of_London), many designed by [Sir Christopher Wren](/wiki/Sir_Christopher_Wren), who also designed St Paul's.

[Prince Henry's Room](/wiki/Prince_Henry%27s_Room) and [2 King's Bench Walk](/wiki/2_King%27s_Bench_Walk) are notable historic survivors of heavy bombing of [the Temple](/wiki/Temple,_London) area, which has largely been rebuilt to its historic form. Another example of a bomb-damaged place having been restored is [Staple Inn](/wiki/Staple_Inn) on Holborn. A few small sections of the Roman [London Wall](/wiki/London_Wall) exist, for example near the Tower of London and in the Barbican area. Among the twentieth-century [listed buildings](/wiki/Listed_buildings) are [Bracken House](/wiki/Bracken_House,_London), the first post World War II buildings in the country to be given statutory protection, and the whole of the [Barbican](/wiki/Barbican_Estate) and [Golden Lane Estate](/wiki/Golden_Lane_Estate).

The [Tower of London](/wiki/Tower_of_London) is not in the city, but is a notable visitor attraction which brings tourists to the southeast of the city. Other landmark buildings with historical significance include the [Bank of England](/wiki/Bank_of_England), the [Old Bailey](/wiki/Old_Bailey), the [Custom House](/wiki/Custom_House,_City_of_London), [Smithfield Market](/wiki/Smithfield,_London), [Leadenhall Market](/wiki/Leadenhall_Market) and [St Bartholomew's Hospital](/wiki/St_Bartholomew%27s_Hospital). Noteworthy contemporary buildings include a number of modern high-rise buildings (see section below) as well as the [Lloyd's building](/wiki/Lloyd%27s_building).

The Bank of England (left) and the Royal Exchange (centre) are two of the many significant buildings in the City of London.

The [Elizabeth line](/wiki/Elizabeth_line) (constructed by the [Crossrail](/wiki/Crossrail) project) runs east–west underneath the City of London. The line serves two stations in or very near the City – [Farringdon](/wiki/Farringdon_station) and Liverpool Street – which additionally serves the Barbican and Moorgate areas. Elizabeth line services link the City directly to destinations such as Canary Wharf, [Heathrow Airport](/wiki/Heathrow_Airport), and the [M4 Corridor](/wiki/M4_corridor) high-technology hub (serving [Slough](/wiki/Slough) and [Reading](/wiki/Reading,_Berkshire)).

The city is served by a frequent [Thameslink](/wiki/Thameslink) rail service which runs north–south through London. Thameslink services call at Farringdon, [City Thameslink](/wiki/City_Thameslink_railway_station), and London Blackfriars. This provides the city with a direct link to key destinations across London, including [Elephant & Castle](/wiki/Elephant_%26_Castle_railway_station), [London Bridge](/wiki/London_Bridge_station), and [St Pancras International](/wiki/St_Pancras_railway_station) (for the [Eurostar](/wiki/Eurostar) to [mainland Europe](/wiki/Mainland_Europe)). The [Stansted Express](/wiki/Stansted_Express) departs from [Liverpool Street Station](/wiki/Liverpool_Street_station) in the city and runs directly to [Stansted Airport](/wiki/London_Stansted_Airport) in Essex.

The national [A1](/wiki/A1_road_(Great_Britain)), [A10](/wiki/A10_road_(England)) [A3](/wiki/A3_road), [A4](/wiki/A4_road_(England)), and [A40 road](/wiki/A40_road) routes begin in the city. The city is in the [London congestion charge](/wiki/London_congestion_charge) zone, with the small exception on the eastern boundary of the sections of the A1210/A1211 that are part of the [Inner Ring Road](/wiki/Inner_Ring_Road,_London). The following bridges, listed west to east (downstream), cross the River Thames: [Blackfriars Bridge](/wiki/Blackfriars_Bridge), [Blackfriars Railway Bridge](/wiki/Blackfriars_Railway_Bridge), [Millennium Bridge](/wiki/Millennium_Bridge_(London)) (footbridge), [Southwark Bridge](/wiki/Southwark_Bridge), [Cannon Street Railway Bridge](/wiki/Cannon_Street_Railway_Bridge) and London Bridge; [Tower Bridge](/wiki/Tower_Bridge) is not in the city. The city, like most of central London, is well [served by buses](/wiki/Buses_in_London), including night buses. Two bus stations are in the city, at [Aldgate](/wiki/Aldgate_bus_station) on the eastern boundary with Tower Hamlets, and at [Liverpool Street](/wiki/Liverpool_Street_bus_station) by the railway station.
However although the London Road Traffic Act 1924 removed from existing local authorities the powers to prevent the development of road passengers transport services within the London Metropolitan Area, the City of London retained most such powers. As a consequence, neither Trolleybus nor Green Line Coach services were permitted to enter the City to pick up or set down passengers. Hence the building of Aldgate (Minories) Trolleybus and Coach station as well as the complex terminal arrangements at Parliament Hill Fields. This restriction was removed by the Transport Act 1985.

### Cycling

[Cycleway 6](/wiki/List_of_cycle_routes_in_London) runs between [Elephant & Castle](/wiki/Elephant_and_Castle) and [Kentish Town](/wiki/Kentish_Town), passing through the City of London between Blackfriars and Farringdon.

[Cycling infrastructure](/wiki/Cycling_infrastructure) in the city is maintained by the City of London Corporation and [Transport for London](/wiki/Transport_for_London) (TfL).

- [Cycle Superhighway 1](/wiki/List_of_cycle_routes_in_London) runs from [Tottenham](/wiki/Tottenham) to the city. It is a signposted cycle route, passing through [Stoke Newington](/wiki/Stoke_Newington) and [Hackney](/wiki/Hackney,_London) before entering the City south of Old Street.
- [Cycle Superhighway 2](/wiki/List_of_cycle_routes_in_London) runs from [Stratford](/wiki/Stratford,_London) to the city, via [Bow](/wiki/Bow,_London), [Mile End](/wiki/Mile_End), and [Whitechapel](/wiki/Whitechapel). The route enters the city near Aldgate. The route runs primarily on segregated [cycle track](/wiki/Cycle_track).
- [Cycleway 3](/wiki/Cycle_Superhighway_3) is an east–west [bike freeway](/wiki/Bike_freeway) through the city. The route runs along the southern rim of the city, following the route of the Thames. Eastbound, Cycleway 3 provides cyclists with a direct, signposted cycle link to [Shadwell](/wiki/Shadwell), [Poplar](/wiki/Poplar,_London) and Canary Wharf, and [Barking](/wiki/Barking,_London). The route runs Westbound on traffic-free track to [Lancaster Gate](/wiki/Lancaster_Gate) via [Parliament Square](/wiki/Parliament_Square), [Buckingham Palace](/wiki/Buckingham_Palace), and [Hyde Park](/wiki/Hyde_Park,_London).
- [Cycleway 6](/wiki/List_of_cycle_routes_in_London) runs north–south through the city on traffic-free cycle track. The track passes [Farringdon Station](/wiki/Farringdon_station), the [Holborn Viaduct](/wiki/Holborn_Viaduct), [Ludgate Circus](/wiki/Ludgate_Circus), [Blackfriars station](/wiki/Blackfriars_station), and [Blackfriars Bridge](/wiki/Blackfriars_Bridge). Northbound, the route passes through [Clerkenwell](/wiki/Clerkenwell), [Bloomsbury](/wiki/Bloomsbury), [King's Cross](/wiki/Kings_Cross,_London), and [Kentish Town](/wiki/Kentish_Town). The route southbound carries cyclists to [Elephant and Castle](/wiki/Elephant_and_Castle).
- [Cycle Superhighway 7](/wiki/List_of_cycle_routes_in_London) begins in the City at an interchange with Cycleway 3. It leaves the City over [Southwark Bridge](/wiki/Southwark_Bridge) and provides cyclists with an unbroken, signposted route to [Colliers Wood](/wiki/Colliers_Wood) via Elephant and Castle, [Clapham](/wiki/Clapham), and [Tooting](/wiki/Tooting), amongst other destinations.
- [Quietway 11](/wiki/List_of_cycle_routes_in_London) is a northbound continuation of Cycleway 7. It is a signposted cycle route which runs from Southwark Bridge to [Hoxton](/wiki/Hoxton), via the Barbican and Moorgate.

The [Sandander Cycles](/wiki/Santander_Cycles) and Beryl bike sharing systems operate in the City of London.

### River

One [London River Services](/wiki/London_River_Services) pier is on the Thames in the city, [Blackfriars Millennium Pier](/wiki/Blackfriars_Millennium_Pier), though the [Tower Millennium Pier](/wiki/Tower_Millennium_Pier) lies adjacent to the boundary near the Tower of London. One of the [Port of London](/wiki/Port_of_London)'s 25 [safeguarded wharves](/wiki/Safeguarded_wharf), [Walbrook Wharf](/wiki/Walbrook_Wharf), is adjacent to Cannon Street station, and is used by the corporation to transfer waste via the river. Swan Lane Pier, just upstream of London Bridge, is proposed to be replaced and upgraded for regular passenger services, planned to take place in 2012–2015. Before then, Tower Pier is to be extended.

There is a public riverside walk along the river bank, part of the [Thames Path](/wiki/Thames_Path), which opened in stages – the route within the city was completed by the opening of a stretch at [Queenhithe](/wiki/Queenhithe) in 2023. The walk along Walbrook Wharf is closed to pedestrians when waste is being transferred onto barges.

### Travel to work (by residents)

According to a survey conducted in March 2011, the methods by which employed residents 16–74 get to work varied widely: 48.4% go on foot; 19.5% via light rail, (i.e. [the Underground](/wiki/London_Underground), [DLR](/wiki/Docklands_Light_Railway), etc.); 9.2% work mainly from home; 5.8% take the train; 5.6% travel by bus, minibus, or coach; and 5.3% go by bicycle; with just 3.4% commuting by car or van, as driver or passenger.

## Education

[The Aldgate School](/wiki/The_Aldgate_School) (using its former name)

The city is home to a number of higher education institutions including: the [Guildhall School of Music and Drama](/wiki/Guildhall_School_of_Music_and_Drama), the [Cass Business School](/wiki/Cass_Business_School), [The London Institute of Banking & Finance](/wiki/The_London_Institute_of_Banking_%26_Finance) and parts of three of the [universities in London](/wiki/Universities_in_London): the [Maughan Library](/wiki/Maughan_Library) of [King's College London](/wiki/King%27s_College_London) on [Chancery Lane](/wiki/Chancery_Lane), the business school of [London Metropolitan University](/wiki/London_Metropolitan_University), and a campus of the [University of Chicago Booth School of Business](/wiki/University_of_Chicago_Booth_School_of_Business). [The College of Law](/wiki/University_of_Law) has its London campus in [Moorgate](/wiki/Moorgate). Part of [Barts and The London School of Medicine and Dentistry](/wiki/Barts_and_The_London_School_of_Medicine_and_Dentistry) is on the Barts hospital site at West Smithfield.

The city has only one directly maintained primary school, [The Aldgate School](/wiki/The_Aldgate_School) (formerly Sir John Cass's Foundation Primary School) at [Aldgate](/wiki/Aldgate) (ages 4 to 11). It is a Voluntary-Aided (VA) [Church of England](/wiki/Church_of_England) school, maintained by the Education Service of the City of London.

City residents send their children to schools in neighbouring [Local Education Authorities](/wiki/Local_Education_Authority), such as [Islington](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Islington), [Tower Hamlets](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Tower_Hamlets), [Westminster](/wiki/City_of_Westminster) and [Southwark](/wiki/London_Borough_of_Southwark).

The City controls three independent schools, [City of London School](/wiki/City_of_London_School) (a boys' school) and [City of London School for Girls](/wiki/City_of_London_School_for_Girls) in the city, and the [City of London Freemen's School](/wiki/City_of_London_Freemen%27s_School) (co-educational day and boarding) in [Ashtead](/wiki/Ashtead), Surrey. The City of London School for Girls and City of London Freemen's School have their own preparatory departments for entrance at age seven. It is the principal sponsor of The [City Academy, Hackney](/wiki/City_Academy,_Hackney), [City of London Academy Islington](/wiki/City_of_London_Academy_Islington), and [City of London Academy, Southwark](/wiki/City_of_London_Academy,_Southwark).

### Public libraries

The [Maughan Library](/wiki/Maughan_Library), [King's College London](/wiki/King%27s_College_London), located on [Chancery Lane](/wiki/Chancery_Lane)

Libraries operated by the Corporation include three lending libraries; Barbican Library, Shoe Lane Library and Artizan Street Library and Community Centre. Membership is open to all – with one official proof of address required to join.

Guildhall Library, and City Business Library are also public reference libraries, specialising in the history of London and business reference resources.

## Money laundering

The City of London's role in illicit financial activity such as [money laundering](/wiki/Money_laundering) has earned the financial hub sobriquets such as 'The Laundromat' and 'Londongrad'.

In May 2024, the UK's then deputy foreign secretary, [Andrew Mitchell](/wiki/Andrew_Mitchell), said that 40% of the dirty money in the world goes through London and crown dependencies.
