# List of highest mountains on Earth

This article is about mountains by height above sea level. For a list of mountains by topographic prominence, see [List of mountain peaks by prominence](/wiki/List_of_mountain_peaks_by_prominence). For other uses, see [List of mountains](/wiki/List_of_mountains).

Aerial view of [Mount Everest](/wiki/Mount_Everest) from the south. The peak rises over [Lhotse](/wiki/Lhotse), while [Nuptse](/wiki/Nuptse) is the ridge on the left.

There are at least 108 [mountains](/wiki/Mountain) on Earth with [elevations](/wiki/Elevation) of 7,200 m (23,622 ft; 4 mi) or greater above [sea level](/wiki/Sea_level). Of these, 14 are more than 8,000 m (26,247 ft; 5 mi). The vast majority of these mountains are part of either the [Himalayas](/wiki/Himalayas) or the [Karakoram](/wiki/Karakoram) mountain ranges located on the edge of the [Indian plate](/wiki/Indian_plate) and [Eurasian plate](/wiki/Eurasian_plate) in [China](/wiki/China), [India](/wiki/India), [Nepal](/wiki/Nepal), and [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan).

## Discussion

Figure demonstrating the concept of [topographic prominence](/wiki/Topographic_prominence): The prominence of a peak is the height of the peak's [summit](/wiki/Summit) above the lowest contour line encircling it and no higher summit. For example, vertical arrows show the topographic prominence of three peaks on an island. A dotted horizontal line links each peak (except the highest) to its key [col](/wiki/Col).

The dividing line between a mountain with multiple peaks and separate mountains is not always clear (see also [Highest unclimbed mountain](/wiki/Highest_unclimbed_mountain)). A popular and intuitive way to distinguish mountains from subsidiary peaks is by their height above the highest saddle connecting it to a higher summit, a measure called [topographic prominence](/wiki/Topographic_prominence) or re-ascent (the higher summit is called the "parent peak"). A common definition of a mountain is a summit with 300 m (984 ft) prominence. Alternatively, a relative prominence (prominence/height) is used (usually 7–8%) to reflect that in higher mountain ranges everything is on a larger scale. The table below lists the highest 100 summits with at least 500 m (1,640 ft) prominence, approximating a 7% relative prominence. A drawback of a prominence-based list is that it may exclude well-known or spectacular mountains that are connected via a high ridge to a taller summit, such as [Eiger](/wiki/Eiger), [Nuptse](/wiki/Nuptse) or [Annapurna IV](/wiki/Annapurna_IV). A few such peaks and mountains with nearly sufficient prominence are included in this list, and given a rank of "S".

It is very unlikely that all given heights are correct to the nearest metre; indeed, the sea level is often problematic to define when a mountain is remote from the sea. Different sources often differ by many metres, and the heights given below may well differ from those elsewhere in this encyclopedia. As an extreme example, [Ulugh Muztagh](/wiki/Ulugh_Muztagh) on the north [Tibetan Plateau](/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau) is often listed as 7,723 m (25,338 ft) to 7,754 m (25,440 ft), but appears to be only 6,973 m (22,877 ft) to 6,987 m (22,923 ft). Some mountains differ by more than 100 m (330 ft) on different maps, while even very thorough current measurements of [Mount Everest](/wiki/Mount_Everest#Surveys) range from 8,840 m (29,003 ft) to 8,849 m (29,032 ft). These discrepancies serve to emphasize the uncertainties in the listed heights.

Though some parts of the world, especially the most mountainous parts, have never been thoroughly mapped, it is unlikely that any mountains this high have been overlooked, because [synthetic aperture radar](/wiki/Synthetic_aperture_radar) can and [has been](/wiki/Shuttle_Radar_Topography_Mission) used to measure elevations of most otherwise inaccessible places. Still, heights or prominences may be revised, so that the order of the list may change and even new mountains could enter the list over time. To be safe, the list has been extended to include all 7,200 m (23,622 ft) peaks.

The highest mountains above sea level are generally not the highest mountains above the surrounding terrain, also called the highest free-standing mountains. There is no precise definition of surrounding base, but [Denali](/wiki/Denali), [Mount Kilimanjaro](/wiki/Mount_Kilimanjaro) and [Nanga Parbat](/wiki/Nanga_Parbat) are possible candidates for the tallest mountain on land by this measure.

The bases of mountain islands are below sea level, and given this consideration. [Mauna Kea](/wiki/Mauna_Kea) (4,207 m (13,802 ft) above sea level) is the [world](/wiki/World)'s tallest mountain and [volcano](/wiki/Volcano), rising about 10,203 m (33,474 ft) from the [Pacific Ocean](/wiki/Pacific_Ocean) floor. [Mount Lamlam](/wiki/Mount_Lamlam) on [Guam](/wiki/Guam) is periodically claimed to be among the world's highest mountains because it is adjacent to the [Mariana Trench](/wiki/Mariana_Trench); the most extreme claim is that, measured from [Challenger Deep](/wiki/Challenger_Deep) 313 kilometres (194 mi) away, Mount Lamlam is 11,530 metres (37,820 ft) tall. [Ojos del Salado](/wiki/Ojos_del_Salado) has the greatest rise on [Earth](/wiki/Earth): 13,420 m (44,029 ft) vertically to the summit from the bottom of the [Atacama Trench](/wiki/Atacama_Trench), which is about 560 km (350 mi) away, although most of this rise is not part of the mountain.

The highest mountains are also not generally the most voluminous. [Mauna Loa](/wiki/Mauna_Loa) (4,169 m or 13,678 ft) is the largest mountain on Earth in terms of base area (about 5,200 km2 or 2,000 sq mi) and volume (about 42,000 km3 or 10,000 mi3), although, due to the intergrade of [lava](/wiki/Lava) from [Kilauea](/wiki/Kilauea), [Hualalai](/wiki/Hualalai) and [Mauna Kea](/wiki/Mauna_Kea), the volume can only be estimated based on surface area and height of the edifice. [Mount Kilimanjaro](/wiki/Mount_Kilimanjaro) is the largest non-shield volcano in terms of both base area (635 km2 or 245 sq mi) and volume (4,793 km3 or 1,150 mi3). [Mount Logan](/wiki/Mount_Logan) is the largest non-volcanic mountain in base area (311 km2 or 120 sq mi).

The highest mountains above sea level are also not those with peaks farthest from the centre of the Earth, because the [shape of the Earth](/wiki/Shape_of_the_Earth) is not spherical. Sea level closer to the equator is several kilometres farther from the centre of the Earth. The summit of [Chimborazo](/wiki/Chimborazo_(volcano)), [Ecuador](/wiki/Ecuador)'s tallest mountain, is usually considered to be the farthest point from the Earth's centre, although the southern summit of [Peru](/wiki/Peru)'s tallest mountain, [Huascarán](/wiki/Huascar%C3%A1n), is another contender. Both have elevations above sea level more than 2 km (1.2 mi) less than that of Everest.

Almost all mountains in the list are located in the [Himalaya](/wiki/Himalaya) and [Karakoram](/wiki/Karakoram) ranges to the south and west of the Tibetan Plateau. All peaks 7,000 m (22,966 ft) or higher are located in [East](/wiki/East_Asia), [Central](/wiki/Central_Asia) or [South Asia](/wiki/South_Asia) in a rectangle edged by [Noshaq](/wiki/Noshaq) (7,492 m or 24,580 ft) on the [Afghanistan–Pakistan border](/wiki/Durand_Line) in the west, [Jengish Chokusu](/wiki/Jengish_Chokusu) (Tuōmù'ěr Fēng, 7,439 m or 24,406 ft) on the [Kyrgyzstan](/wiki/Kyrgyzstan)–[Xinjiang](/wiki/Xinjiang) border to the north, [Gongga Shan](/wiki/Gongga_Shan) (Minya Konka, 7,556 m or 24,790 ft) in [Sichuan](/wiki/Sichuan) to the east, and [Kabru](/wiki/Kabru) (7,412 m or 24,318 ft) on the [Sikkim](/wiki/Sikkim)–[Nepal](/wiki/Nepal) border to the south.

As of November 2025, the highest peaks on four of the mountains—[Gangkhar Puensum](/wiki/Gangkhar_Puensum), [Labuche Kang III](/wiki/Labuche_Kang_III/East), [Tongshanjiabu](/wiki/Tongshanjiabu) and [Apsarasas Kangri](/wiki/Apsarasas_Kangri), all located in [India](/wiki/India), [Bhutan](/wiki/Bhutan) or [China](/wiki/China)—have not been ascended. The most recent peak to have its first ever ascent is [Karjiang](/wiki/Karjiang), in [China](/wiki/China), on 13 August 2024.

The highest mountain [outside of Asia](/wiki/Seven_Summits) is [Aconcagua](/wiki/Aconcagua) (6,962 m or 22,841 ft), the 188th highest in the world.
