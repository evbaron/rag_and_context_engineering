**Croatia**, officially the **Republic of Croatia**, is a country in [Central](/wiki/Central_Europe) and [Southeast Europe](/wiki/Southeast_Europe), on the coast of the [Adriatic Sea](/wiki/Adriatic_Sea). It borders [Slovenia](/wiki/Slovenia) to the northwest, [Hungary](/wiki/Hungary) to the northeast, [Serbia](/wiki/Serbia) to the east, [Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and [Montenegro](/wiki/Montenegro) to the southeast, and shares a maritime border with [Italy](/wiki/Italy) to the west. The [Croatian archipelago](/wiki/List_of_inhabited_islands_of_Croatia) contains over 1,000 islands and islets, the [largest overseas territory](/wiki/Adriatic_Sea#Geography) on the Adriatic Sea. Its capital, largest city and main cultural and economic centre is [Zagreb](/wiki/Zagreb). Major urban centers include [Split](/wiki/Split,_Croatia), [Rijeka](/wiki/Rijeka), and [Osijek](/wiki/Osijek). The country is composed of [twenty counties](/wiki/Counties_of_Croatia) spanning 56,594 square kilometres (21,851 square miles) within [four administrative regions](/wiki/Regions_of_Croatia). Croatia has [a population](/wiki/Demographics_of_Croatia) of nearly 3.9 million as of 2026.

[Croatian history](/wiki/History_of_Croatia) began with the 6th century arrival of the [Croats](/wiki/Croats) to [Roman Illyria](/wiki/Illyria). Settled as [two duchies](/wiki/Duchy_of_Croatia) a century later, Croatia was granted independence in 878 under Duke [Branimir](/wiki/Branimir,_Duke_of_Croatia) and elevated into [a kingdom](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia_(925%E2%80%931102)) under King [Tomislav](/wiki/Tomislav,_King_of_Croatia) in 925. The [Trpimirović dynastic](/wiki/Trpimirovi%C4%87_dynasty) succession crisis [placed Croatia](/wiki/Croatia_in_personal_union_with_Hungary) into a [personal union](/wiki/Personal_union) with [Hungary](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary_(1000%E2%80%931301)) in 1102. In 1527, amid [Ottoman conflict](/wiki/Hundred_Years%27_Croatian%E2%80%93Ottoman_War), Croatia aligned with the [Habsburg monarchy](/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy). It reorganized into the [State of Slovenes, Croats, and Serbs](/wiki/State_of_Slovenes,_Croats_and_Serbs) before [merging into](/wiki/Creation_of_Yugoslavia) the [Kingdom of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia) in 1918. During [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), most of Croatia was invaded by the [Axis powers](/wiki/Axis_powers) who installed a [puppet state](/wiki/List_of_World_War_II_puppet_states) known as the [Independent State of Croatia](/wiki/Independent_State_of_Croatia). A [resistance movement](/wiki/Croatian_Partisans) restored sovereignty, emerging as the [Socialist Republic of Croatia](/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Croatia) within the [Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia) in 1943. Croatia declared [independence from Yugoslavia](/wiki/Independence_of_Croatia) in 1991, successfully fighting the [War of Independence](/wiki/Croatian_War_of_Independence) until 1995.

Modern Croatia is a [representative democracy](/wiki/Representative_democracy) with a [prime minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Croatia), [president](/wiki/President_of_Croatia), and [unicameral parliament](/wiki/Croatian_Parliament). Its [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_Croatia) ensures separation of powers between the [executive](/wiki/Government_of_Croatia), [legislative](/wiki/Law_of_Croatia), and [judicial branches](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Croatia). [Croatian politics](/wiki/Politics_of_Croatia) reflect [a multi-party system](/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Croatia). Croatia is part of many international organizations and forums including the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union), [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations), [WTO](/wiki/World_Trade_Organization), and [NATO](/wiki/NATO), among others. It [exerts regional influence](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Croatia) as a [small power](/wiki/Small_power) in Europe, particularly within neighboring Southeast Europe. Since 1999, the [Croatian armed forces](/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_Croatia) have deployed internationally for a variety of [UN peacekeeping missions](/wiki/United_Nations_peacekeeping) and multilateral military interventions. Croatia [was elected](/wiki/2007_United_Nations_Security_Council_election) to serve on the non-permanent seat of the [UN Security Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council) from 2008 to 2009.

A [developed country](/wiki/Developed_country), Croatia has [an advanced economy](/wiki/Economy_of_Croatia) considered [high-income](/wiki/High-income) and dominated by its service, industrial, and agricultural sectors. Its [tourism sector](/wiki/Tourism_in_Croatia) is prominent, generating significant economic activity across the country. Croatia has heavily invested in infrastructure, especially in [transport routes and facilities](/wiki/Transport_in_Croatia) along [European corridors](/wiki/Pan-European_corridors). An [emerging energy power](/wiki/Energy_in_Croatia) in Europe, it has strategic investments in [liquefied natural gas](/wiki/Liquefied_natural_gas), [geothermal networks](/wiki/Geothermal_energy), and electric transport. It has extensive biodiversity, wildlife, and [natural resources](/wiki/Geology_of_Croatia) across [its geography](/wiki/Geography_of_Croatia). Croatia provides [social security](/wiki/Welfare_spending), [universal health care](/wiki/Healthcare_in_Croatia), and subsidized [primary and secondary education](/wiki/Education_in_Croatia) while supporting [culture](/wiki/Culture_of_Croatia) through [media](/wiki/Mass_media_in_Croatia) and [publishing](/wiki/Croatian_literature).

## Etymology

Main article: [Names of the Croats and Croatia](/wiki/Names_of_the_Croats_and_Croatia)

Croatia's [non-native](/wiki/Endonym_and_exonym) [name](/wiki/Names_of_the_Croats_and_Croatia) derives from [Medieval Latin](/wiki/Medieval_Latin) *Croātia*, itself a derivation of [North-West Slavic](/wiki/West_Slavic_languages) \**Xərwate*, by [liquid metathesis](/wiki/Slavic_liquid_metathesis_and_pleophony) from Common Slavic period *\*Xorvat*, from proposed [Proto-Slavic](/wiki/Proto-Slavic_language) *[\*Xъrvátъ](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction:Proto-Slavic/x%D1%8Arvat%D1%8A)* which possibly comes from the 3rd-century [Scytho-Sarmatian](/wiki/Scythian_languages) form attested in the [Tanais Tablets](/wiki/Tanais_Tablets) as Χοροάθος (**Khoroáthos**. Alternate forms are *Khoróatos* and **Khoroúathos**). The origin of the ethnonym is uncertain, but most probably is from [Proto-Ossetian](/wiki/Ossetian_language) / [Alanian](/wiki/Scythian_languages#Alanian) \**xurvæt-* or \**xurvāt-*, in the meaning of "one who guards" ("guardian, protector").

The oldest preserved record of the Croatian ethnonym's native variation *\*xъrvatъ* is of the variable stem, attested in the [Baška tablet](/wiki/Ba%C5%A1ka_tablet) in [style](/wiki/Style_(form_of_address)) zvъnъmirъ kralъ xrъvatъskъ ("[Zvonimir](/wiki/Demetrius_Zvonimir_of_Croatia), [Croatian king](/wiki/List_of_rulers_of_Croatia)"), while the Latin variation *Croatorum* is archaeologically confirmed on a church inscription found in [Bijaći](/wiki/Bija%C4%87i) near [Trogir](/wiki/Trogir) dated to the end of the 8th or early 9th century. The presumably oldest stone [inscription](/wiki/Epigraphy) with fully preserved ethnonym is the 9th-century [Branimir inscription](/wiki/Branimir_inscription) found near [Benkovac](/wiki/Benkovac), where Duke [Branimir](/wiki/Branimir_of_Croatia) is styled *Dux Cruatorvm*, likely dated between 879 and 892, during his rule. The Latin term **Chroatorum** is attributed to a charter of Duke [Trpimir I of Croatia](/wiki/Trpimir_I_of_Croatia), dated to 852 in a 1568 copy of a lost original, but it is not certain if the original was found to be older than the Branimir inscription.

## History

Main article: [History of Croatia](/wiki/History_of_Croatia)

### Prehistory and antiquity

Main article: [History of Croatia before the Croats](/wiki/History_of_Croatia_before_the_Croats)

Left: The [Vučedol dove](/wiki/Vu%C4%8Dedol_dove), a sculpture from 2800 to 2500 BC.
Right: *[Croatian Apoxyomenos](/wiki/Croatian_Apoxyomenos)*, [Ancient Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greece) statue, 2nd or 1st century BC.

The area known as modern Croatia was inhabited throughout the [prehistoric period](/wiki/Prehistoric_period). [Neanderthal](/wiki/Neanderthal) fossils dating to the middle [Palaeolithic](/wiki/Palaeolithic) period were unearthed in northern Croatia, best presented at the [Krapina site](/wiki/Krapina_Neanderthal_site). Remnants of [Neolithic](/wiki/Neolithic) and [Chalcolithic](/wiki/Chalcolithic) cultures were found in all regions. The largest proportion of sites is in the valleys of northern Croatia. The most significant are [Baden](/wiki/Baden_culture), [Starčevo](/wiki/Star%C4%8Devo_culture), and [Vučedol cultures](/wiki/Vu%C4%8Dedol_culture). [Iron Age](/wiki/Iron_Age) hosted the early Illyrian [Hallstatt culture](/wiki/Hallstatt_culture) and the Celtic [La Tène culture](/wiki/La_T%C3%A8ne_culture).

The region of modern-day Croatia was settled by [Illyrians](/wiki/Illyrians) and [Liburnians](/wiki/Liburnians), while the first Greek colonies were established on the islands of [Hvar](/wiki/Hvar), [Korčula](/wiki/Kor%C4%8Dula), and [Vis](/wiki/Vis_(island)). In 9 AD, the territory of today's Croatia became part of the [Roman Empire](/wiki/Roman_Empire). Emperor [Diocletian](/wiki/Diocletian) was native to the region. He [had a large palace built](/wiki/Diocletian%27s_Palace) in [Split](/wiki/Split,_Croatia), to which he retired after abdicating in AD 305. During the 5th century, the [last de jure Western Roman Emperor](/wiki/Last_de_jure_Western_Roman_Emperor) [Julius Nepos](/wiki/Julius_Nepos) ruled a small realm from the palace after fleeing Italy in 475.

### Middle Ages

Main articles: [Duchy of Croatia](/wiki/Duchy_of_Croatia), [Kingdom of Croatia (925–1102)](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia_(925%E2%80%931102)), [Kingdom of Croatia (1102–1526)](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia_(1102%E2%80%931526)), and [Republic of Ragusa](/wiki/Republic_of_Ragusa)

[Kingdom of Croatia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia_(925%E2%80%931102)) c. 925, during the reign of [King Tomislav](/wiki/King_Tomislav)

The Roman period ends with [Avar](/wiki/Avars_(Carpathians)) and [Croat](/wiki/Croat) invasions in the late 6th and first half of the 7th century and the destruction of almost all Roman towns. Roman survivors retreated to more favourable sites on the coast, islands, and mountains. The city of [Dubrovnik](/wiki/Dubrovnik) was founded by such survivors from [Epidaurum](/wiki/Epidaurum).

There's some uncertainty about the [ethnogenesis of Croats](/wiki/Origin_hypotheses_of_the_Croats). The most accepted theory, the Slavic theory, proposes migration of [White Croats](/wiki/White_Croats) from [White Croatia](/wiki/White_Croatia) during the [Migration Period](/wiki/Migration_Period). Conversely, the Iranian theory proposes [Sarmatian](/wiki/Sarmatian)-[Alanic](/wiki/Alans) origin of Proto-Croats, based on [Tanais Tablets](/wiki/Tanais_Tablets) containing [Ancient Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greek) inscriptions of given names *Χορούαθος*, *Χοροάθος*, and *Χορόαθος* (Khoroúathos, Khoroáthos, and Khoróathos) and their interpretation as [anthroponyms](/wiki/Anthroponym) related to the [Croatian ethnonym](/wiki/Names_of_the_Croats_and_Croatia).

According to the work *[De Administrando Imperio](/wiki/De_Administrando_Imperio)* written by 10th-century Byzantine Emperor [Constantine VII](/wiki/Constantine_VII), [Croats](/wiki/Croats) settled in the [Roman province of Dalmatia](/wiki/Roman_province_of_Dalmatia) in the first half of the 7th century after they defeated the [Avars](/wiki/Pannonian_Avars). Although there exist some scholarly disputes about the account's reliability and interpretation, recent archaeological data has established that the migration and settlement of the Slavs/Croats was in the late 6th and early 7th century. Eventually, a [dukedom](/wiki/Dukedom_(administrative_division)) was formed, [Duchy of Croatia](/wiki/Duchy_of_Croatia), ruled by [Borna](/wiki/Borna_(duke)), as attested by chronicles of [Einhard](/wiki/Einhard) starting in 818. The record represents the first document of Croatian realms, [vassal states](/wiki/Vassal_states) of [Francia](/wiki/Francia) at the time. Its neighbor to the North was [Principality of Lower Pannonia](/wiki/Principality_of_Lower_Pannonia), at the time ruled by duke [Ljudevit](/wiki/Ljudevit_(Lower_Pannonia)) who ruled the territories between the [Drava](/wiki/Drava) and [Sava](/wiki/Sava) rivers, centred from his fort at [Sisak](/wiki/Sisak). This population and territory throughout history was tightly related and connected to Croats and Croatia.

[Christianisation](/wiki/Christianisation) of Croats began in the 7th century at the time of archon [Porga of Croatia](/wiki/Porga_of_Croatia), initially probably encompassed only the elite and related people, but mostly finished by the 9th century. The Frankish overlordship ended during the reign of [Mislav](/wiki/Mislav_of_Croatia), or his successor [Trpimir I](/wiki/Trpimir_I_of_Croatia). The native Croatian royal dynasty was founded by duke Trpimir I in the mid 9th century, who defeated the Byzantine and Bulgarian forces. The first native Croatian ruler recognised by the Pope was duke [Branimir](/wiki/Branimir_of_Croatia), who received papal recognition from [Pope John VIII](/wiki/Pope_John_VIII) on 7 June 879. [Tomislav](/wiki/Tomislav_of_Croatia) was the first [king of Croatia](/wiki/King_of_Croatia), noted as such in a letter of [Pope John X](/wiki/Pope_John_X) in 925. Tomislav defeated Hungarian and Bulgarian invasions. The medieval Croatian kingdom reached its peak in the 11th century during the reigns of [Petar Krešimir IV](/wiki/Petar_Kre%C5%A1imir_IV) (1058–1074) and [Dmitar Zvonimir](/wiki/Dmitar_Zvonimir) (1075–1089). When [Stjepan II](/wiki/Stephen_II_of_Croatia) died in 1091, ending the Trpimirović dynasty, Dmitar Zvonimir's brother-in-law [Ladislaus I of Hungary](/wiki/Ladislaus_I_of_Hungary) claimed the Croatian crown. This led to a [war](/wiki/Battle_of_Gvozd_Mountain) and [personal union with Hungary](/wiki/Personal_union_of_Croatia_and_Hungary) in 1102 under [Coloman](/wiki/Coloman,_King_of_Hungary).

### Union with Hungary and Austria

Main articles: [Kingdom of Croatia (Habsburg)](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia_(Habsburg)) and [Austria-Hungary](/wiki/Austria-Hungary)

Further information: [Croatian–Ottoman wars](/wiki/Croatian%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars)

Ban [Josip Jelačić](/wiki/Josip_Jela%C4%8Di%C4%87) at the opening of the first modern [Croatian Parliament](/wiki/Croatian_Parliament) (*Sabor*), 5 June 1848. The Croatian tricolour flag can be seen in the background.

For the next four centuries, the Kingdom of Croatia was ruled by the [Sabor](/wiki/Sabor) (parliament) and a [Ban](/wiki/Ban_of_Croatia) (viceroy) appointed by the king. This period saw the rise of influential nobility such as the [Frankopan](/wiki/Frankopan) and [Šubić](/wiki/%C5%A0ubi%C4%87) families to prominence, and ultimately numerous Bans from the two families. An increasing threat of [Ottoman](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) conquest and a struggle against the [Republic of Venice](/wiki/Republic_of_Venice) for control of coastal areas ensued. The Venetians controlled most of Dalmatia by 1428, except the [city-state of Dubrovnik](/wiki/City-state_of_Dubrovnik), which became independent. [Ottoman conquests](/wiki/Hundred_Years%27_Croatian%E2%80%93Ottoman_War) led to the 1493 [Battle of Krbava field](/wiki/Battle_of_Krbava_field) and the 1526 [Battle of Mohács](/wiki/Battle_of_Moh%C3%A1cs), both ending in decisive Ottoman victories. King [Louis II](/wiki/Louis_II_of_Hungary) died at Mohács, and in 1527, the [Croatian Parliament met in Cetin](/wiki/Croatian_Parliament_met_in_Cetin) and chose Ferdinand I of the [House of Habsburg](/wiki/House_of_Habsburg) as the new ruler of Croatia, under the condition that he protects Croatia against the Ottoman Empire while respecting its political rights.

Following the decisive Ottoman victories, Croatia was split into civilian and military territories in 1538. The military territories became known as the [Croatian Military Frontier](/wiki/Croatian_Military_Frontier) and were under direct Habsburg control. Ottoman advances in Croatia continued until the 1593 [Battle of Sisak](/wiki/Battle_of_Sisak), the first decisive Ottoman defeat, when borders stabilised. During the [Great Turkish War](/wiki/Great_Turkish_War) (1683–1698), [Slavonia](/wiki/Slavonia) was regained, but western [Bosnia](/wiki/Bosnia_(region)), which had been part of Croatia before the Ottoman conquest, remained outside Croatian control. The present-day border between the two countries is a remnant of this outcome. [Dalmatia](/wiki/Dalmatia), the southern part of the border, was similarly defined by the [Fifth](/wiki/Cretan_War_(1645%E2%80%931669)) and the [Seventh Ottoman–Venetian Wars](/wiki/Seventh_Ottoman%E2%80%93Venetian_Wars).

The [Ottoman wars](/wiki/Croatian%E2%80%93Ottoman_wars) drove demographic changes. During the 16th century, Croats from western and northern [Bosnia](/wiki/Bosnia_(region)), [Lika](/wiki/Lika), [Krbava](/wiki/Krbava), the area between the rivers [Una](/wiki/Una_(Sava)) and [Kupa](/wiki/Kupa), and especially from western [Slavonia](/wiki/Slavonia), migrated towards [Austria](/wiki/Austria). Present-day [Burgenland Croats](/wiki/Burgenland_Croats) are direct descendants of these settlers. To replace the fleeing population, the Habsburgs encouraged Bosnians to provide military service in the [Military Frontier](/wiki/Military_Frontier). The [Croatian Parliament](/wiki/Croatian_Parliament) supported [King Charles III](/wiki/Emperor_Charles_VI)'s [Pragmatic Sanction](/wiki/Pragmatic_Sanction_of_1713) and signed their own [Pragmatic Sanction in 1712](/wiki/Pragmatic_Sanction_in_1712). Subsequently, the emperor pledged to respect all privileges and political rights of the [Kingdom of Croatia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia_(Habsburg)), and [Queen Maria Theresa](/wiki/Queen_Maria_Theresa) made significant contributions to Croatian affairs, such as introducing compulsory education.

The [Kingdom of Croatia-Slavonia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia-Slavonia) was an autonomous kingdom within [Austria-Hungary](/wiki/Austria-Hungary) created in 1868 following the [Croatian–Hungarian Settlement](/wiki/Croatian%E2%80%93Hungarian_Settlement).

Between 1797 and 1809, the [First French Empire](/wiki/First_French_Empire) increasingly occupied the eastern [Adriatic](/wiki/Adriatic) coastline and its hinterland, ending the Venetian and the [Ragusan republics](/wiki/Republic_of_Ragusa), establishing the [Illyrian Provinces](/wiki/Illyrian_Provinces). In response, the [Royal Navy](/wiki/Royal_Navy) [blockaded the Adriatic Sea](/wiki/Blockade_of_the_Adriatic_Sea), leading to the [Battle of Vis](/wiki/Battle_of_Lissa_(1811)) in 1811. The Illyrian provinces were captured by the Austrians in 1813 and absorbed by the [Austrian Empire](/wiki/Austrian_Empire) following the [Congress of Vienna](/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna) in 1815. This led to the formation of the [Kingdom of Dalmatia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Dalmatia) and the restoration of the [Croatian Littoral](/wiki/Croatian_Littoral) to the Kingdom of Croatia under one crown. The 1830s and 1840s featured [romantic nationalism](/wiki/Romantic_nationalism) that inspired the [Croatian National Revival](/wiki/Croatian_National_Revival), a political and cultural campaign advocating the unity of [South Slavs](/wiki/South_Slavs) within the empire. Its primary focus was establishing a standard language as a counterweight to [Hungarian](/wiki/Hungarian_language) while promoting [Croatian literature](/wiki/Croatian_literature) and culture. During the [Hungarian Revolution of 1848](/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1848), Croatia sided with Austria. Ban [Josip Jelačić](/wiki/Josip_Jela%C4%8Di%C4%87) helped defeat the Hungarians in 1849 and ushered in a [Germanisation](/wiki/Germanisation) policy. This is around the time that [Nikola Tesla](/wiki/Nikola_Tesla) was born in [Smiljan](/wiki/Smiljan,_Croatia).

By the 1860s, the failure of the policy became apparent, leading to the [Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867](/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Compromise_of_1867). The creation of a [personal union](/wiki/Personal_union) between the Austrian Empire and the [Kingdom of Hungary](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary) followed. The treaty left Croatia's status to Hungary, which was resolved by the [Croatian–Hungarian Settlement](/wiki/Croatian%E2%80%93Hungarian_Settlement) of 1868 when the kingdoms of Croatia and Slavonia were united. The Kingdom of Dalmatia remained under de facto Austrian control, while [Rijeka](/wiki/Rijeka) retained the status of [*corpus separatum*](/wiki/Corpus_separatum_(Fiume)) previously introduced in 1779. After [Austria-Hungary](/wiki/Austria-Hungary) [occupied Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_rule_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) following the [1878 Treaty of Berlin](/wiki/1878_Treaty_of_Berlin), the Military Frontier was abolished. The Croatian and [Slavonian](/wiki/Slavonian_Military_Frontier) sectors of the Frontier returned to Croatia in 1881, under provisions of the Croatian–Hungarian Settlement. Renewed efforts to [reform Austria-Hungary](/wiki/Reform_Austria-Hungary), entailing [federalisation](/wiki/Federalisation) with Croatia as a federal unit, were stopped by [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I).

### The World Wars and Yugoslavia

Main articles: [Creation of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Creation_of_Yugoslavia), [Kingdom of Yugoslavia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia), [Banovina of Croatia](/wiki/Banovina_of_Croatia), [World War II in Yugoslavia](/wiki/World_War_II_in_Yugoslavia), and [Socialist Republic of Croatia](/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Croatia)

Mass protests in Zagreb against the unification of the [State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs](/wiki/State_of_Slovenes,_Croats_and_Serbs) with the [Kingdom of Serbia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Serbia) in 1918

On 29 October 1918, the Croatian Parliament (*Sabor*) declared independence and decided to join the newly formed [State of Slovenes, Croats, and Serbs](/wiki/State_of_Slovenes,_Croats,_and_Serbs), which in turn entered into union with the [Kingdom of Serbia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Serbia) on 4 December 1918 to form the [Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Serbs,_Croats,_and_Slovenes). The Croatian Parliament never ratified the union with Serbia and Montenegro. The [1921 constitution](/wiki/1921_constitution) defining the country as a [unitary state](/wiki/Unitary_state) and abolition of Croatian Parliament and historical administrative divisions effectively ended Croatian autonomy.

The new constitution was opposed by the most widely supported national political party—the [Croatian Peasant Party](/wiki/Croatian_Peasant_Party) (HSS) led by [Stjepan Radić](/wiki/Stjepan_Radi%C4%87). The political situation deteriorated further as Radić was assassinated in the [National Assembly](/wiki/Yugoslav_Parliament) by [NRS](/wiki/People%27s_Radical_Party) member, [Serbian](/wiki/Serbs) nationalist politician [Puniša Račić](/wiki/Puni%C5%A1a_Ra%C4%8Di%C4%87) in 1928, culminating in King [Alexander I](/wiki/Alexander_I_of_Yugoslavia)'s establishment of the [6 January Dictatorship](/wiki/6_January_Dictatorship) in 1929. The dictatorship formally ended in 1931 when the king imposed a more unitary constitution. The HSS, now led by [Vladko Maček](/wiki/Vladko_Ma%C4%8Dek), continued to advocate federalisation, resulting in the [Cvetković–Maček Agreement](/wiki/Cvetkovi%C4%87%E2%80%93Ma%C4%8Dek_Agreement) of August 1939 and the autonomous [Banovina of Croatia](/wiki/Banovina_of_Croatia). The Yugoslav government retained control of defence, internal security, foreign affairs, trade, and transport while other matters were left to the Croatian Sabor and a crown-appointed Ban.

People of Zagreb celebrating [liberation](/wiki/Zagreb_in_World_War_II) on 12 May 1945 by [Croatian Partisans](/wiki/Croatian_Partisans)

In April 1941, [Yugoslavia was invaded](/wiki/Invasion_of_Yugoslavia) by [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany) and [Fascist Italy](/wiki/Fascist_Italy_(1922%E2%80%931943)). Following the invasion, a German-Italian installed puppet state named the [Independent State of Croatia](/wiki/Independent_State_of_Croatia) (NDH) was established. Most of Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and the region of [Syrmia](/wiki/Syrmia) were incorporated into this state. Parts of Dalmatia were [annexed by Italy](/wiki/Treaties_of_Rome_(1941)), Hungary annexed the northern Croatian regions of [Baranja](/wiki/Baranya_(region)) and [Međimurje](/wiki/Me%C4%91imurje_(region)). The NDH regime was led by [Ante Pavelić](/wiki/Ante_Paveli%C4%87) and the ultranationalist [Ustaše](/wiki/Usta%C5%A1e), a fringe movement in pre-war Croatia. With German and Italian military and political support, the regime introduced [racial laws](/wiki/Nazi_racial_theories) and launched a [genocide campaign against Serbs](/wiki/Genocide_of_Serbs_in_the_Independent_State_of_Croatia), [Jews](/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Croatia), and [Roma](/wiki/Romani_people_in_Croatia). Many were imprisoned in [concentration camps](/wiki/Concentration_camps_in_the_Independent_State_of_Croatia); the largest was the [Jasenovac complex](/wiki/Jasenovac_concentration_camp). [Anti-fascist Croats](/wiki/Croatian_Partisans) were targeted by the regime as well. Several [concentration camps](/wiki/Italian_concentration_camps) (most notably the [Rab](/wiki/Rab_concentration_camp), [Gonars](/wiki/Gonars_concentration_camp) and [Molat](/wiki/Molat_concentration_camp) camps) were established in Italian-occupied territories, mostly for [Slovenes](/wiki/Slovenes) and Croats. At the same time, the Yugoslav Royalist and [Serbian nationalist](/wiki/Serbian_nationalism) [Chetniks](/wiki/Chetniks) pursued a [genocidal campaign](/wiki/Chetnik_war_crimes_in_World_War_II) against Croats and [Muslims](/wiki/Bosniaks), aided by Italy. Nazi German forces committed crimes and reprisals against civilians in retaliation for Partisan actions, such as in the villages of [Kamešnica](/wiki/Massacre_of_villages_under_Kame%C5%A1nica) and [Lipa](/wiki/Memorial_Centre_Lipa_Remembers) in 1944.

A [resistance movement](/wiki/National_Liberation_Movement_in_Croatia) emerged. On 22 June 1941, the [1st Sisak Partisan Detachment](/wiki/1st_Sisak_Partisan_Detachment) was formed near [Sisak](/wiki/Sisak), the first military unit formed by a resistance movement in [occupied Europe](/wiki/German-occupied_Europe). That sparked the beginning of the [Yugoslav Partisan](/wiki/Yugoslav_Partisan) movement, a communist, multi-ethnic anti-fascist resistance group led by [Josip Broz Tito](/wiki/Josip_Broz_Tito). In ethnic terms, Croats were the second-largest contributors to the Partisan movement after Serbs. In per capita terms, Croats contributed proportionately to their population within Yugoslavia. By May 1944 (according to Tito), Croats made up 30% of the Partisan's ethnic composition, despite making up 22% of the population. The movement grew fast, and at the [Tehran Conference](/wiki/Tehran_Conference) in December 1943, the Partisans gained recognition from the [Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II).

Cardinal [Aloysius Stepinac](/wiki/Aloysius_Stepinac) with the Croatian communist leader [Vladimir Bakarić](/wiki/Vladimir_Bakari%C4%87) at the celebration of [May Day](/wiki/International_Workers%27_Day) in 1945.

With Allied support in logistics, equipment, training and airpower, and with the assistance of [Soviet troops](/wiki/Red_Army) taking part in the 1944 [Belgrade Offensive](/wiki/Belgrade_Offensive), the Partisans gained control of Yugoslavia and the border regions of [Italy](/wiki/Trieste#World_War_II_and_aftermath) and [Austria](/wiki/Carinthia_(state)#From_1920_to_the_Present) by May 1945. Members of the [NDH armed forces](/wiki/Croatian_Armed_Forces_(Independent_State_of_Croatia)) and other Axis troops, as well as civilians, were in retreat towards Austria. Following their surrender, many were killed in the [Yugoslav death march of Nazi collaborators](/wiki/Yugoslav_death_march_of_Nazi_collaborators). In the following years, [ethnic Germans](/wiki/Germans_of_Croatia) faced [persecution in Yugoslavia](/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_(1944%E2%80%9350)#Yugoslavia), and many were interned. The political aspirations of the Partisan movement were reflected in the [State Anti-fascist Council for the National Liberation of Croatia](/wiki/State_Anti-fascist_Council_for_the_National_Liberation_of_Croatia), which developed in 1943 as the bearer of Croatian statehood and later transformed into the Parliament in 1945, and [AVNOJ](/wiki/AVNOJ)—its counterpart at the Yugoslav level.

Based on the studies on [wartime and post-war casualties](/wiki/World_War_II_in_Yugoslavia_casualties) by demographer [Vladimir Žerjavić](/wiki/Vladimir_%C5%BDerjavi%C4%87) and statistician [Bogoljub Kočović](/wiki/Bogoljub_Ko%C4%8Dovi%C4%87), a total of 295,000 people from the territory (not including territories [ceded from Italy](/wiki/Treaty_of_Peace_with_Italy,_1947) after the war) died, which amounted to 7.3% of the population, among whom were 125–137,000 Serbs, 118–124,000 Croats, 16–17,000 Jews, and 15,000 Roma. In addition, from areas joined to Croatia after the war, a total of 32,000 people died, among whom 16,000 were Italians and 15,000 were Croats. Approximately 200,000 Croats from the entirety of Yugoslavia (including Croatia) and abroad were killed in total throughout the war and its immediate aftermath, approximately 5.4% of the population.

[Josip Broz Tito](/wiki/Josip_Broz_Tito) led [Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia) from 1944 to 1980; Pictured: Tito with the US president [Richard Nixon](/wiki/Richard_Nixon) in the [White House](/wiki/White_House), 1971

After [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), Croatia became a [single-party](/wiki/Single-party) socialist [federal unit](/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Croatia) of the [SFR Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia), ruled by the [Communists](/wiki/League_of_Communists_of_Croatia), but having a degree of autonomy within the federation. In 1967, Croatian authors and linguists published a [Declaration on the Status and Name of the Croatian Standard Language](/wiki/Declaration_on_the_Status_and_Name_of_the_Croatian_Standard_Language) demanding equal treatment for their language. The declaration contributed to a national movement seeking greater civil rights and redistribution of the Yugoslav economy, culminating in the [Croatian Spring](/wiki/Croatian_Spring) of 1971, which was suppressed by Yugoslav leadership. Still, the [1974 Yugoslav Constitution](/wiki/1974_Yugoslav_Constitution) gave increased autonomy to federal units, basically fulfilling a goal of the Croatian Spring and providing a legal basis for independence of the federative constituents. At the same time, there was a substantial Croatian diaspora during the Cold War, which included efforts aimed at forming a government in exile.

Following Tito's death in 1980, the political situation in Yugoslavia deteriorated. National tension was fanned by the 1986 [SANU Memorandum](/wiki/SANU_Memorandum) and the [1989 coups in Vojvodina, Kosovo, and Montenegro](/wiki/1989_coups_in_Vojvodina,_Kosovo_and_Montenegro). In January 1990, the Communist Party fragmented along national lines, with the Croatian [faction](/wiki/Political_faction) demanding a looser federation. In the same year, the [first multi-party elections](/wiki/1990_Croatian_parliamentary_election) were held in Croatia, while [Franjo Tuđman](/wiki/Franjo_Tu%C4%91man)'s win exacerbated nationalist tensions. Some of the [Serbs in Croatia](/wiki/Serbs_in_Croatia) left Sabor and declared autonomy of the unrecognised [Republic of Serbian Krajina](/wiki/Republic_of_Serbian_Krajina), intent on achieving independence from Croatia.

### Independence

Main articles: [Independence of Croatia](/wiki/Independence_of_Croatia), [Croatian War of Independence](/wiki/Croatian_War_of_Independence), and [History of Croatia since 1995](/wiki/History_of_Croatia_since_1995)

The Memorial Cemetery — the central site of the [National Remembrance Day](/wiki/Remembrance_Day_(Croatia)), commemorating all war victims and the [Vukovar massacre](/wiki/Vukovar_massacre) in 1991

As tensions rose, Croatia [declared independence](/wiki/Independence_of_Croatia) on 25 June 1991. The full implementation of the declaration only came into effect after a [three-month moratorium on the decision](/wiki/Brioni_Agreement) on 8 October 1991. In the meantime, tensions escalated into [overt war](/wiki/Croatian_War_of_Independence) when the Serbian-controlled [Yugoslav People's Army](/wiki/Yugoslav_People%27s_Army) (JNA) and various Serb paramilitary groups attacked Croatia. By the end of 1991, a high-intensity conflict fought along a wide front reduced Croatia's control to about two-thirds of its territory. Serb paramilitary groups then began a campaign of killing, terror, and expulsion of the Croats in the occupied territories, killing thousands of Croat civilians and expelling or displacing as many as 400,000–500,000 Croats and other non-Serbs from their homes. Serbs living in Croatian towns, especially those near the front lines, were subjected to various forms of discrimination. Croatian Serbs in Eastern and Western Slavonia and parts of the Krajina were forced to flee or were expelled by Croatian forces, though on a restricted scale and in lesser numbers. The [Croatian Government](/wiki/Government_of_Croatia) publicly deplored these practices and sought to stop them, indicating that they were not a part of the Government's policy.

On 15 January 1992, Croatia gained [diplomatic recognition](/wiki/Diplomatic_recognition) by the [European Economic Community](/wiki/European_Economic_Community), followed by the United Nations. The war effectively ended in August 1995 with a [decisive victory](/wiki/Operation_Storm) by Croatia; the event is commemorated each year on 5 August as [Victory and Homeland Thanksgiving Day and the Day of Croatian Defenders](/wiki/Victory_Day_(Croatia)). Following the Croatian victory, about 200,000 Serbs from the self-proclaimed [Republic of Serbian Krajina](/wiki/Republic_of_Serbian_Krajina) fled the region and hundreds of mainly elderly Serb civilians were killed in the aftermath of the military operation, often in revenge attacks, accompanied by looting, seizing or burning down of their property. Approximately half have returned since then. Some 19,000 Serb homes were subsequently settled by Croat refugees from Bosnia and Herzegovina. The [remaining occupied areas](/wiki/Eastern_Slavonia,_Baranja_and_Western_Syrmia_(1995%E2%80%931998)) were restored to Croatia following the [Erdut Agreement](/wiki/Erdut_Agreement) of November 1995, concluding with the [UNTAES](/wiki/United_Nations_Transitional_Administration_for_Eastern_Slavonia,_Baranja_and_Western_Sirmium) mission in January 1998. Most sources number the war deaths at around 20,000.

Croatia joined the [EU](/wiki/European_Union) in 2013 and the [Eurozone](/wiki/Eurozone) a decade later in 2023.

At the turn of the 21st century, Croatia experienced sustained economic growth as the nation democratized but struggled with public corruption and bouts of unemployment. The Croatian Parliament passed a set of constitutional amendments in November 2000 and March 2001 to convert from a bicameral to a unicameral body, later limiting presidential powers. Croatia joined the [Partnership for Peace](/wiki/Partnership_for_Peace) in May 2000 and the [World Trade Organization](/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) in November 2000. It joined the military alliance [NATO](/wiki/NATO) in April 2009. Croatia [was elected](/wiki/2007_United_Nations_Security_Council_election) to serve on the non-permanent seat of the [UN Security Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council) from 2008 to 2009. Adversely impacted by the [2008 financial crisis](/wiki/2008_financial_crisis), austerity measures led to wide-spread anti-government protests in 2009 and 2011. In the latter year, parliament was dissolved in May, with Croatian president [Ivo Josipović](/wiki/Ivo_Josipovi%C4%87) calling for new elections to quell the protests by December. After successfully completing [negotiations from 2004 to 2013](/wiki/2013_enlargement_of_the_European_Union), Croatia joined the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union) (EU) as its 28th member in July 2013.

Croatia was affected by the [2015 European migrant crisis](/wiki/2015_European_migrant_crisis) when Hungary's closure of borders with Serbia pushed over 700,000 refugees and migrants through Croatia. On 19 October 2016, [Andrej Plenković](/wiki/Andrej_Plenkovi%C4%87) began serving as the current Croatian Prime Minister. The most recent presidential elections, held on 12 January 2025, elected [Zoran Milanović](/wiki/Zoran_Milanovi%C4%87) as president. In 2022, Croatia began the process to join the [OECD](/wiki/OECD), due to complete membership proceedings by 2026. The nation joined the [Eurozone](/wiki/Eurozone) and [Schengen Area](/wiki/Schengen_Area) in 2023, adopting the [euro](/wiki/Euro) as its official currency.

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Croatia](/wiki/Geography_of_Croatia)

[Satellite image of Croatia](/wiki/Geography_of_Croatia)

Croatia is situated in [Central](/wiki/Central_Europe) and Southeast Europe, on the coast of the [Adriatic Sea](/wiki/Adriatic_Sea). Hungary is to the northeast, [Serbia](/wiki/Serbia) to the east, [Bosnia and Herzegovina](/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and [Montenegro](/wiki/Montenegro) to the southeast and [Slovenia](/wiki/Slovenia) to the northwest. It lies mostly between latitudes [42°](/wiki/42nd_parallel_north) and [47° N](/wiki/47%C2%B0_N) and longitudes [13°](/wiki/13th_meridian_east) and [20° E](/wiki/20%C2%B0_E). Part of the territory in the extreme south surrounding [Dubrovnik](/wiki/Dubrovnik) is a [practical exclave](/wiki/Practical_exclave) connected to the rest of the mainland by [territorial waters](/wiki/Territorial_waters), but separated on land by a short coastline strip belonging to Bosnia and Herzegovina around [Neum](/wiki/Neum). The [Pelješac Bridge](/wiki/Pelje%C5%A1ac_Bridge) connects the exclave with mainland Croatia.

The territory covers 56,594 square kilometres (21,851 square miles), consisting of 56,414 square kilometres (21,782 square miles) of land and 128 square kilometres (49 square miles) of water. It is the world's 127th largest country. Elevation ranges from the mountains of the [Dinaric Alps](/wiki/Dinaric_Alps) with the highest point of the [Dinara](/wiki/Dinara) peak at 1,831 metres (6,007 feet) near the border with Bosnia and Herzegovina in the south to the shore of the Adriatic Sea which makes up its entire southwest border. Insular Croatia consists of over [a thousand islands and islets](/wiki/A_thousand_islands_and_islets) varying in size, 48 of which are [permanently inhabited](/wiki/List_of_inhabited_islands_of_Croatia). The largest islands are [Cres](/wiki/Cres) and [Krk](/wiki/Krk), each of them having an area of around 405 square kilometres (156 square miles). It is the [largest overseas territory](/wiki/Adriatic_Sea#Geography) on the Adriatic Sea.

[Telašćica Nature Park](/wiki/Tela%C5%A1%C4%87ica_Nature_Park)

[Tourist cruise](/wiki/River_cruise) on the [Danube](/wiki/Danube) river, eastern [Slavonia](/wiki/Slavonia)

The hilly northern parts of [Hrvatsko Zagorje](/wiki/Hrvatsko_Zagorje) and the flat plains of Slavonia in the east which is part of the [Pannonian Basin](/wiki/Pannonian_Basin) are traversed by major rivers such as [Danube](/wiki/Danube), [Drava](/wiki/Drava), [Kupa](/wiki/Kupa), and the [Sava](/wiki/Sava). The Danube, Europe's second longest river, runs through the city of [Vukovar](/wiki/Vukovar) in the extreme east and forms part of the border with [Vojvodina](/wiki/Vojvodina). The central and southern regions near the Adriatic coastline and islands consist of low mountains and forested highlands. Natural resources found in quantities significant enough for production include oil, coal, bauxite, low-grade iron ore, calcium, gypsum, natural asphalt, silica, mica, clays, salt, and hydropower. [Karst topography](/wiki/Karst_topography) makes up about half of Croatia and is especially prominent in the Dinaric Alps. Croatia hosts [deep caves](/wiki/List_of_caves_in_Croatia), 49 of which are deeper than 250 m (820.21 ft), 14 deeper than 500 m (1,640.42 ft) and three deeper than 1,000 m (3,280.84 ft). The [Plitvice lakes](/wiki/Plitvice_Lakes_National_Park) are a system of 16 lakes with waterfalls connecting them over [dolomite](/wiki/Dolomite_(mineral)) and [limestone](/wiki/Limestone) cascades. The lakes are known for their distinctive colours, ranging from turquoise to mint green, grey or blue.

### Climate

[Köppen-Geiger climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) map of Croatia

Most of Croatia has a moderately warm and rainy [continental climate](/wiki/Continental_climate) as defined by the [Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification). Mean monthly temperature ranges between −3 [°C](/wiki/Celsius) (27 [°F](/wiki/Fahrenheit)) in January and 18 °C (64 °F) in July. The coldest parts of the country are [Lika](/wiki/Lika) and [Gorski Kotar](/wiki/Gorski_Kotar) featuring a snowy, forested climate at elevations above 1,200 metres (3,900 feet). The warmest areas are at the Adriatic coast and especially in its immediate hinterland characterised by [Mediterranean climate](/wiki/Mediterranean_climate), as the sea moderates temperature highs. Consequently, temperature peaks are more pronounced in continental areas.

The lowest temperature of −35.5 °C (−31.9 °F) was recorded on 3 February 1919 in [Čakovec](/wiki/%C4%8Cakovec), and the highest temperature of 42.8 °C (109.0 °F) was recorded on 4 August 1981 in [Ploče](/wiki/Plo%C4%8De). Mean annual precipitation ranges between 600 millimetres (24 inches) and 3,500 millimetres (140 inches) depending on geographic region and climate type. The least precipitation is recorded in the outer islands ([Biševo](/wiki/Bi%C5%A1evo), [Lastovo](/wiki/Lastovo), [Svetac](/wiki/Svetac), [Vis](/wiki/Vis_(island))) and the eastern parts of Slavonia. However, in the latter case, rain occurs mostly during the [growing season](/wiki/Growing_season). The maximum precipitation levels are observed in the Dinaric Alps, in the Gorski Kotar peaks of [Risnjak](/wiki/Risnjak) and [Snježnik](/wiki/Snje%C5%BEnik).

Prevailing winds in the interior are light to moderate northeast or southwest, and in the coastal area, prevailing winds are determined by local features. Higher wind velocities are more often recorded in cooler months along the coast, generally as the cool northeasterly [bura](/wiki/Bora_(wind)) or less frequently as the warm southerly [jugo](/wiki/Jugo). The sunniest parts are the outer islands, Hvar and Korčula, where more than 2700 hours of sunshine are recorded per year, followed by the middle and southern Adriatic Sea area in general, and northern Adriatic coast, all with more than 2,000 hours of sunshine per year.

### Biodiversity

Main article: [Protected areas of Croatia](/wiki/Protected_areas_of_Croatia)

Further information: [Register of Protected Natural Values of Croatia](/wiki/Register_of_Protected_Natural_Values_of_Croatia) and [Natural and Cultural Heritage of Croatia](/wiki/Natural_and_Cultural_Heritage_of_Croatia)

[Plitvice Lakes National Park](/wiki/Plitvice_Lakes_National_Park)

Landscapes of [Motovun](/wiki/Motovun) in [Istrian peninsula](/wiki/Istria_County)

[Galešnjak](/wiki/Gale%C5%A1njak) island in a shape of a heart

Croatia can be subdivided into [ecoregions](/wiki/Ecoregion) based on climate and geomorphology. The country is one of the richest in Europe in terms of biodiversity. Croatia has four types of biogeographical regions—the [Mediterranean](/wiki/Mediterranean_Biogeographic_Region) along the coast and in its immediate hinterland, [Alpine](/wiki/Alpine_Biogeographic_Region) in most of Lika and Gorski Kotar, [Pannonian](/wiki/Pannonian_Biogeographic_Region) along Drava and Danube, and [Continental](/wiki/Continental_Biogeographic_Region) in the remaining areas. The most significant are [karst](/wiki/Karst) habitats which include submerged karst, such as [Zrmanja](/wiki/Zrmanja) and [Krka](/wiki/Krka_(Croatia)) canyons and tufa barriers, as well as underground habitats. The country contains three ecoregions: [Dinaric Mountains mixed forests](/wiki/Dinaric_Mountains_mixed_forests), [Pannonian mixed forests](/wiki/Pannonian_mixed_forests), and [Illyrian deciduous forests](/wiki/Illyrian_deciduous_forests).

The karst geology harbours approximately [7,000 caves and pits](/wiki/List_of_caves_in_Croatia), some of which are the habitat of the only known aquatic cave [vertebrate](/wiki/Vertebrate)—the [olm](/wiki/Olm). Forests are abundant, covering 2,490,000 hectares (6,200,000 acres) or 44% of Croatian land area. Other habitat types include wetlands, grasslands, bogs, fens, scrub habitats, coastal and marine habitats.

In terms of [phytogeography](/wiki/Phytogeography), Croatia is a part of the [Boreal Kingdom](/wiki/Boreal_Kingdom) and is a part of Illyrian and Central European provinces of the [Circumboreal Region](/wiki/Circumboreal_Region) and the Adriatic province of the [Mediterranean Region](/wiki/Mediterranean_Region). The [World Wide Fund for Nature](/wiki/World_Wide_Fund_for_Nature) divides Croatia between three ecoregions—Pannonian mixed forests, [Dinaric Mountains mixed forests](/wiki/Dinaric_Mountains_mixed_forests) and [Illyrian deciduous forests](/wiki/Illyrian_deciduous_forests).

Croatia hosts 37,000 known plant and animal species, but their actual number is estimated to be between 50,000 and 100,000. More than a thousand species are endemic, especially in Velebit and Biokovo mountains, Adriatic islands and karst rivers. Legislation protects 1,131 species. The most serious threat is habitat loss and degradation. A further problem is presented by invasive alien species, especially *[Caulerpa taxifolia](/wiki/Caulerpa_taxifolia)* algae.

Invasive algae are regularly monitored and removed to protect [benthic habitat](/wiki/Benthic_habitat). Indigenous cultivated plant strains and domesticated animal breeds are numerous. They include five breeds of horses, five of cattle, eight of sheep, two of pigs, and one poultry. Indigenous breeds include nine that are endangered or critically endangered. Croatia has 444 [protected areas](/wiki/Protected_areas_of_Croatia), encompassing 9% of the country. Those include eight [national parks](/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_Croatia), two strict reserves, and ten [nature parks](/wiki/Nature_park). The oldest [national park](/wiki/National_park) in country is [Plitvice Lakes National Park](/wiki/Plitvice_Lakes_National_Park), a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](/wiki/World_Heritage_Sites_in_Croatia). Velebit Nature Park is a part of the UNESCO [Man and the Biosphere Programme](/wiki/Man_and_the_Biosphere_Programme). The strict and special reserves, as well as the national and nature parks, are managed and protected by the central government, while other protected areas are managed by counties. In 2005, the National Ecological Network was set up, as the first step in the preparation of the EU accession and joining of the [Natura 2000](/wiki/Natura_2000) network.

## Governance

Further information: [Politics of Croatia](/wiki/Politics_of_Croatia) and [Human rights in Croatia](/wiki/Human_rights_in_Croatia)

[Zoran Milanović](/wiki/Zoran_Milanovi%C4%87)
[President](/wiki/President_of_Croatia)

[Andrej Plenković](/wiki/Andrej_Plenkovi%C4%87)
[Prime Minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Croatia)

The Republic of Croatia is a unitary constitutional republic using a [parliamentary system](/wiki/Parliamentary_system). Government powers in Croatia are legislative, executive, and judiciary powers. The [president](/wiki/President_of_Croatia) is the [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state), directly elected to a five-year term and is limited by the constitution to two terms. In addition to serving as [commander-in-chief](/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the armed forces, the president has the procedural duty of appointing the prime minister with the parliament and has some influence on foreign policy.

The [government](/wiki/Croatian_Government) is headed by the [prime minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Croatia), who is supported by deputy prime ministers and various ministers in charge of particular sectors. As the [executive branch](/wiki/Executive_(government)), it is responsible for proposing legislation and a budget, enforcing the laws, and guiding foreign and internal policies. The government is seated at [Banski dvori](/wiki/Banski_dvori) in Zagreb.

### Law and judicial system

Further information: [Law of Croatia](/wiki/Law_of_Croatia)

[Croatian Parliament](/wiki/Croatian_Parliament) (Sabor) in Zagreb

[Banski dvori](/wiki/Banski_dvori) in Zagreb, seat of the [Government of Croatia](/wiki/Government_of_Croatia)

A [unicameral parliament](/wiki/Unicameralism) – known as the **Sabor** – holds [legislative power](/wiki/Legislative_power). The number of parliament members can vary from 100 to 160. They are elected by popular vote to serve four-year terms. Legislative sessions take place from 15 January to 15 July, and from 15 September to 15 December annually. The two largest [political parties in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Croatia) are the [Croatian Democratic Union](/wiki/Croatian_Democratic_Union) (HDZ) and the [Social Democratic Party of Croatia](/wiki/Social_Democratic_Party_of_Croatia) (SDP).

Croatia has a [civil law](/wiki/Civil_law_(legal_system)) legal system in which law arises primarily from written statutes, with judges serving as implementers and not creators of law. Its development was largely influenced by German and Austrian legal systems. Croatian law is divided into two principal areas—[private](/wiki/Private_law) and [public law](/wiki/Public_law). Before [EU accession negotiations](/wiki/2013_enlargement_of_the_European_Union) were completed, Croatian legislation had been fully harmonised with the [community acquis](/wiki/Community_acquis).

The main national courts are the [Constitutional Court](/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Croatia), which oversees violations of the Constitution, and the [Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Croatia), which is the highest court of appeal. A variety of administrative, commercial, [county](/wiki/County_Court), misdemeanor, and municipal courts handle cases in their respective domains. Cases falling within judicial jurisdiction are in the first instance decided by a single professional judge, while appeals are deliberated in mixed tribunals of professional judges. Lay magistrates also participate in trials. The [State's Attorney Office](/wiki/State%27s_Attorney_Office_of_the_Republic_of_Croatia) is the judicial body constituted of public prosecutors empowered to instigate prosecution of perpetrators of offences. Law enforcement agencies are organised under the authority of the [Ministry of the Interior](/wiki/Ministry_of_the_Interior_(Croatia)) which consist primarily of the national police force. Croatia's security service is the [Security and Intelligence Agency](/wiki/Security_and_Intelligence_Agency_(Croatia)) (SOA).

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of Croatia](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Croatia)

President [Zoran Milanović](/wiki/Zoran_Milanovi%C4%87) with other Western leaders at the [2025 NATO Summit](/wiki/2025_The_Hague_NATO_summit)

Croatia has established diplomatic relations with most world nations – [189 states in total](/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_relations_of_Croatia) – starting [with Germany](/wiki/Croatia%E2%80%93Germany_relations) (1991) and most recently [with Liberia](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Liberia) (2024). It exerts influence as a [small power](/wiki/Small_power) in this region and within European international institutions. Since the 1990s, Croatia has had unresolved border disputes throughout Southeast Europe, namely [with Serbia](/wiki/Croatia%E2%80%93Serbia_border_dispute), Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, and Slovenia. It is a member of the [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) (UN), the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union), [NATO](/wiki/NATO), the [Council of Europe](/wiki/Council_of_Europe), the [Union for the Mediterranean](/wiki/Union_for_the_Mediterranean), and the [Schengen Area](/wiki/Schengen_Area) as well as the [Eurozone](/wiki/Eurozone). Modern Croatian foreign policy objectives include enhancing relations with neighbouring countries, developing international cooperation and promotion of the Croatian economy.

It supports 57 embassies, 30 consulates and eight permanent diplomatic missions. Many international organisations have regional offices in Croatia, such as the [European Bank for Reconstruction and Development](/wiki/European_Bank_for_Reconstruction_and_Development) (EBRD), [International Organization for Migration](/wiki/International_Organization_for_Migration) (IOM), [Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe](/wiki/Organization_for_Security_and_Co-operation_in_Europe) (OSCE), [World Bank](/wiki/World_Bank), [World Health Organization](/wiki/World_Health_Organization) (WHO), [International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia](/wiki/International_Criminal_Tribunal_for_the_former_Yugoslavia) (ICTY), [United Nations Development Programme](/wiki/United_Nations_Development_Programme) (UNDP), [United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees](/wiki/United_Nations_High_Commissioner_for_Refugees) (UNHCR), and [UNICEF](/wiki/UNICEF).

### Croatian diaspora

Main article: [Croatian diaspora](/wiki/Croatian_diaspora)

The diaspora consists of communities of ethnic [Croats](/wiki/Croats) and [Croatian citizens](/wiki/Croatian_nationality_law) living outside Croatia. The [largest diaspora is in the United States](/wiki/Croatian_Americans), with a large population across neighboring Southeast Europe. The country maintains extensive contact with Croatian communities abroad through administrative and financial support of cultural, sports activities, and economic initiatives. Croatia actively maintains foreign relations to strengthen and guarantee the rights of the Croatian minority abroad.

### Military

Main article: [Armed Forces of Croatia](/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_Croatia)

Further information: [Defence industry of Croatia](/wiki/Defence_industry_of_Croatia)

[Croatian Army](/wiki/Croatian_Army)

[Croatian Navy](/wiki/Croatian_Navy)

[Croatian Air Force](/wiki/Croatian_Air_Force)

The Armed Forces of Croatia (OSRH) consists of three service branches: the [Croatian Army](/wiki/Croatian_Army), the [Croatian Navy](/wiki/Croatian_Navy) and the [Croatian Air Force](/wiki/Croatian_Air_Force). They are headed by the President of Croatia as commander-in-chief while defence policy is overseen by the Government of Croatia. The military supports the foreign policy of Croatia and international security initiatives. It is the sole military wing of the [Croatian security and intelligence system](/wiki/Croatian_security_and_intelligence_system). The Armed Forces are spread across [continental Croatia](/wiki/Continental_Croatia) and capital city Zagreb, with the Navy largely based in [Split](/wiki/Split,_Croatia). According to the 2025 [Global Peace Index](/wiki/Global_Peace_Index), Croatia is the 18th most peaceful country in the world.

Since the [Croatian War of Independence](/wiki/Croatian_War_of_Independence) (1991–1995), the Armed Forces have played a key role in protecting territorial integrity. The military's first foreign deployment was to the [War in Afghanistan](/wiki/War_in_Afghanistan_(2001%E2%80%932021)), in 2003, later joining the military alliance [NATO](/wiki/NATO) in 2009. Croatia maintains a close security and foreign intelligence relationship [with NATO](/wiki/Croatia%E2%80%93NATO_relations) and [the United States](/wiki/Croatia%E2%80%93United_States_relations#Military_cooperation). The military has been deployed to various [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) (UN)-led and NATO-led missions around the world. Since joining the European Union (EU) in 2013, it became a part of the [EU defence forces](/wiki/Defence_forces_of_the_European_Union).

The Armed Forces have seen significant militarization and modernization since the early 2020s due to regional geopolitical risk. Its deep integration within Europe and the U.S. has led to modern defence pacts and a robust [defence industrial base](/wiki/Defense_industrial_base). The Croatian military is among the most [technologically-advanced in Southeast Europe](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_level_of_military_equipment). Croatia has had [military conscription](/wiki/Conscription) since 2025, previously enacted from 1991 to 2008.

Counties of Croatia

Croatia was first divided into counties in the [Middle Ages](/wiki/Middle_Ages). The divisions changed over time to reflect losses of territory to Ottoman conquest and subsequent liberation of the same territory, changes of the political status of Dalmatia, Dubrovnik, and [Istria](/wiki/Istria). The traditional division of the country into counties was abolished in the 1920s when the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes and the subsequent Kingdom of Yugoslavia introduced [oblasts](/wiki/Oblast) and [banovinas](/wiki/Subdivisions_of_the_Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia) respectively.

Communist-ruled Croatia, as a constituent part of post-World War II Yugoslavia, abolished earlier divisions and introduced municipalities, subdividing Croatia into approximately one hundred municipalities. Counties were reintroduced in 1992 legislation, significantly altered in terms of territory relative to the pre-1920s subdivisions. In 1918, the [Transleithanian](/wiki/Transleithania) part was divided into eight counties with their seats in [Bjelovar](/wiki/Bjelovar), [Gospić](/wiki/Gospi%C4%87), [Ogulin](/wiki/Ogulin), [Osijek](/wiki/Osijek), [Požega](/wiki/Po%C5%BEega,_Croatia), [Varaždin](/wiki/Vara%C5%BEdin), Vukovar, and Zagreb.

Since 1992, Croatia has been divided into [20 counties](/wiki/Counties_of_Croatia) and the [capital city of Zagreb](/wiki/Capital_city_of_Zagreb), the latter having the dual authority and legal status of a county and a city. County borders changed in some instances, last revised in 2006. The counties subdivide into [127 cities](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Croatia) and [429 municipalities](/wiki/Municipalities_of_Croatia). [Nomenclature of Territorial Units for Statistics](/wiki/Nomenclature_of_Territorial_Units_for_Statistics) (NUTS) [division](/wiki/Division_of_Croatia) is performed in several tiers. NUTS 1 level considers the entire country in a single unit; three NUTS 2 regions come below that. Those are Northwest Croatia, Central and Eastern (Pannonian) Croatia, and Adriatic Croatia. The latter encompasses the counties along the Adriatic coast. Northwest Croatia includes Koprivnica-Križevci, Krapina-Zagorje, Međimurje, Varaždin, the city of Zagreb, and Zagreb counties and the Central and Eastern (Pannonian) Croatia includes the remaining areas—Bjelovar-Bilogora, Brod-Posavina, Karlovac, Osijek-Baranja, Požega-Slavonia, Sisak-Moslavina, Virovitica-Podravina, and Vukovar-Syrmia counties. Individual counties and the city of Zagreb also represent NUTS 3 level subdivision units in Croatia. The NUTS [local administrative unit](/wiki/Administrative_division) (LAU) divisions are two-tiered. LAU 1 divisions match the counties and the city of Zagreb in effect making those the same as NUTS 3 units, while LAU 2 subdivisions correspond to cities and municipalities.

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Croatia](/wiki/Economy_of_Croatia)

The capital city of [Zagreb](/wiki/Zagreb) is the nation's primary economic and financial centre.

Croatia has a [highly developed](/wiki/Developed_country) [mixed economy](/wiki/Mixed_economy). It is one of the [largest economies in Southeast Europe](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)#Table) by nominal [gross domestic product](/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) (GDP). It maintains a similarly [high regional GDP-per-capita](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)_per_capita). It is an [open economy](/wiki/Open_economy) with accommodative foreign policy, highly dependent on international trade in Europe. Within Croatia, economic development [varies among its counties](/wiki/List_of_Croatian_counties_by_GDP), with strongest growth in [Central Croatia](/wiki/Central_Croatia) and its financial centre, Zagreb. It has a [very high level](/wiki/List_of_Croatian_counties_by_Human_Development_Index) of human development, low levels of [income inequality](/wiki/Gini_coefficient), and a high [quality of life](/wiki/Quality_of_life). Croatia's [labor market](/wiki/Labour_economics) has been perennially inefficient, with an underdeveloped investment climate and an ineffective [corporate and income tax system](/wiki/Taxation_in_Croatia).

[Rimac Automobili](/wiki/Rimac_Automobili), one of Croatia’s major [automotive manufacturers](/wiki/Automotive_industry_in_Croatia).

Croatia's economic history is closely linked to its historic [nation-building](/wiki/Nation-building) efforts. Its pre-industrial economy leveraged the [country's geography](/wiki/Geography_of_Croatia) and natural resources to guide agricultural growth. The 1800s saw a shipbuilding boom, railroading, and widespread industrialization. During the 1900s, Croatia entered into a [planned economy](/wiki/Planned_economy) (with [socialism](/wiki/Socialism)) in 1941 and a [command economy](/wiki/Planned_economy#Command_economy) (with [communism](/wiki/Communism)) during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II). It experienced rapid urbanization in the 1950s and [decentralized](/wiki/Decentralization) in 1965, diversifying its economy before the [independence of Croatia](/wiki/Independence_of_Croatia) in 1990. The [Croatian War of Independence](/wiki/Croatian_War_of_Independence) (1991–95) curbed [21–25% of wartime GDP](/wiki/Croatian_War_of_Independence#Impact_and_aftermath), leaving behind a developing [transition economy](/wiki/Transition_economy).

The modern Croatian economy is considered [high-income](/wiki/High-income), dominated by its [tertiary service](/wiki/Tertiary_sector_of_the_economy) and industry sectors which account for 70% of GDP. Croatia is an [emerging energy power](/wiki/Energy_in_Croatia) in Europe, with strategic investments in liquefied natural gas ([LNG](/wiki/Liquefied_natural_gas)), [geothermal](/wiki/Geothermal_energy) networks, and electric transport. It supports regional economic activity via transportation networks along the [Adriatic Sea](/wiki/Adriatic_Sea) and throughout [Pan-European corridors](/wiki/Pan-European_corridors). Croatia has free-trade agreements with many world nations and is a part of the [World Trade Organization](/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) (2000) and the [EEA](/wiki/European_Economic_Area) (2013). Croatia's largest [trading partner](/wiki/International_trade) was the rest of the European Union, led by Germany, Italy, and Slovenia. The nation has an [emerging defence sector](/wiki/Defence_industry_of_Croatia), with major manufacturers including [HS Produkt](/wiki/HS_Produkt) and [Đuro Đaković](/wiki/%C4%90uro_%C4%90akovi%C4%87_(company)), among others.

### Tourism

Main articles: [Tourism in Croatia](/wiki/Tourism_in_Croatia) and [Croatian National Tourist Board](/wiki/Croatian_National_Tourist_Board)

[Dubrovnik](/wiki/Dubrovnik) is Croatia's most visited and most popular destination.

Tourism is a major industry and economic sector. It has historically represented a large component the country's economic output (GDP), routinely reaching 10% to 15% of total GDP. Croatia is deeply integrated with the European Union (EU), contributing to overall international tourism in [Southeast Europe](/wiki/Southeast_Europe). Tourism is concentrated along the [Adriatic coast](/wiki/Adriatic_Croatia) and is strongly seasonal, peaking in July and August. The most frequented cities are [Dubrovnik](/wiki/Dubrovnik), [Rovinj](/wiki/Rovinj), [Zagreb](/wiki/Zagreb), [Split](/wiki/Split,_Croatia), [Poreč](/wiki/Pore%C4%8D), [Umag](/wiki/Umag), and [Zadar](/wiki/Zadar), respectively.

[Zlatni Rat](/wiki/Zlatni_Rat) beach on the Island of [Brač](/wiki/Bra%C4%8D)

The history of tourism in Croatia dates back to its time as part of [Austria-Hungary](/wiki/Austria-Hungary) when wealthy aristocrats would converge to the sea. Tourism expanded throughout the 1960s to the 1980s before the [independence of Croatia](/wiki/Independence_of_Croatia) in 1990 curbed tourism until the late-1990s. The 2000s saw a significant resurgence of Croatian tourism as it underwent nation-building with a particular emphasis on tourism revenue. By the late-2000s, Croatia became one of the most visited tourist destinations in the [Mediterranean](/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea). A total of 20.2 million tourists visited Croatia in 2024.

Eight areas in the country have been designated national parks and eleven as [nature parks](/wiki/List_of_protected_areas_of_Croatia#Nature_parks). There are [ten](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Croatia) [World Heritage Sites](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site) and 116 [Blue Flag beaches](/wiki/Blue_Flag_beach) across the country. Factors of tourist interest are mainly [culture](/wiki/Culture_of_Croatia), [cuisine](/wiki/Croatian_cuisine), [history](/wiki/History_of_Croatia), fashion, [architecture](/wiki/Architecture_of_Croatia), art, religious sites and routes, nature, maritime access, and nightlife. Tourism has been partially supported by the Croatian film and television industries due to on-location filming. Inflation and [overtourism](/wiki/Overtourism) has led to increased travel regulations and tourist costs since 2024. [Eurostat](/wiki/Eurostat) estimated that nearly 55% of EU tourist accommodation is between Croatia (117,000) and neighboring [Italy](/wiki/Tourism_in_Italy) (230,000). In 2025, Croatia was the ninth-most-visited state in the EU and third-most-visted in [Southern Europe](/wiki/Southern_Europe).

### Infrastructure

See also: [Transport in Croatia](/wiki/Transport_in_Croatia) and [List of tallest buildings in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Croatia)

#### Motorways

[Pelješac Bridge](/wiki/Pelje%C5%A1ac_Bridge) connects the peninsula of [Pelješac](/wiki/Pelje%C5%A1ac) and through it the southernmost part, including [Dubrovnik](/wiki/Dubrovnik), with the [Croatian mainland](/wiki/Croatia_proper).

The [motorway network](/wiki/Motorways_in_Croatia) was largely built in the late 1990s and the 2000s. As of December 2020, Croatia had completed 1,313.8 kilometres (816.4 miles) of motorways, connecting Zagreb to other regions and following various [European routes](/wiki/International_E-road_network) and four [Pan-European corridors](/wiki/Pan-European_corridors). The busiest motorways are the [A1](/wiki/A1_(Croatia)), connecting Zagreb to Split and the [A3](/wiki/A3_(Croatia)), passing east to west through northwest Croatia and Slavonia.

A widespread network of [state roads in Croatia](/wiki/State_roads_in_Croatia) acts as motorway [feeder roads](/wiki/Feeder_line_(network)) while connecting major settlements. The high quality and safety levels of the Croatian motorway network were tested and confirmed by EuroTAP and EuroTest programmes.

The construction of 2.4-kilometre-long [Pelješac Bridge](/wiki/Pelje%C5%A1ac_Bridge) connected the two-halves of Dubrovnik-Neretva County and shortened the route to the [Pelješac](/wiki/Pelje%C5%A1ac) peninsula and the islands of [Korčula](/wiki/Kor%C4%8Dula) and [Lastovo](/wiki/Lastovo) by more than 32 km. The construction of the bridge started in July 2018 after Croatian road operator [Hrvatske ceste](/wiki/Hrvatske_ceste) (HC) signed a 2.08 billion [kuna](/wiki/Croatian_kuna) deal for the works with a Chinese consortium led by [China Road and Bridge Corporation](/wiki/China_Road_and_Bridge_Corporation) (CRBC). The project was co-financed by the European Union with €357 million. The construction was completed in July 2022.

#### Railways

Main article: [Rail transport in Croatia](/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Croatia)

Further information: [List of railways in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_railways_in_Croatia)

[ÖBB](/wiki/%C3%96BB) Train, above [Kaštela](/wiki/Ka%C5%A1tela), departing from Split

[HŽ series 6112](/wiki/H%C5%BD_series_6112) manufactured by the Croatian company [Končar Group](/wiki/Kon%C4%8Dar_Group)

Croatia has an [extensive rail network](/wiki/List_of_railways_in_Croatia) spanning 2,604 kilometres (1,618 miles), including 984 kilometres (611 miles) of electrified railways and 254 kilometres (158 miles) of double track railways. The most significant railways in Croatia are within the Pan-European transport corridors Vb and X connecting [Rijeka](/wiki/Rijeka) to [Budapest](/wiki/Budapest) and Ljubljana to Belgrade, both via [Zagreb](/wiki/Zagreb). In 2024, the [European Investment Bank](/wiki/European_Investment_Bank) committed €400 million to support the revitalization of the railway system, focusing on green and digital transformations. In July 2024, a significant agreement was signed for the acquisition of six [electro-diesel multiple units](/wiki/Electro-diesel_multiple_unit) (EDMUs) to improve connectivity between [Split](/wiki/Split,_Croatia) and Zagreb. Valued at €57.3 million and financed through an EIB loan, this project is part of the broader initiative to modernize Croatia's railway infrastructure.

#### Aviation

Main article: [List of airports in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_Croatia)

Further information: [List of the busiest airports in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_Croatia)

[Airports in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_Croatia) are located and serves [Dubrovnik](/wiki/Dubrovnik_Airport), [Osijek](/wiki/Osijek_Airport), [Pula](/wiki/Pula_Airport), [Rijeka](/wiki/Rijeka_Airport), [Split](/wiki/Split_Airport), [Zadar](/wiki/Zadar_Airport), and [Zagreb](/wiki/Zagreb_International_Airport). The largest and busiest is [Franjo Tuđman Airport](/wiki/Zagreb_Airport) in Zagreb, followed by [Saint Jerome Airport](/wiki/Split_Airport) in Split, [Ruđer Bošković Airport](/wiki/Dubrovnik_Airport) in Dubrovnik and [Zadar Airport](/wiki/Zadar_Airport) in Zadar, with annual passenger numbers exceeding one million respectively. [Croatian airports in terms of passenger arrivals](/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_Croatia) recorded more than 10 million tourists in 2018, 11 million in 2019 and 2023, 13 million in 2024. Airports in Croatia welcomed more than 14 million passengers in 2025. In July 2024, the airline took delivery of its first [Airbus A220](/wiki/Airbus_A220)-300 aircraft, marking the beginning of a transition to a more modern and efficient fleet. This initiative includes the acquisition of 13 A220-300 and two [A220-100](/wiki/Airbus_A220) aircraft, aiming to enhance operational efficiency with 25% lower fuel consumption and reduced emissions. As of 2024, [Croatia Airlines](/wiki/Croatia_Airlines) expanded its international network by introducing direct flights from [Zagreb](/wiki/Zagreb) to [Tirana](/wiki/Tirana), Berlin, and [Stockholm](/wiki/Stockholm). Additionally, the airline launched services connecting [Hamburg](/wiki/Hamburg) to Zagreb starting 1 July 2024.

#### Ports

The busiest cargo seaport is the [Port of Rijeka](/wiki/Port_of_Rijeka). The busiest passenger ports are [Split](/wiki/Port_of_Split) and Zadar. Many minor ports serve ferries connecting numerous islands and coastal cities with ferry lines to several cities in Italy. The largest [river port](/wiki/Inland_port) is [port of Vukovar](/wiki/Port_of_Vukovar), located on the [Danube](/wiki/Danube) in [Vukovar](/wiki/Vukovar), representing the nation's outlet to the Pan-European transport corridor VII.

#### Energy

See also: [Energy in Croatia](/wiki/Energy_in_Croatia)

Poštak Wind Farm near [Gračac](/wiki/Gra%C4%8Dac), [Zadar County](/wiki/Zadar_County)

610 kilometres (380 miles) of crude oil pipelines serve Croatia, connecting the [Rijeka](/wiki/Rijeka) oil terminal with refineries in Rijeka and [Sisak](/wiki/Sisak), and several transhipment terminals. The system has a capacity of 20 million tonnes per year. The natural gas transportation system comprises 2,113 kilometres (1,313 miles) of trunk and regional pipelines, and more than 300 associated structures, connecting production rigs, the Okoli natural gas storage facility, 27 end-users and 37 distribution systems. Croatia also plays an important role in regional energy security. The floating [liquefied natural gas](/wiki/Liquefied_natural_gas) import terminal off [Krk](/wiki/Krk) island [LNG Hrvatska](/wiki/LNG_Hrvatska) commenced operations on 1 January 2021, positioning Croatia as a regional energy leader and contributing to diversification of Europe's energy supply.

In 2010, Croatian energy production covered 85% of nationwide natural gas and 19% of oil demand. In 2016, Croatia's primary energy production involved natural gas (24.8%), hydropower (28.3%), crude oil (13.6%), fuelwood (27.6%), and heat pumps and other renewable energy sources (5.7%). In 2017, net total electrical power production reached 11,543 GWh, while it imported 12,157 GWh or about 40% of its electric power energy needs. Croatia co-owns 50% of the [Krško Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Kr%C5%A1ko_Nuclear_Power_Plant) with neighboring [Slovenia](/wiki/Slovenia). This power plant supplies a large part of Croatian imports and provides 15% of the nation's electricity. According to Eurostat in 2026, Croatia has the highest quantity of water resources per capita in the EU (30,000 m3).

With an estimated population of 3.87 million in 2024, Croatia [ranks 128th by population](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_population) in the world. Its 2018 [population density](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_and_dependent_territories_by_population_density) was 72.9 inhabitants per square kilometre, making Croatia one of the more sparsely populated European countries. The overall [life expectancy in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_life_expectancy) at birth was 77.7 years in 2024.

The [total fertility rate](/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) of 1.46 children per mother, is one of the [lowest in the world](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_and_dependent_territories_by_fertility_rate), far below the replacement rate of 2.1; it remains considerably below the high of 6.18 children rate in 1885. Croatia's [death rate](/wiki/Death_rate) has continuously exceeded its [birth rate](/wiki/Birth_rate) since 1998. Croatia subsequently has one of the world's oldest populations, with an average age of 45.1 years. The population rose steadily from 2.1 million in 1857 until 1991, when it peaked at 4.7 million, with the exceptions of censuses taken in 1921 and 1948, i.e., following the world wars. The natural growth rate is negative with the [demographic transition](/wiki/Demographic_transition) completed in the 1970s. In recent years, the Croatian government has been pressured to increase permit quotas for foreign workers, reaching an all-time high of 68.100 in 2019. In accordance with its immigration policy, Croatia is trying to entice emigrants to return. From 2008 to 2018, Croatia's population dropped by 10%.

Historic demographic shifts are closely tied to Croatia's nation-building efforts and the related 1990s [War of Independence](/wiki/Croatian_War_of_Independence). The war displaced large numbers of the population and emigration increased. In 1991, in predominantly occupied areas, more than 400,000 Croats were either removed from their homes by [Serb](/wiki/Serbs_in_Croatia) forces or fled the violence. During the war's final days, about 150,000–200,000 Serbs fled before the arrival of Croatian forces during [Operation Storm](/wiki/Operation_Storm). After the war, the number of displaced persons fell to about 250,000. The Croatian government cared for displaced persons via the social security system and the Office of Displaced Persons and Refugees. Most of the territories abandoned during the war were settled by Croat refugees from Bosnia and Herzegovina, mostly from north-western Bosnia, while some displaced people returned to their homes.

### Religion

Main articles: [Religion in Croatia](/wiki/Religion_in_Croatia) and [Catholic Church in Croatia](/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Croatia)

Further information: [List of cathedrals in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_cathedrals_in_Croatia) and [List of synagogues in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_synagogues_in_Croatia)

[Šibenik Cathedral](/wiki/%C5%A0ibenik_Cathedral), since 2000 on the [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) [World Heritage List](/wiki/World_Heritage_List)

Croatia has no official religion. [Freedom of religion](/wiki/Freedom_of_religion) is a constitutional right that protects all [religious communities](/wiki/Religious_denomination) as equal before the law and considers them [separate from the state](/wiki/Separation_of_church_and_state).

According to the 2021 census, 87.39% of Croatians identify as Christian; of these, Catholics make up the largest group, accounting for 78.97% of the population, after which follows [Eastern Orthodoxy](/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy) (3.32%), [Protestantism](/wiki/Protestantism) (0.26%), and other Christians (4.83%). The largest religion after Christianity is [Islam](/wiki/Islam) (1.32%). 4.71% of the population describe itself as non-religious. In the [Eurostat](/wiki/Eurostat) Eurobarometer Poll of 2010, 69% of the population responded that "they believe there is a God". In a 2009 Gallup poll, 70% answered yes to the question "Is religion an important part of your daily life?" Approximately 24% of the population attend religious services regularly.

### Languages

Main articles: [Croatian language](/wiki/Croatian_language), [Languages of Croatia](/wiki/Languages_of_Croatia), [Minority languages of Croatia](/wiki/Minority_languages_of_Croatia), and [Bunjevac dialect](/wiki/Bunjevac_dialect)

See also: [Shtokavian dialect](/wiki/Shtokavian_dialect), [Chakavian dialect](/wiki/Chakavian_dialect), and [Kajkavian dialect](/wiki/Kajkavian_dialect)

Croatian is the official language of the Republic of Croatia and has been an [official language of the European Union](/wiki/Official_languages_of_the_European_Union) since Croatia's accession in 2013. Croatian replaced [Latin](/wiki/Latin) as the official language of the Croatian government in the 19th century. Following the [Vienna Literary Agreement](/wiki/Vienna_Literary_Agreement) in 1850, the language and its Latin alphabet underwent reforms to create an unified "Croatian or Serbian" or "[Serbo-Croatian](/wiki/Serbo-Croatian)" standard, which under various names became the official language of [Yugoslavia](/wiki/Yugoslavia). In [SFR Yugoslavia](/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia), from 1972 to 1989, the language was constitutionally designated as the "Croatian literary language" and the "Croatian or Serbian language". It was the result of a resistance to and [secession](/wiki/Language_secessionism) from "Serbo-Croatian" in the form of the [Declaration on the Status and Name of the Croatian Literary Language](/wiki/Declaration_on_the_Status_and_Name_of_the_Croatian_Literary_Language) as part of the [Croatian Spring](/wiki/Croatian_Spring). Since gaining independence in the early 1990s, the Republic of Croatia constitutionally designates the language as "Croatian language" and regulates it through [linguistic prescription](/wiki/Linguistic_prescription). The long-standing aspiration for development of its own expressions and thus enrichment of the language, as opposed to the adoption of foreign solutions in the form of [loanwords](/wiki/Loanwords), has been described by linguists as [Croatian linguistic purism](/wiki/Croatian_linguistic_purism).

Location map of dialects in Croatia and areas in Bosnia and Herzegovina with Croat majority. Chakavian in blue.

[Minority languages are in official use](/wiki/Minority_languages_of_Croatia) in local government units where more than a third of the population consists of national minorities or where local enabling legislation applies. Those languages are [Czech](/wiki/Czech_language), [Hungarian](/wiki/Hungarian_language), [Italian](/wiki/Italian_language_in_Croatia), [Serbian](/wiki/Serbian_language_in_Croatia), and [Slovak](/wiki/Slovak_language). The following minority languages are also recognised: [Albanian](/wiki/Albanian_language), [Bosnian](/wiki/Bosnian_language), [Bulgarian](/wiki/Bulgarian_language), German, [Hebrew](/wiki/Hebrew_language), [Macedonian](/wiki/Macedonian_language), [Montenegrin](/wiki/Montenegrin_language), [Polish](/wiki/Polish_language), [Romanian](/wiki/Romanian_language), [Istro-Romanian](/wiki/Istro-Romanian_language), [Romani](/wiki/Romani_language), Russian, [Rusyn](/wiki/Rusyn_language), [Slovene](/wiki/Slovene_language), [Turkish](/wiki/Turkish_language), and [Ukrainian](/wiki/Ukrainian_language).

According to the 2021 Census, 95.25% of citizens declared Croatian as their native language, 1.16% declared Serbian as their native language, while no other language reaches 0.26%. Croatian is a member of the [South Slavic languages](/wiki/South_Slavic_languages) and is written using the [Latin alphabet](/wiki/Latin_alphabet). There are three major dialects spoken on the territory of Croatia, with standard Croatian based on the [Shtokavian](/wiki/Shtokavian) dialect. The [Chakavian](/wiki/Chakavian) and [Kajkavian](/wiki/Kajkavian) dialects are distinguished from Shtokavian by their [lexicon](/wiki/Lexicon), [phonology](/wiki/Phonology) and [syntax](/wiki/Syntax).

A 2011 survey revealed that 78% of Croats claim knowledge of at least one foreign language. According to a 2005 EC survey, 49% of Croats speak English as the second language, 34% speak German, 14% speak Italian, 10% speak French, 4% speak Russian and 2% speak Spanish. However several large municipalities support [minority languages](/wiki/Minority_languages_of_Croatia). A majority of Slovenes (59%) have some knowledge of Croatian. The country is a part of various language-based international associations, most notably the [European Union Language Association.](/wiki/Languages_of_the_European_Union)

### Education

Main article: [Education in Croatia](/wiki/Education_in_Croatia)

[National and University Library](/wiki/National_and_University_Library_in_Zagreb)

[University of Zagreb](/wiki/University_of_Zagreb) is the largest Croatian university and the oldest university in Southeastern Europe.

As of 2024, the literacy rate in Croatia was 99.45%. Primary education in Croatia starts at the age of six or seven and consists of eight grades. In 2007 a law was passed to increase free, noncompulsory education until 18 years of age. Compulsory education consists of eight grades of elementary school. Secondary education is provided by [gymnasiums](/wiki/Gymnasium_(school)) and vocational schools. As of the 2022-23 school year, there were 2,073 elementary schools and 738 upper secondary schools in Croatia. Primary and secondary education are also available in languages of recognised minorities in Croatia, where classes are held in Czech, Hungarian, Italian, Serbian, German and Slovak languages.

There are 133 elementary and secondary level [music](/wiki/Music_school) and [art schools](/wiki/Art_schools), as well as 83 elementary and 44 secondary schools for disabled children and youth and 11 elementary and 52 secondary schools for adults. Nationwide [leaving exams](/wiki/Leaving_exams) ([Croatian](/wiki/Croatian_language): *državna matura*) were introduced for secondary education students in the school year 2009–2010. It comprises three compulsory subjects (Croatian language, mathematics, and a foreign language) and optional subjects and is a prerequisite for university education. Croatia has eight public universities and two private universities. The [University of Zadar](/wiki/University_of_Zadar), the first university in Croatia, was founded in 1396 and remained active until 1807, when other institutions of higher education took over until the foundation of the renewed University of Zadar in 2002. The [University of Zagreb](/wiki/University_of_Zagreb), founded in 1669, is the oldest continuously operating university in Southeast Europe. There are also 15 [polytechnics](/wiki/Institute_of_technology), of which two are private, and 30 higher education institutions, of which 27 are private. In total, there are 131 institutions of higher education in Croatia, attended by more than 160 thousand students.

In 2022, Croatia's [research and development](/wiki/Research_and_development) (R&D) expenditure was approximately 1.43% of GDP. Among the scientific institutes operating in Croatia, the largest is the [Ruđer Bošković Institute](/wiki/Ru%C4%91er_Bo%C5%A1kovi%C4%87_Institute) in Zagreb. The [Croatian Academy of Sciences and Arts](/wiki/Croatian_Academy_of_Sciences_and_Arts) in Zagreb is a [learned society](/wiki/Learned_society) promoting language, culture, arts and science from its inception in 1866. Croatia was ranked 40th in the [Global Innovation Index](/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2025.

The [European Investment Bank](/wiki/European_Investment_Bank) provided digital infrastructure and equipment to around 150 primary and secondary schools in Croatia. Twenty of these schools got specialised assistance in the form of gear, software, and services to help them integrate the teaching and administrative operations. In 2024, the EIB extended a €207 million loan to the [City of Zagreb](/wiki/Zagreb) for infrastructure upgrades, including investments in renewable energy, energy efficiency projects, social and affordable housing, schools, and kindergartens. Additionally, the EIB signed a €49 million loan with the [City of Split](/wiki/Split,_Croatia) to co-finance its 2023–2027 multi-sector investment programme, which includes refurbishing public buildings, increasing energy efficiency, and improving technical equipment for public information and communication.

### Healthcare

Main article: [Healthcare in Croatia](/wiki/Healthcare_in_Croatia)

[University Hospital Centre Zagreb](/wiki/University_Hospital_Centre_Zagreb) is the largest hospital in Croatia and the teaching hospital of the [University of Zagreb](/wiki/University_of_Zagreb).

Croatia has a [universal health care](/wiki/Universal_health_care) system, whose roots can be traced back to the Hungarian-Croatian Parliament Act of 1891, providing a form of mandatory insurance of all factory workers and craftsmen. The population is covered by a basic health insurance plan provided by statute and optional insurance. In 2017, annual healthcare related expenditures reached 22.2 billion kuna (around €3.0 billion). Healthcare expenditures make up only 0.6% of private health insurance and public spending. In 2022, Croatia spent around 7.2% of its GDP on healthcare. In 2025, Croatia ranked 53rd in the world in life expectancy with 75.8 years for men and 81.9 years for women, and it had a low [infant mortality rate](/wiki/Infant_mortality_rate) of 3.3 per 1,000 [live births](/wiki/Live_birth_(human)).

There are hundreds of healthcare institutions in Croatia, including 75 hospitals, and 13 clinics with 23,049 beds. The hospitals and clinics care for more than 700 thousand patients per year and employ 6,642 [medical doctors](/wiki/Medical_doctors), including 4,773 specialists. There is a total of 69,841 health workers. There are 119 emergency units in health centres, responding to more than a million calls. The principal cause of death in 2022 was [cardiovascular disease](/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease) at 34.3% for men and 43.8% for women, followed by [hypertensive](/wiki/Hypertension) disease. In 2022 it was estimated that 37% of Croatians are smokers. According to 2022 data, 35.65% of the Croatian adult population is obese.

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Croatia](/wiki/Culture_of_Croatia)

Further information: [World Heritage Sites in Croatia](/wiki/World_Heritage_Sites_in_Croatia), [Register of Cultural Goods of Croatia](/wiki/Register_of_Cultural_Goods_of_Croatia), and [List of museums in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_museums_in_Croatia)

The historic centre of [Trogir](/wiki/Trogir) has been included in the [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) list of [World Heritage Site](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site) since 1997.

[Pula Arena](/wiki/Pula_Arena), Roman amphitheatre located in [Pula](/wiki/Pula), constructed between 27 BC and AD 68.

Because of its geographical position, Croatia represents a blend of four different cultural spheres. It has been a crossroads of influences from western culture and the east since the schism between the [Western Roman Empire](/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire) and the [Byzantine Empire](/wiki/Byzantine_Empire), and also from Central Europe and [Mediterranean culture](/wiki/Mediterranean_culture). The [Illyrian movement](/wiki/Illyrian_movement) was the most significant period of national cultural history, as the 19th century proved crucial to the emancipation of Croatians and saw unprecedented developments in all fields of art and culture, giving rise to many [historical figures](/wiki/List_of_people_from_Croatia).

The [Ministry of Culture](/wiki/Ministry_of_Culture_(Croatia)) is tasked with preserving the nation's [cultural and natural heritage](/wiki/Natural_and_Cultural_Heritage_of_Croatia) and overseeing its development. Further activities supporting the development of culture are undertaken at the local government level. The [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO)'s [World Heritage List](/wiki/World_Heritage_Sites_by_country) includes [ten sites in Croatia](/wiki/Ten_sites_in_Croatia) and a list of Intangible Cultural Heritage of Croatia. The country is rich with [intangible culture](/wiki/Intangible_culture) and holds 15 of [UNESCO's World's intangible culture masterpieces](/wiki/UNESCO%27s_World%27s_intangible_culture_masterpieces), ranking fourth in the world. A global cultural contribution from Croatia is the necktie, derived from the [cravat](/wiki/Cravat_(early)) originally worn by the 17th-century Croatian mercenaries in France.

In 2019, Croatia had 95 professional theatres, 30 professional children's theatres, and 51 amateur theatres visited by more than 2.27 million viewers per year. Professional theatres employ 1,195 artists. There are 42 professional orchestras, ensembles, and choirs, attracting an annual attendance of 297 thousand. There are 75 cinemas with 166 screens and attendance of 5.026 million.

Croatia has 222 museums, visited by more than 2.71 million people in 2023. Furthermore, there are 1,768 libraries, containing 26.8 million volumes, and 19 state archives. The book publishing market is dominated by several major publishers and the industry's centrepiece event—[Interliber](/wiki/Interliber) exhibition held annually at [Zagreb Fair](/wiki/Zagreb_Fair).

### Arts, literature, and music

Main articles: [Croatian art](/wiki/Croatian_art), [Architecture of Croatia](/wiki/Architecture_of_Croatia), [Croatian literature](/wiki/Croatian_literature), and [Croatian music](/wiki/Croatian_music)

Historical nucleus of [Split](/wiki/Split,_Croatia) with the 4th-century [Diocletian's Palace](/wiki/Diocletian%27s_Palace) was inscribed on the [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) list of [World Heritage Sites](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site) in 1979.

[Euphrasian Basilica](/wiki/Euphrasian_Basilica) in [Poreč](/wiki/Pore%C4%8D), example of early [Byzantine architecture](/wiki/Byzantine_architecture), on the [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) [World Heritage List](/wiki/World_Heritage_List) since 1997

Architecture in Croatia reflects influences of bordering nations. Austrian and Hungarian influence is visible in public spaces and buildings in the north and the central regions, architecture found along coasts of Dalmatia and Istria exhibits Venetian influence. Squares named after culture heroes, parks, and pedestrian-only zones, are features of Croatian towns and cities, especially where large scale [Baroque](/wiki/Baroque_architecture) urban planning took place, for instance in Osijek ([Tvrđa](/wiki/Tvr%C4%91a)), Varaždin, and Karlovac. The subsequent influence of the [Art Nouveau](/wiki/Art_Nouveau) was reflected in contemporary architecture. The architecture is the Mediterranean with a Venetian and Renaissance influence in major coastal urban areas exemplified in works of [Giorgio da Sebenico](/wiki/Giorgio_da_Sebenico) and Nicolas of Florence such as the [Cathedral of St. James](/wiki/%C5%A0ibenik_Cathedral) in Šibenik. The oldest preserved examples of Croatian architecture are the 9th-century churches, with the largest and the most representative among them being [Church of St. Donatus](/wiki/Church_of_St._Donatus) in [Zadar](/wiki/Zadar).

[Marko Marulić](/wiki/Marko_Maruli%C4%87) (18 August 1450 – 5 January 1524), Croatian poet, lawyer, judge, and Renaissance humanist who coined the term "psychology". He is the [national poet](/wiki/National_poet) of Croatia.

Besides the architecture encompassing the oldest artworks, there is a history of artists in Croatia reaching the Middle Ages. In that period the stone portal of the [Trogir Cathedral](/wiki/Trogir_Cathedral) was made by [Radovan](/wiki/Radovan_(master)), representing the most important monument of [Romanesque](/wiki/Romanesque_art) sculpture from [Medieval Croatia](/wiki/Croatia_in_the_union_with_Hungary). The [Renaissance](/wiki/Renaissance_in_Croatia) had the greatest impact on the Adriatic Sea coast since the remainder was embroiled in the Hundred Years' Croatian–Ottoman War. With the waning of the Ottoman Empire, art flourished during the [Baroque](/wiki/Baroque) and [Rococo](/wiki/Rococo). The 19th and 20th centuries brought affirmation of numerous Croatian artisans, helped by several patrons of the arts such as bishop [Josip Juraj Strossmayer](/wiki/Josip_Juraj_Strossmayer). Croatian artists of the period achieving renown were [Vlaho Bukovac](/wiki/Vlaho_Bukovac), [Ivan Meštrović](/wiki/Ivan_Me%C5%A1trovi%C4%87), and [Ivan Generalić](/wiki/Ivan_Generali%C4%87).

The Baška tablet, a stone inscribed with the [glagolitic alphabet](/wiki/Glagolitic_alphabet) found on the Krk island and dated to c. 1100, is considered to be the oldest surviving prose in Croatian. The beginning of more vigorous development of Croatian literature is marked by the Renaissance and [Marko Marulić](/wiki/Marko_Maruli%C4%87). Besides Marulić, Renaissance playwright [Marin Držić](/wiki/Marin_Dr%C5%BEi%C4%87), Baroque poet [Ivan Gundulić](/wiki/Ivan_Gunduli%C4%87), [Croatian national revival](/wiki/Croatian_national_revival) poet [Ivan Mažuranić](/wiki/Ivan_Ma%C5%BEurani%C4%87), novelist, playwright, and poet [August Šenoa](/wiki/August_%C5%A0enoa), children's writer [Ivana Brlić-Mažuranić](/wiki/Ivana_Brli%C4%87-Ma%C5%BEurani%C4%87), writer and journalist [Marija Jurić Zagorka](/wiki/Marija_Juri%C4%87_Zagorka), poet and writer [Antun Gustav Matoš](/wiki/Antun_Gustav_Mato%C5%A1), poet [Antun Branko Šimić](/wiki/Antun_Branko_%C5%A0imi%C4%87), [expressionist](/wiki/Expressionist) and [realist](/wiki/Realism_(arts)) writer [Miroslav Krleža](/wiki/Miroslav_Krle%C5%BEa), poet [Tin Ujević](/wiki/Tin_Ujevi%C4%87) and novelist, and short story writer [Ivo Andrić](/wiki/Ivo_Andri%C4%87) are often cited as the greatest figures in Croatian literature.

Croatian music varies from classical operas to modern-day rock. [Vatroslav Lisinski](/wiki/Vatroslav_Lisinski) created the country's first opera, *Love and Malice*, in 1846. [Ivan Zajc](/wiki/Ivan_Zajc) composed more than a thousand pieces of music, including masses and oratorios. Pianist [Ivo Pogorelić](/wiki/Ivo_Pogoreli%C4%87) has performed across the world.

### Media

Main articles: [Media of Croatia](/wiki/Media_of_Croatia) and [List of radio stations in Croatia](/wiki/List_of_radio_stations_in_Croatia)

In Croatia, the Constitution guarantees the freedom of the press and the freedom of speech. Croatia ranked 64th in the 2019 [Press Freedom Index](/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index) report compiled by [Reporters Without Borders](/wiki/Reporters_Without_Borders) which noted that journalists who investigate corruption, organised crime or war crimes face challenges and that the Government was trying to influence the public broadcaster [HRT](/wiki/Croatian_Radiotelevision)'s editorial policies. In its 2019 Freedom in the World report, the [Freedom House](/wiki/Freedom_House) classified freedoms of press and speech in Croatia as generally free from political interference and manipulation, noting that journalists still face threats and occasional attacks. The state-owned news agency [HINA](/wiki/HINA) runs a [wire service](/wiki/Wire_service) in Croatian and English on politics, economics, society, and culture.

[Radio Zagreb](/wiki/Croatian_Radio), now a part of [Croatian](/wiki/Croatian_language) national [public broadcasting](/wiki/Public_broadcasting) company, [Croatian Radiotelevision](/wiki/Croatian_Radiotelevision), was the first public radio station in Southeast Europe.

As of January 2021, there are thirteen nationwide free-to-air [DVB-T](/wiki/DVB-T) television channels, with [Croatian Radiotelevision](/wiki/Croatian_Radiotelevision) (HRT) operating four, [RTL Televizija](/wiki/RTL_(Croatian_TV_channel)) three, and [Nova TV](/wiki/Nova_TV_(Croatia)) operating two channels, and the [Croatian Olympic Committee](/wiki/Croatian_Olympic_Committee), Kapital Net d.o.o., and Author d.o.o. companies operate the remaining three. Also, there are 21 regional or local DVB-T television channels. The HRT is also broadcasting a satellite TV channel. In 2020, there were 147 radio stations and 27 TV stations in Croatia. Cable television and [IPTV](/wiki/IPTV) networks are gaining ground. Cable television already serves 450 thousand people, around 10% of the total population of the country.

In 2010, 267 newspapers and 2,676 magazines were published in Croatia. The print media market is dominated by the Croatian-owned [Hanza Media](/wiki/Hanza_Media) and Austrian-owned [Styria Media Group](/wiki/Styria_Media_Group) who publish their flagship dailies *[Jutarnji list](/wiki/Jutarnji_list)*, *[Večernji list](/wiki/Ve%C4%8Dernji_list)* and *[24sata](/wiki/24sata_(Croatia))*. Other influential newspapers are *[Novi list](/wiki/Novi_list)* and *[Slobodna Dalmacija](/wiki/Slobodna_Dalmacija)*. In 2020, *24sata* was the most widely circulated daily newspaper, followed by *Večernji list* and *Jutarnji list*.

[Croatia](/wiki/Croatia_in_the_Eurovision_Song_Contest) competed in the [Eurovision Song Contest](/wiki/Eurovision_Song_Contest) as part of [Yugoslavia](/wiki/Yugoslavia_in_the_Eurovision_Song_Contest) since 1961. The first and only victory Yugoslavia achieved in the competition was accomplished by the Croatian pop band [Riva](/wiki/Riva_(band)) in 1989. Since its debut at the [1993 contest](/wiki/Eurovision_Song_Contest_1993), Croatia won two fourth places at the [1996](/wiki/Eurovision_Song_Contest_1996) and [1999](/wiki/Eurovision_Song_Contest_1999) contests, and one second place at the [2024 contest](/wiki/Eurovision_Song_Contest_2024), marking the country's best result to date as an independent nation.

### Film industry

Main articles: [Cinema of Croatia](/wiki/Cinema_of_Croatia) and [Television in Croatia](/wiki/Television_in_Croatia)

Croatia's film industry is small and heavily subsidised by the government, mainly through grants approved by the Ministry of Culture with films often being co-produced by HRT. Croatian cinema produces between five and ten feature films per year. [Pula Film Festival](/wiki/Pula_Film_Festival), the national film awards event held annually in [Pula](/wiki/Pula), is the most prestigious film event featuring national and international productions. [Animafest Zagreb](/wiki/Animafest_Zagreb), founded in 1972, is the prestigious annual film festival dedicated to the animated film. The first greatest accomplishment by Croatian filmmakers was achieved by [Dušan Vukotić](/wiki/Du%C5%A1an_Vukoti%C4%87) when he won the 1961 [Academy Award for Best Animated Short Film](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Animated_Short_Film) for *[Ersatz](/wiki/Surogat)* ([Croatian](/wiki/Croatian_language): *Surogat*). Croatian film producer [Branko Lustig](/wiki/Branko_Lustig) won the [Academy Awards for Best Picture](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Picture) for *[Schindler's List](/wiki/Schindler%27s_List)* and *[Gladiator](/wiki/Gladiator_(2000_film))*. In addition to that, Croatian filmmaker [Nebojša Slijepčević](/wiki/Neboj%C5%A1a_Slijep%C4%8Devi%C4%87) got nominated for [97th Academy Awards](/wiki/97th_Academy_Awards) in category for [Best Live Action Short Film](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Live_Action_Short_Film) for his 2024 movie *[The Man Who Could Not Remain Silent](/wiki/The_Man_Who_Could_Not_Remain_Silent)* ([Croatian](/wiki/Croatian_language): *Čovjek koji nije mogao šutjeti*), making it first Croatian nomination in that category, and first since its [independence](/wiki/Croatian_independence).

Before and since its [independence](/wiki/Independence_of_Croatia), Croatia has become a popular filming destination amongs international filming productions, and a lot of [blockbuster](/wiki/Blockbuster_(entertainment)) films and TV series have been filmed in Croatia including: *[Game of Thrones](/wiki/Game_of_Thrones)*, *[Star Wars: The Last Jedi](/wiki/Star_Wars:_The_Last_Jedi)*, *[Robin Hood](/wiki/Robin_Hood_(2018_film))* in Dubrovnik, *[Speak No Evil](/wiki/Speak_No_Evil_(2024_film))*, *[Season of the Witch](/wiki/Season_of_the_Witch_(2011_film))* and *[Titius](/wiki/Titus_(film))* in Istria, *[Infinity Pool](/wiki/Infinity_Pool_(film))* in Šibenik, *[Canary Black](/wiki/Canary_Black)*, *[Hitman's Wife's Bodyguard](/wiki/Hitman%27s_Wife%27s_Bodyguard)*, *[Sophie's Choice](/wiki/Sophie%27s_Choice_(film))*, *[Armour of God](/wiki/Armour_of_God_(film))* and *[Fiddler on the roof](/wiki/Fiddler_on_the_Roof_(film))* in Zagreb, *[Mamma Mia! Here We Go Again](/wiki/Mamma_Mia!_Here_We_Go_Again)* on island of Vis, *[Succession](/wiki/Succession_(TV_series))* on the island of Korčula, *[Hercules](/wiki/Hercules_(2014_film))*, *[The Weekend Away](/wiki/The_Weekend_Away)*, *[Bliss](/wiki/Bliss_(2021_film))* in Split, *[The Peacemaker](/wiki/The_Peacemaker_(1997_film))*, *[Lee](/wiki/Lee_(2023_film))* and many others. Croatia became international filming location due to its biodiversity, landscape that can accommodate every visual requirements and cheaper filming costs. In last 11 years there has been 122 projects for international films in Croatia, and €263 million spent as part of the Filming in Croatia which increased in last years due to high demand for its location.

### Cuisine

Main articles: [Croatian cuisine](/wiki/Croatian_cuisine), [Croatian wine](/wiki/Croatian_wine), and [Beer in Croatia](/wiki/Beer_in_Croatia)

See also: [List of Croatian dishes](/wiki/List_of_Croatian_dishes)

[Zagorski štrukli](/wiki/Zagorski_%C5%A1trukli)

Croatian traditional cuisine varies from one region to another. Dalmatia and Istria have culinary influences of [Italian](/wiki/Italian_cuisine) and other [Mediterranean cuisines](/wiki/Mediterranean_cuisine) which prominently feature various seafood, cooked vegetables and pasta, and condiments such as olive oil and garlic. [Austrian](/wiki/Austrian_cuisine), [Hungarian](/wiki/Hungarian_cuisine), [Turkish](/wiki/Turkish_cuisine), and [Balkan](/wiki/Balkan_cuisine) culinary styles influenced continental cuisine. In that area, meats, freshwater fish, and vegetable dishes are predominant.

There are two distinct wine-producing regions in Croatia. The continental in the northeast of the country, especially Slavonia, produces premium wines, particularly whites. Along the north coast, Istrian and Krk wines are similar to those in neighbouring Italy, while further south in Dalmatia, Mediterranean-style red wines are the norm. Annual production of wine exceeds 72 million litres as of 2017. Croatia was almost exclusively a wine-consuming country up until the late 18th century when a more massive beer production and consumption started.

There are 11 restaurants in Croatia with a [Michelin star](/wiki/Michelin_star) and 89 restaurants bearing some of the Michelin's marks.

### Sports

Main articles: [Sport in Croatia](/wiki/Sport_in_Croatia) and [Croatia at the Olympics](/wiki/Croatia_at_the_Olympics)

[Croatia national football team](/wiki/Croatia_national_football_team) in [World Cup 2018](/wiki/World_Cup_2018) in Russia

Croatia has a long tradition of sports dating back to Roman times, followed by popular medieval [knights' tournaments](/wiki/Tournament_(medieval)). Modern organized sports began in the late 19th century with the founding of sports associations like Hrvatski Sokol in 1874. By the early 20th century, many sports organizations emerged, including the [Croatian Sports Association](/wiki/Croatian_Sports_Association) established in 1909. Croatian sports' development has been closely related to the Olympic movement, with [Franjo Bučar](/wiki/Franjo_Bu%C4%8Dar) playing a key role in promoting sports for [Croatian independence](/wiki/Independence_of_Croatia). Sport is part of the school curriculum, and many children engage in extracurricular activities. To support sports development, Croatia enacted the National Sport Programme in 2019. Funding for sports comes from the state budget, sponsorship, and membership fees. The [Croatian Olympic Committee](/wiki/Croatian_Olympic_Committee), established in 1991, oversees over 80 national sports associations.

The most popular sports in Croatia are football, basketball, handball, and water polo. In 2024, there were over 4,500 chess association members. With about 12,500 sports associations, [football is the most popular sport](/wiki/Football_in_Croatia), hosting nearly 1,500 clubs and 110,000 players, achieving significant milestones, including bronze at the [1998 World Cup](/wiki/1998_World_Cup), silver at the [2018 World Cup](/wiki/2018_World_Cup) and another bronze at [2022 World Cup](/wiki/2022_World_Cup). [Croatians have participated in the Olympics](/wiki/Croatia_at_the_Olympics) since 1992 and have won [59 Olympic medals, including 20 gold](/wiki/Croatia_at_the_Olympics#Medals). The [national tennis team](/wiki/Croatia_Davis_Cup_team) has won two [Davis Cup](/wiki/Davis_Cup) titles and won a gold medal at the [2020 Summer Olympics](/wiki/Tennis_at_the_2020_Summer_Olympics) in men's doubles. The [national handball](/wiki/Croatia_men%27s_national_handball_team) and [water polo teams](/wiki/Croatia_men%27s_national_water_polo_team) are also been successful, each achieving multiple championships and medals. [Rowing](/wiki/Croatian_Rowing_Federation) has seen success as well, winning multiple Olympic and World Championship medals. In gymnastics, athletes have also made their mark, winning medals in European and World Championships.

Croatia hosted several major sports competitions, including the [2009 World Men's Handball Championship](/wiki/2009_World_Men%27s_Handball_Championship), the [2007 World Table Tennis Championships](/wiki/2007_World_Table_Tennis_Championships), the [2010](/wiki/2010) [European Individual Chess Championship](/wiki/European_Individual_Chess_Championship), the [2000 World Rowing Championships](/wiki/2000_World_Rowing_Championships), the [1987 Summer Universiade](/wiki/1987_Summer_Universiade), the [1979 Mediterranean Games](/wiki/1979_Mediterranean_Games), and several [European Championships](/wiki/European_Championship), including the [2000](/wiki/2000_European_Men%27s_Handball_Championship), [2018](/wiki/2018_European_Men%27s_Handball_Championship) and [2025 World Men's Handball Championship](/wiki/2025_World_Men%27s_Handball_Championship), [2024 Men's European Water Polo Championship](/wiki/2024_Men%27s_European_Water_Polo_Championship).
