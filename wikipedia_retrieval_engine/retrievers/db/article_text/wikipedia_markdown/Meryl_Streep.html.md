**Mary Louise "Meryl" Streep** (born June 22, 1949) is an American actress. Recognized as one of the most versatile performers of her era, Streep is noted for her technical precision, command of dialects, and professional longevity. She is an alumna of [Vassar College](/wiki/Vassar_College) and the [Yale School of Drama](/wiki/Yale_School_of_Drama), holding a [Bachelor of Arts](/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) and a [Master of Fine Arts](/wiki/Master_of_Fine_Arts). Her artistic process often includes refining her characters' dialogue so that their motivations possess a psychological depth and agency that transcend traditional archetypes. Beyond her creative work, she is a prominent advocate for gender parity, labor protections, and a challenge to the influence of the [male gaze](/wiki/Male_gaze) in film criticism and production.

Streep began her professional stage career in 1975 in *[Trelawny of the Wells](/wiki/Trelawny_of_the_Wells)* and earned a [Tony Award nomination](/wiki/Tony_Award_for_Best_Featured_Actress_in_a_Play) the following year for *[27 Wagons Full of Cotton](/wiki/27_Wagons_Full_of_Cotton)*. She transitioned to film in *[Julia](/wiki/Julia_(1977_film))* (1977), followed by her first Academy Award nomination for *[The Deer Hunter](/wiki/The_Deer_Hunter)* (1978). For *[Kramer vs. Kramer](/wiki/Kramer_vs._Kramer)* (1979), she won her first Oscar after advocating to rewrite her character's courtroom testimony, shifting the role from a secondary antagonist to a portrayal focused on a woman's autonomy. She became a leading actress of the 1980s with performances in *[The French Lieutenant's Woman](/wiki/The_French_Lieutenant%27s_Woman_(film))* (1981), *[Sophie's Choice](/wiki/Sophie%27s_Choice_(film))* (1982), *[Out of Africa](/wiki/Out_of_Africa_(film))* (1985), and *[A Cry in the Dark](/wiki/Evil_Angels_(film))* (1988), for which she received the [Best Actress Award at Cannes](/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival_Award_for_Best_Actress).

After expanding into various genres in the 1990s, Streep achieved a new level of commercial stardom in the 2000s. She transitioned from her established dramatic niche to lead high-grossing mainstream successes, portraying a formidable antagonist in *[The Devil Wears Prada](/wiki/The_Devil_Wears_Prada_(film))* (2006) and a singing lead in the musical *[Mamma Mia!](/wiki/Mamma_Mia!_(film))* (2008). During this period, she continued to receive critical acclaim for her dramatic turns in *[Doubt](/wiki/Doubt_(2008_film))* (2008) and *[The Iron Lady](/wiki/The_Iron_Lady_(film))* (2011), the latter earning her a third Academy Award.

Her career honors include three [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Awards), eight [Golden Globe Awards](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Awards), two [British Academy Film Awards](/wiki/British_Academy_Film_Awards), three [Primetime Emmy Awards](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Awards), one [Children's and Family Emmy Award](/wiki/Children%27s_and_Family_Emmy_Award), and two [Screen Actors Guild Awards](/wiki/Screen_Actors_Guild_Awards). She is a recipient of the [AFI Life Achievement Award](/wiki/AFI_Life_Achievement_Award), the [Honorary Palme d'Or](/wiki/Honorary_Palme_d%27Or), the [Kennedy Center Honor](/wiki/Kennedy_Center_Honor), the [Golden Globe Cecil B. DeMille Award](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Cecil_B._DeMille_Award), and an [Honorary César](/wiki/Honorary_C%C3%A9sar). In 2014, she was awarded the [Presidential Medal of Freedom](/wiki/Presidential_Medal_of_Freedom). She holds the record for the most Academy Award and Globe nominations of any performer, with [21](/wiki/List_of_Academy_Award_records#Acting_records) and 34 respectively. A prolific artist, her career spans over 64 films and 18 television projects, alongside more than 20 musical recordings and 30 [audiobooks](/wiki/Audiobook).

## Early life and education

Streep as a cheerleader at [Bernards High School](/wiki/Bernards_High_School), 1966

Mary Louise Streep was born on June 22, 1949, in [Summit, New Jersey](/wiki/Summit,_New_Jersey), to artist [Mary Wilkinson Streep](/wiki/Mary_Wilkinson_Streep) and pharmaceutical executive Harry William Streep Jr. She has two younger brothers, Harry William Streep III and Dana David Streep, both actors. Streep's mother, who she claims looks like Dame [Judi Dench](/wiki/Judi_Dench), strongly encouraged her daughter and instilled confidence in her from a very young age. Streep said, "She was a mentor because she said to me, 'Meryl, you're capable. You're so great.' She was saying, 'You can do whatever you put your mind to. If you're lazy, you're not going to get it done. But if you put your mind to it, you can do anything.' And I believed her." Although she was naturally more introverted than her mother, when she later needed an injection of confidence in adulthood, she would consult her mother at times for advice.

Streep was raised as a [Presbyterian](/wiki/Presbyterian) in [Basking Ridge, New Jersey](/wiki/Basking_Ridge,_New_Jersey), and attended [Cedar Hill Elementary School](/wiki/Cedar_Hill_School,_Bernards_Township,_New_Jersey) and the Oak Street School, which was a junior high school at that time. In her junior high debut, she starred as Louise Heller in the play *The Family Upstairs*. In 1963, the family moved to [Bernardsville, New Jersey](/wiki/Bernardsville,_New_Jersey), where she attended [Bernards High School](/wiki/Bernards_High_School). As a "gawky kid with glasses and frizzy hair", she liked to show off in front of the camera in family home movies from a young age. At age 12, Streep was selected to sing at a school recital, leading to her having opera lessons from [Estelle Liebling](/wiki/Estelle_Liebling). Despite her talent, she later remarked, "I was singing something I didn't feel and understand. That was an important lesson—not to do that. To find the thing that I could feel through." She quit after four years. Streep had many [Catholic](/wiki/Catholicism) school friends and regularly attended [Mass](/wiki/Mass_(religion)). She was a high school cheerleader for the Bernards High School Mountaineers. She was also chosen as the homecoming queen her senior year.

Streep as a senior in high school, 1966

Although Streep appeared in numerous school plays during her high school years, she was uninterested in serious theater until acting in the play *[Miss Julie](/wiki/Miss_Julie)* at [Vassar College](/wiki/Vassar_College) in 1969, in which she gained attention across the campus. Vassar drama professor Clinton J. Atkinson noted, "I don't think anyone ever taught Meryl acting. She really taught herself." Streep demonstrated an early ability to mimic accents and to quickly memorize her lines. She earned her [AB](/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) in drama *[cum laude](/wiki/Cum_laude)* in 1971, before applying for a [Master of Fine Arts](/wiki/Master_of_Fine_Arts) (MFA) degree from the [Yale School of Drama](/wiki/Yale_School_of_Drama). At Yale, she supplemented her course fees by working as a waitress and typist, and appeared in over a dozen stage productions per year; at one point, she became overworked and developed ulcers, so she contemplated quitting acting and switching to study law. Streep played a variety of roles on stage, from [Helena](/wiki/Helena_(A_Midsummer_Night%27s_Dream)) in *[A Midsummer Night's Dream](/wiki/A_Midsummer_Night%27s_Dream)* to an 80-year-old woman in a wheelchair in a comedy written by then-unknown playwrights [Christopher Durang](/wiki/Christopher_Durang) and [Albert Innaurato](/wiki/Albert_Innaurato). She was a student of choreographer [Carmen de Lavallade](/wiki/Carmen_de_Lavallade), whom she introduced at the 2017 [Kennedy Center Honors](/wiki/Kennedy_Center_Honors). Another of her teachers was [Robert Lewis](/wiki/Robert_Lewis_(director)), a co-founder of the [Actors Studio](/wiki/Actors_Studio). Streep disapproved of some of the acting exercises she was asked to do, remarking that one professor taught the emotional recall technique by delving into personal lives in a way she found "obnoxious". She earned her MFA in drama from Yale in 1975. She also enrolled as a visiting student at [Dartmouth College](/wiki/Dartmouth_College) in 1970 and received an honorary Doctor of Arts degree from the college in 1981.

## Career

### 1970s: Early work and breakthrough

Streep started her career acting in numerous productions with [The Public Theater](/wiki/The_Public_Theater)

One of Streep's first professional jobs in 1975 was at the [Eugene O'Neill Theater Center](/wiki/Eugene_O%27Neill_Theater_Center)'s National Playwrights Conference, during which she acted in five plays over six weeks. She moved to [New York City](/wiki/New_York_City) in 1975, and was cast by [Joseph Papp](/wiki/Joseph_Papp) in a production of *[Trelawny of the Wells](/wiki/Trelawny_of_the_Wells)* at the Vivian Beaumont Theater, opposite [Mandy Patinkin](/wiki/Mandy_Patinkin) and [John Lithgow](/wiki/John_Lithgow). She went on to appear in five more roles in her first year in New York, including in Papp's [New York Shakespeare Festival](/wiki/New_York_Shakespeare_Festival) productions of *[Henry V](/wiki/Henry_V_(play))*, *[The Taming of the Shrew](/wiki/The_Taming_of_the_Shrew)* with [Raul Julia](/wiki/Raul_Julia), and *[Measure for Measure](/wiki/Measure_for_Measure)* opposite [Sam Waterston](/wiki/Sam_Waterston) and [John Cazale](/wiki/John_Cazale). She entered into a relationship with Cazale at this time, and resided with him until his death three years later. She starred in the musical *[Happy End](/wiki/Happy_End_(musical))* on Broadway, and won an [Obie](/wiki/Obie_Award) for her performance in the [off-Broadway](/wiki/Off-Broadway) play *Alice at the Palace*.

Although Streep had not aspired to become a film actor, [Robert De Niro](/wiki/Robert_De_Niro)'s performance in *[Taxi Driver](/wiki/Taxi_Driver)* (1976) had a profound impact on her; she said to herself, 'That's the kind of actor I want to be when I grow up.' Streep began auditioning for film roles, and underwent an unsuccessful audition for the lead role in [Dino De Laurentiis](/wiki/Dino_De_Laurentiis)'s remake of the action adventure *[King Kong](/wiki/King_Kong_(1976_film))* which was released in 1976. De Laurentiis, referring to Streep as she stood before him, said in Italian to his son: "This is so ugly. Why did you bring me this?" Unknown to Laurentiis, Streep understood Italian, and she remarked, "I'm very sorry that I'm not as beautiful as I should be, but, you know – this is it. This is what you get." She continued to work on [Broadway](/wiki/Broadway_theatre), appearing in the 1976 double bill of [Tennessee Williams](/wiki/Tennessee_Williams)' *[27 Wagons Full of Cotton](/wiki/One_act_plays_by_Tennessee_Williams#27_Wagons_Full_of_Cotton)* and [Arthur Miller](/wiki/Arthur_Miller)'s *[A Memory of Two Mondays](/wiki/A_Memory_of_Two_Mondays)*. She received a Tony Award nomination for [Best Featured Actress in a Play](/wiki/Tony_Award_for_Best_Featured_Actress_in_a_Play). Streep's other Broadway credits include [Anton Chekhov](/wiki/Anton_Chekhov)'s *[The Cherry Orchard](/wiki/The_Cherry_Orchard)* and the [Bertolt Brecht](/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht)-[Kurt Weill](/wiki/Kurt_Weill) musical *[Happy End](/wiki/Happy_End_(musical))*, in which she had originally appeared off-Broadway at the [Chelsea Theater Center](/wiki/Chelsea_Theater_Center). She received [Drama Desk Award](/wiki/Drama_Desk_Award) nominations for both productions.

Streep in 1978

Streep's first feature film role came opposite [Jane Fonda](/wiki/Jane_Fonda) in the 1977 film *[Julia](/wiki/Julia_(1977_film))*, in which she had a small role during a flashback sequence. Most of her scenes were edited out, but the brief time on screen horrified the actress, "I had a bad wig and they took the words from the scene I shot with Jane and put them in my mouth in a different scene. I thought, I've made a terrible mistake, no more movies. I hate this business." However, Streep stated in 2015 that Fonda had a lasting influence on her as an actress, and credited her with opening "probably more doors than I probably even know about". Robert De Niro, who had spotted Streep in her stage production of *The Cherry Orchard*, suggested that she play the role of his girlfriend in the war film *[The Deer Hunter](/wiki/The_Deer_Hunter)* (1978). Cazale, who had been diagnosed with [lung cancer](/wiki/Lung_cancer), was also cast in the film, and Streep took on the role of a "vague, stock girlfriend" to remain with Cazale for the duration of filming. [Pauline Kael](/wiki/Pauline_Kael), who later became a strong critic of Streep, remarked that she was a "real beauty" who brought much freshness to the film with her performance. The film's success exposed Streep to a wider audience and earned her a nomination for the [Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Supporting_Actress).

In the 1978 miniseries *[Holocaust](/wiki/Holocaust_(miniseries))*, Streep played the leading role of a German woman married to a Jewish artist played by [James Woods](/wiki/James_Woods) in [Nazi era](/wiki/Nazi_era) Germany. She found the material to be "unrelentingly noble" and professed to have taken on the role for financial gain. Streep travelled to Germany and Austria for filming while Cazale remained in New York. Upon her return, Streep found that Cazale's illness had progressed, and she nursed him until his death on March 12, 1978. With an estimated audience of 109 million, *Holocaust* brought a wider degree of public recognition to Streep, who found herself "on the verge of national visibility". She won the [Primetime Emmy Award for Outstanding Lead Actress in a Miniseries or a Movie](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award_for_Outstanding_Lead_Actress_in_a_Miniseries_or_a_Movie) for her performance. Despite the awards success, Streep was still not enthusiastic towards her film career and preferred acting on stage.

She played the supporting role of Leilah in [Wendy Wasserstein](/wiki/Wendy_Wasserstein)'s *[Uncommon Women and Others](/wiki/Uncommon_Women_and_Others_(film))* in a May 1978 "Theater in America" television production for [PBS](/wiki/PBS)'s [Great Performances](/wiki/Great_Performances). She replaced [Glenn Close](/wiki/Glenn_Close), who played the role in the [Off-Broadway](/wiki/Off-Broadway) production at the [Phoenix Theatre](/wiki/Phoenix_Theatre_(New_York_City)). Hoping to divert herself from the grief of Cazale's death, Streep accepted a role in *[The Seduction of Joe Tynan](/wiki/The_Seduction_of_Joe_Tynan)* (1979) as the chirpy love interest of [Alan Alda](/wiki/Alan_Alda), later commenting that she played it on "automatic pilot". She performed the role of [Katherine](/wiki/Kate_(The_Taming_of_the_Shrew)) in *[The Taming of the Shrew](/wiki/The_Taming_of_the_Shrew)* for [Shakespeare in the Park](/wiki/Shakespeare_in_the_Park). That same year she played a supporting role as the former girlfriend turned lesbian in *[Manhattan](/wiki/Manhattan_(1979_film))* (1979) for [Woody Allen](/wiki/Woody_Allen). Streep later said that Allen did not provide her with a complete script, giving her only the six pages of her own scenes, and did not permit her to improvise a word of her dialogue. [Vincent Canby](/wiki/Vincent_Canby) of *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* described her performance as being "beautifully played".

Streep in 1977

In the drama *[Kramer vs. Kramer](/wiki/Kramer_vs._Kramer)*, Streep was cast opposite [Dustin Hoffman](/wiki/Dustin_Hoffman) as an unhappily married woman who abandons her husband and child. Streep thought that the script portrayed the female character as "too evil" and insisted that it was not representative of real women who faced marriage breakdown and child custody battles. The makers agreed with her, and the script was revised. In preparing for the part, Streep spoke to her own mother about her life as a wife with a career, and frequented the [Upper East Side](/wiki/Upper_East_Side) neighborhood in which the film was set, watching the interactions between parents and children. The director [Robert Benton](/wiki/Robert_Benton) allowed Streep to write her own dialogue in two key scenes, despite some objection from Hoffman, who "hated her guts" at first. Hoffman and producer [Stanley R. Jaffe](/wiki/Stanley_R._Jaffe) later spoke of Streep's tirelessness, with Hoffman commenting: "She's extraordinarily hard-working, to the extent that she's obsessive. I think that she thinks about nothing else, but what she's doing." The film was controversial among feminists, but it was a role which film critic Stephen Farber believed displayed Streep's "own emotional intensity", writing that she was one of the "rare performers who can imbue the most routine moments with a hint of mystery".

For the film, Streep won both the [Golden Globe Award](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Award_for_Best_Supporting_Actress_-_Motion_Picture) and the [Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Supporting_Actress), which she famously left in the ladies' room after giving her speech. She received awards from the [Los Angeles Film Critics Association](/wiki/Los_Angeles_Film_Critics_Association_Award_for_Best_Supporting_Actress), [National Board of Review](/wiki/National_Board_of_Review_Award_for_Best_Supporting_Actress) and [National Society of Film Critics](/wiki/National_Society_of_Film_Critics_Award_for_Best_Supporting_Actress) for her collective work in her three film releases of 1979. Both *The Deer Hunter* and *Kramer vs. Kramer* were major commercial successes and were consecutive winners of the [Academy Award for Best Picture](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Picture).

### 1980s: Rise to prominence

In 1979, Streep began workshopping *Alice in Concert*, a musical version of *[Alice's Adventures in Wonderland](/wiki/Alice%27s_Adventures_in_Wonderland)*, with writer and composer [Elizabeth Swados](/wiki/Elizabeth_Swados) and director [Joseph Papp](/wiki/Joseph_Papp); the show was put on at New York's Public Theater from December 1980. [Frank Rich](/wiki/Frank_Rich) of *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* referred to Streep as the production's "one wonder", but questioned why she devoted so much energy to it. By 1980, Streep had progressed to leading roles in films. She was featured on the cover of *[Newsweek](/wiki/Newsweek)* magazine with the headline "A Star for the 80s"; [Jack Kroll](/wiki/Jack_Kroll) commented, "There's a sense of mystery in her acting; she doesn't simply imitate (although she's a great mimic in private). She transmits a sense of danger, a primal unease lying just below the surface of normal behavior".

Streep denounced her fervent media coverage at the time as "excessive hype". The [story within a story](/wiki/Story_within_a_story) drama *[The French Lieutenant's Woman](/wiki/The_French_Lieutenant%27s_Woman_(film))* (1981) was Streep's first leading role. The film paired Streep with [Jeremy Irons](/wiki/Jeremy_Irons) as contemporary actors, telling their modern story, as well as the [Victorian era](/wiki/Victorian_era) drama they were performing. Streep developed an English accent for the part, but considered herself a misfit for the role: "I couldn't help wishing that I was more beautiful". A *[New York](/wiki/New_York_(magazine))* magazine article commented that, while many female stars of the past had cultivated a singular identity in their films, Streep was a "[chameleon](/wiki/Chameleon)", willing to play any type of role. Streep was awarded a [BAFTA Award for Best Actress in a Leading Role](/wiki/BAFTA_Award_for_Best_Actress_in_a_Leading_Role) for her work. The following year, she re-united with Robert Benton for the [psychological thriller](/wiki/Psychological_thriller), *[Still of the Night](/wiki/Still_of_the_Night_(film))* (1982), co-starring [Roy Scheider](/wiki/Roy_Scheider) and [Jessica Tandy](/wiki/Jessica_Tandy). [Vincent Canby](/wiki/Vincent_Canby), writing for *The New York Times*, noted that the film was an homage to the works of [Alfred Hitchcock](/wiki/Alfred_Hitchcock), but that one of its main weaknesses was a lack of chemistry between Streep and Scheider, concluding that Streep "is stunning, but she's not on screen anywhere near long enough".

Greater success came later in the year when Streep starred in the drama *[Sophie's Choice](/wiki/Sophie%27s_Choice_(film))* (also 1982), portraying a Polish survivor of [Auschwitz](/wiki/Auschwitz_concentration_camp) caught in a love triangle between a young naïve writer ([Peter MacNicol](/wiki/Peter_MacNicol)) and a Jewish intellectual ([Kevin Kline](/wiki/Kevin_Kline)). Streep's emotional dramatic performance and her apparent mastery of a Polish accent drew praise. [William Styron](/wiki/William_Styron) wrote the novel with [Ursula Andress](/wiki/Ursula_Andress) in mind for the role of Sophie, but Streep was determined to get the role. Streep filmed the "choice" scene in one take and refused to do it again, finding it extremely painful and emotionally exhausting. That scene, in which Streep is ordered by an [SS](/wiki/Schutzstaffel) guard at [Auschwitz](/wiki/Auschwitz_concentration_camp) to choose which of her two children would be gassed and which would proceed to the labor camp, is her most famous scene, according to [Emma Brockes](/wiki/Emma_Brockes) of *The Guardian* who wrote in 2006: "It's classic Streep, the kind of scene that makes your scalp tighten, but defter in a way is her handling of smaller, harder-to-grasp emotions".

Among several acting awards, Streep won the [Academy Award for Best Actress](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Actress) for her performance, and her characterization was voted the third greatest movie performance of all time by *[Premiere](/wiki/Premiere_(magazine))* magazine. [Roger Ebert](/wiki/Roger_Ebert) said of her delivery, "Streep plays the Brooklyn scenes with an enchanting Polish-American accent (she has the first accent I've ever wanted to hug), and she plays the flashbacks in subtitled German and Polish. There is hardly an emotion that Streep doesn't touch in this movie, and yet we're never aware of her straining. This is one of the most astonishing and yet one of the most unaffected and natural performances I can imagine". [Pauline Kael](/wiki/Pauline_Kael), on the contrary, called the film an "infuriatingly bad movie", and thought that Streep "decorporealizes" herself, which she believed explained why her movie heroines "don't seem to be full characters, and why there are no incidental joys to be had from watching her".

In 1983, Streep played her first non-fictional character, the [nuclear whistleblower](/wiki/Nuclear_whistleblowers) and labor union activist [Karen Silkwood](/wiki/Karen_Silkwood), who died in a suspicious car accident while investigating alleged wrongdoing at the [Kerr-McGee](/wiki/Kerr-McGee) [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium) plant, in [Mike Nichols](/wiki/Mike_Nichols)' biographical film *[Silkwood](/wiki/Silkwood)*. Streep felt a personal connection to Silkwood, and in preparation, she met with people close to the woman, and in doing so realized that each person saw a different aspect of her personality. She said, "I didn't try to turn myself into Karen. I just tried to look at what she did. I put together every piece of information I could find about her ... What I finally did was look at the events in her life, and try to understand her from the inside". Jack Kroll of *[Newsweek](/wiki/Newsweek)* considered Streep's characterization to have been "brilliant", while Silkwood's boyfriend Drew Stephens expressed approval in that Streep had played Karen as a human being rather than a myth, despite Karen's father Bill thinking that Streep and the film had dumbed his daughter down. Pauline Kael believed that Streep had been miscast.

Streep next played opposite [Robert De Niro](/wiki/Robert_De_Niro) in the romance *[Falling in Love](/wiki/Falling_in_Love_(1984_film))* (1984), which was poorly received. She portrayed a fighter for the [French Resistance](/wiki/French_Resistance) during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) in the British drama *[Plenty](/wiki/Plenty_(film))* (1985), adapted from the play by [David Hare](/wiki/David_Hare_(playwright)). For the latter, Roger Ebert wrote that she conveyed "great subtlety; it is hard to play an unbalanced, neurotic, self-destructive woman, and do it with such gentleness and charm ... Streep creates a whole character around a woman who could have simply been a catalogue of symptoms." In 2008, [Molly Haskell](/wiki/Molly_Haskell) praised Streep's performance in *Plenty*, believing it to be "one of Streep's most difficult and ambiguous" films and "most feminist" role.

In her next film, *[Out of Africa](/wiki/Out_of_Africa_(film))* (1985), Streep starred as the Danish writer [Karen Blixen](/wiki/Karen_Blixen), opposite [Robert Redford](/wiki/Robert_Redford)'s [Denys Finch Hatton](/wiki/Denys_Finch_Hatton). Director [Sydney Pollack](/wiki/Sydney_Pollack) was initially dubious about Streep in the role, as he did not think she was sexy enough, and had considered [Jane Seymour](/wiki/Jane_Seymour_(British_actress)) for the part. Pollack recalls that Streep impressed him in a different way: "She was so direct, so honest, so without bullshit. There was no shielding between her and me." Streep and Pollack often clashed during the 101-day shoot in Kenya, particularly over Blixen's voice. Streep had spent much time listening to tapes of Blixen, and began speaking in an old-fashioned and aristocratic fashion, which Pollack thought excessive. A significant commercial success, the film won a Golden Globe for Best Picture. It also earned Streep another Academy Award nomination for Best Actress, and the film ultimately won Best Picture. Film critic [Stanley Kauffmann](/wiki/Stanley_Kauffmann) praised her performance, writing "Meryl Streep is back in top form. This means her performance in *Out of Africa* is at the highest level of acting in film today."

Streep in 1989

Streep's next films did not appeal to a wide audience; she co-starred with [Jack Nicholson](/wiki/Jack_Nicholson) in the dramas *[Heartburn](/wiki/Heartburn_(film))* (1986) and *[Ironweed](/wiki/Ironweed_(film))* (1987). In *[Evil Angels](/wiki/Evil_Angels_(film))* (1988), she played [Lindy Chamberlain](/wiki/Lindy_Chamberlain), an Australian woman who had been convicted of the [murder of her infant daughter](/wiki/Death_of_Azaria_Chamberlain) despite claiming that the baby had been taken by a [dingo](/wiki/Dingo). Filmed in Australia, Streep won the [Australian Film Institute Award for Best Actress in a Leading Role](/wiki/Australian_Film_Institute_Award_for_Best_Actress_in_a_Leading_Role), a [Best Actress](/wiki/Prix_d%27interpr%C3%A9tation_f%C3%A9minine) at the [Cannes Film Festival](/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival), and the [New York Film Critics Circle Award for Best Actress](/wiki/New_York_Film_Critics_Circle_Award_for_Best_Actress). Streep has said of developing the Australian accent in the film: "I had to study a little bit for Australian because it's not dissimilar [to American], so it's like coming from Italian to Spanish. You get a little mixed up." Vincent Canby of *The New York Times* referred to her performance as "another stunning performance", played with "the kind of virtuosity that seems to re-define the possibilities of screen acting".

In 1989, Streep lobbied to play the lead role in [Oliver Stone](/wiki/Oliver_Stone)'s adaption of the play *Evita*, but two months before filming was due to commence, she dropped out, citing "exhaustion" initially, although it was later revealed that there was a dispute over her salary. By the end of the decade, Streep actively looked to star in a comedy. She found the role in *[She-Devil](/wiki/She-Devil_(1989_film))* (1989), a satire that parodied societal obsession with beauty and cosmetic surgery, in which she played a glamorous writer. Though the film was not a success, [Richard Corliss](/wiki/Richard_Corliss) of *[Time](/wiki/Time_(magazine))* wrote that Streep was the "one reason" to see it, and observed that it marked a departure from the dramatic roles she was known to play. Reacting to her string of poorly received films, Streep said: "Audiences are shrinking; as the marketing strategy defines more and more narrowly who they want to reach males from 16 to 25 – it's become a chicken-and-egg syndrome. Which came first? First, they release all these summer movies, then do a demographic survey of who's going to see them."

### 1990s: Commercial fluctuations

Biographer Karen Hollinger described the early 1990s as a downturn in the popularity of Streep's films, attributing this partly to a critical perception that her comedies had been an attempt to convey a lighter image following several serious, but commercially unsuccessful, dramas, and, more significantly, to the lack of options available to an actress in her forties. Streep commented that she had limited her options by her preference to work in Los Angeles, close to her family, a situation that she had anticipated in a 1981 interview when she commented, "By the time an actress hits her mid-forties, no one's interested in her anymore. And if you want to fit a couple of babies into that schedule as well, you've got to pick your parts with great care." At the [Screen Actor's Guild](/wiki/Screen_Actor%27s_Guild) National Women's Conference in 1990, Streep keynoted the first national event, emphasizing the decline in women's work opportunities, pay parity, and role models within the film industry. She criticized the film industry for downplaying the importance of women both on screen and off.

Streep at the [32nd Grammy Awards](/wiki/32nd_Grammy_Awards) in 1990

After roles in the comedy-drama *[Postcards from the Edge](/wiki/Postcards_from_the_Edge_(film))* (1990), and the comedy-fantasy *[Defending Your Life](/wiki/Defending_Your_Life)* (1991), Streep starred with [Goldie Hawn](/wiki/Goldie_Hawn) in the farcical black comedy, *[Death Becomes Her](/wiki/Death_Becomes_Her)* (1992), with [Bruce Willis](/wiki/Bruce_Willis) as their co-star. Streep persuaded writer [David Koepp](/wiki/David_Koepp) to re-write several of the scenes, particularly the one in which her character has an affair with a younger man, which she believed was "unrealistically male" in its conception. The seven-month shoot was the longest of Streep's career, during which she got into character by "thinking about being slightly pissed off all of the time". Due to Streep's allergies to numerous cosmetics, special prosthetics had to be designed to age her by ten years to look 54, although Streep believed that they made her look nearer 70. Although it was a commercial success, earning $15.1 million in just five days, Streep's contribution to comedy was generally not taken well by critics. *Time*'s Richard Corliss wrote approvingly of Streep's "wicked-witch routine" but dismissed the film as "*She-Devil* with a make-over" and one which "hates women". Streep later admitted to having disliked filming the scenes involving heavy special effects, and vowed never to work again on a film with heavy special effects.

Streep appeared with [Jeremy Irons](/wiki/Jeremy_Irons), [Glenn Close](/wiki/Glenn_Close) and [Winona Ryder](/wiki/Winona_Ryder) in *[The House of the Spirits](/wiki/The_House_of_the_Spirits_(film))* (1993), set in Chile during [Augusto Pinochet](/wiki/Augusto_Pinochet)'s dictatorship. The film was not well received by critics. [Anthony Lane](/wiki/Anthony_Lane) of *[The New Yorker](/wiki/The_New_Yorker)* wrote: "This is really quite an achievement. It brings together Jeremy Irons, Meryl Streep, Winona Ryder, [Antonio Banderas](/wiki/Antonio_Banderas), and [Vanessa Redgrave](/wiki/Vanessa_Redgrave) and insures that, without exception, they all give their worst performances ever". The following year, Streep starred in *[The River Wild](/wiki/The_River_Wild)*, as the mother of children on a whitewater rafting trip who encounter two violent criminals ([Kevin Bacon](/wiki/Kevin_Bacon) and [John C. Reilly](/wiki/John_C._Reilly)) in the wilderness. Though critical reaction was generally mixed, [Peter Travers](/wiki/Peter_Travers) of *[Rolling Stone](/wiki/Rolling_Stone)* found her to be "strong, sassy and looser than she has ever been onscreen".

Streep's most successful film of the decade was the romantic drama *[The Bridges of Madison County](/wiki/The_Bridges_of_Madison_County_(film))* (1995) directed by [Clint Eastwood](/wiki/Clint_Eastwood), who adapted the film from [Robert James Waller](/wiki/Robert_James_Waller)'s [novel of the same name](/wiki/The_Bridges_of_Madison_County). It relates the story of Robert Kincaid (Eastwood), a photographer working for *[National Geographic](/wiki/National_Geographic_(magazine))*, who has a love affair with a middle-aged Italian farm wife Francesca (Streep). Though Streep disliked the novel it was based on, she found the script to be a special opportunity for an actress her age. She gained weight for the part and dressed differently from the character in the book to emulate voluptuous Italian film stars such as [Sophia Loren](/wiki/Sophia_Loren). Both Loren and [Anna Magnani](/wiki/Anna_Magnani) were an influence in her portrayal, and Streep viewed [Pier Paolo Pasolini](/wiki/Pier_Paolo_Pasolini)'s *[Mamma Roma](/wiki/Mamma_Roma)* (1962) prior to filming. The film was a box office hit and grossed over $70 million in the United States. The film, unlike the novel, was warmly received by critics. [Janet Maslin](/wiki/Janet_Maslin) of *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* wrote that Eastwood had managed to create "a moving, elegiac love story at the heart of Mr. Waller's self-congratulatory overkill", while [Joe Morgenstern](/wiki/Joe_Morgenstern) of *[The Wall Street Journal](/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal)* described it as "one of the most pleasurable films in recent memory". Film critic [Karina Longworth](/wiki/Karina_Longworth) claimed it was the role in which Streep became "arguably the first middle-aged actress to be taken seriously by Hollywood as a romantic heroine".

Streep played the estranged sister of Bessie ([Diane Keaton](/wiki/Diane_Keaton)), a woman battling [leukemia](/wiki/Leukemia), in *[Marvin's Room](/wiki/Marvin%27s_Room_(film))* (1996), an adaptation of [the play](/wiki/Marvin%27s_Room_(play)) by [Scott McPherson](/wiki/Scott_McPherson). Streep recommended Keaton for the role. [Roger Ebert](/wiki/Roger_Ebert) stated that, "Streep and Keaton, in their different styles, find ways to make Lee and Bessie into much more than the expression of their problems." The film was well received, and Streep earned another Golden Globe nomination for her performance.

Streep's performance in *[...First Do No Harm](/wiki/...First_Do_No_Harm)* (1997) garnered her a second [Emmy Award](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award) nomination for [Outstanding Lead Actress – Miniseries or a Movie](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award_for_Outstanding_Lead_Actress_%E2%80%93_Miniseries_or_a_Movie). In 1998, Streep first appeared opposite [Michael Gambon](/wiki/Michael_Gambon) and [Catherine McCormack](/wiki/Catherine_McCormack) in [Pat O'Connor](/wiki/Pat_O%27Connor_(director))'s *[Dancing at Lughnasa](/wiki/Dancing_at_Lughnasa_(film))*, another [Broadway adaptation](/wiki/Dancing_at_Lughnasa), which was entered into the [Venice Film Festival](/wiki/Venice_Film_Festival) in its year of release. [Janet Maslin](/wiki/Janet_Maslin) of *The New York Times* remarked that "Meryl Streep has made many a grand acting gesture in her career, but the way she simply peers out a window in *Dancing at Lughnasa* ranks with the best. Everything the viewer need know about Kate Mundy, the woman she plays here, is written on that prim, lonely face and its flabbergasted gaze." Later that year, she played a housewife dying of cancer in *[One True Thing](/wiki/One_True_Thing)*. The film met with positive reviews. [Mick LaSalle](/wiki/Mick_LaSalle) in the *[San Francisco Chronicle](/wiki/San_Francisco_Chronicle)* declared, "After *One True Thing*, critics who persist in the fiction that Streep is a cold and technical actress will need to get their heads examined. She is so instinctive and natural – so thoroughly in the moment and operating on flights of inspiration – that she's able to give us a woman who's at once wildly idiosyncratic and utterly believable." *[Los Angeles Times](/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)* film critic [Kenneth Turan](/wiki/Kenneth_Turan) noted that her role "is one of the least self-consciously dramatic and surface showy of her career," but she "adds a level of honesty and reality that makes [her performance] one of her most moving".

Streep portrayed [Roberta Guaspari](/wiki/Roberta_Guaspari) in the music drama *[Music of the Heart](/wiki/Music_of_the_Heart)* (1999). Streep received nominations for an Academy Award, a Golden Globe and a [Screen Actors Guild Award](/wiki/Screen_Actors_Guild_Award) for her performance. Roger Ebert wrote:

> Meryl Streep is known for her mastery of accents; she may be the most versatile speaker in the movies. Here you might think she has no accent, unless you've heard her real speaking voice; then you realize that Guaspari's speaking style is no less a particular achievement than Streep's other accents. This is not Streep's voice, but someone else's – with a certain flat quality, as if later education and refinement came after a somewhat unsophisticated childhood.

### 2000s: Career resurgence and stage work

Main article: [Meryl Streep in the 2000s](/wiki/Meryl_Streep_in_the_2000s)

Streep in [Saint Petersburg](/wiki/Saint_Petersburg), Russia, in 2004

Streep entered the 2000s with a voice cameo in [Steven Spielberg](/wiki/Steven_Spielberg)'s *[A.I. Artificial Intelligence](/wiki/A.I._Artificial_Intelligence)* (2001), a [science fiction film](/wiki/Science_fiction_film) about a childlike [android](/wiki/Android_(robot)), played by [Haley Joel Osment](/wiki/Haley_Joel_Osment). The same year, Streep co-hosted the annual [Nobel Peace Prize Concert](/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize_Concert) with [Liam Neeson](/wiki/Liam_Neeson) which was held in [Oslo, Norway](/wiki/Oslo,_Norway), on December 11, 2001, in honour of the [Nobel Peace Prize](/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize) laureate, the United Nations and [Kofi Annan](/wiki/Kofi_Annan). In 2001, Streep returned to the stage for the first time in more than twenty years, playing Arkadina in [The Public Theater](/wiki/The_Public_Theater)'s revival of [Anton Chekhov](/wiki/Anton_Chekhov)'s *[The Seagull](/wiki/The_Seagull)*, directed by [Mike Nichols](/wiki/Mike_Nichols) and co-starring [Kevin Kline](/wiki/Kevin_Kline), [Natalie Portman](/wiki/Natalie_Portman), [John Goodman](/wiki/John_Goodman), [Marcia Gay Harden](/wiki/Marcia_Gay_Harden), [Stephen Spinella](/wiki/Stephen_Spinella), [Debra Monk](/wiki/Debra_Monk), [Larry Pine](/wiki/Larry_Pine) and [Philip Seymour Hoffman](/wiki/Philip_Seymour_Hoffman).

The same year, Streep began work on [Spike Jonze](/wiki/Spike_Jonze)'s comedy-drama *[Adaptation.](/wiki/Adaptation_(film))* (2002), in which she portrayed real-life journalist [Susan Orlean](/wiki/Susan_Orlean). Lauded by critics and viewers alike, the film won Streep her fourth [Golden Globe](/wiki/Golden_Globe) in the Best Supporting Actress category. [A. O. Scott](/wiki/A._O._Scott) in *The New York Times* considered Streep's portrayal of Orlean to have been "played with impish composure", noting the contrast in her "wittily realized" character with love interest [Chris Cooper](/wiki/Chris_Cooper)'s "lank-haired, toothless charisma" as the autodidact arrested for poaching rare orchids. Streep appeared alongside [Nicole Kidman](/wiki/Nicole_Kidman) and [Julianne Moore](/wiki/Julianne_Moore) in [Stephen Daldry](/wiki/Stephen_Daldry)'s *[The Hours](/wiki/The_Hours_(film))* (2002), based on the 1999 [novel](/wiki/The_Hours_(novel)) by [Michael Cunningham](/wiki/Michael_Cunningham). Focusing on three women of different generations whose lives are interconnected by the novel *[Mrs. Dalloway](/wiki/Mrs._Dalloway)* by [Virginia Woolf](/wiki/Virginia_Woolf), the film was generally well received and won all three leading actresses a [Silver Bear for Best Actress](/wiki/Silver_Bear_for_Best_Actress).

In 2003, Streep re-united with [Mike Nichols](/wiki/Mike_Nichols) to star with [Al Pacino](/wiki/Al_Pacino) and [Emma Thompson](/wiki/Emma_Thompson) in the [HBO](/wiki/HBO)'s [adaptation](/wiki/Angels_in_America_(miniseries)) of [Tony Kushner](/wiki/Tony_Kushner)'s six-hour play *[Angels in America](/wiki/Angels_in_America)*, the story of two couples whose relationships dissolve amidst the backdrop of [Reagan era](/wiki/Reagan_era) politics. Streep, who was cast in four roles in the miniseries, received her second [Emmy Award](/wiki/Emmy_Award) and fifth Golden Globe for her performance. She appeared in [Jonathan Demme](/wiki/Jonathan_Demme)'s moderately successful [remake](/wiki/The_Manchurian_Candidate_(2004_film)) of *[The Manchurian Candidate](/wiki/The_Manchurian_Candidate_(1962_film))* in 2004, co-starring [Denzel Washington](/wiki/Denzel_Washington), playing the role of a woman who is both a [U.S. senator](/wiki/United_States_Senate) and the manipulative, ruthless mother of a vice-presidential candidate. The same year, she played the supporting role of Aunt Josephine in *[Lemony Snicket's A Series of Unfortunate Events](/wiki/Lemony_Snicket%27s_A_Series_of_Unfortunate_Events)* alongside [Jim Carrey](/wiki/Jim_Carrey), based on the first three novels in [Snicket](/wiki/Lemony_Snicket)'s book [series](/wiki/A_Series_of_Unfortunate_Events). The [black comedy](/wiki/Black_comedy) received generally favorable reviews from critics, and won the [Academy Award for Best Makeup](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Makeup). Streep also narrated the film *Monet's Palate*. Streep was next cast in the comedy film *[Prime](/wiki/Prime_(film))* (2005), directed by [Ben Younger](/wiki/Ben_Younger). In the film, she played Lisa Metzger, the Jewish psychoanalyst of a divorced and lonesome business-woman, played by [Uma Thurman](/wiki/Uma_Thurman), who enters a relationship with Metzger's 23-year-old son ([Bryan Greenberg](/wiki/Bryan_Greenberg)). A modest mainstream success, it eventually grossed US$67.9 million internationally. [Roger Ebert](/wiki/Roger_Ebert) noted how Streep had "that ability to cut through the solemnity of a scene with a zinger that reveals how all human effort is, after all, comic at some level".

In August and September 2006, Streep starred onstage at [The Public Theater](/wiki/The_Public_Theater)'s production of *[Mother Courage and Her Children](/wiki/Mother_Courage_and_Her_Children)* at the Delacorte Theatre in Central Park. The Public Theater production was a new translation by playwright Tony Kushner, with songs in the Weill/Brecht style written by composer [Jeanine Tesori](/wiki/Jeanine_Tesori); veteran director [George C. Wolfe](/wiki/George_C._Wolfe) was at the helm. Streep starred alongside Kevin Kline and [Austin Pendleton](/wiki/Austin_Pendleton) in this three-and-a-half-hour play. Around the same time, Streep, along with [Lily Tomlin](/wiki/Lily_Tomlin), portrayed the last two members of what was once a popular family [country music](/wiki/Country_music) act in [Robert Altman](/wiki/Robert_Altman)'s final film *[A Prairie Home Companion](/wiki/A_Prairie_Home_Companion_(film))* (2006). A comedic [ensemble piece](/wiki/Ensemble_cast) featuring [Lindsay Lohan](/wiki/Lindsay_Lohan), [Tommy Lee Jones](/wiki/Tommy_Lee_Jones), [Kevin Kline](/wiki/Kevin_Kline) and [Woody Harrelson](/wiki/Woody_Harrelson), the film revolves around the behind-the-scenes activities at the long-running [public radio show of the same name](/wiki/A_Prairie_Home_Companion). The film grossed more than US$26 million, the majority of which came from domestic markets.

Streep (right) at the [Venice](/wiki/Venice) premiere of *[The Devil Wears Prada](/wiki/The_Devil_Wears_Prada_(film))* in 2006

Commercially, Streep fared better with a role in *[The Devil Wears Prada](/wiki/The_Devil_Wears_Prada_(film))* (also 2006), a loose [screen adaptation](/wiki/Film_adaptation) of [Lauren Weisberger](/wiki/Lauren_Weisberger)'s 2003 [novel of the same name](/wiki/The_Devil_Wears_Prada_(novel)). Streep portrayed the powerful and demanding [Miranda Priestly](/wiki/Miranda_Priestly), [fashion magazine editor](/wiki/Fashion_journalism) (and boss of a recent college graduate played by [Anne Hathaway](/wiki/Anne_Hathaway)). Though the overall film received mixed reviews, her portrayal, of what Ebert calls the "poised and imperious Miranda", drew rave reviews from critics, and earned her many award nominations, including her record-setting 14th Oscar bid, as well as another Golden Globe. On its commercial release, the film became Streep's biggest commercial success to this point, grossing more than US$326.5 million worldwide.

She portrayed a wealthy university patron in [Chen Shi-zheng](/wiki/Chen_Shi-zheng)'s much-delayed feature drama *[Dark Matter](/wiki/Dark_Matter_(film))*, a film about a Chinese science graduate student who becomes violent after dealing with academic politics at a U.S. university. Inspired by the events of the 1991 [University of Iowa shooting](/wiki/University_of_Iowa_shooting), and initially scheduled for a 2007 release, producers and investors decided to shelve *Dark Matter* out of respect for the victims of the [Virginia Tech shooting](/wiki/Virginia_Tech_shooting) in April 2007. The drama received negative to mixed reviews upon its limited 2008 release. Streep played a U.S. government official who investigates an Egyptian [foreign national](/wiki/Foreign_national) suspected of terrorism in the political thriller *[Rendition](/wiki/Rendition_(film))* (2007), directed by [Gavin Hood](/wiki/Gavin_Hood). Keen to get involved in a thriller film, Streep welcomed the opportunity to star in a film genre for which she was not usually offered scripts, and immediately signed on to the project. Upon its release, *Rendition* was less commercially successful, and received mixed reviews.

Streep at the 2008 [San Sebastián International Film Festival](/wiki/San_Sebasti%C3%A1n_International_Film_Festival)

In this period, Streep had a short role alongside [Vanessa Redgrave](/wiki/Vanessa_Redgrave), [Glenn Close](/wiki/Glenn_Close), and her eldest daughter [Mamie Gummer](/wiki/Mamie_Gummer) in [Lajos Koltai](/wiki/Lajos_Koltai)'s drama film *[Evening](/wiki/Evening_(film))* (2007), based on the 1998 novel of the same name by [Susan Minot](/wiki/Susan_Minot). Switching between the present and the past, it tells the story of a bedridden woman, who remembers her tumultuous life in the mid-1950s. The film was released to a lukewarm reaction from critics, who called it "beautifully filmed, but decidedly dull [and] a colossal waste of a talented cast". She had a role in [Robert Redford](/wiki/Robert_Redford)'s *[Lions for Lambs](/wiki/Lions_for_Lambs)* (also 2007), a film about the connection between a platoon of United States soldiers in Afghanistan, a U.S. senator, a reporter, and a California college professor. Like *Evening*, critics felt that the talent of the cast was wasted, and that it suffered from slow pacing, although one critic announced that Streep positively stood out, being "natural, unforced, quietly powerful", in comparison to Redford's forced performance.

Streep found major commercial success when she starred in [Phyllida Lloyd](/wiki/Phyllida_Lloyd)'s *[Mamma Mia!](/wiki/Mamma_Mia!_(film))* (2008), a film adaptation of the [musical of the same name](/wiki/Mamma_Mia!_(musical)), based on the songs of Swedish pop group [ABBA](/wiki/ABBA). Co-starring [Amanda Seyfried](/wiki/Amanda_Seyfried), [Pierce Brosnan](/wiki/Pierce_Brosnan), [Stellan Skarsgård](/wiki/Stellan_Skarsg%C3%A5rd), [Colin Firth](/wiki/Colin_Firth), [Julie Walters](/wiki/Julie_Walters), and [Christine Baranski](/wiki/Christine_Baranski), Streep played a single mother and a former girl-group singer, whose daughter (Seyfried), a bride-to-be who never met her father, invites three likely paternal candidates to her wedding on the idyllic Greek island of [Skopelos](/wiki/Skopelos) known in the film as Kalokairi. An instant box office success, *Mamma Mia!* became Streep's highest-grossing film to date, with box office receipts of US$602.6 million, also ranking it first among the highest-grossing musical films. Nominated for another Golden Globe, Streep's performance was generally well received by critics, with [Wesley Morris](/wiki/Wesley_Morris) of *[The Boston Globe](/wiki/The_Boston_Globe)* commenting: "The greatest actor in American movies has finally become a movie star."

*[Doubt](/wiki/Doubt_(2008_film))* (also 2008) features Streep with [Philip Seymour Hoffman](/wiki/Philip_Seymour_Hoffman), [Amy Adams](/wiki/Amy_Adams), and [Viola Davis](/wiki/Viola_Davis). A drama revolving around the stern principal nun (Streep) of a [Bronx](/wiki/Bronx) [Catholic school](/wiki/Catholic_school) in 1964 who brings accusations of [pedophilia](/wiki/Pedophilia) against a popular priest (Hoffman), the film became a moderate box office success, and was hailed by many critics as one of the best films of 2008. The film received five Academy Awards nominations, for its four lead actors and for [John Patrick Shanley](/wiki/John_Patrick_Shanley)'s script. Ebert, who awarded the film the full four stars, highlighted Streep's caricature of a nun, who "hates all inroads of the modern world", while Kelly Vance of *[The East Bay Express](/wiki/East_Bay_Express)* remarked: "It's thrilling to see a pro like Streep step into an already wildly exaggerated role, and then ramp it up a few notches just for the sheer hell of it. Grim, red-eyed, deathly pale Sister Aloysius may be the scariest nun of all time."

Streep with [Alec Baldwin](/wiki/Alec_Baldwin) and Josh Wood at the [2009 Screen Actors Guild Awards](/wiki/15th_Screen_Actors_Guild_Awards)

In 2009, Streep played chef [Julia Child](/wiki/Julia_Child) in [Nora Ephron](/wiki/Nora_Ephron)'s *[Julie & Julia](/wiki/Julie_%26_Julia)*, co-starring with [Stanley Tucci](/wiki/Stanley_Tucci), and again with [Amy Adams](/wiki/Amy_Adams). (Tucci and Streep had worked together earlier in *Devil Wears Prada*.) The first major motion picture based on a blog, *Julie and Julia* contrasts the life of Child in the early years of her culinary career with the life of young New Yorker [Julie Powell](/wiki/Julie_Powell) (Adams), who aspires to cook all 524 recipes in [Simone Beck](/wiki/Simone_Beck), [Louisette Bertholle](/wiki/Louisette_Bertholle) and Child's cookbook *[Mastering the Art of French Cooking](/wiki/Mastering_the_Art_of_French_Cooking)*. In [Nancy Meyers](/wiki/Nancy_Meyers)' romantic comedy *[It's Complicated](/wiki/It%27s_Complicated_(film))* (also 2009), Streep starred with [Alec Baldwin](/wiki/Alec_Baldwin) and [Steve Martin](/wiki/Steve_Martin). She received nominations for the Golden Globe Award for Best Actress – Motion Picture Musical or Comedy for both *Julie & Julia* and *It's Complicated*; she won the award for *Julie & Julia*, and later received her 16th Oscar nomination for it. She also lent her voice to Mrs. Felicity Fox in [Wes Anderson](/wiki/Wes_Anderson)'s stop-motion film *[Fantastic Mr. Fox](/wiki/Fantastic_Mr._Fox_(film))*.

### 2010s: Further critical and commercial success

Streep re-teamed with *Mamma Mia!* director [Phyllida Lloyd](/wiki/Phyllida_Lloyd) on *[The Iron Lady](/wiki/The_Iron_Lady_(film))* (2011), a British biographical film about [Margaret Thatcher](/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher), which takes a look at the [Prime Minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom) during the [Falklands War](/wiki/Falklands_War) and her years in retirement. Streep, who attended a session of the [House of Commons](/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom) to see British [Members of Parliament](/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_(United_Kingdom)) (MPs) in action in preparation for her role as Thatcher, called her casting "a daunting and exciting challenge". While the film had a mixed reception, Streep's performance gained rave reviews, earning her Best Actress awards at the [Golden Globes](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Award_for_Best_Actress_in_a_Motion_Picture_Drama) and the [BAFTAs](/wiki/BAFTA_Award_for_Best_Actress_in_a_Leading_Role), as well as her third win at the [84th Academy Awards](/wiki/84th_Academy_Awards). Former advisers, friends, and family of Thatcher criticized Streep's portrayal of her as "inaccurate" and "biased". The following year, after [Thatcher's death](/wiki/Death_and_funeral_of_Margaret_Thatcher), Streep issued a formal statement describing Thatcher's "hard-nosed fiscal measures" and "hands-off approach to financial regulation", while praising her "personal strength and grit".

Streep re-united with *Prada* director [David Frankel](/wiki/David_Frankel) on the set of the romantic comedy-drama film *[Hope Springs](/wiki/Hope_Springs_(2012_film))* (2012), co-starring [Tommy Lee Jones](/wiki/Tommy_Lee_Jones) and [Steve Carell](/wiki/Steve_Carell). Streep and Jones play a middle-aged couple, who attend a week of intensive marriage counseling to try to bring back the intimacy missing in their relationship. Reviews for the film were mostly positive, with critics praising the "mesmerizing performances ... which offer filmgoers some grown-up laughs – and a thoughtful look at mature relationships". In 2013, Streep starred alongside [Julia Roberts](/wiki/Julia_Roberts) and [Ewan McGregor](/wiki/Ewan_McGregor) in the [black comedy](/wiki/Black_comedy) drama *[August: Osage County](/wiki/August:_Osage_County_(film))* (2013) about a [dysfunctional family](/wiki/Dysfunctional_family) that re-unites into the familial house when their patriarch suddenly disappears. Based on [Tracy Letts](/wiki/Tracy_Letts)'s Pulitzer Prize-winning [eponymous play](/wiki/August:_Osage_County), Streep received positive reviews for her portrayal of the family's strong-willed and contentious matriarch, who is suffering from [oral cancer](/wiki/Oral_cancer) and an addiction to narcotics. She was subsequently nominated for another Golden Globe, SAG, and Academy Award.

Streep at the [2014 Screen Actors Guild Awards](/wiki/20th_Screen_Actors_Guild_Awards)

In 2014's *[The Giver](/wiki/The_Giver_(film))*, a motion picture adaptation of [the young adult novel](/wiki/The_Giver), Streep played a community leader. Set in 2048, the social science fiction film recounts the story of a post-apocalyptic community without war, pain, suffering, differences or choice, where a young boy is chosen to learn the real world. Streep was aware of the book before being offered the role by co-star and producer [Jeff Bridges](/wiki/Jeff_Bridges). Upon its release, *The Giver* was met with generally mixed to negative reviews from critics. Streep also had a small role in the period drama film *[The Homesman](/wiki/The_Homesman)* (2014). Set in the [1850s midwest](/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_(1849%E2%80%9365)), the film stars [Hilary Swank](/wiki/Hilary_Swank) and [Tommy Lee Jones](/wiki/Tommy_Lee_Jones) as an unusual pair who help three women driven to madness by the frontier to get back East. Streep does not appear until near the end of the film, playing a preacher's wife, who takes the women into care. *The Homesman* premiered at the [2014 Cannes Film Festival](/wiki/2014_Cannes_Film_Festival) where it garnered largely positive reviews from critics.

Directed by [Rob Marshall](/wiki/Rob_Marshall), *[Into the Woods](/wiki/Into_the_Woods_(film))* (also 2014) is a Disney film adaptation of [the Broadway musical](/wiki/Into_the_Woods) with music and lyrics by [Stephen Sondheim](/wiki/Stephen_Sondheim) in which Streep plays a witch. A fantasy genre crossover inspired by the [Grimm Brothers](/wiki/Brothers_Grimm)' fairy tales, it centers on a childless couple who set out to end a curse placed on them by Streep's vengeful witch. Though the film was dismissed by some critics such as [Mark Kermode](/wiki/Mark_Kermode) as "irritating naffness", Streep's performance earned her Academy Award, Golden Globe, SAG, and Critic's Choice Award nominations for Best Supporting Actress. In July 2014, it was announced that Streep would portray [Maria Callas](/wiki/Maria_Callas) in *[Master Class](/wiki/Master_Class)*, but the project was pulled after director [Mike Nichols](/wiki/Mike_Nichols)'s death in November of the same year.

In 2015, Streep starred in [Jonathan Demme](/wiki/Jonathan_Demme)'s *[Ricki and the Flash](/wiki/Ricki_and_the_Flash)*, playing a grocery store checkout worker by day who is a rock musician at night, and who has one last chance to reconnect with her estranged family. Streep learned to play the guitar for the semi-autobiographical drama-comedy film, which again featured Streep with her eldest daughter Mamie Gummer. Reviews of the film were generally mixed. Streep's other film of this time was director [Sarah Gavron](/wiki/Sarah_Gavron)'s period drama [*Suffragette*](/wiki/Suffragette_(film)) (also 2015), co-starring [Carey Mulligan](/wiki/Carey_Mulligan) and [Helena Bonham Carter](/wiki/Helena_Bonham_Carter). In the film, she played the small, but pivotal, role of [Emmeline Pankhurst](/wiki/Emmeline_Pankhurst), a British political activist and leader of the British [suffragette](/wiki/Suffragette) movement who helped women win the right to vote. The film received mostly positive reviews, particularly for the performances of the cast, though its distributor earned criticism that Streep's prominent position within the marketing was misleading.

Streep at the [Embassy of the United States, Berlin](/wiki/Embassy_of_the_United_States,_Berlin), in 2016

Following the duties of the president at the [66th Berlin International Film Festival](/wiki/66th_Berlin_International_Film_Festival) in 2016, Streep starred in the [Stephen Frears](/wiki/Stephen_Frears)-directed comedy *[Florence Foster Jenkins](/wiki/Florence_Foster_Jenkins_(film))* (2016), an eponymous biopic about a blithely unaware tone-deaf [opera singer](/wiki/Florence_Foster_Jenkins) who insists upon public performance. Other cast members were [Hugh Grant](/wiki/Hugh_Grant) and [Simon Helberg](/wiki/Simon_Helberg). Robbie Collin considered it to be one of her most "human performance" and felt that it was "full of warmth that gives way to heart-pinching pathos". She won the [Critics' Choice Movie Award for Best Actress in a Comedy](/wiki/Critics%27_Choice_Movie_Award_for_Best_Actress_in_a_Comedy), and received Academy Award, Golden Globe, SAG, and BAFTA nominations.

Streep next starred as the first American female newspaper publisher, [Katharine Graham](/wiki/Katharine_Graham), to [Tom Hanks](/wiki/Tom_Hanks)' [Ben Bradlee](/wiki/Ben_Bradlee), in [Steven Spielberg](/wiki/Steven_Spielberg)'s political drama *[The Post](/wiki/The_Post_(film))* (2017), which centers on *[The Washington Post](/wiki/The_Washington_Post)*'s publication of the 1971 [Pentagon Papers](/wiki/Pentagon_Papers). The film received positive reviews with praise directed to the performances of the two leads. [Manohla Dargis](/wiki/Manohla_Dargis) wrote that "Streep creates an acutely moving portrait of a woman who in liberating herself helps instigate a revolution". It earned over $177 million against a budget of $50 million. Streep received her 31st Golden Globe nomination and 21st Academy Award nomination for Best Actress.

Streep in 2018

In 2018, Streep briefly reprised her role in the musical sequel *[Mamma Mia! Here We Go Again](/wiki/Mamma_Mia!_Here_We_Go_Again)*. She also played a supporting part in Rob Marshall's *[Mary Poppins Returns](/wiki/Mary_Poppins_Returns)*, a musical sequel to the 1964 film *[Mary Poppins](/wiki/Mary_Poppins_(film))* starring [Emily Blunt](/wiki/Emily_Blunt) in the titular role. Streep next featured in her first main role in a television series by starring in the second season of the HBO drama series *[Big Little Lies](/wiki/Big_Little_Lies_(TV_series))* in 2019. She took on the part of Mary Louise Wright, the mother-in-law of [Nicole Kidman](/wiki/Nicole_Kidman)'s character. [Liane Moriarty](/wiki/Liane_Moriarty), author of the [novel of the same name](/wiki/Big_Little_Lies_(novel)), on which the first season is based, wrote a 200-page novella that served as the basis for the second season. Moriarty decided to name the new character Mary Louise, after Streep's legal name. Streep subsequently agreed to the part without reading a script for the first time in her career. Writing for the [BBC](/wiki/BBC), [Caryn James](/wiki/Caryn_James) labeled her performance "delicious and wily" and found her to be the "embodiment of a passive-aggressive granny". She received an [Emmy](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award_for_Outstanding_Supporting_Actress_in_a_Drama_Series) nomination for the show.

The same year, Streep then starred in the [Steven Soderbergh](/wiki/Steven_Soderbergh)-directed biographical comedy *[The Laundromat](/wiki/The_Laundromat_(2019_film))*, about the [Panama Papers](/wiki/Panama_Papers), opposite [Gary Oldman](/wiki/Gary_Oldman) and [Antonio Banderas](/wiki/Antonio_Banderas). It was the first movie distributed by [Netflix](/wiki/Netflix) in which Streep starred. She also played Aunt March in [Greta Gerwig](/wiki/Greta_Gerwig)'s *[Little Women](/wiki/Little_Women_(2019_film))*, co-starring with [Saoirse Ronan](/wiki/Saoirse_Ronan), [Emma Watson](/wiki/Emma_Watson), [Florence Pugh](/wiki/Florence_Pugh), [Timothée Chalamet](/wiki/Timoth%C3%A9e_Chalamet), and [Laura Dern](/wiki/Laura_Dern).
David Rooney of *[The Hollywood Reporter](/wiki/The_Hollywood_Reporter)* praised Streep's performance writing, "Streep is clearly having a ball as the imperious snob who snorts with disapproval...[and] does her best to hide her affection for her nieces behind her narrowed gaze and all-purpose disdain". The film received critical acclaim and grossed over $218 million against its $40 million budget.

## Other ventures

After Streep starred in *Mamma Mia!*, her rendition of the [titular song](/wiki/Mamma_Mia_(ABBA_song)) rose to popularity on the Portuguese music charts, where it peaked at number eight in October 2008. At the [35th People's Choice Awards](/wiki/35th_People%27s_Choice_Awards), her version of "Mamma Mia" won an award for "Favorite Song From A Soundtrack". In 2008, Streep was nominated for a [Grammy Award](/wiki/Grammy_Award) (her fifth nomination) for her work on the *Mamma Mia!* soundtrack. Streep has narrated numerous audio books, including three by children's book author [William Steig](/wiki/William_Steig): *Brae Irene*, *Spinky Sulks*, and *The One and Only [Shrek!](/wiki/Shrek!)*.

Streep is the spokesperson for the [National Women's History Museum](/wiki/National_Women%27s_History_Museum) (NWHM), to which she has made significant donations (including her fee for *The Iron Lady,* which was $1 million), and hosted numerous events. In 2026, Streep made a seven-figure donation to the NWHM. In recognition of her contribution the NWHM established the Meryl Streep Educator Award "honoring an exceptional educator each year who advances the teaching of women's history and expands access to these vital narratives in classrooms and communities nationwide."

On October 4, 2012, Streep donated $1 million to The Public Theater in honor of both its late founder, [Joseph Papp](/wiki/Joseph_Papp), and her friend, the author [Nora Ephron](/wiki/Nora_Ephron). She also supports [Gucci](/wiki/Gucci)'s "Chime for Change" campaign that aims to spread female empowerment.

In 2014, Streep established two scholarships for students at the [University of Massachusetts Lowell](/wiki/University_of_Massachusetts_Lowell) – the Meryl Streep Endowed Scholarship for English majors, and the Joan Hertzberg Endowed Scholarship (named for Streep's former classmate at [Vassar College](/wiki/Vassar_College)) for math majors.

In April 2015, it was announced that Streep had funded a screenwriters lab for female screenwriters over forty years old, called the Writers Lab, to be run by [New York Women in Film & Television](/wiki/New_York_Women_in_Film_%26_Television) and the collective IRIS. The Lab was the only one of its kind in the world for female screenwriters over forty years old. In 2015, Streep signed an open letter for which [One Campaign](/wiki/ONE_Campaign) had been collecting signatures; the letter was addressed to [Angela Merkel](/wiki/Angela_Merkel) and [Nkosazana Dlamini-Zuma](/wiki/Nkosazana_Dlamini-Zuma), urging them to focus on women as they served as heads of the G7 in Germany and the AU in South Africa, respectively, in setting development funding priorities. Also in 2015, Streep sent each member of the U.S. Congress a letter supporting the [Equal Rights Amendment](/wiki/Equal_Rights_Amendment). Each of her letters was sent with a copy of the book *Equal Means Equal: Why the Time for the ERA is Now* by [Jessica Neuwirth](/wiki/Jessica_Neuwirth), president of the ERA Coalition.

When asked in a 2015 interview with *[Time Out](/wiki/Time_Out_(magazine))* if she was a [feminist](/wiki/Feminism), Streep replied, "I am a [humanist](/wiki/Humanism), I am for nice easy balance." In March 2016, Streep, among others, signed a letter asking for gender equality throughout the world, in observance of [International Women's Day](/wiki/International_Women%27s_Day); this was also organized by One Campaign. In 2018, she collaborated with 300 women in Hollywood to set up the [Time's Up](/wiki/Time%27s_Up_(organization)) initiative to protect women from harassment and discrimination.

On April 25, 2017, Streep publicly backed the campaign to free [Oleg Sentsov](/wiki/Oleg_Sentsov), a Ukrainian filmmaker from Crimea who was subjected to a sham trial by Russia and jailed in Siberia for 20 years in August 2015. She was pictured alongside Ukrainian lawmaker [Mustafa Nayyem](/wiki/Mustafa_Nayyem) with a "Free Sentsov" sign in a photograph taken during the PEN America Annual Literary Gala on April 25, at which Sentsov was honoured with a 2017 PEN/Barbey Freedom to Write award.

## Reception and legacy

Streep receiving an honorary degree from [Harvard University](/wiki/Harvard_University) in 2010

In 2004, Streep was awarded the [AFI Life Achievement Award](/wiki/AFI_Life_Achievement_Award) by the [board of directors of the American Film Institute](/wiki/American_Film_Institute). In 2011, she received a [Kennedy Center Honors](/wiki/Kennedy_Center_Honors), introduced by [Tracey Ullman](/wiki/Tracey_Ullman), and speeches by 2009 Kennedy Center Honoree [Robert De Niro](/wiki/Robert_De_Niro) and 2003 Kennedy Center Honoree [Mike Nichols](/wiki/Mike_Nichols). Those also to honor Streep included, [Kevin Kline](/wiki/Kevin_Kline), [Emily Blunt](/wiki/Emily_Blunt), [Stanley Tucci](/wiki/Stanley_Tucci), and [Anne Hathaway](/wiki/Anne_Hathaway). The tribute ended with the whole cast who sang "She's My Pal", a play on "He's My Pal" from *[Ironweed](/wiki/Ironweed_(film))*.

In November 2014, [President Barack Obama](/wiki/President_Barack_Obama) bestowed upon Streep the [Presidential Medal of Freedom](/wiki/Presidential_Medal_of_Freedom), the nation's highest civilian honor. The citation reads as follows, "Meryl Streep is one of the most widely known and acclaimed actors in history. Ms. Streep has captured our imaginations with her unparalleled ability to portray a wide range of roles and attract an audience that has only grown over time, portraying characters who embody the full range of the human experience." In January 2017, [Viola Davis](/wiki/Viola_Davis) presented Streep with the [Cecil B. DeMille](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Cecil_B._DeMille_Award) at the [Golden Globes](/wiki/Golden_Globes). Davis stated to Streep "You make me proud to be an artist". In her acceptance speech, Streep quoted the recently departed [Carrie Fisher](/wiki/Carrie_Fisher), saying, "Take your broken heart and make it into art."

*[Vanity Fair](/wiki/Vanity_Fair_(magazine))* commented that "it's hard to imagine that there was a time before Meryl Streep was the greatest-living actress". Emma Brockes of *The Guardian* notes that despite Streep's being "one of the most famous actresses in the world", it is "strangely hard to pin an image on Streep", in a career where she has "laboured to establish herself as an actor whose roots lie in ordinary life". Despite her success, Streep has always been modest about her own acting and achievements in cinema. She has stated that she has no particular method when it comes to acting, learning from the days of her early studies that she cannot articulate her practice. She said in 1987, "I have a smattering of things I've learned from different teachers, but nothing I can put into a valise and open it up and say 'Now, which one would you like?' Nothing I can count on, and that makes it more dangerous. But then, the danger makes it more exciting." She has stated that her ideal director is one who gives her complete artistic control, allowing her to have a degree of improvisation and to learn from her mistakes.

> Women are better at acting than men. Why? Because we have to be. If successfully convincing somebody bigger than you of something he doesn't know is a survival skill, this is how women have survived through the millennia. Pretending is not just play. Pretending is imagined possibility. Pretending or acting is a very valuable life skill, and we all do it. All the time.

— Streep on acting

Streep is chameleon-like in her impersonation of characters, "subsuming herself into them, rather than personifying them". Early in her career, Streep was drawn to playing women who were difficult to like and lacked empathy. Streep has stated that many consider her to be a technical actor adding, "I come ready and I don't want to screw around and waste the first 10 takes on adjusting lighting and everybody else getting comfortable". Mike Nichols, who directed Streep in *Silkwood*, *Heartburn*, *Postcards from the Edge*, and *Angels in America*, praised Streep's ability to transform herself into her characters, remarking that, "In every role, she becomes a totally new human being. As she becomes the person she is portraying, the other performers begin to react to her as if she were that person." He said that directing her is "so much like falling in love that it has the characteristics of a time which you remember as magical, but which is shrouded in mystery". He also noted that Streep's acting ability had a profound impact on her co-stars, and that "one could improve by 1000% purely by watching her". Streep has at times infused a feminist point of view into her character portrayals; film critic [Molly Haskell](/wiki/Molly_Haskell) has stated, "None of her heroines are feminist, strictly speaking. Yet, they uncannily embody various crosscurrents of experience in the last twenty years, as women have re-defined themselves against the background of the women's movement".

Streep's star on the [Hollywood Walk of Fame](/wiki/Hollywood_Walk_of_Fame)

Streep is well known for her ability to imitate a wide range of accents – from Danish in *[Out of Africa](/wiki/Out_of_Africa_(film))* (1985) to British [Received Pronunciation](/wiki/Received_Pronunciation) in *[The French Lieutenant's Woman](/wiki/The_French_Lieutenant%27s_Woman_(film))* (1981), *[Plenty](/wiki/Plenty_(film))* (1985), and *[The Iron Lady](/wiki/The_Iron_Lady_(film))* (2011); Italian in *[The Bridges of Madison County](/wiki/The_Bridges_of_Madison_County_(film))* (1995); a southern American accent in *[The Seduction of Joe Tynan](/wiki/The_Seduction_of_Joe_Tynan)* (1979); a [Minnesota accent](/wiki/North-Central_American_English) in *[A Prairie Home Companion](/wiki/A_Prairie_Home_Companion_(film))* (2006); [Upstate New York](/wiki/Western_New_England_English) in *[Ironweed](/wiki/Ironweed_(film))* (1987); and a heavy [Bronx](/wiki/Bronx) accent in *[Doubt](/wiki/Doubt_(2008_film))* (2008). Streep has stated that she grew up listening to artists such as [Barbra Streisand](/wiki/Barbra_Streisand), [the Beatles](/wiki/The_Beatles), and [Bob Dylan](/wiki/Bob_Dylan), and she learned a lot about how to use her voice, her "instrument", by listening to Barbra Streisand's albums. In the film *[Evil Angels](/wiki/Evil_Angels_(film))* (1988, released in the U.S. as *A Cry in the Dark*), in which she portrays a New Zealand transplant to Australia, Streep developed a hybrid of Australian and New Zealand English. Her performance received the [Australian Film Institute Award for Best Actress in a Leading Role](/wiki/Australian_Film_Institute_Award_for_Best_Actress_in_a_Leading_Role), as well as [Best Actress](/wiki/Prix_d%27interpr%C3%A9tation_f%C3%A9minine) at the [Cannes Film Festival](/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival), and the [New York Film Critics Circle Award for Best Actress](/wiki/New_York_Film_Critics_Circle_Award_for_Best_Actress).

For her role in the film *[Sophie's Choice](/wiki/Sophie%27s_Choice_(film))* (1982), Streep spoke both English and German with a Polish accent, as well as Polish itself. In *The Iron Lady*, she reproduced the vocal style of [Margaret Thatcher](/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher) from the time before Thatcher became Britain's Prime Minister, and after she had taken elocution lessons to change her pitch, pronunciation, and delivery. Streep has commented that using accents as part of her acting is a technique she views as an obvious requirement in her portrayal of a character.
When questioned in [Belfast](/wiki/Belfast) as to how she reproduces different accents, Streep replied in a reportedly "perfect" Belfast accent: "I listen."

## Activism and advocacy

Streep receiving the [Presidential Medal of Freedom](/wiki/Presidential_Medal_of_Freedom) from [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama) in 2014

Politically, Streep has described herself as part of the [American Left](/wiki/American_Left). She gave a speech at the [2016 Democratic National Convention](/wiki/2016_Democratic_National_Convention) in support of presidential nominee [Hillary Clinton](/wiki/Hillary_Clinton). In January 2017, she received the [Cecil B. DeMille Award for Lifetime Achievement](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Cecil_B._DeMille_Award) at the [74th Golden Globe Awards](/wiki/74th_Golden_Globe_Awards). During her acceptance speech, she criticized the conduct of then-President-elect Donald Trump, referring to him as "the person asking to sit in the most respected seat in our country." She specifically condemned his public mocking of disabled reporter Serge F. Kovaleski as an abuse of "privilege, power, and the capacity to fight back." Streep argued that such an "instinct to humiliate" from a powerful figure "filters down into everybody's life" and "gives permission for other people to do the same thing." The speech drew widespread media attention and became a focal point of national political discourse.

Streep has also focused her advocacy on systemic industry issues and labor rights. While promoting *[Suffragette](/wiki/Suffragette_(film))* in 2015, she challenged the [male gaze](/wiki/Male_gaze) in film criticism, citing a ratio of 760 men to 168 women among [Rotten Tomatoes](/wiki/Rotten_Tomatoes) contributors. She argued that this imbalance creates a skewed consensus that adversely affects the commercial performance and perceived authenticity of female-driven narratives. In June 2023, she was among over 300 high-profile members of [SAG-AFTRA](/wiki/SAG-AFTRA) who signed a letter threatening to strike to secure a "transformative" deal protecting performers' craft and likenesses from the unregulated rise of [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence).

## Personal life

### Relationships and family

Streep met actor [John Cazale](/wiki/John_Cazale) in 1976, when they were both in a production of William Shakespeare's *Measure for Measure* at the Delacorte Theater. They were living together when he was diagnosed with [lung cancer](/wiki/Lung_cancer) in 1977; Streep cared for him until he died in March 1978. Streep said of his death:

> I didn't get over it. I don't want to get over it. No matter what you do, the pain is always there in some recess of your mind, and it affects everything that happens afterwards. I think you can assimilate the pain and go on without making an obsession of it.

Streep married sculptor [Don Gummer](/wiki/Don_Gummer) on September 30, 1978, six months after Cazale's death. They have four children: musician [Henry Wolfe Gummer](/wiki/Henry_Wolfe) (born 1979), and actresses [Mary Willa "Mamie" Gummer](/wiki/Mamie_Gummer) (born 1983), [Grace Jane Gummer](/wiki/Grace_Gummer) (born 1986), and [Louisa Jacobson Gummer](/wiki/Louisa_Gummer) (born 1991). In 1985, the family moved into a $1.8-million private estate in Connecticut and lived there until they bought a $3-million mansion in [Brentwood, Los Angeles](/wiki/Brentwood,_Los_Angeles), in 1990. They later moved back to [Connecticut](/wiki/Connecticut). In 2023, it was reported that Streep and Gummer had been separated for more than six years. They were publicly last seen together at the [90th Academy Awards](/wiki/90th_Academy_Awards) in 2018.

Streep is the godmother of [Billie Lourd](/wiki/Billie_Lourd), daughter of the late actress [Carrie Fisher](/wiki/Carrie_Fisher), who was a close friend to Streep. Fisher wrote the screenplay for Streep's 1990 film *Postcards from the Edge,* based on [Fisher's book](/wiki/Postcards_from_the_Edge).

### Beliefs

When asked if religion plays a part in her life in 2009, Streep replied: "I follow no doctrine. I don't belong to a church or a temple or a synagogue or an [ashram](/wiki/Ashram)." In an interview in December 2008, she alluded to her lack of religious belief when she said:

> So, I've always been really, deeply interested because I think I can understand the solace that's available in the whole construct of religion. But I really don't believe in the power of prayer, or things would have been avoided that have happened, that are awful. So, it's a horrible position as an intelligent, emotional, yearning human being to sit outside of the available comfort there. But I just can't go there.

When asked where she draws consolation in the face of aging and death, Streep responded:

> Consolation? I'm not sure I have it. I have a belief, I guess, in the power of the aggregate human attempt – the best of ourselves. In love and hope and optimism – you know, the magic things that seem inexplicable. Why we are the way we are. I do have a sense of trying to make things better. Where does that come from?

## Acting credits and awards

Main articles: [Meryl Streep on screen and stage](/wiki/Meryl_Streep_on_screen_and_stage) and [List of awards and nominations received by Meryl Streep](/wiki/List_of_awards_and_nominations_received_by_Meryl_Streep)

One of the most prolific actresses of [screen and stage](/wiki/Meryl_Streep_on_screen_and_stage) since her career's inception in the late 1970s, Streep's most acclaimed and highest-grossing films, according to the review aggregator site [Rotten Tomatoes](/wiki/Rotten_Tomatoes), include *[Julia](/wiki/Julia_(1977_film))* (1977), *[The Deer Hunter](/wiki/The_Deer_Hunter)* (1978), *[Kramer vs. Kramer](/wiki/Kramer_vs._Kramer)* (1979), *[The French Lieutenant's Woman](/wiki/The_French_Lieutenant%27s_Woman_(film))* (1981), *[Sophie's Choice](/wiki/Sophie%27s_Choice_(film))* (1982), *[Silkwood](/wiki/Silkwood)* (1983), *[A Cry in the Dark](/wiki/Evil_Angels_(film))* (1988), *[Postcards from the Edge](/wiki/Postcards_from_the_Edge_(film))* (1990), *[Defending Your Life](/wiki/Defending_Your_Life)* (1991), *[The Bridges of Madison County](/wiki/The_Bridges_of_Madison_County_(film))* (1995), *[Marvin's Room](/wiki/Marvin%27s_Room_(film))* (1996), *[Adaptation](/wiki/Adaptation_(film))* (2002), *[The Devil Wears Prada](/wiki/The_Devil_Wears_Prada_(film))* (2007), *[Mamma Mia!](/wiki/Mamma_Mia!_(film))* (2008), *[Fantastic Mr. Fox](/wiki/Fantastic_Mr._Fox_(film))* (2009), *[The Homesman](/wiki/The_Homesman)* (2014), *[Florence Foster Jenkins](/wiki/Florence_Foster_Jenkins_(film))* (2016), *[Little Women](/wiki/Little_Women_(2019_film))* (2019), and *[Let Them All Talk](/wiki/Let_Them_All_Talk)* (2020). Her television projects include the miniseries *[Holocaust](/wiki/Holocaust_(miniseries))* (1978), the television film *[...First Do No Harm](/wiki/...First_Do_No_Harm)* (1997), the miniseries *[Angels in America](/wiki/Angels_in_America_(miniseries))* (2003), and the drama series *[Big Little Lies](/wiki/Big_Little_Lies_(TV_series))* (2019). Her stage roles include the [Broadway theatre](/wiki/Broadway_theatre) productions *[A Memory of Two Mondays](/wiki/A_Memory_of_Two_Mondays)*, *[27 Wagons Full of Cotton](/wiki/27_Wagons_Full_of_Cotton)* (both 1976) and *[The Cherry Orchard](/wiki/The_Cherry_Orchard)* (1977), as well as multiple plays at the [Delacorte Theater](/wiki/Delacorte_Theater).

These nominations make Streep the [most Academy Award-nominated performer in history](/wiki/List_of_actors_with_two_or_more_Academy_Award_nominations_in_acting_categories), with 21 in total (17 for [Best Actress](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Actress) and four for [Best Supporting Actress](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Supporting_Actress)), as well as [one of only 13 performers to win an Oscar in both acting categories](/wiki/List_of_actors_with_two_or_more_Academy_Awards_in_acting_categories) and one of only three performers to win three Academy Awards across the two acting categories (with [Ingrid Bergman](/wiki/Ingrid_Bergman) and [Jack Nicholson](/wiki/Jack_Nicholson) being the only others to achieve this feat).

She has also received six [Grammy Award](/wiki/Grammy_Award) nominations, six [Primetime Emmy Award](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award) nominations (with three wins plus a [Children's and Family Emmy Award](/wiki/Children%27s_and_Family_Emmy_Awards) win), and one [Tony Award](/wiki/Tony_Award) nomination. Streep is one of few performers to be nominated for the [Triple Crown of Acting](/wiki/Triple_Crown_of_Acting#Missing_a_Tony_Award) and [EGOT](/wiki/List_of_EGOT_winners#Four_nominations). Her [other accolades](/wiki/List_of_awards_and_nominations_received_by_Meryl_Streep) include two [BAFTA Awards for Best Actress in a Leading Role](/wiki/BAFTA_Award_for_Best_Actress_in_a_Leading_Role) (for *The French Lieutenant's Woman* and *The Iron Lady*), nine [Golden Globe Awards](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Awards) (as well as the honorary [Cecil B. DeMille Award](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Cecil_B._DeMille_Award)), a [Silver Bear](/wiki/Silver_Bear_for_Best_Actress) and [Honorary Golden Bear](/wiki/Honorary_Golden_Bear) in [Berlin International Film Festival](/wiki/Berlin_International_Film_Festival), [Best Actress](/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival_Award_for_Best_Actress), [Honorary Palme d'Or](/wiki/Palme_d%27Or) in [Cannes Film Festival](/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival) and two [Screen Actors Guild Awards](/wiki/Screen_Actors_Guild_Awards).
