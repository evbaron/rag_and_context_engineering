A **nuclear weapon** is an [explosive device](/wiki/Explosive_device) that derives its destructive force from [nuclear reactions](/wiki/Nuclear_reaction), either [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission) (fission or atomic bomb) or a combination of fission and [nuclear fusion](/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) reactions ([thermonuclear weapon](/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon)), producing a [nuclear explosion](/wiki/Nuclear_explosion). Both bomb types release large quantities of [energy](/wiki/Energy) from relatively small amounts of [matter](/wiki/Matter). [Nine sovereign states](/wiki/List_of_states_with_nuclear_weapons) are believed to possess nuclear weapons as of 2026: [United States](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_the_United_States), [Russia](/wiki/Russia_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), the [United Kingdom](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_the_United_Kingdom), [France](/wiki/France_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [China](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_China), [India](/wiki/India_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) and [Israel](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_and_Israel).

The majority of nuclear weapons have [energy yields](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon_yield) between 100 and 1,000 [kilotons of TNT](/wiki/Kilotons_of_TNT). Yields in the low kilotons can [destroy cities](/wiki/Indiscriminate_attack). The [effects of nuclear weapons](/wiki/Effects_of_nuclear_weapons) include extreme [blast damage](/wiki/Effects_of_nuclear_explosions#Blast_damage), [heat](/wiki/Thermal_radiation), [ionizing radiation](/wiki/Ionizing_radiation), and [firestorms](/wiki/Firestorm), followed by radioactive [nuclear fallout](/wiki/Nuclear_fallout), an [electromagnetic pulse](/wiki/Nuclear_electromagnetic_pulse), [radar blackout](/wiki/Radar_blackout), and [acute](/wiki/Acute_radiation_syndrome) or [chronic radiation syndrome](/wiki/Chronic_radiation_syndrome) in humans and animals. Hundreds of nuclear explosions would likely cause [nuclear winter](/wiki/Nuclear_winter) and [nuclear famine](/wiki/Nuclear_famine).

The first nuclear weapons were developed by the United States in collaboration with the United Kingdom and [Canada](/wiki/Canada_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II) in the [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project). Production requires a large scientific-industrial complex, primarily for [fissile material](/wiki/Fissile_material), either [nuclear reactors](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor) and [reprocessing plants](/wiki/Nuclear_reprocessing) producing [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium), or [uranium enrichment](/wiki/Enriched_uranium) facilities. Nuclear weapons have been used twice in [war](/wiki/War), in the 1945 [atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki](/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki) that killed between 150,000 and 246,000 people. [Nuclear deterrence](/wiki/Deterrence_theory), including [mutually assured destruction](/wiki/Mutually_assured_destruction), aims to prevent [nuclear warfare](/wiki/Nuclear_warfare) via the threat of unacceptable damage and the danger of escalation to [nuclear holocaust](/wiki/Nuclear_holocaust). A [nuclear arms race](/wiki/Nuclear_arms_race) for weapons and their delivery systems was a defining component of the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War). Nuclear weapons have had yields between 50 megatons for the [Tsar Bomba](/wiki/Tsar_Bomba), and 10 tons for the [W54](/wiki/W54).

[Strategic nuclear weapons](/wiki/Strategic_nuclear_weapon) are targeted against civilian, industrial, and military infrastructure, while [tactical nuclear weapons](/wiki/Tactical_nuclear_weapons) are intended for battlefield use. Strategic weapons led to the development of dedicated [intercontinental ballistic missiles](/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile), [submarine-launched ballistic missile](/wiki/Submarine-launched_ballistic_missile), and nuclear [strategic bombers](/wiki/Strategic_bomber), collectively known as the [nuclear triad](/wiki/Nuclear_triad). Tactical weapons options included shorter-range ground-, air-, and sea-launched missiles, [artillery](/wiki/Nuclear_artillery), [demolition munitions](/wiki/Atomic_demolition_munition), [torpedoes](/wiki/Nuclear_torpedo), and [depth charges](/wiki/Nuclear_depth_charge), but they have become less salient since the end of the Cold War.

Two nuclear-armed states have [nuclear sharing](/wiki/Nuclear_sharing) arrangements, stationing their weapons in six other countries: [US weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_the_United_States) are deployed in [Belgium](/wiki/Belgium_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [Germany](/wiki/Germany_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [Italy](/wiki/Italian_nuclear_weapons_program#Nuclear_weapons_in_Italy_since_1975), [Netherlands](/wiki/Netherlands_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), and [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction); [Russian weapons](/wiki/Russia_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) are deployed in [Belarus](/wiki/Belarus_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction). Roughly 40 countries have defense policies based on nuclear deterrence, including over 30 non-nuclear-weapon states under extended deterrence or [nuclear umbrella](/wiki/Nuclear_umbrella) agreements such as the [North Atlantic Treaty Organization](/wiki/North_Atlantic_Treaty_Organization). Nuclear weapons are [weapons of mass destruction](/wiki/Weapons_of_mass_destruction), and their control is a focus of [international security](/wiki/International_security) through measures to prevent [nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation), [arms control](/wiki/Arms_control), or [nuclear disarmament](/wiki/Nuclear_disarmament). The total global stockpile peaked at over 64,000 weapons in 1986, and is around 9,600 as of 2025. Key international agreements and organizations include the [Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Non-Proliferation_of_Nuclear_Weapons), the [Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty](/wiki/Comprehensive_Nuclear-Test-Ban_Treaty) and [Organization](/wiki/Comprehensive_Nuclear-Test-Ban_Treaty_Organization), the [International Atomic Energy Agency](/wiki/International_Atomic_Energy_Agency), [nuclear-weapon-free zones](/wiki/Nuclear-weapon-free_zone), and the [Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Prohibition_of_Nuclear_Weapons).

## Testing and deployment

Nuclear weapons have only twice been used in warfare, both times by the [United States](/wiki/United_States) against [Japan](/wiki/Empire_of_Japan) at the end of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II). On 6 August 1945, the [United States Army Air Forces](/wiki/United_States_Army_Air_Forces) (USAAF) detonated a [uranium](/wiki/Uranium) gun-type [fission bomb](/wiki/Fission_bomb) nicknamed "[Little Boy](/wiki/Little_Boy)" over the Japanese city of [Hiroshima](/wiki/Hiroshima); three days later, on 9 August, the USAAF detonated a [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium) implosion-type fission bomb nicknamed "[Fat Man](/wiki/Fat_Man)" over the Japanese city of [Nagasaki](/wiki/Nagasaki). These bombings caused injuries that resulted in the deaths of approximately 200,000 [civilians](/wiki/Civilian) and [military personnel](/wiki/Military_personnel). The ethics of these bombings and their role in [Japan's surrender](/wiki/Surrender_of_Japan) are to this day, still [subjects of debate](/wiki/Debate_over_the_atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki).

Since the [atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki](/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki), nuclear weapons have been detonated over 2,000 times for [testing](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_testing) and demonstration. Only [a few nations](/wiki/List_of_states_with_nuclear_weapons) possess such weapons or are suspected of seeking them. The only countries known to have detonated nuclear weapons—and acknowledge possessing them—are (chronologically by date of first test) the [United States](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_the_United_States), the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union) (succeeded as a nuclear power by [Russia](/wiki/Russia_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction)), the [United Kingdom](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_the_United_Kingdom), [France](/wiki/France_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [China](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_China), [India](/wiki/India_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) and [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction). [Israel](/wiki/Israel_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) is believed to possess nuclear weapons, though, in a [policy of deliberate ambiguity](/wiki/Policy_of_deliberate_ambiguity), it does not acknowledge having them. [Germany](/wiki/Germany_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [Italy](/wiki/Italian_nuclear_weapons_program#Nuclear_weapons_in_Italy_since_1975), [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [Netherlands](/wiki/Netherlands_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [Belgium](/wiki/Belgium_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), and [Belarus](/wiki/Belarus_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) are [nuclear weapons sharing](/wiki/Nuclear_sharing) states. [South Africa](/wiki/South_Africa) is the only country to have [independently developed](/wiki/South_Africa_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) and then [renounced and dismantled](/wiki/South_Africa_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction#Dismantling) its nuclear weapons.

On the 30 October 2025, US President [Donald Trump](/wiki/Donald_Trump) called for renewed nuclear weapons testing in order to keep pace with other nuclear armed states such as [Russia](/wiki/Russia) and [China](/wiki/China), but did not make clear whether he referred to [nuclear explosive testing](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_testing) or testing of [delivery systems](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_delivery) for nuclear warheads.

## Types

Main article: [Nuclear weapon design](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon_design)

The [Trinity test](/wiki/Trinity_(nuclear_test)) of the [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project) was the first detonation of a nuclear weapon, which led [J. Robert Oppenheimer](/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer) to recall verses from the [Hindu](/wiki/Hindu) scripture *[Bhagavad Gita](/wiki/Bhagavad_Gita)*: "If the radiance of a thousand suns were to burst at once into the sky, that would be like the splendor of the mighty one" ... "I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds".

There are two basic types of nuclear weapons: those that derive the majority of their energy from [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission) reactions alone, and those that use fission reactions to begin [nuclear fusion](/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) reactions that produce a large amount of the total energy output.

### Fission weapons

The two basic [fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission) weapon designs

All existing nuclear weapons derive some of their explosive energy from nuclear fission reactions. Weapons whose explosive output is exclusively from fission reactions are commonly referred to as **atomic bombs** or **atom bombs** (abbreviated as **A-bombs**). This has long been noted as something of a [misnomer](/wiki/Misnomer), as their energy comes from the [nucleus](/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) of the atom, just as it does with fusion weapons.

In fission weapons, a mass of [fissile material](/wiki/Fissile_material) ([enriched uranium](/wiki/Enriched_uranium) or [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium)) is forced into [supercriticality](/wiki/Critical_mass)—allowing an [exponential growth](/wiki/Exponential_growth) of [nuclear chain reactions](/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction)—either by shooting one piece of sub-critical material into another (the "gun" method) or by compression of a sub-critical sphere or cylinder of fissile material using chemically fueled [explosive lenses](/wiki/Explosive_lens). The latter approach, the "implosion" method, is more sophisticated and more efficient (smaller, less massive, and requiring less of the expensive fissile fuel) than the former.

A major challenge in all nuclear weapon designs is to ensure that a significant fraction of the fuel is consumed before the weapon destroys itself. The amount of energy released by fission bombs can range from the equivalent of just under a ton to upwards of 500,000 tons (500 [kilotons](/wiki/Kiloton)) of [TNT](/wiki/TNT) (4.2 to 2.1×106 GJ).

All fission reactions generate [fission products](/wiki/Nuclear_fission_product), the remains of the split atomic nuclei. Many fission products are either highly [radioactive](/wiki/Radioactive_decay) (but short-lived) or moderately radioactive (but long-lived), and as such, they are a serious form of [radioactive contamination](/wiki/Radioactive_contamination). Fission products are the principal radioactive component of [nuclear fallout](/wiki/Nuclear_fallout). Another source of radioactivity is the burst of free neutrons produced by the weapon. When they collide with other nuclei in the surrounding material, the neutrons transmute those nuclei into other isotopes, altering their stability and making them radioactive.

The most commonly used fissile materials for nuclear weapons applications have been [uranium-235](/wiki/Uranium-235) and [plutonium-239](/wiki/Plutonium-239). Less commonly used has been [uranium-233](/wiki/Uranium-233). [Neptunium-237](/wiki/Neptunium-237) and some isotopes of [americium](/wiki/Americium) may be usable for nuclear explosives as well, but it is not clear that this has ever been implemented, and their plausible use in nuclear weapons is a matter of dispute.

### Fusion weapons

Main article: [Thermonuclear weapon](/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon)

The basics of the [Teller–Ulam design](/wiki/Teller%E2%80%93Ulam_design) for a hydrogen bomb: a fission bomb uses radiation to compress and heat a separate section of fusion fuel.

The other basic type of nuclear weapon produces a large proportion of its energy in nuclear fusion reactions. Such fusion weapons are generally referred to as **[thermonuclear weapons](/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon)** or more colloquially as **hydrogen bombs** (abbreviated as **H-bombs**), as they rely on fusion reactions between isotopes of [hydrogen](/wiki/Hydrogen) ([deuterium](/wiki/Deuterium) and [tritium](/wiki/Tritium)). All such weapons derive a significant portion of their energy from fission reactions used to "trigger" fusion reactions, and fusion reactions can themselves trigger additional fission reactions.

Only six countries—the [United States](/wiki/United_States), [Russia](/wiki/Russia), the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom), [China](/wiki/China), [France](/wiki/France), and [India](/wiki/India)—have conducted thermonuclear weapon tests. Whether India has detonated a "true" multi-staged [thermonuclear weapon](/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon) is controversial. [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea) claims to have tested a fusion weapon as of January 2016, though this claim is disputed. Thermonuclear weapons are considered much more difficult to successfully design and execute than primitive fission weapons. Almost all deployed nuclear weapons use the thermonuclear design because it results in an explosion hundreds of times stronger than that of a fission bomb of similar weight.

Thermonuclear bombs use the energy of a fission bomb to compress and heat fusion fuel. In the [Teller-Ulam design](/wiki/Teller-Ulam_design), which accounts for all multi-megaton yield hydrogen bombs, this is accomplished by placing a fission bomb and fusion fuel ([tritium](/wiki/Tritium), [deuterium](/wiki/Deuterium), or [lithium deuteride](/wiki/Lithium_deuteride)) in proximity within a special, radiation-reflecting container. When the fission bomb is detonated, [gamma rays](/wiki/Gamma_ray) and [X-rays](/wiki/X-ray) emitted first compress the fusion fuel, then heat it to thermonuclear temperatures. The ensuing fusion reaction creates enormous numbers of high-speed [neutrons](/wiki/Neutron), which can then induce fission in materials not normally prone to it, such as [depleted uranium](/wiki/Depleted_uranium). Each of these components is known as a "stage", with the fission bomb as the "primary" and the fusion capsule as the "secondary". In large, megaton-range hydrogen bombs, about half of the yield comes from the final fissioning of depleted uranium.

Virtually all deployed thermonuclear weapons use this "two-stage" design, but it is possible to add additional fusion stages—each stage igniting a larger amount of fusion fuel in the next stage. This technique can be used to construct thermonuclear weapons of arbitrarily large yield. This is in contrast to fission bombs, which are limited in their explosive power due to [criticality](/wiki/Nuclear_criticality_safety) danger (premature nuclear chain reaction caused by too-large amounts of pre-assembled fissile fuel). The largest nuclear weapon ever detonated, the [Tsar Bomba](/wiki/Tsar_Bomba) of the USSR, which released an energy equivalent of over 50 megatons of TNT (210 PJ), was a three-stage weapon. Most thermonuclear weapons are considerably smaller than this, due to practical constraints from missile warhead space and weight requirements. In the early 1950s the [Livermore Laboratory](/wiki/Lawrence_Livermore_National_Laboratory) in the United States had plans for the testing of two massive bombs, Gnomon and [Sundial](/wiki/Sundial_(weapon)), 1 gigaton of TNT and 10 gigatons of TNT respectively.

[Edward Teller](/wiki/Edward_Teller), often referred to as the "father of the hydrogen bomb"

Fusion reactions do not create fission products, and thus contribute far less to the creation of [nuclear fallout](/wiki/Nuclear_fallout) than fission reactions, but because all [thermonuclear weapons](/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon) contain at least one [fission](/wiki/Fission_barrier) stage, and many high-yield thermonuclear devices have a final fission stage, thermonuclear weapons can generate at least as much nuclear fallout as fission-only weapons. Furthermore, high yield thermonuclear explosions (most dangerously ground bursts) have the force to lift radioactive debris upwards past the [tropopause](/wiki/Tropopause) into the [stratosphere](/wiki/Stratosphere), where the calm non-turbulent winds permit the debris to travel great distances from the burst, eventually settling and unpredictably contaminating areas far removed from the target of the explosion.

### Other types

Main articles: [Boosted fission weapon](/wiki/Boosted_fission_weapon), [Neutron bomb](/wiki/Neutron_bomb), [Radiological warfare](/wiki/Radiological_warfare), [Induced gamma emission](/wiki/Induced_gamma_emission), and [Antimatter weapon](/wiki/Antimatter_weapon)

There are other types of nuclear weapons as well. For example, a [boosted fission weapon](/wiki/Boosted_fission_weapon) is a fission bomb that increases its explosive yield through a small number of fusion reactions, but it is not a fusion bomb. In the boosted bomb, the neutrons produced by the fusion reactions serve primarily to increase the efficiency of the fission bomb. There are two types of boosted fission bomb: internally boosted, in which a deuterium-tritium mixture is injected into the bomb core, and externally boosted, in which concentric shells of lithium-deuteride and depleted uranium are layered on the outside of the fission bomb core. The external method of boosting enabled the [USSR](/wiki/USSR) to field the first partially thermonuclear weapons, but it is considered obsolete because it demands a spherical bomb geometry, which was adequate during the 1950s arms race when bomber aircraft were the only available delivery vehicles.

The detonation of any nuclear weapon is accompanied by a blast of [neutron radiation](/wiki/Neutron_radiation). Surrounding a nuclear weapon with suitable materials (such as [cobalt](/wiki/Cobalt) or [gold](/wiki/Gold)) creates a weapon known as a [salted bomb](/wiki/Salted_bomb). This device can produce exceptionally large quantities of long-lived [radioactive contamination](/wiki/Radioactive_contamination). It has been conjectured that such a device could serve as a "doomsday weapon" because such a large quantity of radioactivities with half-lives of decades, lifted into the stratosphere where winds would distribute it around the globe, would make all life on the planet extinct.

In connection with the [Strategic Defense Initiative](/wiki/Strategic_Defense_Initiative), research into the [nuclear pumped laser](/wiki/Nuclear_pumped_laser) was conducted under the DOD program [Project Excalibur](/wiki/Project_Excalibur) but this did not result in a working weapon. The concept involves the tapping of the energy of an exploding nuclear bomb to power a single-shot laser that is directed at a distant target.

During the [Starfish Prime](/wiki/Starfish_Prime) high-altitude nuclear test in 1962, an unexpected effect was produced which is called a [nuclear electromagnetic pulse](/wiki/Nuclear_electromagnetic_pulse). This is an intense flash of electromagnetic energy produced by a rain of high-energy electrons which in turn are produced by a nuclear bomb's gamma rays. This flash of energy can permanently destroy or disrupt electronic equipment if insufficiently shielded. It has been proposed to use this effect to disable an enemy's military and civilian infrastructure as an adjunct to other nuclear or conventional military operations. By itself it could as well be useful to terrorists for crippling a nation's economic electronics-based infrastructure. Because the effect is most effectively produced by high altitude nuclear detonations (by military weapons delivered by air, though ground bursts also produce EMP effects over a localized area), it can produce damage to electronics over a wide, even continental, geographical area.

Research has been done into the possibility of [pure fusion bombs](/wiki/Pure_fusion_weapon): nuclear weapons that consist of fusion reactions without requiring a fission bomb to initiate them. Such a device might provide a simpler path to thermonuclear weapons than one that required the development of fission weapons first, and pure fusion weapons would create significantly less nuclear fallout than other thermonuclear weapons because they would not disperse fission products. In 1998, the [United States Department of Energy](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy) divulged that the United States had "...made a substantial investment," in the past to develop pure fusion weapons, but that "The U.S. does not have and is not developing a pure fusion weapon," and that "No credible design for a pure fusion weapon resulted from the DOE investment".

[Nuclear isomers](/wiki/Nuclear_isomers) provide a possible pathway to fissionless fusion bombs. These are naturally occurring [isotopes](/wiki/Isotopes) ([178m2Hf](/wiki/Isotopes_of_hafnium) being a prominent example) which exist in an elevated energy state. Mechanisms to release this energy as bursts of gamma radiation (as in the [hafnium controversy](/wiki/Hafnium_controversy)) have been proposed as possible triggers for conventional thermonuclear reactions.

[Antimatter](/wiki/Antimatter), which consists of [particles](/wiki/Particles) resembling ordinary [matter](/wiki/Matter) particles in most of their properties but having opposite [electric charge](/wiki/Electric_charge), has been considered as a trigger mechanism for nuclear weapons. A major obstacle is the difficulty of producing antimatter in large enough quantities, and there is no evidence that it is feasible beyond the military domain. However, the US Air Force funded studies of the physics of antimatter in the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War), and began considering its possible use in weapons, not just as a trigger, but as the explosive itself. A fourth generation nuclear weapon design is related to, and relies upon, the same principle as [antimatter-catalyzed nuclear pulse propulsion](/wiki/Antimatter-catalyzed_nuclear_pulse_propulsion).

Most variation in [nuclear weapon design](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon_design) is for the purpose of achieving [different yields for different situations](/wiki/Dial-a-yield), and in manipulating design elements to attempt to minimize weapon size, [radiation hardness](/wiki/Nuclear_fratricide) or requirements for special materials, especially fissile fuel or tritium.

#### Tactical nuclear weapons

Soviet [OTR-21 Tochka](/wiki/OTR-21_Tochka) missile. Capable of firing a 100-kiloton nuclear warhead a distance of 185 km

Some nuclear weapons are designed for special purposes; most of these are for non-strategic (decisively war-winning) purposes and are referred to as [tactical nuclear weapons](/wiki/Tactical_nuclear_weapon).

The [neutron bomb](/wiki/Neutron_bomb) purportedly conceived by [Sam Cohen](/wiki/Samuel_T._Cohen) is a thermonuclear weapon that yields a relatively small explosion but a relatively large amount of neutron [radiation](/wiki/Radiation). Such a weapon could, according to tacticians, be used to cause massive biological casualties while leaving inanimate infrastructure mostly intact and creating minimal fallout. Because high energy neutrons are capable of penetrating dense matter, such as tank armor, neutron warheads were procured in the 1980s (though not deployed in Europe) for use as tactical payloads for US Army artillery shells (200 mm [W79](/wiki/W79_Artillery-Fired_Atomic_Projectile) and 155 mm [W82](/wiki/W82)) and [short range missile](/wiki/MGM-52_Lance) forces. Soviet authorities announced similar intentions for neutron warhead deployment in Europe; indeed, they claimed to have originally invented the neutron bomb, but their deployment on USSR tactical nuclear forces is unverifiable.

A type of nuclear explosive most suitable for use by ground special forces was the [Special Atomic Demolition Munition](/wiki/Special_Atomic_Demolition_Munition), or SADM, sometimes popularly known as a [suitcase nuke](/wiki/Suitcase_nuclear_device). This is a nuclear bomb that is man-portable, or at least truck-portable, and though of a relatively small yield (one or two kilotons) is sufficient to destroy important tactical targets such as bridges, dams, tunnels, important military or commercial installations, etc. either behind enemy lines or pre-emptively on friendly territory soon to be overtaken by invading enemy forces. These weapons require plutonium fuel and are particularly "dirty". They also demand especially stringent security precautions in their storage and deployment.

Small "tactical" nuclear weapons were deployed for use as antiaircraft weapons. Examples include the USAF [AIR-2 Genie](/wiki/AIR-2_Genie), the [AIM-26 Falcon](/wiki/AIM-26_Falcon) and US Army [Nike Hercules](/wiki/Nike_Hercules). Missile interceptors such as the [Sprint](/wiki/Sprint_(missile)) and the [Spartan](/wiki/LIM-49_Spartan) also used small nuclear warheads (optimized to produce neutron or X-ray flux) but were for use against enemy strategic warheads.

Other small, or tactical, nuclear weapons were deployed by naval forces for use primarily as [antisubmarine](/wiki/Antisubmarine) weapons. These included nuclear [depth bombs](/wiki/Depth_charge) or nuclear armed torpedoes. Nuclear mines for use on land or at sea are also possibilities.

## Weapons delivery

See also: [Nuclear weapons delivery](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_delivery), [Nuclear triad](/wiki/Nuclear_triad), [Strategic bomber](/wiki/Strategic_bomber), [Intercontinental ballistic missile](/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile), and [Submarine-launched ballistic missile](/wiki/Submarine-launched_ballistic_missile)

The first nuclear weapons were [gravity bombs](/wiki/Gravity_bomb), such as the "[Fat Man](/wiki/Fat_Man)" weapon dropped on [Nagasaki](/wiki/Nagasaki,_Nagasaki), Japan. They were large and could only be delivered by [heavy bomber](/wiki/Heavy_bomber) aircraft

A demilitarized, [commercial launch](/wiki/Dnepr_(rocket)) of the Russian [Strategic Rocket Forces](/wiki/Strategic_Rocket_Forces) R-36 [ICBM](/wiki/ICBM); also known by the NATO reporting name: [SS-18 Satan](/wiki/SS-18_Satan). Upon its first fielding in the late 1960s, the SS-18 remains the single highest [throw weight](/wiki/Throw_weight) missile delivery system ever built.

The system used to [deliver](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_delivery) a nuclear weapon to its target is an important factor affecting both [nuclear weapon design](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon_design) and [nuclear strategy](/wiki/Nuclear_warfare). The design, development, and maintenance of delivery systems are among the most expensive parts of a nuclear weapons program; they account, for example, for 57% of the financial resources spent by the United States on nuclear weapons projects since 1940.

### Gravity bomb

Main article: [Nuclear gravity bomb](/wiki/Nuclear_gravity_bomb)

The simplest method for delivering a nuclear weapon is a [gravity bomb](/wiki/Gravity_bomb) dropped from [aircraft](/wiki/Aircraft); this was the method used by the [United States against Japan](/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki) in 1945. This method places few restrictions on the size of the weapon. It does, however, limit attack range, response time to an impending attack, and the number of weapons that a country can field at the same time. With miniaturization, nuclear bombs can be delivered by both [strategic bombers](/wiki/Strategic_bomber) and tactical [fighter-bombers](/wiki/Fighter-bomber). This method is the primary means of nuclear weapons delivery; the majority of US nuclear warheads, for example, are free-fall gravity bombs, namely the [B61](/wiki/B61_nuclear_bomb), which is being improved upon to this day.

### Missile

Montage of an inert test of a United States [Trident](/wiki/Trident_missile) [SLBM](/wiki/SLBM) (submarine launched ballistic missile), from submerged to the [terminal](/wiki/Missile_defense#Terminal_phase), or re-entry phase, of the [multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles](/wiki/Multiple_independently_targetable_reentry_vehicle)

Preferable from a strategic point of view is a nuclear weapon mounted on a [missile](/wiki/Missile), which can use a [ballistic](/wiki/Ballistics) trajectory to deliver the warhead over the horizon. Although even short-range missiles allow for a faster and less vulnerable attack, the development of long-range [intercontinental ballistic missiles](/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile) (ICBMs) and [submarine-launched ballistic missiles](/wiki/Submarine-launched_ballistic_missile) (SLBMs) has given some nations the ability to plausibly deliver missiles anywhere on the globe with a high likelihood of success.

More advanced systems, such as [multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles](/wiki/Multiple_independently_targetable_reentry_vehicle) (MIRVs), can launch multiple warheads at different targets from one missile, reducing the chance of a successful [missile defense](/wiki/Missile_defense). Today, missiles are most common among systems designed for delivery of nuclear weapons. Making a warhead small enough to fit onto a missile, though, can be difficult.

### Others

[Tactical weapons](/wiki/Tactical_nuclear_weapon) have involved the most variety of delivery types, including not only gravity bombs and missiles but also [artillery](/wiki/Nuclear_artillery) shells, [land mines](/wiki/Atomic_demolition_munition), and [nuclear depth charges](/wiki/Nuclear_depth_charge) and [torpedoes](/wiki/Nuclear_torpedo) for [anti-submarine warfare](/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare). An atomic [mortar](/wiki/Mortar_(weapon)) has been tested by the United States. Small, two-man portable tactical weapons (somewhat misleadingly referred to as [suitcase bombs](/wiki/Suitcase_bomb)), such as the [Special Atomic Demolition Munition](/wiki/Special_Atomic_Demolition_Munition), have been developed, although the difficulty of combining sufficient yield with portability limits their military utility.

## Nuclear strategy

Main articles: [Nuclear strategy](/wiki/Nuclear_strategy) and [Deterrence theory](/wiki/Deterrence_theory)

See also: [Pre-emptive nuclear strike](/wiki/Pre-emptive_nuclear_strike), [Nuclear peace](/wiki/Nuclear_peace), [Essentials of Post–Cold War Deterrence](/wiki/Essentials_of_Post%E2%80%93Cold_War_Deterrence), [Single Integrated Operational Plan](/wiki/Single_Integrated_Operational_Plan), [Nuclear warfare](/wiki/Nuclear_warfare), and [On Thermonuclear War](/wiki/On_Thermonuclear_War)

Nuclear warfare strategy is a set of policies that deal with preventing or fighting a nuclear war. The policy of trying to prevent an attack by a nuclear weapon from another country by threatening nuclear retaliation is known as the strategy of [nuclear deterrence](/wiki/Deterrence_theory). The goal in deterrence is to always maintain a second strike capability (the ability of a country to respond to a nuclear attack with one of its own) and potentially to strive for [first strike](/wiki/Pre-emptive_nuclear_strike) status (the ability to destroy an enemy's nuclear forces before they could retaliate). During the Cold War, policy and military theorists considered the sorts of policies that might prevent a nuclear attack, and they developed [game theory](/wiki/Game_theory) models that could lead to stable deterrence conditions.

The now decommissioned United States' [Peacekeeper missile](/wiki/LG-118A_Peacekeeper) was an [ICBM](/wiki/ICBM) developed to replace the [Minuteman missile](/wiki/Minuteman_missile) in the late 1980s. Each missile, like the [heavier lift](/wiki/Throw_weight) Russian [SS-18 Satan](/wiki/SS-18_Satan), could contain up to ten nuclear warheads (shown in red), each of which could be aimed at a different target. A factor in the development of [MIRVs](/wiki/MIRV) was to make complete [missile defense](/wiki/Missile_defense) difficult for an enemy country.

Different forms of [nuclear weapons delivery](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_delivery) (see above) allow for different types of nuclear strategies. The goals of any strategy are generally to make it difficult for an enemy to launch a pre-emptive strike against the weapon system and difficult to defend against the delivery of the weapon during a potential conflict. This can mean keeping weapon locations hidden, such as deploying them on [submarines](/wiki/Submarine) or land mobile [transporter erector launchers](/wiki/Transporter_erector_launcher) whose locations are difficult to track, or it can mean protecting weapons by burying them in hardened [missile silo](/wiki/Missile_silo) bunkers. Other components of nuclear strategies included using missile defenses to destroy the missiles before they land or implementing [civil defense](/wiki/Civil_defense) measures using early-warning systems to evacuate citizens to safe areas before an attack.

Weapons designed to threaten large populations or to deter attacks are known as *[strategic weapons](/wiki/Strategic_nuclear_weapons).* Nuclear weapons for use on a [battlefield](/wiki/Battle) in military situations are called *[tactical weapons](/wiki/Tactical_nuclear_weapons).*

Critics of nuclear war strategy often suggest that a nuclear war between two nations would result in mutual annihilation. From this point of view, the significance of nuclear weapons is to deter war because any nuclear war would escalate out of mutual distrust and fear, resulting in [mutually assured destruction](/wiki/Mutually_assured_destruction). This threat of national, if not global, destruction has been a strong motivation for anti-nuclear weapons activism.

Critics from the peace movement and within the military establishment have questioned the usefulness of such weapons in the current military climate. According to an [advisory opinion](/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice_advisory_opinion_on_the_Legality_of_the_Threat_or_Use_of_Nuclear_Weapons) issued by the [International Court of Justice](/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice) in 1996, the use of (or threat of use of) such weapons would generally be contrary to the rules of international law applicable in armed conflict, but the court did not reach an opinion as to whether or not the threat or use would be lawful in specific extreme circumstances such as if the survival of the state were at stake.

[Ballistic missile submarines](/wiki/Ballistic_missile_submarine) have been of great strategic importance for the United States, Russia, and other nuclear powers since they entered service in the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War), as they can hide from [reconnaissance satellites](/wiki/Reconnaissance_satellite) and fire their nuclear weapons with virtual impunity.

Another [deterrence](/wiki/Deterrence_theory) position is that [nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation) can be desirable. In this case, it is argued that, unlike conventional weapons, nuclear weapons deter all-out war between states, and they succeeded in doing this during the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) between the US and the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union). In the late 1950s and early 1960s, Gen. [Pierre Marie Gallois](/wiki/Pierre_Marie_Gallois) of France, an adviser to [Charles de Gaulle](/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle), argued in books like *The Balance of Terror: Strategy for the Nuclear Age* (1961) that mere possession of a nuclear arsenal was enough to ensure deterrence, and thus concluded that the spread of nuclear weapons could increase [international stability](/wiki/Nuclear_peace). Some prominent [neo-realist](/wiki/Neorealism_(international_relations)) scholars, such as [Kenneth Waltz](/wiki/Kenneth_Waltz) and [John Mearsheimer](/wiki/John_Mearsheimer), have argued, along the lines of Gallois, that some forms of nuclear proliferation would decrease the likelihood of [total war](/wiki/Total_war), especially in troubled regions of the world where there exists a single nuclear-weapon state. Aside from the public opinion that opposes proliferation in any form, there are two schools of thought on the matter: those, like Mearsheimer, who favored selective proliferation, and Waltz, who was somewhat more non-[interventionist](/wiki/Interventionism_(politics)). Interest in proliferation and the [stability-instability paradox](/wiki/Stability-instability_paradox) that it generates continues to this day, with ongoing debate about indigenous Japanese and [South Korean](/wiki/South_Korea) nuclear deterrent against [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea).

The threat of potentially suicidal terrorists possessing nuclear weapons (a form of [nuclear terrorism](/wiki/Nuclear_terrorism)) complicates the decision process. The prospect of [mutually assured destruction](/wiki/Mutually_assured_destruction) might not deter an enemy who expects to die in the confrontation. Further, if the initial act is from a stateless [terrorist](/wiki/Terrorist) instead of a sovereign nation, there might not be a nation or specific target to retaliate against. It has been argued, especially after the [September 11, 2001, attacks](/wiki/September_11_attacks), that this complication calls for a new nuclear strategy, one that is distinct from that which gave relative stability during the Cold War. Since 1996, the United States has had a policy of allowing the targeting of its nuclear weapons at terrorists armed with [weapons of mass destruction](/wiki/Weapons_of_mass_destruction).

A [Minuteman III](/wiki/LGM-30_Minuteman) ICBM test launch from [Vandenberg Space Force Base](/wiki/Vandenberg_Space_Force_Base), United States. [MIRVed](/wiki/MIRV) land-based [ICBMs](/wiki/ICBM) are considered destabilizing because they tend to put a premium on [striking first](/wiki/First_strike_(nuclear_strategy)).

[Robert Gallucci](/wiki/Robert_Gallucci) argues that although traditional deterrence is not an effective approach toward terrorist groups bent on causing a nuclear catastrophe, Gallucci believes that "the United States should instead consider a policy of expanded deterrence, which focuses not solely on the would-be nuclear terrorists but on those states that may deliberately transfer or inadvertently leak nuclear weapons and materials to them. By threatening retaliation against those states, the United States may be able to deter that which it cannot physically prevent."

[Graham Allison](/wiki/Graham_Allison) makes a similar case, arguing that the key to expanded deterrence is coming up with ways of tracing nuclear material to the country that forged the fissile material. "After a nuclear bomb detonates, [nuclear forensics](/wiki/Nuclear_forensics) cops would collect debris samples and send them to a laboratory for radiological analysis. By identifying unique attributes of the fissile material, including its impurities and contaminants, one could trace the path back to its origin." The process is analogous to identifying a criminal by fingerprints. "The goal would be twofold: first, to deter leaders of nuclear states from selling weapons to terrorists by holding them accountable for any use of their weapons; second, to give leaders every incentive to tightly secure their nuclear weapons and materials."

According to the Pentagon's June 2019 "[Doctrine for Joint Nuclear Operations](/wiki/Doctrine_for_Joint_Nuclear_Operations)" of the Joint Chiefs of Staffs website Publication, "Integration of nuclear weapons employment with conventional and special operations forces is essential to the success of any mission or operation."

## Governance, control, and law

Main articles: [International Atomic Energy Agency](/wiki/International_Atomic_Energy_Agency), [Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Non-Proliferation_of_Nuclear_Weapons), [Strategic Arms Limitation Talks](/wiki/Strategic_Arms_Limitation_Talks), [Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty](/wiki/Intermediate-Range_Nuclear_Forces_Treaty), [START I](/wiki/START_I), [START II](/wiki/START_II), [Strategic Offensive Reductions Treaty](/wiki/Strategic_Offensive_Reductions_Treaty), [Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty](/wiki/Comprehensive_Nuclear-Test-Ban_Treaty), [Lahore Declaration](/wiki/Lahore_Declaration), [New START](/wiki/New_START), and [Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Prohibition_of_Nuclear_Weapons)

See also: [Anti-nuclear movement](/wiki/Anti-nuclear_movement)

Because they are weapons of mass destruction, the proliferation and possible use of nuclear weapons are important issues in international relations and diplomacy. In most countries, the use of nuclear force can only be authorized by the [head of government](/wiki/Head_of_government) or [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state). Despite controls and regulations governing nuclear weapons, there is an inherent danger of "accidents, mistakes, false alarms, blackmail, theft, and sabotage".

In the late 1940s, lack of mutual trust prevented the United States and the Soviet Union from making progress on arms control agreements. The [Russell–Einstein Manifesto](/wiki/Russell%E2%80%93Einstein_Manifesto) was issued in [London](/wiki/London) on 9 July 1955, by [Bertrand Russell](/wiki/Bertrand_Russell) in the midst of the Cold War. It highlighted the dangers posed by nuclear weapons and called for world leaders to seek peaceful resolutions to international conflict. The signatories included eleven preeminent intellectuals and scientists, including [Albert Einstein](/wiki/Albert_Einstein), who signed it just days before his death on 18 April 1955. A few days after the release, philanthropist [Cyrus S. Eaton](/wiki/Cyrus_S._Eaton) offered to sponsor a conference—called for in the manifesto—in [Pugwash, Nova Scotia](/wiki/Pugwash,_Nova_Scotia), Eaton's birthplace. This conference was to be the first of the [Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs](/wiki/Pugwash_Conferences_on_Science_and_World_Affairs), held in July 1957.

By the 1960s, steps were taken to limit both the proliferation of nuclear weapons to other countries and the environmental effects of [nuclear testing](/wiki/Nuclear_testing). The [Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty](/wiki/Partial_Nuclear_Test_Ban_Treaty) (1963) restricted all nuclear testing to [underground nuclear testing](/wiki/Underground_nuclear_testing), to prevent contamination from nuclear fallout, whereas the [Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Non-Proliferation_of_Nuclear_Weapons) (1968) attempted to place restrictions on the types of activities signatories could participate in, with the goal of allowing the transference of non-military [nuclear technology](/wiki/Nuclear_technology) to member countries without fear of proliferation.

In 1957, the [International Atomic Energy Agency](/wiki/International_Atomic_Energy_Agency) (IAEA) was established under the mandate of the [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) to encourage development of peaceful applications of nuclear technology, provide international safeguards against its misuse, and facilitate the application of safety measures in its use. In 1996, many nations signed the [Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty](/wiki/Comprehensive_Nuclear-Test-Ban_Treaty), which prohibits all testing of nuclear weapons. A testing ban imposes a significant hindrance to nuclear arms development by any complying country. The treaty requires the ratification by 44 specific states before it can go into force; as of 2012, the ratification of eight of these states is still required.

Additional treaties and agreements have governed nuclear weapons stockpiles between the countries with the two largest stockpiles, the United States and the Soviet Union, and later between the United States and Russia. These include treaties such as [SALT II](/wiki/SALT_II) (never ratified), [START I](/wiki/START_I) (expired), [INF](/wiki/Intermediate-Range_Nuclear_Forces_Treaty), [START II](/wiki/START_II) (never in effect), [SORT](/wiki/SORT), and [New START](/wiki/New_START), as well as non-binding agreements such as [SALT I](/wiki/SALT_I) and the Presidential Nuclear Initiatives of 1991. Even when they did not enter into force, these agreements helped limit and later reduce the numbers and types of nuclear weapons between the United States and the Soviet Union/Russia.

Nuclear weapons have also been opposed by agreements between countries. Many nations have been declared [Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zones](/wiki/Nuclear-Weapon-Free_Zone), areas where nuclear weapons production and deployment are prohibited, through the use of treaties. The [Treaty of Tlatelolco](/wiki/Treaty_of_Tlatelolco) (1967) prohibited any production or deployment of nuclear weapons in [Latin America](/wiki/Latin_America) and the [Caribbean](/wiki/Caribbean), and the [Treaty of Pelindaba](/wiki/Treaty_of_Pelindaba) (1964) prohibits nuclear weapons in many African countries. As recently as 2006 a [Central Asian Nuclear Weapon Free Zone](/wiki/Central_Asian_Nuclear_Weapon_Free_Zone) was established among the former Soviet republics of Central Asia prohibiting nuclear weapons.

The number of nuclear warheads by country in 2024, based on an estimation by the [Federation of American Scientists](/wiki/Federation_of_American_Scientists)

In 1996, the [International Court of Justice](/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice), the highest court of the United Nations, issued an Advisory Opinion concerned with the "[Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons](/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice_advisory_opinion_on_the_Legality_of_the_Threat_or_Use_of_Nuclear_Weapons)". The court ruled that the use or threat of use of nuclear weapons would violate various articles of [international law](/wiki/International_law), including the [Geneva Conventions](/wiki/Geneva_Conventions), the [Hague Conventions](/wiki/Hague_Conventions_(1899_and_1907)), the [UN Charter](/wiki/UN_Charter), and the [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights). Given the unique, destructive characteristics of nuclear weapons, the [International Committee of the Red Cross](/wiki/International_Committee_of_the_Red_Cross) calls on States to ensure that these weapons are never used, irrespective of whether they consider them lawful or not.

Additionally, there have been other, specific actions meant to discourage countries from developing nuclear arms. In the wake of the tests by India and Pakistan in 1998, economic sanctions were (temporarily) levied against both countries, though neither were signatories with the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. One of the stated *[casus belli](/wiki/Casus_belli)* for the initiation of the 2003 [Iraq War](/wiki/Iraq_War) was an accusation by the United States that Iraq was actively pursuing nuclear arms (though this was soon discovered [not to be the case](/wiki/Niger_uranium_forgeries) as the program had been discontinued). In 1981, Israel had [bombed a nuclear reactor](/wiki/Operation_Opera) being constructed in [Osirak](/wiki/Osirak), [Iraq](/wiki/Iraq), in what it called an attempt to halt Iraq's previous nuclear arms ambitions; in 2007, Israel [bombed another reactor](/wiki/Operation_Orchard) being constructed in [Syria](/wiki/Syria).

In 2013, [Mark Diesendorf](/wiki/Mark_Diesendorf) said that governments of France, India, North Korea, Pakistan, UK, and South Africa have used nuclear power or research reactors to assist nuclear weapons development or to contribute to their supplies of nuclear explosives from military reactors. In 2017, 122 countries mainly in the [Global South](/wiki/Global_South) voted in favor of adopting the [Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Prohibition_of_Nuclear_Weapons), which eventually entered into force in 2021.

The [Doomsday Clock](/wiki/Doomsday_Clock) measures the likelihood of a human-made [global catastrophe](/wiki/Global_catastrophic_risk) and is published annually by the [Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](/wiki/Bulletin_of_the_Atomic_Scientists). The Doomsday Clock is set a certain time from midnight, midnight being the time of global catastrophe. The two years with the highest likelihood had previously been 1953, when the Clock was set to 2 minutes until midnight after the US and the Soviet Union began testing hydrogen bombs, and 2018, following the failure of world leaders to address tensions relating to nuclear weapons and climate change issues. In 2023, following the [escalation of nuclear threats](/wiki/Nuclear_threats_during_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) during the [Russo-Ukrainian war](/wiki/Russo-Ukrainian_war_(2022%E2%80%93present)), the doomsday clock was set to 90 seconds, the highest likelihood of global catastrophe since the existence of the Doomsday Clock. Given the lack of progress towards peace in Ukraine, the Doomsday Clock was moved to 89 seconds to midnight in 2025.

As of 2024, Russia has intensified nuclear threats in Ukraine and is reportedly planning to place nuclear weapons in orbit, breaching the [1967 Outer Space Treaty](/wiki/Outer_Space_Treaty). China is significantly expanding its nuclear arsenal, with projections of over 1,000 warheads by 2030 and up to 1,500 by 2035. North Korea is progressing in intercontinental ballistic missile tests and has a mutual-defense treaty with Russia, exchanging artillery for possible missile technology. Iran is currently viewed as a nuclear "threshold" state.

### Disarmament

Main article: [Nuclear disarmament](/wiki/Nuclear_disarmament)

For statistics on possession and deployment, see [List of states with nuclear weapons](/wiki/List_of_states_with_nuclear_weapons).

The [USSR](/wiki/USSR) and United States nuclear weapon stockpiles throughout the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) until 2015, with a precipitous drop in total numbers following the end of the Cold War in 1991.

Nuclear disarmament refers to both the act of reducing or eliminating nuclear weapons and to the end state of a nuclear-free world, in which nuclear weapons are eliminated.

Beginning with the 1963 [Partial Test Ban Treaty](/wiki/Partial_Test_Ban_Treaty) and continuing through the 1996 [Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty](/wiki/Comprehensive_Nuclear-Test-Ban_Treaty), there have been many treaties to limit or reduce nuclear weapons testing and stockpiles. The 1968 [Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty](/wiki/Nuclear_Non-Proliferation_Treaty) has as one of its explicit conditions that all signatories must "pursue negotiations in good faith" towards the long-term goal of "complete disarmament". The nuclear-weapon states have largely treated that aspect of the agreement as "decorative" and without force.

Only one country—South Africa—has ever fully renounced nuclear weapons they had independently developed. The former Soviet republics of [Belarus](/wiki/Belarus), [Kazakhstan](/wiki/Kazakhstan), and [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine) returned Soviet nuclear arms stationed in their countries to Russia after the [collapse of the USSR](/wiki/Collapse_of_the_USSR).

Proponents of nuclear disarmament say that it would lessen the probability of nuclear war, especially accidentally. Critics of nuclear disarmament say that it would undermine the present [nuclear peace](/wiki/Nuclear_peace) and deterrence and would lead to increased global instability. Various American elder statesmen, who were in office during the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War) period, have been advocating the elimination of nuclear weapons. These officials include [Henry Kissinger](/wiki/Henry_Kissinger), [George Shultz](/wiki/George_Shultz), [Sam Nunn](/wiki/Sam_Nunn) and [William Perry](/wiki/William_J._Perry). In January 2010, [Lawrence M. Krauss](/wiki/Lawrence_M._Krauss) stated that "no issue carries more importance to the long-term health and security of humanity than the effort to reduce, and perhaps one day, rid the world of nuclear weapons".

[Ukrainian](/wiki/Ukraine) workers use equipment provided by the US [Defense Threat Reduction Agency](/wiki/Defense_Threat_Reduction_Agency) to dismantle a Soviet-era missile silo. After the end of the Cold War, Ukraine and the other non-Russian, post-Soviet republics relinquished Soviet nuclear stockpiles to Russia.

In January 1986, Soviet leader [Mikhail Gorbachev](/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev) publicly proposed a three-stage program for abolishing the world's nuclear weapons by the end of the 20th century. In the years after the end of the Cold War, there have been numerous campaigns to urge the abolition of nuclear weapons, such as that organized by the [Global Zero](/wiki/Global_Zero_(campaign)) movement, and the goal of a "world without nuclear weapons" was advocated by United States President [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama) in an April 2009 speech in [Prague](/wiki/Prague). A [CNN](/wiki/CNN) poll from April 2010 indicated that the American public was nearly evenly split on the issue.

Some analysts have argued that nuclear weapons have made the world relatively safer, with peace through [deterrence](/wiki/Deterrence_theory) and through the [stability–instability paradox](/wiki/Stability%E2%80%93instability_paradox), including in south Asia. [Kenneth Waltz](/wiki/Kenneth_Waltz) has argued that nuclear weapons have helped keep an uneasy peace, and further nuclear weapon proliferation might even help avoid the large scale conventional wars that were so common before their invention at the end of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II). But former Secretary [Henry Kissinger](/wiki/Henry_Kissinger) said in 2010 that there is a new danger, which cannot be addressed by deterrence: "The classical notion of deterrence was that there was some consequences before which aggressors and evildoers would recoil. In a world of suicide bombers, that calculation doesn't operate in any comparable way". [George Shultz](/wiki/George_Shultz) has said, "If you think of the people who are doing suicide attacks, and people like that get a nuclear weapon, they are almost by definition not deterrable".

As of early 2019, more than 90% of world's 13,865 nuclear weapons were owned by Russia and the United States.

### United Nations

Main article: [United Nations Office for Disarmament Affairs](/wiki/United_Nations_Office_for_Disarmament_Affairs)

The UN Office for Disarmament Affairs (UNODA) is a department of the [United Nations Secretariat](/wiki/United_Nations_Secretariat) established in January 1998 as part of the [United Nations Secretary-General](/wiki/United_Nations_Secretary-General) [Kofi Annan](/wiki/Kofi_Annan)'s plan to reform the UN as presented in his report to the [General Assembly](/wiki/United_Nations_General_Assembly) in July 1997.

Its goal is to promote nuclear disarmament and [non-proliferation](/wiki/Non-proliferation) and the strengthening of the disarmament regimes in respect to other weapons of mass destruction, [chemical](/wiki/Chemical_weapon) and [biological weapons](/wiki/Biological_agent). It also promotes disarmament efforts in the area of [conventional weapons](/wiki/Conventional_weapon), especially [land mines](/wiki/Land_mine) and [small arms](/wiki/Small_arms), which are often the weapons of choice in contemporary conflicts.

### Political effects

Comparative studies have shown that nuclear weapons programs produce systems of secrecy, reduction in democratic practices, and concentration of power in the executive branch. These have had different specific expressions in different countries, ranging from systems with some ostensible transparency and accountability, to systems where there is essentially no public participation in nuclear decision-making, even in some democracies.

Different nuclear-armed states, over time, developed different approaches to the question of nuclear weapons use decisions are made, and by whom they can be made. This is referred to as "launch authority." As many of these procedures are treated as deeply secret, the exact details are often debated. A general historical trend, however, is that new nuclear states have often taken many years to formalize their procedures for authorizing nuclear weapons use, and that there are often an early reliance on military control of the weapons, with differing degrees of centralized civilian control. At times, states have relied to greater or lesser extents on automation, computerization, and increasingly complex [command, control, and communications](/wiki/Command,_control,_and_communications) systems to facilitate the use of nuclear weapons and the detection of potential use against them.

## Controversy

See also: [Nuclear weapons debate](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_debate) and [History of the anti-nuclear movement](/wiki/History_of_the_anti-nuclear_movement)

### Ethics

Main article: [Nuclear ethics](/wiki/Nuclear_ethics)

[Anti-nuclear weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_disarmament) protest march in Oxford, 1980

Even before the first nuclear weapons had been developed, scientists involved with the [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project) were divided over the use of the weapon. The role of the two atomic bombings of the country in [Japan's surrender](/wiki/Surrender_of_Japan) and the US's [ethical](/wiki/Ethics) justification for them has been the subject of scholarly and popular debate for decades. The question of whether nations should have nuclear weapons, or test them, has been continually and nearly universally controversial.

### Notable nuclear weapons accidents

Main articles: [Nuclear and radiation accidents and incidents](/wiki/Nuclear_and_radiation_accidents_and_incidents) and [List of military nuclear accidents](/wiki/List_of_military_nuclear_accidents)

See also: [List of nuclear close calls](/wiki/List_of_nuclear_close_calls)

The production and deployment of nuclear weapons has involved many accidents that resulted in either some radiation casualties, or the near-miss possibility of the unauthorized and unintended detonation of a nuclear weapon. These include:

- August 21, 1945: While conducting experiments on a plutonium-gallium core at [Los Alamos National Laboratory](/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory), physicist [Harry Daghlian](/wiki/Harry_Daghlian) received a lethal dose of radiation when an error caused it to enter [prompt criticality](/wiki/Prompt_criticality). He died 25 days later, on September 15, 1945, from [radiation poisoning](/wiki/Acute_radiation_syndrome).
- May 21, 1946: While conducting further experiments on the same core at Los Alamos National Laboratory, physicist [Louis Slotin](/wiki/Louis_Slotin) accidentally caused the core to become briefly [supercritical](/wiki/Critical_mass). He received a lethal dose of [gamma](/wiki/Gamma_ray) and [neutron radiation](/wiki/Neutron_radiation), and died nine days later on May 30, 1946. After the death of Daghlian and Slotin, the mass became known as the "[demon core](/wiki/Demon_core)". It was ultimately used to construct a bomb for use on the Nevada Test Range.
- February 13, 1950: a [Convair B-36B crashed](/wiki/1950_British_Columbia_B-36_crash) in northern [British Columbia](/wiki/British_Columbia) after jettisoning a [Mark IV](/wiki/Mark_4_nuclear_bomb) atomic bomb. This was the first such [nuclear weapon loss](/wiki/United_States_military_nuclear_incident_terminology) in history. The accident was designated a "[Broken Arrow](/wiki/Broken_Arrow_(nuclear))"—an accident involving a nuclear weapon, but which does not present a risk of war. Experts believe that up to 50 nuclear weapons were lost during the Cold War.
- May 22, 1957: a 42,000-pound (19,000 kg) [Mark-17 hydrogen bomb](/wiki/Mark_17_nuclear_bomb) accidentally fell from a bomber near Albuquerque, New Mexico. The detonation of the device's conventional explosives destroyed it on impact and formed a crater 25 feet (7.6 m) in diameter on land owned by the [University of New Mexico](/wiki/University_of_New_Mexico). According to a researcher at the Natural Resources Defense Council, it was one of the most powerful bombs made to date.
- June 7, 1960: the [1960 Fort Dix IM-99 accident](/wiki/1960_Fort_Dix_IM-99_accident) destroyed a [Boeing CIM-10 Bomarc](/wiki/Boeing_CIM-10_Bomarc) nuclear missile and shelter and contaminated the [BOMARC Missile Accident Site](/wiki/BOMARC_Missile_Accident_Site) in New Jersey.
- January 24, 1961: the [1961 Goldsboro B-52 crash](/wiki/1961_Goldsboro_B-52_crash) occurred near [Goldsboro, North Carolina](/wiki/Goldsboro,_North_Carolina). A [Boeing B-52 Stratofortress](/wiki/Boeing_B-52_Stratofortress) carrying two [Mark 39 nuclear bombs](/wiki/Mark_39_nuclear_bomb) broke up in mid-air, dropping its nuclear payload in the process. One of the weapons went through every one of its stages of its firing sequence, save one safety switch.
- [1965 Philippine Sea A-4 crash](/wiki/1965_Philippine_Sea_A-4_crash), where a [Skyhawk](/wiki/Douglas_A-4_Skyhawk) attack aircraft with a nuclear weapon fell into the sea. The pilot, the aircraft, and the [B43 nuclear bomb](/wiki/B43_nuclear_bomb) were never recovered. It was not until 1989 that [the Pentagon](/wiki/The_Pentagon) revealed the loss of the one-megaton bomb.
- January 17, 1966: the [1966 Palomares B-52 crash](/wiki/1966_Palomares_B-52_crash) occurred when a [B-52G bomber](/wiki/B-52_Stratofortress) of the [USAF](/wiki/United_States_Air_Force) collided with a [KC-135 tanker](/wiki/KC-135_Stratotanker) during [mid-air refuelling](/wiki/Aerial_refueling) off the coast of [Spain](/wiki/Spain). The KC-135 was completely destroyed when its fuel load ignited, killing all four crew members. The B-52G broke apart, killing three of the seven crew members aboard. Of the four [Mk28](/wiki/B28_nuclear_bomb) type [hydrogen bombs](/wiki/Teller%E2%80%93Ulam_design) the B-52G carried, three were found on land near [Almería](/wiki/Almer%C3%ADa), Spain. The non-nuclear explosives in two of the weapons detonated upon impact with the ground, resulting in the contamination of a 2-square-kilometer (490-acre) (0.78 square mile) area by [radioactive](/wiki/Radioactive_decay) [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium). The fourth, which fell into the [Mediterranean Sea](/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea), was recovered intact after a 21⁄2-month-long search.
- January 21, 1968: the [1968 Thule Air Base B-52 crash](/wiki/1968_Thule_Air_Base_B-52_crash) involved a [United States Air Force](/wiki/United_States_Air_Force) (USAF) [B-52 bomber](/wiki/B-52_Stratofortress). The aircraft was carrying four [hydrogen bombs](/wiki/Hydrogen_bomb) when a cabin fire forced the crew to abandon the aircraft. Six crew members ejected safely, but one who did not have an [ejection seat](/wiki/Ejection_seat) was killed while trying to bail out. The bomber crashed onto [sea ice](/wiki/Sea_ice) in [Greenland](/wiki/Greenland), causing the nuclear payload to rupture and disperse, which resulted in widespread [radioactive contamination](/wiki/Radioactive_contamination). One of the bombs remains lost.
- September 18–19, 1980: the [Damascus Accident](/wiki/1980_Damascus_Titan_missile_explosion) occurred in Damascus, Arkansas, where a [Titan Missile](/wiki/Titan_Missile_Museum) equipped with a nuclear warhead exploded. The accident was caused by a maintenance man who dropped a socket from a socket wrench down an 80-foot (24 m) shaft, puncturing a fuel tank on the rocket. Leaking fuel resulted in a [hypergolic](/wiki/Hypergolic) fuel explosion, jettisoning the [W-53 warhead](/wiki/W-53_warhead) beyond the launch site.

Governmental nuclear agencies frequently point to their procedures and technical safeguards as what has prevented accidental nuclear explosions. But it has also been argued by historians, political scientists, and historical participants that in many instances, the avoidance of an unintended nuclear explosions was due less to correct implementation of controls, but instead can be attributed to disobedience, technical failures, or other factors beyond strict human control. Reliance on non-controlled factors is sometimes referred to as "luck": "'Lucky' nuclear close calls can be defined as instances in which unwanted nuclear explosions were avoided either 'independently' of control practices, 'despite' control practices, or ‘because of the failure of’ control practices." General [George Lee Butler](/wiki/George_Lee_Butler), commander of the US [Strategic Air Command](/wiki/Strategic_Air_Command) (1991–1992), argued in 1999 that "...we escaped the Cold War without a nuclear holocaust by some combination of skill, luck, and divine intervention, and I suspect the latter in greatest proportion." Dean Acheson, US [Secretary of State](/wiki/Secretary_of_State) during the [Cuban Missile Crisis](/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis), concluded similarly in 1969 that it was ultimately "plain dumb luck" that resolved that crisis peacefully.

### Nuclear testing and fallout

Main article: [Nuclear fallout](/wiki/Nuclear_fallout)

See also: [Downwinders](/wiki/Downwinders)

Over 2,000 nuclear explosions have been conducted in over a dozen different sites around the world. Red Russia/Soviet Union, blue France, light blue United States, violet Britain, yellow China, orange India, brown Pakistan, green North Korea and light green (territories exposed to nuclear bombs). The black dot indicates the location of the [Vela incident](/wiki/Vela_incident).

This view of downtown [Las Vegas](/wiki/Las_Vegas) shows a [mushroom cloud](/wiki/Mushroom_cloud) in the background. Scenes such as this were typical during the 1950s. From 1951 to 1962 the government conducted 100 atmospheric tests at the nearby [Nevada Test Site](/wiki/Nevada_Test_Site).

Over 500 atmospheric nuclear weapons tests were conducted at various sites around the world from 1945 to 1980. [Radioactive fallout](/wiki/Radioactive_fallout) from nuclear weapons testing was first drawn to public attention in 1954 when the [Castle Bravo](/wiki/Castle_Bravo) hydrogen bomb test at the [Pacific Proving Grounds](/wiki/Pacific_Proving_Grounds) contaminated the crew and catch of the Japanese fishing boat *[Lucky Dragon](/wiki/Daigo_Fukury%C5%AB_Maru)*. One of the fishermen died in Japan seven months later, and the fear of contaminated [tuna](/wiki/Tuna) led to a temporary boycotting of the popular staple in Japan. The incident caused widespread concern around the world, especially regarding the effects of nuclear fallout and atmospheric [nuclear testing](/wiki/Nuclear_testing), and "provided a decisive impetus for the emergence of the anti-nuclear weapons movement in many countries".

As public awareness and concern mounted over the possible health hazards associated with exposure to the nuclear fallout, various studies were done to assess the extent of the hazard. A [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](/wiki/Centers_for_Disease_Control_and_Prevention)/ [National Cancer Institute](/wiki/National_Cancer_Institute) study claims that fallout from atmospheric nuclear tests would lead to perhaps 11,000 excess deaths among people alive during atmospheric testing in the United States from all forms of cancer, including leukemia, from 1951 to well into the 21st century.
As of March 2009, the US is the only nation that compensates nuclear test victims. Since the [Radiation Exposure Compensation Act](/wiki/Radiation_Exposure_Compensation_Act) of 1990, more than $1.38 billion in compensation has been approved. The money is going to people who took part in the tests, notably at the [Nevada Test Site](/wiki/Nevada_Test_Site), and to others exposed to the radiation.

In addition, leakage of byproducts of nuclear weapon production into groundwater has been an ongoing issue, particularly at the [Hanford site](/wiki/Hanford_site).

## Effects of nuclear explosions

Main article: [Effects of nuclear explosions](/wiki/Effects_of_nuclear_explosions)

### Effects of nuclear explosions on human health

Main article: [Effects of nuclear explosions on human health](/wiki/Effects_of_nuclear_explosions_on_human_health)

A photograph of [Sumiteru Taniguchi](/wiki/Sumiteru_Taniguchi)'s back injuries taken in January 1946 by a US Marine photographer

Some scientists estimate that a nuclear war with 100 Hiroshima-size nuclear explosions on cities could cost the lives of tens of millions of people from long-term climatic effects alone. The climatology hypothesis is that *if* each city [firestorms](/wiki/Firestorm), a great deal of soot could be thrown up into the atmosphere which could blanket the earth, cutting out sunlight for years on end, causing the disruption of food chains, in what is termed a [nuclear winter](/wiki/Nuclear_winter).

People near the Hiroshima explosion and who managed to survive the explosion subsequently suffered a variety of horrible medical effects. Some of these effects are still present to this day:

- Initial Stage: From 1–9 weeks. The greatest number of deaths occur in the first 1 to 9 weeks, with 90% due to thermal injury or blast effects and 10% due to super-lethal [radiation](/wiki/Radiation) exposure.
- Intermediate Stage: From 10–12 weeks. The deaths in this period are from [ionizing radiation](/wiki/Ionizing_radiation) in the median lethal range – [LD50](/wiki/Median_lethal_dose).
- Late Period: Lasting from 13–20 weeks. This period has some improvement in survivors' condition.
- Delayed Period: From 20+ weeks. Characterized by numerous complications, mostly related to healing of thermal/mechanical injuries, and if the individual was exposed to a few 100 to a 1,000 [millisieverts](/wiki/Millisievert) of radiation; it is coupled with infertility, sub-fertility and blood disorders. Furthermore, ionizing radiation above a dose of around 50–100-millisievert exposure has been shown to statistically begin increasing one's chance of dying of cancer sometime in their lifetime over the normal unexposed rate of ~ 25%, in the long term, a heightened rate of cancer, proportional to the dose received, would begin to be observed after ~ 5+ years, with lesser problems (such as eye [cataracts](/wiki/Cataract)) and other more minor effects in other organs and tissues also being observed over the long term.

Fallout exposure—depending on if further afield individuals [shelter in place](/wiki/Shelter_in_place) or evacuate perpendicular to the direction of the wind, and therefore avoid contact with the fallout plume, and stay there for the days and weeks after the nuclear explosion, their exposure to fallout, and therefore their total dose, will vary. With those who do shelter in place, and or evacuate, experiencing a total dose that would be negligible in comparison to someone who just went about their life as normal.

For example, staying indoors until after the most hazardous fallout [isotope](/wiki/Isotope), [I-131](/wiki/I-131) decays away to 0.1% of its initial quantity after ten [half-lives](/wiki/Half-life)—roughly 80 days for I-131, could avoid most of the risk of contracting [thyroid cancer](/wiki/Thyroid_cancer).

### Effects of nuclear war

See also: [Nuclear holocaust](/wiki/Nuclear_holocaust), [Doomsday Clock](/wiki/Doomsday_Clock), [Doomsday device](/wiki/Doomsday_device), [World War III](/wiki/World_War_III), and [Nuclear famine](/wiki/Nuclear_famine)

Mushroom cloud from the explosion of [Castle Bravo](/wiki/Castle_Bravo), the largest nuclear weapon detonated by the US, in 1954

Nuclear war could yield unprecedented human death tolls and [habitat destruction](/wiki/Habitat_destruction). Detonating large numbers of nuclear weapons would have an immediate, short term and long-term effects on the climate, potentially causing cold weather known as a "[nuclear winter](/wiki/Nuclear_winter)". In 1982, [Brian Martin](/wiki/Brian_Martin_(social_scientist)) estimated that a [US–Soviet nuclear exchange](/wiki/Nuclear_arms_race) might kill 400–450 million directly, mostly in the United States, Europe and Russia, and maybe several hundred million more through follow-up consequences in those same areas. Many scholars have posited that a global thermonuclear war with Cold War-era stockpiles, or even with the current smaller stockpiles, may lead to the [extinction of the human race](/wiki/Human_extinction). The *International Physicians for the Prevention of Nuclear War* believe that nuclear war could indirectly contribute to human extinction via secondary effects, including environmental consequences, [societal breakdown](/wiki/Societal_collapse), and economic collapse. It has been estimated that a relatively small-scale nuclear exchange between [India and Pakistan](/wiki/India%E2%80%93Pakistan_relations) involving 100 [Hiroshima](/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki) yield (15 kilotons) weapons, could cause a nuclear winter and kill more than a billion people.

According to a peer-reviewed study published in the journal *[Nature Food](/wiki/Nature_Food)* in August 2022, a full-scale nuclear war between the US and Russia would directly kill 360 million people and more than 5 billion people would die from [starvation](/wiki/Starvation). More than 2 billion people could die from a smaller-scale nuclear war between India and Pakistan.

### Public opposition

See also: [Nuclear disarmament](/wiki/Nuclear_disarmament) and [International Day against Nuclear Tests](/wiki/International_Day_against_Nuclear_Tests)

Protest in Bonn against the [nuclear arms race](/wiki/Nuclear_arms_race) between the US/NATO and the Warsaw Pact, 1981

Demonstration against nuclear testing in [Lyon](/wiki/Lyon), France, in the 1980s

Peace movements emerged in Japan and in 1954 they converged to form a unified "[Japan Council against Atomic and Hydrogen Bombs](/wiki/Japan_Council_against_Atomic_and_Hydrogen_Bombs)." Japanese opposition to nuclear weapons tests in the Pacific Ocean was widespread, and "an estimated 35 million signatures were collected on petitions calling for bans on nuclear weapons".

In the United Kingdom, the [Aldermaston Marches](/wiki/Aldermaston_Marches) organised by the [Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament](/wiki/Campaign_for_Nuclear_Disarmament) (CND) took place at [Easter](/wiki/Easter) 1958, when, according to the CND, several thousand people marched for four days from [Trafalgar Square](/wiki/Trafalgar_Square), London, to the [Atomic Weapons Research Establishment](/wiki/Atomic_Weapons_Establishment) close to [Aldermaston](/wiki/Aldermaston) in [Berkshire](/wiki/Berkshire), England, to demonstrate their opposition to nuclear weapons. The Aldermaston marches continued into the late 1960s when tens of thousands of people took part in the four-day marches.

In 1959, a letter in the *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists* was the start of a successful campaign to stop the [Atomic Energy Commission](/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission) dumping [radioactive waste](/wiki/Radioactive_waste) in the sea 19 kilometers from [Boston](/wiki/Boston). In 1962, [Linus Pauling](/wiki/Linus_Pauling) won the [Nobel Peace Prize](/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize) for his work to stop the atmospheric testing of nuclear weapons, and the "Ban the Bomb" movement spread.

In 1963, many countries ratified the [Partial Test Ban Treaty](/wiki/Partial_Test_Ban_Treaty) prohibiting atmospheric nuclear testing. Radioactive fallout became less of an issue and the anti-nuclear weapons movement went into decline for some years. A resurgence of interest occurred amid European and American [fears of nuclear war](/wiki/Radiophobia) in the 1980s.

## Costs and technology spin-offs

See also: [Global Positioning System](/wiki/Global_Positioning_System), [Nuclear weapons delivery](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_delivery), [History of computing hardware](/wiki/History_of_computing_hardware), [ENIAC](/wiki/ENIAC), and [Swords to ploughshares](/wiki/Swords_to_ploughshares)

According to an audit by the [Brookings Institution](/wiki/Brookings_Institution), between 1940 and 1996, the US spent $11.9 trillion in present-day terms on nuclear weapons programs, 57% of which was spent on building [nuclear weapons delivery](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_delivery) systems. 6.3% of the total amount, $749 billion in present-day terms, was spent on [environmental remediation](/wiki/Environmental_remediation) and [nuclear waste management](/wiki/Nuclear_waste_management)—for example, cleaning up the [Hanford site](/wiki/Hanford_site)—and 7% of the total $820 billion was spent on making nuclear weapons themselves.

## Non-weapons uses

Main article: [Peaceful nuclear explosion](/wiki/Peaceful_nuclear_explosion)

Peaceful nuclear explosions (PNEs) are [nuclear explosions](/wiki/Nuclear_explosion) conducted for non-military purposes, such as activities related to [economic development](/wiki/Economic_development) including the creation of [canals](/wiki/Canal). During the 1960s and 1970s, both the United States and the Soviet Union conducted a number of PNEs. The United States created plans for several uses of PNEs, including [Operation Plowshare](/wiki/Project_Plowshare). Six of the explosions by the Soviet Union are considered to have been of an applied nature, not just tests.

The United States and the Soviet Union later halted their programs. Definitions and limits are covered in the Peaceful Nuclear Explosions Treaty of 1976. The stalled [Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty](/wiki/Comprehensive_Nuclear-Test-Ban_Treaty) of 1996 would prohibit all nuclear explosions, regardless of whether they are for peaceful purposes or not.

## History of development

Main article: [History of nuclear weapons](/wiki/History_of_nuclear_weapons)

See also: [Soviet atomic bomb project](/wiki/Soviet_atomic_bomb_project), [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project), [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War), and [History of the Teller–Ulam design](/wiki/History_of_the_Teller%E2%80%93Ulam_design)

This section is an excerpt from [History of nuclear weapons § Background](/wiki/History_of_nuclear_weapons#Background).[(https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_nuclear_weapons&action=edit)]

In [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission), the nucleus of a fissile atom (in this case, [enriched uranium](/wiki/Enriched_uranium)) absorbs a thermal neutron, becomes unstable and splits into two new atoms, releasing some energy and between one and three new neutrons, which can perpetuate the process.

In the first decades of the 19th century, [physics](/wiki/Physics) was revolutionized with developments in the understanding of the nature of [atoms](/wiki/Atom) including the discoveries in [atomic theory](/wiki/Atomic_theory) by [John Dalton](/wiki/John_Dalton). Around the turn of the 20th century, it was discovered by [Hans Geiger](/wiki/Hans_Geiger) and [Ernest Marsden](/wiki/Ernest_Marsden) and then [Ernest Rutherford](/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford), that atoms had a highly dense, very small, charged central core called an atomic nucleus. In 1898, [Pierre](/wiki/Pierre_Curie) and [Marie Curie](/wiki/Marie_Curie) discovered that [pitchblende](/wiki/Uraninite), an ore of [uranium](/wiki/Uranium), contained a substance—which they named [radium](/wiki/Radium)—that emitted large amounts of [radiation](/wiki/Radiation). Ernest Rutherford and [Frederick Soddy](/wiki/Frederick_Soddy) identified that atoms were breaking down and turning into different elements. Hopes were raised among scientists and laymen that the elements around us could contain tremendous amounts of unseen energy, waiting to be harnessed. In 1905, [Albert Einstein](/wiki/Albert_Einstein) described this potential in his famous equation, [*E* = *mc*2](/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence).

At the time however, there was no known mechanism which could be used to unlock the vast energy potential that was theorized to exist inside the atom. The only particle then known to exist within the nucleus was the positively-charged proton, which would act to repel protons set in motion towards it. Then in 1932, a key breakthrough was made with the discovery of the [neutron](/wiki/Neutron). Having no electric charge, the neutron is able to penetrate the nucleus with relative ease.

In January 1933, the Nazis came to power in Germany and suppressed Jewish scientists. Physicist [Leo Szilard](/wiki/Leo_Szilard) fled to London where, in 1934, he patented the idea of a [nuclear chain reaction](/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction) using neutrons. The patent also introduced the term *[critical mass](/wiki/Critical_mass)* to describe the minimum amount of material required to sustain the chain reaction and its potential to cause an [explosion](/wiki/Nuclear_explosion) (British patent 630,726). The patent was not about an atomic bomb *per se*, as the possibility of chain reaction was still very speculative. Szilard subsequently assigned the patent to the [British Admiralty](/wiki/British_Admiralty) so that it could be covered by the [Official Secrets Act](/wiki/Official_Secrets_Act_1911#Section_2_-_Wrongful_communication,_&c._of_information). This work of Szilard's was ahead of the time, five years before the public discovery of nuclear fission and eight years before a working nuclear reactor. When he coined the term *neutron inducted chain reaction*, he was not sure about the use of isotopes or standard forms of elements. Despite this uncertainty, he correctly theorized uranium and thorium as primary candidates for such a reaction, along with [beryllium](/wiki/Beryllium) which was later determined to be unnecessary in practice. Szilard joined Enrico Fermi in developing the first uranium-fuelled nuclear reactor, [Chicago Pile-1](/wiki/Chicago_Pile-1), which was activated at the University of Chicago in 1942.

In Paris in 1934, [Irène](/wiki/Ir%C3%A8ne_Joliot-Curie) and [Frédéric Joliot-Curie](/wiki/Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric_Joliot-Curie) discovered that [artificial radioactivity](/wiki/Artificial_radioactivity) could be induced in stable elements by bombarding them with [alpha particles](/wiki/Alpha_particle); in Italy [Enrico Fermi](/wiki/Enrico_Fermi) reported similar results when bombarding uranium with neutrons. He mistakenly believed he had discovered elements 93 and 94, naming them [ausenium and hesperium](/wiki/Ausenium_and_hesperium). In 1938 it was realized these were in fact [fission products](/wiki/Fission_products).

[Leo Szilard](/wiki/Leo_Szilard), pictured in about 1960, invented the electron microscope, linear accelerator, cyclotron, nuclear chain reaction and patented the nuclear reactor

In December 1938, [Otto Hahn](/wiki/Otto_Hahn) and [Fritz Strassmann](/wiki/Fritz_Strassmann) reported that they had detected the element [barium](/wiki/Barium) after bombarding uranium with neutrons. [Lise Meitner](/wiki/Lise_Meitner) and [Otto Robert Frisch](/wiki/Otto_Robert_Frisch) correctly interpreted these results as being due to the splitting of the uranium atom. Frisch confirmed this experimentally on January 13, 1939. They gave the process the name "fission" because of its similarity to the [splitting of a cell](/wiki/Fission_(biology)) into two new cells. Even before it was published, news of Meitner's and Frisch's interpretation crossed the Atlantic. In their second publication on nuclear fission in February 1939, Hahn and Strassmann predicted the existence and liberation of additional neutrons during the fission process, opening up the possibility of a [nuclear chain reaction](/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction).

Between 1939 and 1940, [Joliot-Curie](/wiki/Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric_Joliot-Curie)'s team applied for a [patent family](/wiki/Patent_family) covering different use cases of atomic energy, one (case III, in patent FR 971,324 - *Perfectionnements aux charges explosives*, meaning *Improvements in Explosive Charges*) being the first official document explicitly mentioning a nuclear explosion as a purpose, including for war. This patent was applied for on May 4, 1939, but only granted in 1950, being withheld by French authorities in the meantime.

Uranium appears in nature primarily in two isotopes: [uranium-238](/wiki/Uranium-238) and [uranium-235](/wiki/Uranium-235). When the nucleus of uranium-235 absorbs a neutron, it undergoes nuclear fission, releasing energy and, on average, 2.5 neutrons. Because uranium-235 releases more neutrons than it absorbs, it can support a chain reaction and so is described as [fissile](/wiki/Fissile). Uranium-238, on the other hand, is not fissile as it does not normally undergo fission when it absorbs a neutron.

By the start of the war in September 1939, many scientists likely to be persecuted by the Nazis had already escaped. Physicists on both sides were well aware of the possibility of utilizing nuclear fission as a weapon, but no one was quite sure how it could be engineered. In August 1939, concerned that Germany might have [its own project](/wiki/German_nuclear_weapons_program) to develop fission-based weapons, [Albert Einstein](/wiki/Albert_Einstein) signed [a letter](/wiki/Einstein%E2%80%93Szil%C3%A1rd_letter) to U.S. President Franklin D. Roosevelt warning him of the threat.

The final iteration of the *Gadget* nuclear device prior to its successful test on July 16, 1945, the culmination of the United States' three-year [Manhattan Project](/wiki/Manhattan_Project)'s research and development of nuclear weapons

Roosevelt responded by setting up the [Uranium Committee](/wiki/Uranium_Committee) under [Lyman James Briggs](/wiki/Lyman_James_Briggs) but, with little initial funding ($6,000), progress was slow. It was not until the U.S. entered the war in December 1941 that Washington decided to commit the necessary resources to a top-secret high priority bomb project.

Organized research first began in Britain as part of the [Tube Alloys](/wiki/Tube_Alloys) project, the world's first nuclear weapons project, which also involved Canada. The [Maud Committee](/wiki/Maud_Committee) was set up following the work of Frisch and [Rudolf Peierls](/wiki/Rudolf_Peierls) who calculated uranium-235's critical mass and found it to be much smaller than previously thought which meant that a deliverable bomb should be possible. In the February 1940 [Frisch–Peierls memorandum](/wiki/Frisch%E2%80%93Peierls_memorandum) they stated that: "The energy liberated in the explosion of such a super-bomb...will, for an instant, produce a temperature comparable to that of the interior of the sun. The blast from such an explosion would destroy life in a wide area. The size of this area is difficult to estimate, but it will probably cover the centre of a big city."
