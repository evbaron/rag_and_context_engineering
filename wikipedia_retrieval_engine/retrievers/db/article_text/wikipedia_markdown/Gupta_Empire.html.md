The **Gupta Empire** was an Indian empire during the classical period of the [Indian subcontinent](/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) which existed from the mid 3rd century to mid 6th century CE. At its peak, the dynasty ruled over an empire that spanned much of the northern Indian subcontinent. This period has been considered as the [Golden Age of India](/wiki/Golden_Age_of_India) by some historians, although this characterisation has been disputed by others. The ruling dynasty of the empire was founded by [Gupta](/wiki/Gupta_(king)).

The high points of the period are the great cultural developments which took place primarily during the reigns of [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta), [Chandragupta II](/wiki/Chandragupta_II) and [Kumaragupta I](/wiki/Kumaragupta_I). Many [Hindu](/wiki/Hinduism) [epics](/wiki/Hindu_epics) and [literary](/wiki/Hindu_literature) sources, such as the *[Mahabharata](/wiki/Mahabharata)* and *[Ramayana](/wiki/Ramayana)*, were canonised during this period. The Gupta period produced scholars such as [Kalidasa](/wiki/Kalidasa), [Aryabhata](/wiki/Aryabhata), [Varahamihira](/wiki/Varahamihira) and [Vatsyayana](/wiki/Vatsyayana), who made significant advancements in many academic fields. [Science](/wiki/History_of_science_and_technology_in_the_Indian_subcontinent) and political administration reached new heights during the [Gupta era](/wiki/Gupta_era). The period, sometimes described as *[Pax Gupta](/wiki/Pax_Gupta)*, gave rise to achievements in architecture, sculpture, and painting that "set standards of form and taste [that] determined the whole subsequent course of art, not only in [India](/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) but far beyond her borders". Strong trade ties also made the region an important cultural centre and established the region as a base that would influence nearby kingdoms and regions in India and [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia). The [Puranas](/wiki/Puranas), earlier long poems on a variety of subjects, are also thought to have been committed to written texts around this period. Hinduism was followed by the rulers and the Brahmins flourished in the Gupta empire but the Guptas were tolerant towards people of other faiths as well.

The empire eventually died out because of factors such as substantial loss of territory and imperial authority caused by their own erstwhile feudatories, as well as the invasion by the [Huna peoples](/wiki/Huna_people) ([Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites) and [Alchon Huns](/wiki/Alchon_Huns)) from [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia). After the collapse of the Gupta Empire in the 6th century, India was again ruled by numerous regional kingdoms.

## Origin

Main article: [Origin of the Gupta dynasty](/wiki/Origin_of_the_Gupta_dynasty)

Guptas were originally from [Magadha](/wiki/Magadha).
The first evidence of Gupta connection with Magadha comes from a foreign source. The Chinese traveller [I-tsing](/wiki/Yijing_(monk)), who came to India in A.D. 672 heard of Mahārāja Śrī-Gupta (Che-li-ki-to) who built a temple near [Mrigaśikhāvana](/wiki/M%E1%B9%9Bga%C5%9Bikh%C4%81vana) for Chinese pilgrims and endowed it with 24 villages.

Recent numismatic research suggests that [Sri Gupta](/wiki/Sri_Gupta), traditionally regarded as the founder of the Gupta dynasty, likely ruled as an independent monarch in the [Magadha](/wiki/Magadha) region during the late 3rd century CE. A hoard of over one hundred silver coins bearing the legend "Śrī Gupta" in Brahmi script, along with a portrait bust, have been discovered in [Bihar](/wiki/Bihar), particularly in the [Hajipur](/wiki/Hajipur)–[Muzaffarpur](/wiki/Muzaffarpur_district) region, a core area of ancient [Magadha](/wiki/Magadha). These coins, uniform in typology and silver standard, reflect deliberate state issuance rather than imitation or forgery. Scholars argue that this coinage indicates Sri Gupta's sovereign authority, issued to meet local economic demands following the decline of the [Kushans](/wiki/Kushan_Empire) and to assert political identity. The use of the title [Maharaja](/wiki/Maharaja)—also common among other independent tribal states of the period—further supports his autonomous status. Moreover, the later marriage alliance between [Chandra Gupta I](/wiki/Chandra_Gupta_I) and the [Lichchhavi](/wiki/Licchavis_of_Vaishali) princess [Kumaradevi](/wiki/Kumaradevi) likely reflects the already elevated position of the Gupta family, established during the reigns of [Sri Gupta](/wiki/Sri_Gupta) and his son [Ghatotkacha](/wiki/Ghatotkacha). This interpretation places the origin of the Gupta Empire firmly within a local [Magadhan](/wiki/Magadha) context, grounded in both political independence and early monetary sovereignty.

According to one theory, they originated in the present-day lower-Doab region of [Uttar Pradesh](/wiki/Uttar_Pradesh), where most of the inscriptions and coin hoards of the early Gupta emperors have been discovered. The recently found silver coin of [Sri Gupta](/wiki/Gupta_(king)) in Uttar Pradesh further attest the origin of Guptas around [Kāśī](/wiki/Varanasi)–[Kannauj](/wiki/Kannauj) region and his rule was only limited to Kāśī (present day [Varanasi](/wiki/Varanasi)). There also remains a possibility that this Śrīgupta coin was issued by another king.

Another theory locates the Gupta homeland in the present-day [Bengal](/wiki/Bengal) region in Ganges basin, based on the account of the 7th-century Chinese Buddhist monk [Yijing](/wiki/Yijing_(monk)). According to Yijing, king Che-li-ki-to (identified with the dynasty's founder *Shri* [Gupta](/wiki/Gupta_(king))) built a temple for Chinese pilgrims near Mi-li-kia-si-kia-po-no (apparently a transcription of [Mriga-shikha-vana](/wiki/M%E1%B9%9Bga%C5%9Bikh%C4%81vana)). Yijing states that this temple was located more than 40 *[yojanas](/wiki/Yojana)* east of [Nalanda](/wiki/Nalanda), which would mean it was situated somewhere in the modern Bengal region. Another proposal is that the early Gupta kingdom extended from Prayaga in the west to northern Bengal in the east.

The Gupta records do not mention the dynasty's [varna](/wiki/Varna_in_Hinduism) (social class). Some historians, such as [A.S. Altekar](/wiki/A.S._Altekar), have theorised that they were of [Vaishya](/wiki/Vaishya) origin, as certain ancient Indian texts prescribe the name "Gupta" for the members of the Vaishya varna. According to historian [R. S. Sharma](/wiki/Ram_Sharan_Sharma), the Vaishyas – who were traditionally associated with trade – may have become rulers after resisting oppressive taxation by the previous rulers. Critics of the Vaishya-origin theory point out that the suffix Gupta features in the names of several non-Vaishyas before as well as during the Gupta period, and the dynastic name "Gupta" may have simply derived from the name of the dynasty's first king [Gupta](/wiki/Gupta_(king)). Some scholars, such as [S. R. Goyal](/wiki/S._R._Goyal), theorise that the Guptas were [Brahmins](/wiki/Brahmins), because they had matrimonial relations with Brahmins, but others reject this evidence as inconclusive. Based on the [Pune](/wiki/Pune) and Riddhapur inscriptions of the Gupta princess [Prabhavatigupta](/wiki/Prabhavatigupta), some scholars believe that the name of her paternal [gotra](/wiki/Gotra) (clan) was "Dharana", but an alternative reading of these inscriptions suggests that Dharana was the *gotra* of her mother Kuberanaga.

## History

For historical calendar era, see [Gupta era](/wiki/Gupta_era).

### Early rulers

Gupta ([Gupta script](/wiki/Gupta_script):  *gu-pta*, fl. late 3rd century CE) is the earliest known king of the Gupta dynasty. Different historians variously date the beginning of his reign from the mid-to-late 3rd century CE. Gupta founded the Gupta Empire c. 240-280 CE, and was succeeded by his son, [Ghatotkacha](/wiki/Ghatotkacha_(king)), c. 280-319 CE, followed by Ghatotkacha's son, [Chandragupta I](/wiki/Chandragupta_I), c. 319-335 CE. "Che-li-ki-to", the name of a king mentioned by the 7th century Chinese Buddhist monk [Yijing](/wiki/Yijing_(monk)), is believed to be a transcription of "[Shri](/wiki/Shri)-Gupta" ([IAST](/wiki/IAST): Śrigupta), "Shri" being an honorific prefix. According to Yijing, this king built a temple for Chinese Buddhist pilgrims near "Mi-li-kia-si-kia-po-no" (believed to be a transcription of [Mṛgaśikhāvana](/wiki/M%E1%B9%9Bga%C5%9Bikh%C4%81vana)).

Queen Kumaradevi and King [Chandragupta I](/wiki/Chandragupta_I), depicted on a gold coin

In the [Allahabad Pillar](/wiki/Allahabad_Pillar) inscription, Gupta and his successor Ghatotkacha are described as *[Maharaja](/wiki/Maharaja)* ("Great King"), while the next king Chandragupta I is called a [Maharajadhiraja](/wiki/Maharajadhiraja) ("[King of Great Kings](/wiki/King_of_Kings)"). In the later period, the title *Maharaja* was used by feudatory rulers, which has led to suggestions that Gupta and Ghatotkacha were vassals (possibly of the [Kushan Empire](/wiki/Kushan_Empire)). However, there are several instances of paramount sovereigns using the title *Maharaja*, in both pre-Gupta and post-Gupta periods, so this cannot be said with certainty. That said, there is no doubt that Gupta and Ghatotkacha held a lower status and were less powerful than Chandragupta I.

Chandragupta I married the [Licchavi](/wiki/Licchavis_of_Nepal) princess Kumaradevi, which may have helped him extend his political power and dominions, enabling him to adopt the prestigious title *Maharajadhiraja*. According to the dynasty's official records, he was succeeded by his son [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta). However, the discovery of the coins issued by a Gupta emperor named [Kacha](/wiki/Kacha_(king)) have led to some debate on this topic: according to one theory, Kacha was another name for Samudragupta; another possibility is that Kacha was a rival claimant to the throne.

### Samudragupta

Main article: [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta)

[Gupta script](/wiki/Gupta_script) inscription *Maharaja Sri Gupta*       ("Great King, Lord Gupta"), mentioning the first ruler of the dynasty, king [Gupta](/wiki/Gupta_(king)). Inscription by [Samudragupta](/wiki/Samudragupta) on the [Allahabad Pillar](/wiki/Allahabad_Pillar), where Samudragupta presents king Gupta as his great-grandfather. Dated circa 350 CE.

Samudragupta succeeded his father around 335 or 350 CE, and ruled until c. 375. The Allahabad Pillar inscription, composed by his courtier [Harisena](/wiki/Harisena), credits him with extensive conquests. The inscription asserts that Samudragupta uprooted 8 kings of [Āryāvarta](/wiki/%C4%80ry%C4%81varta), the northern region, including the [Nagas](/wiki/Nagas_of_Padmavati). It further claims that he subjugated all the kings of the forest region, which was most probably located in central India. It also credits him with defeating 12 rulers of [Dakshinapatha](/wiki/Dakshinapatha), the southern region: the exact identification of several of these kings is debated among modern scholars, but it is clear that these kings ruled areas located on the eastern coast of India. The inscription suggests that Samudragupta advanced as far as the [Pallava](/wiki/Pallava_dynasty) kingdom in the south, and defeated Vishnugopa, the Pallava regent of [Kanchi](/wiki/Kanchipuram). During this southern campaign, Samudragupta most probably passed through the forest tract of central India, reached the eastern coast in present-day [Odisha](/wiki/Odisha), and then marched south along the coast of the [Bay of Bengal](/wiki/Bay_of_Bengal).

The Allahabad Pillar inscription mentions that rulers of several frontier kingdoms and tribal [aristocracies](/wiki/Aristocracy) paid Samudragupta tributes, obeyed his orders, and performed obeisance before him. These polities and tribes included [Samatata](/wiki/Samatata), [Davaka](/wiki/Davaka), [Kamarupa](/wiki/Kamarupa), [Nepal](/wiki/Licchavis_of_Nepal), [Karttripura](/wiki/Katyuri_kings), [Malavas](/wiki/Malavas), [Arjunayanas](/wiki/Arjunayanas), [Yaudheyas](/wiki/Yaudheyas), [Madrakas](/wiki/Madra_Kingdom), and [Abhiras](/wiki/Abhira_dynasty).

The inscription also mentions that several foreign kings tried to please Samudragupta by personal attendance, offered him their daughters in marriage (or according to another interpretation, gifted him maidens), and sought the use of the [Garuda](/wiki/Garuda)-depicting Gupta seal for administering their own territories. However, this is likely an exaggeration, and Samudragupta's panegyrist appears to have described acts of diplomacy as ones of subservience. For example, the King of [Simhala](/wiki/Anuradhapura_kingdom) is listed among these foreign rulers, but it is known that from Chinese sources that the Simhala king [Meghavarna](/wiki/Sirimeghavanna_of_Anuradhapura) merely sent presents to the Gupta emperor requesting his permission to build a Buddhist monastery; he did not express subservience.

Samudragupta appears to have been [Vaishnavite](/wiki/Vaishnavite), as attested by his [Eran](/wiki/Eran) inscription, and performed several [Brahmanical](/wiki/Brahmanism) ceremonies. The Gupta records credit him with making generous donations of cows and gold. He performed the [Ashvamedha](/wiki/Ashvamedha) ritual (horse sacrifice), which was used by the ancient Indian kings and emperors to prove their imperial sovereignty, and issued gold coins (see [Coinage](#Coinage) below) to mark this performance.

The Allahabad Pillar inscription presents Samudragupta as a wise king and strict administrator, who was also compassionate enough to help the poor and the helpless. It also alludes to the king's talents as a musician and a poet, and calls him the "king of poets". Such claims are corroborated by Samudragupta's gold coins, which depict him playing a [veena](/wiki/Ancient_veena).

Samudragupta appears to have directly controlled a large part of the [Indo-Gangetic Plain](/wiki/Indo-Gangetic_Plain) in present-day India, as well as a substantial part of central India. His empire comprised a number of monarchical and tribal tributary states of northern India, and of the south-eastern coastal region of India.

### Ramagupta

Standing [Buddha](/wiki/Buddha) in red sandstone, [Art of Mathura](/wiki/Art_of_Mathura), Gupta period c. 5th century CE. [Mathura Museum](/wiki/Mathura_Museum)

Main article: [Ramagupta](/wiki/Ramagupta)

Ramagupta is known from a sixth-century play, the *[Devichandragupta](/wiki/Devichandragupta)*, in which he surrenders his queen to the enemy [Sakas](/wiki/Saka), forcing his brother Chandragupta to sneak into the enemy camp to rescue her and kill the Saka king. The historicity of these events is unclear, but Ramagupta's existence is confirmed by three [Jain](/wiki/Jainism) statues found at [Durjanpur](/wiki/Durjanpur), with inscriptions referring to him as the *Maharajadhiraja*. A large number of his copper coins also have been found from the [Eran](/wiki/Eran)-[Vidisha](/wiki/Vidisha) region and classified in five distinct types, which include the *Garuda*, *Garudadhvaja*, *lion* and *border legend* types. The [Brahmi](/wiki/Brahmi) legends on these coins are written in the early Gupta style.

### Chandragupta II "Vikramaditya"

Main article: [Chandragupta II](/wiki/Chandragupta_II)

According to the Gupta records, among his sons, Samudragupta nominated prince Chandragupta II, born of queen [Dattadevi](/wiki/Dattadevi), as his successor. Chandragupta II, *Vikramaditya* (Brave as the Sun), ruled from 375 until 415. He married a Kadamba princess of [Kuntala](/wiki/Kuntala_country) and of Naga lineage (*Nāgakulotpannnā*), Kuberanaga. His daughter [Prabhavatigupta](/wiki/Prabhavatigupta) from this Naga queen was married to [Rudrasena II](/wiki/Rudrasena_II), the [Vakataka](/wiki/Vakataka) king of [Deccan](/wiki/Deccan_Plateau). His son Kumaragupta I was married to a Kadamba princess of the Karnataka region. Chandragupta II expanded his realm westwards, defeating the Saka [Western Kshatrapas](/wiki/Western_Kshatrapas) of [Malwa](/wiki/Malwa), [Gujarat](/wiki/History_of_Gujarat) and [Saurashtra](/wiki/Saurashtra_(region)) in a campaign lasting until 409. His main opponent [Rudrasimha III](/wiki/Rudrasimha_III) was defeated by 395, and he crushed the Bengal chiefdoms. This extended his control from coast to coast, established a second capital at [Ujjain](/wiki/Ujjain) and was the high point of the empire. Kuntala inscriptions indicate rule of Chandragupta II in [Kuntala country](/wiki/Kuntala_country) of [Karnataka](/wiki/History_of_Karnataka). [Hunza inscription](/wiki/Sacred_Rock_of_Hunza) also indicate that Chandragupta was able to rule north western Indian subcontinent and proceeded to conquer [Balkh](/wiki/Balkh), although some scholars have also disputed the identity of the Gupta emperor. [Chalukya](/wiki/Chalukya_dynasty) king [Vikramaditya VI](/wiki/Vikramaditya_VI) (r. 1076 – 1126 CE) mentions Chandragupta with his title and states: "Why should the glory of the Kings Vikramaditya and Nanda be a hindrance any longer? He with a loud command abolished that (era), which has the name of Saka, and made that (era) which has the Chalukya counting".

Gold coins of [Chandragupta II](/wiki/Chandragupta_II)

Despite the creation of the empire through war, his reign is remembered for its very influential style of [Hindu art](/wiki/Hindu_art), [literature](/wiki/Hindu_literature), [culture](/wiki/Hindu_culture) and [science](/wiki/Hindu_science). Some excellent works of Hindu art such as the panels at the [Dashavatara Temple](/wiki/Dashavatara_Temple,_Deogarh) in [Deogarh](/wiki/Deogarh,_Uttar_Pradesh) serve to illustrate the magnificence of Gupta art during his reign. Above all, it was the synthesis of elements that gave Gupta art its distinctive flavour. During this period, the Guptas were supportive of thriving [Buddhist](/wiki/Buddhism) and [Jain](/wiki/Jain) cultures as well, and for this reason, there is also a long history of non-Hindu [Gupta period art](/wiki/Gupta_period_art). In particular, Gupta period Buddhist art was to be influential in most of East and Southeast Asia. Many advances were recorded by the Chinese scholar and traveller [Faxian](/wiki/Faxian) in his diary and published afterwards.

The court of Chandragupta II was made even more illustrious by the fact that it was graced by the *Navaratna* (Nine Jewels), a group of nine who excelled in the literary arts. Among these men was [Kālidāsa](/wiki/K%C4%81lid%C4%81sa), whose works dwarfed the works of many other literary geniuses, not only in his own age but in the years to come. Kalidasa was mainly known for his subtle exploitation of the *shringara* (romantic) element in his verse.

#### Campaigns against foreign tribes

Sculpture of Vishnu (red sandstone), 5th century CE.

The 4th century [Sanskrit](/wiki/Sanskrit) poet [Kalidasa](/wiki/Kalidasa) credits Chandragupta Vikramaditya with conquering about twenty-one kingdoms, both in and outside India. After finishing his campaign in East and West India, Vikramaditya (Chandragupta II) proceeded northwards, subjugated the [Parasika](/wiki/Parasika_kingdom), then the [Huna](/wiki/Huna_people) and [Kamboja](/wiki/Kambojas) tribes located in the west and east [Oxus](/wiki/Oxus) valleys respectively. Thereafter, the king proceeded into the [Himalaya](/wiki/Himalaya) mountains to reduce the mountain tribes of the [Kinnaras](/wiki/Kinnara_kingdom), [Kiratas](/wiki/Kiratas), as well as India proper. In one of his works Kalidasa also credits him with the removal of the [Sakas](/wiki/Saka) from the country. He wrote 'Wasn't it Vikramaditya who drove the Sakas out from the lovely city of [Ujjain](/wiki/Ujjain)?'.

The *Brihatkathamanjari* of the [Kashmiri](/wiki/Kashmiris) writer [Kshemendra](/wiki/Kshemendra) states, King Vikramaditya (Chandragupta II) had "unburdened the sacred earth of the [barbarians](/wiki/Barbarian) like the Sakas, [Mlecchas](/wiki/Mleccha), [Kambojas](/wiki/Kambojas), [Greeks](/wiki/Yavana), [Tusharas](/wiki/Tushara_kingdom), [Saka-Greeks](/wiki/Parasika_kingdom), [Hunas](/wiki/Huna_people), and others, by annihilating these sinful Mlecchas completely".

#### Faxian

[Faxian](/wiki/Faxian), a Chinese [Buddhist monk](/wiki/Buddhist_monk), was one of the pilgrims who visited India during the reign of the Gupta emperor [Chandragupta II](/wiki/Chandragupta_II). He started his journey from China in 399 CE and reached India in 405 CE. During his stay in India up to 411 CE, he went on a pilgrimage to [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura), [Kannauj](/wiki/Kannauj), [Kapilavastu](/wiki/Kapilavastu_(ancient_city)), [Kushinagar](/wiki/Kushinagar), [Vaishali](/wiki/Vaishali_(ancient_city)), [Pataliputra](/wiki/Pataliputra), [Kashi](/wiki/Varanasi), and [Rajagriha](/wiki/Rajagriha), and made careful observations about the empire's conditions. Faxian was pleased with the mildness of administration. The penal code was mild, and offences were punished by fines only. From his accounts, the Gupta Empire was a prosperous period. His writings form one of the most important sources for the history of this period.

Faxian on reaching [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura) comments––

> "The snow and heat are finely tempered, and there is neither hoarfrost nor snow. The people are numerous and happy. They have not to register their households. Only those who cultivate the royal land have to pay (a portion of) the gain from it. If they want to go, they go. If they want to stay on, they stay on. The king governs without decapitation or (other) corporal punishments. Criminals are simply fined according to circumstances. Even in cases of repeated attempts at wicked rebellion, they only have their right-hand cut off. The king's bodyguards & attendants all have salaries. Throughout the whole country, the people do not kill any living creature, not drink any intoxicating liquor, nor eat onions or garlic."

### Kumaragupta I

Main article: [Kumaragupta I](/wiki/Kumaragupta_I)

Silver coin of the Gupta Emperor [Kumaragupta I](/wiki/Kumaragupta_I) (Coin of his Western territories, design derived from the [Western Satraps](/wiki/Western_Satraps)).
*Obv*: Bust of king with crescents, with traces of corrupt Greek script.
*Rev*: [Garuda](/wiki/Garuda) standing facing with spread wings. Brahmi legend: *Parama-bhagavata [rajadhiraja](/wiki/Rajadhiraja) Sri Kumaragupta Mahendraditya*.

Chandragupta II was succeeded by his second son [Kumaragupta I](/wiki/Kumara_Gupta_I), born of *Mahadevi* Dhruvasvamini. Kumaragupta I assumed the title, *Mahendraditya*. He ruled until 455. Towards the end of his reign a tribe in the [Narmada](/wiki/Narmada) valley, the [Pushyamitras](/wiki/Pushyamitras), rose in power to threaten the empire. The [Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites) as well probably confronted the Gupta Empire towards the end of the rule of Kumaragupta I, as his son [Skandagupta](/wiki/Skandagupta) mentions in the [Bhitari pillar inscription](/wiki/Bhitari_pillar_inscription_of_Skandagupta) his efforts at reshaping a country in disarray, through reorganisation and military victories over the Pushyamitras and the [Hunas](/wiki/Hunas).

He was the founder of [Nalanda University](/wiki/Nalanda_(university)) which on 15 July 2016 was declared as a [UNESCO world heritage site](/wiki/UNESCO_world_heritage_site). Kumaragupta I was also a worshipper of [Kartikeya](/wiki/Kartikeya).

### Skandagupta

Main article: [Skandagupta](/wiki/Skandagupta)

[Skandagupta](/wiki/Skandagupta), son and successor of Kumaragupta I is generally considered to be the last of the great Gupta emperors. He assumed the titles of *Vikramaditya* and *Kramaditya*. He defeated the Pushyamitra threat, but then was faced with invading [Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites) (sometimes described as the [Hephthalites](/wiki/Hephthalite) or "White Huns", known in India as the [Sweta Huna](/wiki/Huna_(people))), from the northwest.

He repelled a *Huna* attack around 455 CE, but the expense of the wars drained the empire's resources and contributed to its decline. The Bhitari Pillar inscription of [Skandagupta](/wiki/Skandagupta), the successor of Chandragupta, recalls the near annihilation of the Gupta Empire following the attacks of the [Kidarites](/wiki/Kidarites). The Kidarites seem to have retained the western part of the Gupta Empire.

Skandagupta died in 467 and was succeeded by his agnate brother [Purugupta](/wiki/Purugupta).

### Decline of the empire

[Jain](/wiki/Jain) [tirthankara](/wiki/Tirthankara) relief [Parshvanatha](/wiki/Parshvanatha) on [Kahaum pillar](/wiki/Kahaum_pillar) erected by person named Madra during the reign of Skandagupta

Following Skandagupta's death, the empire was clearly in decline, and the later Gupta coinage indicates their loss of control over much of western India after 467–469. Skandagupta was followed by [Purugupta](/wiki/Purugupta) (467–473), [Kumaragupta II](/wiki/Kumaragupta_II) (473–476), [Budhagupta](/wiki/Budhagupta) (476–495), [Narasimhagupta](/wiki/Narasimhagupta) (495–530), [Kumaragupta III](/wiki/Kumaragupta_III) (530–540), [Vishnugupta](/wiki/Vishnugupta_(Gupta_Empire)) (540–550), two lesser known kings namely, [Vainyagupta](/wiki/Vainyagupta) and [Bhanugupta](/wiki/Bhanugupta).

In the late 490's the [Alchon Huns](/wiki/Alchon_Huns) under [Toramana](/wiki/Toramana) and [Mihirakula](/wiki/Mihirakula) broke through the Gupta defences in the northwest, and much of the empire in the northwest was overrun by the Huns by 500. According to some scholars the empire disintegrated under the attacks of [Toramana](/wiki/Toramana) and his successor [Mihirakula](/wiki/Mihirakula). It appears from inscriptions that the Guptas, although their power was much diminished, continued to resist the Huns. The Hun invader Toramana was defeated by [Bhanugupta](/wiki/Bhanugupta) in 510. The Huns were defeated and driven out of India in 528 by King [Yashodharman](/wiki/Yashodharman) from Malwa, and possibly Gupta emperor [Narasimhagupta](/wiki/Narasimhagupta).

These invasions, although only spanning a few decades, had long term effects on India, and in a sense brought an end to [Classical Indian civilisation](/wiki/Classical_India). Soon after the invasions, the Gupta Empire, already weakened by these invasions and the rise of local rulers such as [Yashodharman](/wiki/Yashodharman), ended as well. Following the invasions, northern India was left in disarray, with numerous smaller Indian powers emerging after the crumbling of the Guptas. The Huna invasions are said to have seriously damaged India's trade with [Europe](/wiki/Europe) and [Central Asia](/wiki/Central_Asia). In particular, [Indo-Roman trade relations](/wiki/Indo-Roman_trade_relations), which the Gupta Empire had greatly benefited from. The Guptas had been exporting numerous luxury products such as [silk](/wiki/Silk), leather goods, fur, iron products, [ivory](/wiki/Ivory), [pearl](/wiki/Pearl), and pepper from centres such as [Nasik](/wiki/Nasik), [Paithan](/wiki/Paithan), [Pataliputra](/wiki/Pataliputra), and [Benares](/wiki/Benares). The Huna invasion probably disrupted these trade relations and the tax revenues that came with them.

Furthermore, Indian urban culture was left in decline, and [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism), gravely weakened by the destruction of monasteries and the killing of monks by the hand of the vehemently anti-Buddhist [Shaivist](/wiki/Shaivism) Huna king [Mihirakula](/wiki/Mihirakula), started to collapse. Great centres of learning were destroyed, such as the city of [Taxila](/wiki/Taxila), bringing cultural regression. During their rule of 60 years, the Alchons are said to have altered the hierarchy of ruling families and the Indian [caste system](/wiki/Caste_system_in_India). For example, the Hunas are often said to have become the precursors of the [Rajputs](/wiki/Rajputs).

Political fragmentation of South Asia after the retreat of the [Alchon Huns](/wiki/Alchon_Huns) to the northwest and the end of the Gupta Empire, c. 600 CE

The succession of the 6th-century Guptas is not entirely clear, but the tail end recognised ruler of the dynasty's main line was King [Vishnugupta](/wiki/Vishnugupta_(Gupta_Empire)), reigning from 540 to 550. In addition to the Huna invasion, the factors, which contribute to the decline of the empire include competition from the [Vakatakas](/wiki/Vakatakas) and the rise of [Yashodharman](/wiki/Yashodharman) in [Malwa](/wiki/Malwa).

The last known inscription by a Gupta emperor is from the reign of Vishnugupta (the [Damudarpur](/wiki/Damudarpur) copper-plate inscription), in which he makes a land grant in the area of [Kotivarsha](/wiki/Kotivarsha) ([Bangarh](/wiki/Bangarh) in [West Bengal](/wiki/West_Bengal)) in 542/543 CE. This follows the occupation of most of northern and central India by the [Aulikara](/wiki/Aulikara) King Yashodharman c. 532 CE.

Archaeologist Shanker Sharma concluded, based on a 2019 study, that the cause of the Gupta Empire's downfall was a devastating flood which happened around the middle of the 6th century in [Uttar Pradesh](/wiki/History_of_Uttar_Pradesh) and [Bihar](/wiki/History_of_Bihar).

### Post-Gupta successor dynasties

In the heart of the former Gupta Empire, in the Gangetic region, the Guptas were succeeded by the [Maukhari dynasty](/wiki/Maukhari_dynasty) and the [Pushyabhuti dynasty](/wiki/Pushyabhuti_dynasty). The coinage of the Maukharis and Pushyabhutis followed the silver coin type of the Guptas, with portrait of the ruler in profile (although facing in the reverse direction compared to the Guptas, a possible symbol of antagonism) and the peacock on the reverse, the Brahmi legend being kept except for the name of the ruler.

In the western regions, they were succeeded by [Gurjaradesa](/wiki/Gurjaradesa), the [Gurjara-Pratiharas](/wiki/Gurjara-Pratihara_dynasty), and later the [Chaulukya](/wiki/Chaulukya_dynasty)-[Paramara](/wiki/Paramara_dynasty) dynasties, who issued so-called [Indo-Sasanian coinage](/wiki/Indo-Sasanian_coinage), on the model of the [coinage of the Sasanian Empire](/wiki/Sasanian_coinage), which had been introduced in India by the Alchon Huns.

## Military

An 8 gm gold coin featuring [Chandragupta II](/wiki/Chandragupta_II) astride a caparisoned horse with a bow in his left hand

In contrast to the [Maurya Empire](/wiki/Maurya_Empire), the Guptas introduced several military innovations to Indian warfare. Chief among these was the use of [siege engines](/wiki/Siege_engine), heavy [cavalry archers](/wiki/Mounted_archery) and heavy sword cavalry. [Heavy cavalry](/wiki/Heavy_cavalry) formed the core of the Gupta Army and were supported by the traditional Indian Army elements of [war elephants](/wiki/War_elephant) and [light infantry](/wiki/Light_infantry).

The utilisation of horse archers in the Gupta period is evidenced on the coinage of [Chandragupta II](/wiki/Chandragupta_II), [Kumaragupta I](/wiki/Kumaragupta_I) and Prakasaditya (postulated to be [Purugupta](/wiki/Purugupta)) that depicts the kings as horse-archers.

There is a paucity of contemporary sources detailing the tactical operations of the Imperial Gupta Army. The best extant information comes from the Sanskrit mahakavya (epic poem) [Raghuvaṃśa](/wiki/Raghuva%E1%B9%83%C5%9Ba) written by the Classical Sanskrit writer and dramatist [Kalidasa](/wiki/Kalidasa). Many modern scholars put forward the view that Kalidasa lived from the reign of Chandragupta II to the reign of [Skandagupta](/wiki/Skandagupta) and that the campaigns of Raghu – his protagonist in the Raghuvaṃśa – reflect those of Chandragupta II. In Canto IV of the Raghuvamsa, Kalidasa relates how the king's forces clash against the powerful, cavalry-centric, forces of the Persians and later the Yavanas (probably Huns) in the North-West. Here he makes special mention of the use horse-archers in the king's army and that the horses needed much rest after the hotly contested battles. The five arms of the Gupta military included infantry, cavalry, [chariotry](/wiki/Ratha), [elephantry](/wiki/War_elephant) and [ships](/wiki/History_of_the_Indian_Navy). *Gunaighar* copper plate inscription of [Vainya Gupta](/wiki/Vainyagupta) mentions ships but not chariots.

## Religion

[Dharmachakra Pravartana Buddha at Sarnath](/wiki/Dharmachakra_Pravartana_Buddha_at_Sarnath) from the Gupta era, 5th century CE

The Guptas were traditionally a [Hindu](/wiki/Hindu) dynasty. They were patronizers of [Brahmanism](/wiki/Brahmanism) and allowed followers of [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism) and [Jainism](/wiki/Jainism) to practice their religions. [Sanchi](/wiki/Sanchi) remained an important centre of Buddhism. [Kumaragupta I](/wiki/Kumaragupta_I) (455 CE) is said to have founded [Nalanda](/wiki/Nalanda). Modern genetic studies indicate that it was during the Gupta period that Indian caste groups ceased to intermarry (started practising/enforcing [endogamy](/wiki/Endogamy)).

Some later rulers however seem to have especially promoted [Buddhism](/wiki/Buddhism). [Narasimhagupta Baladitya](/wiki/Narasimhagupta_Baladitya) (c. 495–?), according to contemporary writer [Paramartha](/wiki/Paramartha), was brought up under the influence of the [Mahayanist](/wiki/Mahayanist) philosopher, [Vasubandhu](/wiki/Vasubandhu). He built a [sangharama](/wiki/Sangharama) at [Nalanda](/wiki/Nalanda) and also a 300 ft (91 m) high [vihara](/wiki/Vihara) with a [Buddha statue](/wiki/Buddha_statue) within which, according to [Xuanzang](/wiki/Xuanzang), resembled the "great Vihara built under the [Bodhi tree](/wiki/Bodhi_tree)". According to the *[Manjushrimulakalpa](/wiki/Manjushrimulakalpa)* (c. 800 CE), King Narasimhsagupta became a Buddhist monk, and left the world through meditation ([Dhyana](/wiki/Dhy%C4%81na_in_Buddhism)). The Chinese monk [Xuanzang](/wiki/Xuanzang) also noted that Narasimhagupta Baladitya's son, Vajra, who commissioned a sangharama as well, "possessed a heart firm in faith".

The Gupta Empire and other polities c. 420 CE.

A study of the epigraphical records of the Gupta Empire shows that there was a hierarchy of administrative divisions from top to bottom. It was divided into 26 provinces, which were called *Bhukti*, *Desha* or *Rajya*. Provinces were also divided into *[vishayas](/wiki/Vishaya)* or *pradeshas* (districts) and put under the control of *Vishayapati*s (district [lords](/wiki/Pati_(title))). A *Vishayapati* administered the *Vishaya* with the help of the *Adhikarana* (council of representatives), which comprised four representatives: *Nagarasreshesthi*, *Sarthavaha*, *Prathamakulika* and *Prathama Kayastha*. A part of the *Vishaya* was called *Vithi*. The Gupta also had trading links with the [Sassanid](/wiki/Sasanian_Empire) and [Byzantine](/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) Empires. The four-fold varna system was observed under the Gupta period but occupations were not rigidly constrained by caste. Some Brahmins followed non-Brahmanical professions. Kshatriyas were often involved in trade and commerce.

### Urban centres

Gupta administration proved to be highly conducive for the rapid growth of urban centres. The principal and original capital of the Gupta Empire is regarded to be [Pataliputra](/wiki/Pataliputra)
In the fifth century, the capital was moved to [Ayodhya](/wiki/Ayodhya) under either [Kumaragupta](/wiki/Kumaragupta) or [Skandagupta](/wiki/Skandagupta). [Chandragupta Vikramaditya](/wiki/Chandragupta_Vikramaditya) took personal interest in the development of [Ujjain](/wiki/Ujjain) as a major cultural center after its conquest. [Kāśī](/wiki/K%C4%81%C5%9B%C4%AB) is sometimes considered by some scholars to have been a capital, either as the original capital, the principal late 6th-century capital or a minor later capital. The Chinese author [Faxian](/wiki/Faxian) described [Magadha](/wiki/Magadha_(Mahajanapada)) as a prosperous country with rich towns and large populations.

## Legacy

### Mathematics

[Indian mathematics](/wiki/Indian_mathematics) flourished in the Gupta Empire. The [Indian numerals](/wiki/Indian_numerals) which were the first [positional](/wiki/Positional) [base 10](/wiki/Base_10) [numeral systems](/wiki/Numeral_systems) in the world originated from Gupta India. The [Surya Siddhanta](/wiki/Surya_Siddhanta) contains the Sine table. [Aryabhata](/wiki/Aryabhata) wrote the *[Aryabhatiya](/wiki/Aryabhatiya)*, making significant contributions to mathematics including developing a [Place value system](/wiki/Place_value_system), an approximation of π of 4 decimal places, trigonometric functions, and [Squared triangular numbers](/wiki/Squared_triangular_number). [Varāhamihira](/wiki/Var%C4%81hamihira) wrote the *[Pancha Siddhanta](/wiki/Pancha-siddhantika)* developing various formulas relating [sine and cosine](/wiki/Sine_and_cosine) functions. [Yativṛṣabha](/wiki/Yativ%E1%B9%9B%E1%B9%A3abha) made contributions on units of measurement. [Virahanka](/wiki/Virahanka) described [Fibonacci numbers](/wiki/Fibonacci_sequence).

### Astronomy

[Indian astronomy](/wiki/Indian_astronomy) also saw progress in this era. The [names of the seven days in a week](/wiki/Hindu_calendar#Weekday/Vāsara) appeared at the start of the Gupta period based on [Hindu deities](/wiki/Hindu_deities) and [planets](/wiki/Navagraha) corresponding to the Roman names. [Aryabhata](/wiki/Aryabhata) made several contributions such as assigning the start of each day to midnight. the earth's rotation on its axis, westward motion of the stars. Aryabhata also mentioned that reflected sunlight is the cause behind the shining of the Moon. In his book, Aryabhata, he suggested that the Earth was sphere, containing a circumference of 24,835 miles (39,967 km). [Varāhamihira](/wiki/Var%C4%81hamihira) approximates the method for determination of the meridian direction from any three positions of the shadow using a [gnomon](/wiki/Gnomon).

### Medicine

The [Sushruta Samhita](/wiki/Sushruta_Samhita), which is a Sanskrit redaction text on all of the major concepts of [Ayurveda](/wiki/Ayurveda) medicine with innovative chapters on surgery, dates to the Gupta period.

### Metallurgy and Engineering

The Gupta bronze [Helgö Buddha](/wiki/Helg%C3%B6_Buddha) reached [Sweden](/wiki/Sweden) along the [Silk Road](/wiki/Silk_Road), between the 6th and 8th century CE.

The [Iron Pillar of Delhi](/wiki/Iron_pillar_of_Delhi) is highly resistant to [corrosion](/wiki/Corrosion) . The corrosion resistance results from an even layer of [crystalline](/wiki/Water_of_crystallization) [iron(III)](/wiki/Iron(III)) hydrogen phosphate [hydrate](/wiki/Hydrate) forming on the high-[phosphorus](/wiki/Phosphorus)-content iron, which serves to protect it from the effects of the corrosion The earliest evidence of the [cotton gin](/wiki/Cotton_gin) was found in the fifth century, in the form of [Buddhist](/wiki/Buddhist) paintings depicting a single-roller gin in the [Ajanta Caves](/wiki/Ajanta_Caves). The gins consisted of a single roller made of iron or wood and a flat piece of stone or wood.

### Education

Various [Mahavihara](/wiki/Mahavihara) operated throughout the Gupta Empire serving as centuries of education. Nalanda played a vital role in promoting the patronage of arts and academics during the 5th and 6th century CE.

### Literature

The highest point of [Sanskrit literature](/wiki/Sanskrit_literature) is also said to have belonged to this period. [Harisena](/wiki/Harisena) was an early writer of [Kāvya](/wiki/K%C4%81vya) poetry. his works include *Apabramsa Dharmapariksa*, *Karpuraprakara* (*Suktavall*), the medical treatise *Jagatsundari-Yogamaladhikara*, *Yasodharacanta*, *Astahnikakatha* and *Brhatkathakosa*. [Amarasimha](/wiki/Amarasimha) wrote various on Sanskrit grammar. [Kalidasa](/wiki/Kalidasa), a playwright, wrote plays such as the [Abhijnanashakuntalam](/wiki/Abhijnanashakuntalam) and [Shakuntala](/wiki/Shakuntala_(play)). [Bhartṛhari](/wiki/Bhart%E1%B9%9Bhari) published major works including the *[Trikāṇḍī](/wiki/Trik%C4%81%E1%B9%87%E1%B8%8D%C4%AB)* and *[Śatakatraya](/wiki/%C5%9Aatakatraya).*

### Leisure

[Chess](/wiki/Chess) is said to have developed in this period. Its early form in the 6th century, *[*caturaṅga*](/wiki/Chaturanga)*, which translates as "four divisions [of the military]" ([infantry](/wiki/Infantry), [cavalry](/wiki/Cavalry), [chariotry](/wiki/Ratha), and [elephantry](/wiki/War_elephant)), was represented by the pieces that would evolve into the modern pawn, knight, rook, and bishop respectively. Doctors also invented several medical instruments, and even performed surgical operations. The ancient Gupta text [Kama Sutra](/wiki/Kama_Sutra) by the Indian scholar [Vatsyayana](/wiki/Vatsyayana) is widely considered to be the standard work on [human sexual behaviour](/wiki/Human_sexual_behaviour) in Sanskrit literature.

### Art and architecture

Main article: [Gupta art](/wiki/Gupta_art)

- A [tetrastyle](/wiki/Tetrastyle) [prostyle](/wiki/Prostyle) Gupta period temple at [Sanchi](/wiki/Sanchi) besides the Apsidal hall with [Maurya](/wiki/Maurya) foundation, an example of [Buddhist architecture](/wiki/Buddhist_architecture) and [Hindu architecture](/wiki/Hindu_architecture). 5th century CE.
- [Pataini temple](/wiki/Pataini_temple) is a Jain temple built during the Gupta period.

The Gupta period is generally regarded as a classic peak of North [Indian art](/wiki/Indian_art) for all the major religious groups. Although painting was evidently widespread, the surviving works are almost all religious sculptures. The period saw the emergence of the iconic carved stone deity in Hindu art, as well as the Buddha-figure and [Jain](/wiki/Jain) *[tirthankara](/wiki/Tirthankara)* figures, the latter often on a very large scale. The two great centres of sculpture were [Mathura](/wiki/Mathura) and [Gandhara](/wiki/Gandhara), the latter the centre of [Greco-Buddhist art](/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_art). Both exported sculpture to other parts of northern India.

The most famous remaining monuments in a broadly Gupta style, the caves at [Ajanta](/wiki/Ajanta_Caves), [Elephanta](/wiki/Elephanta_Caves), and [Ellora](/wiki/Ellora_Caves) (respectively Buddhist, Hindu, and mixed including Jain) were in fact produced under later dynasties, but primarily reflect the monumentality and balance of Gupta style. Ajanta contains by far the most significant survivals of painting from this and the surrounding periods, showing a mature form which had probably had a long development, mainly in painting palaces. The Hindu [Udayagiri Caves](/wiki/Udayagiri_Caves) actually record connections with the dynasty and its ministers, and the [Dashavatara Temple](/wiki/Vishnu_Temple,_Deogarh) at [Deogarh](/wiki/Deogarh,_Uttar_Pradesh) is a major temple, one of the earliest to survive, with important sculpture.
