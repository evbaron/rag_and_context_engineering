# Robert Zemeckis

- **Other name:** Bob Zemeckis

**Robert Lee Zemeckis** (born May 14, 1951), sometimes referred to as **Bob Zemeckis**, is an American filmmaker. Known for directing and producing a range of successful and influential films that often blend cutting-edge [visual effects](/wiki/Visual_effects) with storytelling, he has received accolades such as two [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Award) and a [Golden Globe Award](/wiki/Golden_Globe_Award), as well as nominations for five [British Academy Film Awards](/wiki/British_Academy_Film_Awards) and a [Daytime Emmy Award](/wiki/Daytime_Emmy_Award).

Zemeckis gained some recognition for his short film *A Field of Honor* (1973), which earned him a [Student Academy Award](/wiki/Student_Academy_Awards) for Special Jury Prize at USC. He started his career directing the comedy films *[I Wanna Hold Your Hand](/wiki/I_Wanna_Hold_Your_Hand_(film))* (1978), *[Used Cars](/wiki/Used_Cars)* (1980), and *[Romancing the Stone](/wiki/Romancing_the_Stone)* (1984). He gained prominence directing the sci-fi comedy *[Back to the Future](/wiki/Back_to_the_Future_(franchise))* trilogy (1985–1990), the fantasy comedy *[Who Framed Roger Rabbit](/wiki/Who_Framed_Roger_Rabbit)* (1988), and the comedy–drama *[Forrest Gump](/wiki/Forrest_Gump)* (1994), the latter of which earned him the [Academy Award for Best Director](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Director). He is one of only a few people to win Academy Awards for both student and competitive work.

Zemeckis has also directed the satirical black comedy *[Death Becomes Her](/wiki/Death_Becomes_Her)* (1992), the science fiction film *[Contact](/wiki/Contact_(1997_American_film))* (1997), and the drama films *[Cast Away](/wiki/Cast_Away)* (2000), *[Flight](/wiki/Flight_(2012_film))* (2012), *[The Walk](/wiki/The_Walk_(2015_film))* (2015), and *[Allied](/wiki/Allied_(film))* (2016). His exploration of [motion capture](/wiki/Motion_capture) techniques is seen in the animated films *[The Polar Express](/wiki/The_Polar_Express_(film))* (2004) and *[A Christmas Carol](/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol_(2009_film))* (2009), as well as the action fantasy drama *[Beowulf](/wiki/Beowulf_(2007_film))* (2007) and the drama *[Welcome to Marwen](/wiki/Welcome_to_Marwen)* (2018). He has collaborated with film composer [Alan Silvestri](/wiki/Alan_Silvestri) since 1984, and directed [Tom Hanks](/wiki/Tom_Hanks) in five films.

## Early life

Robert Lee Zemeckis was born in [Chicago](/wiki/Chicago) on May 14, 1951, the son of Italian-American mother Rosa (née Nespeca) and Lithuanian-American father Alphonse Zemeckis. He grew up in a [working-class](/wiki/Working_class) family on Chicago's [South Side](/wiki/South_Side,_Chicago), where he attended a [Catholic](/wiki/Catholic) grade school and [Fenger Academy High School](/wiki/Fenger_Academy_High_School_(Chicago)). He said of his childhood, "The truth was that in my family there was no art. I mean, there was no music, there were no books, there was no theater ... the only thing I had that was inspirational was television."

As a child, Zemeckis loved television and was fascinated by his parents' [8 mm film](/wiki/8_mm_film) home movie camera. Starting off by filming family events like birthdays and holidays, he gradually began producing narrative films with his friends that incorporated [stop-motion](/wiki/Stop-motion) work and other visual effects. He remained an avid television viewer, about which he later said, "You hear so much about the problems with television but I think that it saved my life." Television gave him his first glimpse of a world outside of his upbringing, specifically after he learned that [film schools](/wiki/Film_school) existed from an episode of *[The Tonight Show Starring Johnny Carson](/wiki/The_Tonight_Show_Starring_Johnny_Carson)*. After seeing *[Bonnie and Clyde](/wiki/Bonnie_and_Clyde_(film))* with his father, he decided that he wanted to go to film school. His parents disapproved of the idea. He later clarified that they disapproved "only in the sense that they were concerned" because "this was the kind of dream that really was impossible" for family and my friends in the world he grew up in. He said, "My parents would sit there and say, 'Don't you see where you come from? You can't be a movie director.' I guess maybe some of it I felt I had to do in spite of them, too."

Zemeckis first attended [Northern Illinois University](/wiki/Northern_Illinois_University) (NIU) in [DeKalb, Illinois](/wiki/DeKalb,_Illinois), and gained early experience in film as a film cutter for NBC News in Chicago during a summer break. He also edited commercials in his home state. He applied to transfer from NIU to the [University of Southern California](/wiki/University_of_Southern_California)'s (USC) [School of Cinematic Arts](/wiki/USC_School_of_Cinematic_Arts) in [Los Angeles](/wiki/Los_Angeles), where he went into the Film School on the strength of an essay and a music video based on a [Beatles](/wiki/The_Beatles) song. After waiting to hear back from the university, he called them directly and was told he had been rejected because of his average grades. He gave an "impassioned plea" to the university official during the phone call, promising to go to summer school and improve his studies, and eventually convinced the school to accept him.

Arriving at USC that fall, Zemeckis encountered a program that he later described as being "made up of a bunch of hippies [and] considered an embarrassment by the university". The classes were difficult, with professors constantly stressing how hard the film industry was. Zemeckis remembered not being much fazed by this, citing the "healthy cynicism" that had been bred into him from his Chicago upbringing. He met a fellow student, writer [Bob Gale](/wiki/Bob_Gale), who later recalled, "The graduate students at USC had this veneer of intellectualism ... so Bob and I gravitated toward one another because we wanted to make Hollywood movies. We weren't interested in the [French New Wave](/wiki/French_New_Wave). We were interested in [Clint Eastwood](/wiki/Clint_Eastwood) and [James Bond](/wiki/James_Bond) and [Walt Disney](/wiki/Walt_Disney), because that's how we grew up." Zemeckis graduated from USC in 1973, and he and Gale co-wrote the unproduced screenplays *Tank* and *[Bordello of Blood](/wiki/Bordello_of_Blood)*, which they pitched to [John Milius](/wiki/John_Milius), the latter of which was later developed into a film that was released in 1996.

## Career

### 1978–1984: Early comedy films

As a result of winning a [Student Academy Award](/wiki/Student_Academy_Awards) at USC for his film *A Field of Honor*, Zemeckis came to the attention of [Steven Spielberg](/wiki/Steven_Spielberg). Spielberg said, "He barged right past my secretary and sat me down and showed me this student film ... and I thought it was spectacular, with police cars and a riot, all dubbed to [Elmer Bernstein](/wiki/Elmer_Bernstein)'s score for *[The Great Escape](/wiki/The_Great_Escape_(film))*." Spielberg became Zemeckis's mentor and executive produced his first two films, both of which Gale and Zemeckis co-wrote.

Spielberg produced *[I Wanna Hold Your Hand](/wiki/I_Wanna_Hold_Your_Hand_(film))* (1978, starring [Nancy Allen](/wiki/Nancy_Allen_(actress))) and *[Used Cars](/wiki/Used_Cars)* (1980, starring [Kurt Russell](/wiki/Kurt_Russell)); both were critical, but not commercial, successes. *I Wanna Hold Your Hand* was the first of several Zemeckis films to incorporate historic figures and celebrities into his movies; he used archival footage and doubles to simulate the presence of the Beatles. After the failure of his first two films, and the Spielberg-directed *[1941](/wiki/1941_(film))* (1979) (written by Gale and Zemeckis), the pair gained a reputation for writing "scripts that everyone thought were great [but] somehow didn't translate into movies people wanted to see."

As a result of his reputation within the industry, Zemeckis had trouble finding work in the early 1980s, though he and Gale kept busy. They wrote scripts for other directors, including *Car Pool* for [Brian De Palma](/wiki/Brian_De_Palma) and *Growing Up* for Spielberg; neither ended up getting made. Another Zemeckis–Gale project, *[Back to the Future](/wiki/Back_to_the_Future)*, about a teenager who travels back in time to the 1950s, was turned down by every major studio. The director was jobless until [Michael Douglas](/wiki/Michael_Douglas) hired him in 1984 to direct *[Romancing the Stone](/wiki/Romancing_the_Stone)*. A romantic adventure starring Douglas and [Kathleen Turner](/wiki/Kathleen_Turner), *Stone* was expected to flop (to the point that, after viewing a rough cut of the film, the producers of the then-in-the-works *[Cocoon](/wiki/Cocoon_(film))* fired Zemeckis as director), but the film became a sleeper hit. While working on *Romancing the Stone*, Zemeckis met composer [Alan Silvestri](/wiki/Alan_Silvestri), who has scored all his subsequent pictures.

### 1985–1999: Breakthrough and acclaim

Zemeckis in January 1997

After *Romancing the Stone*, Zemeckis had the clout to direct his time-traveling screenplay. Starring [Michael J. Fox](/wiki/Michael_J._Fox), [Lea Thompson](/wiki/Lea_Thompson), [Crispin Glover](/wiki/Crispin_Glover), and [Christopher Lloyd](/wiki/Christopher_Lloyd), the 1985 film was wildly successful upon its release and was followed by two sequels, released as *[Back to the Future Part II](/wiki/Back_to_the_Future_Part_II)* in 1989 and *[Back to the Future Part III](/wiki/Back_to_the_Future_Part_III)* in 1990. Before the *Back to the Future* sequels were released, Zemeckis collaborated with Disney and directed another film, the madcap 1940s-set mystery *[Who Framed Roger Rabbit](/wiki/Who_Framed_Roger_Rabbit)*, which combined [live-action](/wiki/Live-action) and [traditional animation](/wiki/Traditional_animation); its $70 million budget made it one of the most expensive films made up to that point. The film was both a financial and critical success and won three [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Award). In 1990, Zemeckis commented, when asked if he would want to make non-comedies, "I would like to be able to do everything. Just now, though, I'm too restless to do anything that's not really zany."

In 1992, Zemeckis directed the black comedy *[Death Becomes Her](/wiki/Death_Becomes_Her)*, starring [Meryl Streep](/wiki/Meryl_Streep), [Goldie Hawn](/wiki/Goldie_Hawn), and [Bruce Willis](/wiki/Bruce_Willis). Although his next film would have some comedic elements, it was Zemeckis's first with dramatic elements and was also his biggest commercial success to date, *[Forrest Gump](/wiki/Forrest_Gump)*. Starring [Tom Hanks](/wiki/Tom_Hanks) in the title role, *Forrest Gump* tells the story of a man with a low [I.Q.](/wiki/I.Q.), who unwittingly participates in some of the major events of the twentieth century, falls in love, and interacts with several major historical figures in the process. The film grossed $677 million worldwide and became the top-grossing American film of 1994; it won six Academy Awards, including [Best Picture](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Picture), [Best Actor](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Actor) for Hanks, and [Best Director](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Director) for Zemeckis. From this point, Hanks and Zemeckis became frequent collaborators. In 1997, Zemeckis directed *[Contact](/wiki/Contact_(1997_American_film))*, a long-gestating project based on [Carl Sagan](/wiki/Carl_Sagan)'s 1985 [novel of the same name](/wiki/Contact_(novel)). The film centers on Eleanor Arroway ([Jodie Foster](/wiki/Jodie_Foster)), who believes she has made contact with extraterrestrials. In the early 1990s, he founded South Side Amusement Company, which later became [ImageMovers](/wiki/ImageMovers).

During this same time period, Zemeckis was an executive producer of [HBO](/wiki/HBO)'s *[Tales from the Crypt](/wiki/Tales_from_the_Crypt_(TV_series))* (1989–1996) and directed three episodes. In 1999, Zemeckis donated $5 million towards the Robert Zemeckis Center for Digital Arts at USC, a 35,000-square-foot (3,300 m2) center. When the Center opened in March 2001, Zemeckis spoke in a panel about the future of film, alongside friends Steven Spielberg and [George Lucas](/wiki/George_Lucas). Of those (including Spielberg) who clung to celluloid and disparaged the idea of shooting digitally, Zemeckis said, "These guys are the same ones who have been saying that [LPs](/wiki/LP_album) sound better than CDs. You can argue that until you're blue in the face, but I don't know anyone who's still buying vinyl. The film, as we have traditionally thought of it, is going to be different. But the continuum is man's desire to tell stories around the campfire. The only thing that keeps changing is the campfire." The Robert Zemeckis Center currently hosts many film school classes, much of the [Interactive Media Division](/wiki/USC_Interactive_Media_Division), and [Trojan Vision](/wiki/Trojan_Vision), USC's student television station, which has been voted the number one college television station in the country.

In 1996, Zemeckis had begun developing a project titled *The Castaway* with [Tom Hanks](/wiki/Tom_Hanks) and writer [William Broyles Jr.](/wiki/William_Broyles_Jr.) The story, inspired by *[Robinson Crusoe](/wiki/Robinson_Crusoe)*, is about a man who becomes stranded on a tropical island and undergoes a profound physical and spiritual change. While working on *The Castaway*, Zemeckis also became attached to a [Hitchcockian](/wiki/Alfred_Hitchcock) thriller titled *[What Lies Beneath](/wiki/What_Lies_Beneath)*, the story of a married couple experiencing an extreme case of [empty nest syndrome](/wiki/Empty_nest_syndrome) that was based on an idea by Steven Spielberg. Because Hanks's character needed to undergo a dramatic weight loss over the course of *The Castaway* (retitled *[Cast Away](/wiki/Cast_Away)* for release), Zemeckis decided that the only way to retain the same crew while Hanks lost the weight was to shoot *What Lies Beneath* in between. He shot the first part of *Cast Away* in early 1999, and shot *What Lies Beneath* in fall 1999, completing work on the former in early 2000. Zemeckis later quipped, when asked about shooting two films back-to-back, "I wouldn't recommend it to anyone." *What Lies Beneath*, starring [Harrison Ford](/wiki/Harrison_Ford) and [Michelle Pfeiffer](/wiki/Michelle_Pfeiffer), was released in July 2000 to mixed reviews, but did well at the box office, grossing over $155 million domestically. *Cast Away*, starring Hanks and *[Twister](/wiki/Twister_(1996_film))* actress [Helen Hunt](/wiki/Helen_Hunt), was released that December and grossed $233 million domestically; Hanks received a nomination for the [Academy Award for Best Actor](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Actor) for his portrayal of Chuck Noland.

### 2004–present: Career fluctuations

In 2004, Zemeckis re-collaborated with Hanks for *[The Polar Express](/wiki/The_Polar_Express_(film))*, based on [Chris Van Allsburg](/wiki/Chris_Van_Allsburg)'s [children's book of the same name](/wiki/The_Polar_Express). *The Polar Express* utilized the [computer animation](/wiki/Computer_animation) technique known as [motion capture](/wiki/Motion_capture), where the movements of the actors are captured digitally and used as the basis for the animated characters. As the first film to use motion capture, *The Polar Express* caused *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* to write that, "Whatever critics and audiences make of this movie, from a technical perspective it could mark a turning point in the gradual transition from an analog to a digital cinema." Zemeckis served as an executive producer for *[Monster House](/wiki/Monster_House_(film))* (2006), a family-friendly horror comedy film that used motion capture.

Zemeckis's star on Walk of Fame, Hollywood, LA

In February 2007, Zemeckis and [Walt Disney Studios](/wiki/Walt_Disney_Studios_(production)) chairman [Dick Cook](/wiki/Dick_Cook) announced plans for a new motion capture film company devoted to CGI-created, 3-D movies. The company, [ImageMovers Digital](/wiki/ImageMovers_Digital), created films using the motion capture technology, with Zemeckis directing most of the projects which Disney distributed and marketed worldwide. Zemeckis used the motion capture technology again in his film, *[Beowulf](/wiki/Beowulf_(2007_film))*, to retell the Anglo-Saxon [epic poem of the same name](/wiki/Beowulf). It featured [Ray Winstone](/wiki/Ray_Winstone), [Angelina Jolie](/wiki/Angelina_Jolie), and [Anthony Hopkins](/wiki/Anthony_Hopkins). [Neil Gaiman](/wiki/Neil_Gaiman), who co-wrote the adaptation with [Roger Avary](/wiki/Roger_Avary), described the film as a "cheerfully violent and strange take on the Beowulf legend." The film was released on November 16, 2007, to mostly positive reviews and grossed $196 million worldwide. In July 2007, *[Variety](/wiki/Variety_(magazine))* announced that Zemeckis had written a screenplay for *[A Christmas Carol](/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol_(2009_film))*, based on [Charles Dickens](/wiki/Charles_Dickens)'s 1843 [short story of the same name](/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol), with plans to use motion capture and release it under the aegis of ImageMovers Digital. Zemeckis wrote the script with [Jim Carrey](/wiki/Jim_Carrey) in mind, and Carrey agreed to play a multitude of roles in the film, including [Ebenezer Scrooge](/wiki/Ebenezer_Scrooge) as a young, middle-aged, and old man, and the three ghosts who haunt Scrooge. The film began production in February 2008 and was released on November 6, 2009, to mixed reviews and grossed $325 million at the box office. Actor [Gary Oldman](/wiki/Gary_Oldman) also appeared in the film. Zemeckis is an avid supporter of 3-D Digital Cinema and has stated that since the 3-D presentations of *[Beowulf](/wiki/Beowulf_(2007_film))*, all of his future films would be done in 3-D using digital [motion capture](/wiki/Motion_capture). He has reportedly backed away from that statement and said that the decision to use 3-D will be on a film-by-film basis.

On August 19, 2009, it was reported that Zemeckis and his company were in talks with [Apple Corps Ltd.](/wiki/Apple_Corps) to remake the animated film *[Yellow Submarine](/wiki/Yellow_Submarine_(film))* utilizing motion capture. However, on March 12, 2010, with Zemeckis's biggest Disney ally, former chairman Dick Cook, gone, and amid drastic cost-cutting by the new management team, Disney announced that it was ending its relationship with ImageMovers Digital. The studio's final film, 2011's Zemeckis-produced *[Mars Needs Moms](/wiki/Mars_Needs_Moms)*, was the second-worst box office failure in history, with a net loss of roughly $130 million. Zemeckis made his comeback to live-action filmmaking with *[Flight](/wiki/Flight_(2012_film))*, a 2012 drama for Paramount, starring [Denzel Washington](/wiki/Denzel_Washington). On January 31, 2014, it was announced that a [stage](/wiki/Back_to_the_Future_(musical)) [musical](/wiki/Musical_theatre) adaptation of Zemeckis's first *Back to the Future* film was in production. The show would be co-written by original writers Robert Zemeckis and Bob Gale. According to Gale, the musical would be "true to the spirit of the film without being a slavish remake".

Zemeckis with his wife [Leslie](/wiki/Leslie_Zemeckis) in January 2013

In August 2008, *[IGN](/wiki/IGN)* revealed in an interview with [Philippe Petit](/wiki/Philippe_Petit) that Zemeckis was working with Petit to turn Petit's memoir *To Reach the Clouds* into a feature film. In 2015, he directed *[The Walk](/wiki/The_Walk_(2015_film))*, about Philippe Petit ([Joseph Gordon-Levitt](/wiki/Joseph_Gordon-Levitt)) and his ambition to tightrope walk between the towers of New York City's World Trade Center. [Paramount Pictures](/wiki/Paramount_Pictures) and [New Regency](/wiki/New_Regency) announced in February 2015 that Zemeckis would direct [Brad Pitt](/wiki/Brad_Pitt) in *[Allied](/wiki/Allied_(film))*, a romantic thriller set during World War II. The film was released on November 23, 2016. Next, Zemeckis directed the fantasy drama *[Welcome to Marwen](/wiki/Welcome_to_Marwen)*, starring [Steve Carell](/wiki/Steve_Carell), which was released in December 2018 to mixed reviews and flopped at the box office. Zemeckis's film *[The Witches](/wiki/The_Witches_(2020_film))*, an adaptation of the Roald Dahl [novel of the same name](/wiki/The_Witches_(novel)), premiered on October 22, 2020, on [HBO Max](/wiki/HBO_Max), also to mixed reviews.

On October 18, 2019, it was announced that Zemeckis was in talks to direct Disney's [live-action adaptation](/wiki/Pinocchio_(2022_live-action_film)) of *[Pinocchio](/wiki/Pinocchio_(1940_film))*. Zemeckis was officially announced as the film's director and co-writer of the script in January 2020. In addition, [Tom Hanks](/wiki/Tom_Hanks) was reportedly announced as playing [Geppetto](/wiki/Geppetto) in the film, marking the fourth collaboration with Hanks since *Forrest Gump*, *Cast Away*, and *The Polar Express*. The film was later released, as part of [Disney+ Day](/wiki/Disney%2B_Day) on [Disney+](/wiki/Disney%2B), on September 8, 2022, to highly negative reviews from critics. *Pinocchio* was nominated for six [Razzies](/wiki/Razzies), including [Worst Picture](/wiki/Worst_Picture) and [Worst Director](/wiki/Golden_Raspberry_Award_for_Worst_Director) for Zemeckis ("winning" [Worst Remake](/wiki/Golden_Raspberry_Award_for_Worst_Remake,_Rip-off_or_Sequel)), losing to *[Blonde](/wiki/Blonde_(2022_film))*. Despite this, it also received a [Visual Effects Society Award](/wiki/Visual_Effects_Society_Award) nomination for Outstanding Animated Character in a Photoreal Feature.

On February 17, 2022, Zemeckis signed on to direct *[Here](/wiki/Here_(2024_film))*, an adaptation of the [graphic novel](/wiki/Here_(comics)) by [Richard McGuire](/wiki/Richard_McGuire), with [Tom Hanks](/wiki/Tom_Hanks) set to star and *Forrest Gump* screenwriter [Eric Roth](/wiki/Eric_Roth) working on the screenplay with Zemeckis. On May 11, it was announced that [Robin Wright](/wiki/Robin_Wright) had been cast and that [TriStar Pictures](/wiki/TriStar_Pictures) had acquired distribution rights for the United States, with [Miramax](/wiki/Miramax) handling international sales and production expected to begin in September 2022 for a theatrical release in 2024. On October 31, 2024, Zemeckis, during an appearance on Josh Horowitz's *Happy Sad Confused* podcast to promote *Here*, hinted at his plans to produce and direct a film adaptation of the stage musical version of *Back to the Future* that would also serve as a remake of the original film. Universal has yet to green-light the project after an initial rejection of Zemeckis's pitch to the studio.

## Style and recognition

Zemeckis is regarded as an innovator in [visual effects](/wiki/Visual_effects). His exploration of state-of-the-art visual effects includes the early use of [computer graphics inserted into live-action footage](/wiki/Match_moving)
in *[Back to the Future Part II](/wiki/Back_to_the_Future_Part_II)* (1989) and *Forrest Gump*, the insertion of hand-drawn animation into live-action footage in *Who Framed Roger Rabbit*, and the [motion capture](/wiki/Motion_capture) techniques seen in *[The Polar Express](/wiki/The_Polar_Express_(film))* (2004), *[Beowulf](/wiki/Beowulf_(2007_film))* (2007), *[A Christmas Carol](/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol_(2009_film))* (2009), and *[Welcome to Marwen](/wiki/Welcome_to_Marwen)* (2018). He is known for his collaboration with composer [Alan Silvestri](/wiki/Alan_Silvestri), with whom he has worked since *Romancing the Stone*. [David Thomson](/wiki/David_Thomson_(film_critic)), a prominent film critic, wrote that "no other contemporary director has used visual effects to more dramatic and narrative purpose."

## Personal life

Zemeckis was married to actress [Mary Ellen Trainor](/wiki/Mary_Ellen_Trainor) from 1980 until their divorce in 2000, and they had a son together named Alexander Francis. He described the marriage as difficult to balance with his career. In 2001, he married actress [Leslie Harter](/wiki/Leslie_Zemeckis) in [Venice](/wiki/Venice). They have three children together. They reside in [Santa Barbara, California](/wiki/Santa_Barbara,_California), and own a villa in [Tuscany](/wiki/Tuscany) which is located on the ruins of a 10th century castle.

Zemeckis has discussed sacrificing his personal life for his career over a long period of time, stating, "I won an Academy Award when I was 42 years old, but I paid for it with my 20s. That decade of my life from film school 'til 30 was nothing but work, nothing but absolute, driving work. I had no money. I had no life."

Zemeckis is a [private pilot](/wiki/Private_pilot) who has logged approximately 1,600 hours of flight time as of October 2012, flying a [Cirrus SR22](/wiki/Cirrus_SR22).

According to campaign donation records, Zemeckis has frequently contributed to [Democratic](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)) politicians and [PACs](/wiki/Political_action_committee) that support the interests of aircraft owners and pilots, [family planning](/wiki/Family_planning), and a group that advocates for women in Hollywood.
