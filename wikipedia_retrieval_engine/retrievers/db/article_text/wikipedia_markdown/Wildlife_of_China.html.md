# Wildlife of China

The [giant panda](/wiki/Giant_panda) is [endemic](/wiki/Endemism) to China, where it is an [endangered and protected species](/wiki/List_of_endangered_and_protected_species_of_China).

The [snub-nosed monkey](/wiki/Snub-nosed_monkey), another endangered and endemic species

[China](/wiki/China)'s [vast and diverse landscape](/wiki/Geography_of_China) is home to a profound variety and abundance of wildlife. As of one of 17 [megadiverse countries](/wiki/Megadiverse_countries) in the world, China has, according to one measure, 7,516 species of [vertebrates](/wiki/Vertebrates) including 4,936 fish, 1,269 bird, 562 [mammal](/wiki/List_of_mammals_of_China), 403 reptile and 346 [amphibian](/wiki/Amphibian) species. In terms of the number of species, China ranks third in the world in mammals, eighth in birds, seventh in reptiles and seventh in amphibians.

Many species of animals are [endemic](/wiki/Endemism) to China, including the country's most famous wildlife species, the [giant panda](/wiki/Giant_panda). In all, about one-sixth of mammal species and two-thirds of amphibian species in China are endemic to the country.

Wildlife in China share habitat with and bear acute pressure from the world's second largest population of [humans](/wiki/Human). At least 840 [species are threatened, vulnerable or in danger of local extinction](/wiki/List_of_endangered_and_protected_species_of_China) in China, due mainly to human activity such as habitat destruction, pollution and poaching for food, fur and ingredients for [traditional Chinese medicine](/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_medicine). Endangered wildlife is protected by law, and as of 2005, the country has over 2,349 [nature reserves](/wiki/Protected_areas_of_China), covering a total area of 149.95 million [hectares](/wiki/Hectare) (578,960 [square miles](/wiki/Square_mile)), about 15 percent of China's total land area.

### Primates

China is home to 21 [primate](/wiki/Primate) species including [gibbons](/wiki/Gibbon), [macaques](/wiki/Macaque), [leaf monkeys](/wiki/Leaf_monkey), [gray langurs](/wiki/Gray_langur), [snub-nosed monkeys](/wiki/Snub-nosed_monkey) and [lorises](/wiki/Loris). Most of China's primate species are [endangered](/wiki/List_of_endangered_and_protected_species_of_China). Both apes and monkeys, [particularly gibbons and macaques are prominently featured in Chinese culture, folk religion, art and literature](/wiki/Monkeys_in_Chinese_culture). The [monkey](/wiki/Monkey_(zodiac)) is one of the 12 animals of the [Chinese zodiac](/wiki/Chinese_zodiac).

[Western hoolock gibbons](/wiki/Western_hoolock_gibbon)

A female [lar gibbon](/wiki/Lar_gibbon)

A female [northern white-cheeked gibbon](/wiki/Northern_white-cheeked_gibbon) grooming a male

The only [apes](/wiki/Apes) native to China are [gibbons](/wiki/Gibbon). Gibbons are [tree dwellers](/wiki/Arboreal) which use their long arms to [swing from branches](/wiki/Brachiation). Gibbons can be recognized by their loud calls, with mating pairs often singing together as a duet.

The [Hainan black crested gibbon](/wiki/Hainan_black_crested_gibbon) is among the rarest and [most endangered apes](/wiki/The_World%27s_25_Most_Endangered_Primates). Endemic to the [island of Hainan](/wiki/Hainan_Island), there are fewer than 30 individuals left in the [Bawangling National Nature Reserve](/wiki/Hainan_Bawangling_National_Nature_Reserve). Like many other gibbons, male Hainan black crested gibbons are black in color while females are golden brown. The [eastern black crested gibbon](/wiki/Eastern_black_crested_gibbon) is nearly as rare with only 20 or so in the [Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region](/wiki/Guangxi_Zhuang_Autonomous_Region) along with 30 in neighboring Vietnam. About 99% of this ape's habitat in China has been lost.

The [black crested gibbon](/wiki/Black_crested_gibbon) is found across a greater swath of [southwestern China](/wiki/Southwestern_China). The [Yunnan lar gibbon](/wiki/Yunnan_lar_gibbon), a subspecies of the [lar or white-handed gibbon](/wiki/Lar_gibbon), might be extinct in China. The animal was last observed by [zoologists](/wiki/Zoologists) in 1988 and its call was last heard by locals in 2002. A survey in November 2007 in the Nangunhe National Nature Reserve yielded no sign of this gibbon.

The [northern white-cheeked gibbon](/wiki/Northern_white-cheeked_gibbon) is nearly extinct in the wilderness of southern [Yunnan](/wiki/Yunnan) where they are hunted by local people as charms of good luck and for their bones which are made into weaving instrument and [chopsticks](/wiki/Chopsticks). As of 2008, a captive population of eight [northern white-cheeked gibbons](/wiki/Northern_white-cheeked_gibbons) was living in the Mengyang Nature Reserve. Two of the individuals were released into the wild but still relied on tourists for food. The [eastern hoolock gibbon](/wiki/Eastern_hoolock_gibbon), which are distinguished by white tufts of hair above the eyebrows, are found in western Yunnan, along the border with [Myanmar](/wiki/Myanmar). The [western hoolock gibbon](/wiki/Western_hoolock_gibbon) might be found in southeastern Tibet. All gibbons in China are Class I protected species.

The most commonly found primates in China are [macaques](/wiki/Macaques), which have oversized cheeks to store food and live in large troops. The range of the [rhesus or common macaque](/wiki/Rhesus_macaques) extends from as far north as the [Taihang Mountains](/wiki/Taihang_Mountains) of Shanxi and down to Hainan. [Tibetan macaques](/wiki/Tibetan_macaques) are often seen at tourist sites such as [Mount Emei](/wiki/Mount_Emei) and [Huangshan](/wiki/Huangshan). [Stump-tailed macaques](/wiki/Stump-tailed_macaque) have distinct red faces and live throughout southern China. The [Formosan rock macaque](/wiki/Formosan_rock_macaque) is endemic to Taiwan. [Assam macaques](/wiki/Assam_macaque) are found in higher elevation areas of southern Tibet and the [Southwest](/wiki/Southwestern_China), and the [northern pig-tailed macaque](/wiki/Northern_pig-tailed_macaque) in Yunnan.
Macaques are Class I protected species in China but their numbers have fallen sharply. [Monkey brain](/wiki/Monkey_brains_(cuisine)) is a delicacy in parts of Guangxi and Guangdong, and macaques are often hunted for food. The [Monpa](/wiki/Monpa_people) and [Lhoba](/wiki/Lhoba_people) people of southern Tibet eat Assam macaques. From 1998 to 2004, the number of rhesus macaques in China fell from 254,000 to about 77,000. Over the same period, the Tibetan macaque population fell by 83% from about 100,000 to only about 17,000.

[Golden snub-nosed monkeys](/wiki/Golden_snub-nosed_monkey)

[François' langurs](/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois%27_langur)

[Capped langur](/wiki/Capped_langur)

[Snub-nosed monkeys](/wiki/Snub-nosed_monkeys) are so named because they have only nostrils and virtually no nose. Four of the five species in the world are found in China, including three that are endemic. All live in mountainous forests at elevations of 1,500–3,400 m above sea level. The [golden snub-nosed monkey](/wiki/Golden_snub-nosed_monkey) is most famous and most widely distributed, with subspecies in Sichuan, Hubei and Shaanxi. The [gray snub-nosed monkey](/wiki/Gray_snub-nosed_monkey) is the most endangered, with about 700 individuals, found only in Guizhou. The [black snub-nosed monkey](/wiki/Black_snub-nosed_monkey) has about 1,700 individuals living in 17 identified groups in Yunnan and eastern Tibet. A small population of Myanmar snub-nosed monkey was found in western Yunnan in 2011.

Other [Old World monkeys](/wiki/Old_World_monkey) in China include the [François' langur](/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois%27_langur), [white-headed langur](/wiki/White-headed_langur), [Phayre's leaf monkey](/wiki/Phayre%27s_leaf_monkey), [capped langur](/wiki/Capped_langur) and [Shortridge's langur](/wiki/Shortridge%27s_langur), which are collectively categorized as [lutungs](/wiki/Trachypithecus) and the [Nepal gray langur](/wiki/Nepal_gray_langur), which is considered a [true langur](/wiki/Semnopithecus). All of these species are endangered. Lutungs, also called leaf monkeys, have relatively short arms, longer legs and long tails along with a hood of hair above their eyes.

[François' langur](/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois%27_langur) is found only in southwest China and northern Vietnam. The range of the white-headed langur is much smaller—only in southern Guangxi and [Cát Bà Island](/wiki/C%C3%A1t_B%C3%A0_Island) in Vietnam. Phayre's leaf monkey is native to Yunnan and a larger swath of Indochina. The capped and Shortridge's langurs live along the Yunnan-Myanmar border. The Nepal gray langur is larger than the lutungs and found in southern Tibet.

[Pygmy slow loris](/wiki/Pygmy_slow_loris)

[Bengal slow loris](/wiki/Bengal_slow_loris)

Whereas apes and monkeys are grouped as [haplorhine](/wiki/Haplorhini) or "dry nose" primates, [lorises](/wiki/Loris) are [strepsirrhine](/wiki/Strepsirrhini) or "wet nose" primates. Lorises have big eyes, tiny ears, live in trees and are active at night. The [pygmy slow loris](/wiki/Pygmy_slow_loris) and [Bengal slow loris](/wiki/Bengal_slow_loris) are both found in southern Yunnan and Guangxi and are Class I protected species.

### Carnivores

#### Cats

A [South China tiger](/wiki/South_China_tiger) at the [Shanghai Zoo](/wiki/Shanghai_Zoo)

China's [big cat](/wiki/Big_cat) species include the [tiger](/wiki/Tiger), [leopard](/wiki/Leopard), [snow leopard](/wiki/Snow_leopard) and [clouded leopard](/wiki/Clouded_leopard).
The [tiger](/wiki/Tiger_(zodiac)) is one of the 12 animals of the [Chinese zodiac](/wiki/Chinese_zodiac), and figures prominently in [Chinese culture](/wiki/Tiger_in_Chinese_culture) and history. Tiger bones are used in [traditional Chinese medicine](/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_medicine) and tiger fur is used for decoration. The animal is vulnerable to [poaching](/wiki/Poaching) and habitat loss. Four tiger populations were native to China. All are critically endangered, protected and live in nature reserves.

The [Siberian tiger](/wiki/Siberian_tiger) occurs in the [Northeast](/wiki/Northeast_China), along the border with [Russia](/wiki/Russia) and [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea). The [Caspian tiger](/wiki/Caspian_tiger) was last seen in the [Manasi River](/wiki/Manasi_River) Basin of the [Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region](/wiki/Xinjiang_Uyghur_Autonomous_Region) in the 1960s, where this population is now [extinct](/wiki/Extinct). The [South China tiger](/wiki/South_China_tiger) is an endemic population whose habitat is now confined to the mountain regions of [Jiangxi](/wiki/Jiangxi), [Hunan](/wiki/Hunan), [Guangdong](/wiki/Guangdong) and [Fujian](/wiki/Fujian). A few [Indochinese tigers](/wiki/Indochinese_tiger) were known to live in [Yunnan](/wiki/Yunnan) where six nature reserves have been established for their protection.

[Amur leopards](/wiki/Amur_leopard)

[Snow leopard](/wiki/Snow_leopard)

Three leopard [subspecies](/wiki/Subspecies) are thought to occur in China:

- Leopards recorded in [Qomolangma National Nature Preserve](/wiki/Qomolangma_National_Nature_Preserve) in southern [Tibet](/wiki/Tibet) are subsumed to the [Indian leopard](/wiki/Indian_leopard).
- The [Indochinese leopard](/wiki/Indochinese_leopard) occurs in [Yunnan Province](/wiki/Yunnan_Province) of southern China, where the [Pearl River](/wiki/Pearl_River_(China)) is thought to form a barrier to leopard populations farther north. Camera-trap surveys conducted between 2002 and 2009 in 11 nature reserves in southern China recorded leopards only in [Changqing National Nature Reserve](/wiki/Changqing_National_Nature_Reserve) in the [Qinling](/wiki/Qinling) Mountains, but not in [Sichuan](/wiki/Sichuan_Province)'s [Wolong Nature Reserve](/wiki/Wolong_Nature_Reserve) and other protected areas in Sichuan.
- The [Amur leopard](/wiki/Amur_leopard) is native to northern China including the [Jilin](/wiki/Jilin) province along the border with Russia and North Korea, where it has been recorded by [camera-traps](/wiki/Camera-trap) in Hunchun National Nature Reserve. Leopards cross between China, Russia and North Korea across the [Tumen River](/wiki/Tumen_River) despite a high and long wire fence marking the international boundary. Contemporary records of leopards exist from protected areas in [Hebei](/wiki/Hebei_Province), [Henan](/wiki/Henan_Province) and [Shanxi Provinces](/wiki/Shanxi_Province), and [Ningxia Autonomous Region](/wiki/Ningxia), but not from [Gansu Province](/wiki/Gansu_Province). Whether leopards still occur in [Qinghai Province](/wiki/Qinghai_Province) is uncertain. The species has probably been extirpated in [Hunan](/wiki/Hunan), [Hubei](/wiki/Hubei), [Zhejiang](/wiki/Zhejiang), [Fujian](/wiki/Fujian), [Guangxi](/wiki/Guangxi) and [Jiangxi provinces](/wiki/Jiangxi_province). It is listed as nationally critically endangered, but receives little attention from Chinese wildlife biologists and conservationists. Fragmented leopard populations in central China have been subsumed to the Amur leopard, as there is no notable geographical barrier to northern China that would have prevented [gene flow](/wiki/Gene_flow) in the past.

The range of the [snow leopard](/wiki/Snow_leopard) extends across the [Himalayas](/wiki/Himalayas), [Tibetan Plateau](/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau), [Karakorum Mountains](/wiki/Karakorum_Mountains), and [Tian Shan](/wiki/Tian_Shan) in western China.

#### Canines

The family [Canidae](/wiki/Canidae) has many members in China including the [dog](/wiki/Dog), [wolf](/wiki/Wolf), [dhole](/wiki/Dhole), [red fox](/wiki/Red_fox), [corsac fox](/wiki/Corsac_fox), [Tibetan sand fox](/wiki/Tibetan_sand_fox) and [common raccoon dog](/wiki/Common_raccoon_dog). Two subspecies of wolf live in China—the [Eurasian wolf](/wiki/Eurasian_wolf), which is found in all of mainland China and the [Tibetan wolf](/wiki/Tibetan_wolf), which lives on the [Tibetan Plateau](/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau).

Some of the earliest [dogs](/wiki/Dog) may have been domesticated in East Asia, and several Chinese dog breeds including the [shar-pei](/wiki/Shar-pei) and [chow chow](/wiki/Chow_chow) are among the [most ancient](/wiki/Ancient_dog_breeds) in terms of DNA similarity to the gray wolf.

Dholes are now found in only five provinces: [Gansu](/wiki/Gansu), [Yunnan](/wiki/Yunnan), [Tibet](/wiki/Tibet), [Sichuan](/wiki/Sichuan), and [Xinjiang](/wiki/Xinjiang).

The red fox, the largest fox species, can be found in every part of China except the northwest. The corsac fox is found in [Northeast China](/wiki/Northeast_China) and the Tibetan sand fox in Tibet, Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu and Yunnan.

The raccoon dog, one of the few canids that can climb trees, is native to eastern and northeastern China.

#### Pandas, bears

The [giant panda](/wiki/Giant_panda), perhaps China's most famous wildlife species, lives in six patches of highland valleys of the Min, [Qionglai](/wiki/Qionglai_City), Liang, [Daxiangling](/wiki/Daxiangling), Xiaoxiangling and Qinling mountains of the upper [Yangtze River](/wiki/Yangtze_River) basin, which are spread over 45 counties in [Sichuan](/wiki/Sichuan), [Gansu](/wiki/Gansu) and [Shaanxi](/wiki/Shaanxi). Only about 1,600 live in the wild (80% in Sichuan) along with about 300 in captivity in Chinese breeding centers and zoos. The animal is rare and elusive. Though classified as an omnivore, the giant panda's diet is over 90% bamboo. Its black and white coloration provides a degree camouflage in the dense forests, but the adult animal has no natural predators. Giant pandas are notoriously difficult to breed; they have short mating periods, and give birth to only one or two cubs per year. The giant panda cub is the smallest baby, compared in proportion to the parents, of any placental mammal. The giant panda is considered to be a national treasure and is an endangered species protected by state law. Since the 1970s, giant pandas have been given or lent to foreign zoos as [gesture of diplomatic goodwill](/wiki/Panda_diplomacy).

Other more common bears in China include the [Asiatic black bear](/wiki/Asiatic_black_bear) and the [brown bear](/wiki/Brown_bear) which are found across much of the country. Sub-species of the brown bear include the [Himalayan brown bear](/wiki/Himalayan_brown_bear) and the [Tibetan blue bear](/wiki/Tibetan_blue_bear) in Tibet, and the [Ussuri brown bear](/wiki/Ussuri_brown_bear) in [Northeast China](/wiki/Northeast_China). The [sun bear](/wiki/Sun_bear) is found in Yunnan. Bears, especially black bears, are also raised in captivity to harvest their [bile](/wiki/Bile_bear) for use in traditional Chinese medicine.

The [red panda](/wiki/Red_panda) - which unlike the giant panda is not a bear and more closely resembles a raccoon - is from a separate family by itself (Ailuridae), and is found in Sichuan and Yunnan.

#### Viverridae and Herpestidae

The [viverrid](/wiki/Viverrid) and [mongoose](/wiki/Mongoose) families of small carnivores are represented by numerous members occurring in southern China, including [binturong](/wiki/Binturong), [large Indian civet](/wiki/Large_Indian_civet), [small Indian civet](/wiki/Small_Indian_civet), [Owston's palm civet](/wiki/Owston%27s_palm_civet), [masked palm civet](/wiki/Masked_palm_civet), [Asian palm civet](/wiki/Asian_palm_civet), [small-toothed palm civet](/wiki/Small-toothed_palm_civet), [crab-eating mongoose](/wiki/Crab-eating_mongoose) and [small Indian mongoose](/wiki/Small_Indian_mongoose).

#### Otter, badger, weasel, marten, wolverine

[Sable](/wiki/Sable)

[Beech marten](/wiki/Beech_marten)

[Yellow-throated marten](/wiki/Yellow-throated_marten)

The [largest family](/wiki/Mustelidae) of [carnivorous mammals](/wiki/Carnivora) belongs to the [otters](/wiki/Otters), [badgers](/wiki/Badgers), [weasels](/wiki/Weasels), [martens](/wiki/Martens), and [wolverines](/wiki/Wolverines), all of which are found in China. All of these [mustelids](/wiki/Mustelids) are short, furry animals with short, rounded ears and thick fur, but they differ markedly in size, habit and habitat.

The [sable](/wiki/Sable), a species of marten, is prized for its fine fur, which along with ginseng and deer antler velvet, are known as the "three treasures of Manchuria". The sable is found in Manchuria (also called the Northeast) and Altai region of northern Xinjiang. The [beech marten](/wiki/Beech_marten) of western China and [yellow-throated marten](/wiki/Yellow-throated_marten) of southern China are closely related to the sable.

The [Siberian weasel](/wiki/Siberian_weasel), known locally as the "yellow rat wolf", is the most common weasel in China. It is found throughout [China proper](/wiki/China_proper) and [Manchuria](/wiki/Manchuria), and known to steal poultry from farmers but helps to control the rodent population. Hair from the tail of the Siberian weasel is used to make [ink brush](/wiki/Ink_brush) for traditional Chinese calligraphy. Other weasel species include the [least weasel](/wiki/Least_weasel) and [stoat](/wiki/Stoat) in the north, [yellow-bellied weasel](/wiki/Yellow-bellied_weasel) and [back-striped weasel](/wiki/Back-striped_weasel) in the south, and [mountain weasel](/wiki/Mountain_weasel) in the west. The [steppe polecat](/wiki/Steppe_polecat) is bigger than the Siberian weasel and found across northern China.

In Chinese, the wolverine is called "sable bear" because it is bigger than a sable and smaller than a bear and resembles both animals. The animal lives in caves and dens, which they do not dig but take from other animals such as bears, foxes and [bobak marmots](/wiki/Bobak_marmot). Wolverines are fierce creatures that will fight bears and wolves for food. They are found in the Greater Khingan range of Heilongjiang and Inner Mongolia and the Altai Mountains of northern Xinjiang, and number only about 200.

The [European otter](/wiki/European_otter) is found throughout much of Eurasia and China. It is nearly extinct on Taiwan though some have been found on the island of [Kinmen](/wiki/Kinmen), off the coast of Fujian. The [Oriental small-clawed otter](/wiki/Oriental_small-clawed_otter) is the smallest otter species and lives in mangrove and freshwater swamps of southern China and Taiwan. The [smooth-coated otter](/wiki/Smooth-coated_otter) is confined to parts of Yunnan and Guangdong.

Like sable and martens, otter fur is also used make [clothing](/wiki/Fur_clothing). Sables and wolverines are Class I protected species. Martens and otters are Class II protected species.

[Chinese ferret-badger](/wiki/Chinese_ferret-badger)

[Badgers](/wiki/Badgers) have distinctive white stripes on their faces with one long stripe that extends from nose to tail. The [Asian badger](/wiki/Asian_badger) is found throughout China proper and the eastern Himalayas. The [hog badger](/wiki/Hog_badger) has a pig-like snout and has a slightly smaller range than the Asian badger. [Ferret-badgers](/wiki/Ferret-badger) are the smallest badgers and two species live in China. The [Chinese ferret-badger](/wiki/Chinese_ferret-badger) is found across much of southern China south of the Yangtze River and the [Burmese ferret-badger](/wiki/Burmese_ferret-badger) along Yunnan's border with Laos and Vietnam.

#### Seals, sea lions

[Pinnipeds](/wiki/Pinniped) are also classified as carnivores and are divided between [earless or true seals](/wiki/Earless_seal) and [eared seals](/wiki/Eared_seals). True seals do not have ears and cannot get their hind flippers underneath their bodies to crawl. Eared seals, which include sea lions, in contrast, have protruding ears and can "walk" with all four limbs on land.

[Spotted seal](/wiki/Spotted_seal)

[Northern fur seal](/wiki/Northern_fur_seal)

[Steller sea lion](/wiki/Steller_sea_lion)

True seals in China include the [bearded seal](/wiki/Bearded_seal) which is found along the coast of Zhejiang, Fujian and Guangdong in the [East](/wiki/East_China_Sea) and [South China Sea](/wiki/South_China_Sea), the [ringed seal](/wiki/Ringed_seal) in the [Yellow Sea](/wiki/Yellow_Sea), and [spotted seal](/wiki/Spotted_seal), which is primarily found in the [Bohai Gulf](/wiki/Bohai_Gulf) and the northern Yellow Sea, but have been seen as far south as Guangdong. All seals are Class II protected animal. [Sea lions](/wiki/Sea_lion) have Class I protection.

The spotted seal is the only seal species that breeds in China. Its breeding grounds are found along the rim of [Liaodong Bay](/wiki/Liaodong_Bay) in the Bohai Gulf, including the estuary at the mouth of the Shuangtaizi River near [Panjin](/wiki/Panjin) and [Changxing Island](/wiki/Changxing_Island_(Dalian)) near [Dalian](/wiki/Dalian), and [Baengnyeongdo](/wiki/Baengnyeongdo) sanctuary in the Korean [exclusive economic zone](/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone). These seals have been poached for its fur and genitals, which were used to make an [aphrodisiac](/wiki/Aphrodisiac). Their habitats have also been heavily damaged by land reclamation, fish farming, and petroleum development. A South Korean NGO has been trying to increase public awareness and support for the protection of the seals in China, [North Korea](/wiki/North_Korea) and [South Korea](/wiki/South_Korea). Protection stations have been set up to monitor the breeding grounds and wildlife protection authorities compensate fisherman who turn in live seals caught in their nets. In April 2011, the construction of an express highway along the coast was halted due to its adverse impact on the seal breeding ground. Satellite trackings revealed that not only within Yellow Sea, but also seals can migrate even between [Primorsky Krai](/wiki/Primorsky_Krai) in Russia to Yellow Sea, exceeding 3,300 km in total. Recoveries and recolonizations have been observed recently, such as along the coast of [Shandong](/wiki/Shandong) in 1999, and in the [Miaodao Islands](/wiki/Changdao_County) of the [Bohai Sea](/wiki/Bohai_Sea) since 2000s.

The [northern fur seal](/wiki/Northern_fur_seal), an eared seal, occasionally appears off the coast of eastern and southern China and southern Taiwan. The largest of the eared seals is the [Steller sea lion](/wiki/Steller_sea_lion), who lives primarily in the Arctic but is also seen along the Yellow Sea coast in Jiangsu and Bohai Gulf in Liaoning. Among Yellow Sea – adjacent areas within the Korean EEZ, occurrence can be on locations such as at [Jeju Island](/wiki/Jeju_Island).

### Whales, dolphins, porpoises

China has [cetacean](/wiki/Cetacean) species that live in both freshwater and the sea. The nearly extinct [baiji dolphin](/wiki/Baiji) and [Chinese white dolphin](/wiki/Chinese_white_dolphin) are Class I protected species. All other cetaceans in China are Class II protected species.

In total, 22 species of smaller cetaceans inhabit within Chinese, Taiwanese, Hong Kong's, and Macau's waters including Baiji. Although being not officially recognized, the presence of [Irrawaddy dolphins](/wiki/Irrawaddy_dolphin) have been questioned.

In ancient China, inscriptions of the whales varied and inscriptions of whales and sharks were occasionally mixed. During the [Qing dynasty](/wiki/Qing_dynasty), certain knowledge on whales had been deepened with the establishment of [whaling](/wiki/Whaling) industries in Guangdong, Guangxi and Hainan although both oceanic and freshwater dolphins had been classified as different animals from whales. It is said that climate change during the dynasty caused small fish to flourish within Yellow and Bohai Seas and drew large numbers of whales into the basins.

The [Republic of China](/wiki/Republic_of_China_(1912%E2%80%9349)) was one of the early signatories of the [International Convention for the Regulation of Whaling](/wiki/International_Convention_for_the_Regulation_of_Whaling). The [People's Republic of China](/wiki/People%27s_Republic_of_China) signed convention in September 1980 and banned domestic whaling in 1981, and also signed in the [Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals](/wiki/Convention_on_the_Conservation_of_Migratory_Species_of_Wild_Animals).

Until recently, observing live cetaceans nonetheless of any species including [minke whales](/wiki/Minke_whale) and smaller dolphins and porpoises are very rare in [Bohai](/wiki/Bohai_Sea) and [Yellow Seas](/wiki/Yellow_Sea) within Chinese side, however, increases in confirmation of minke whales and other species have been confirmed in larger part of Yellow Sea basin especially around [Changhai County](/wiki/Changhai_County) due to improves in water quality and productivity achieved by fishery regulations and creating ocean farms on Zhangzi Island, and local industries have been considered to operate whale watching tours as a future prospect. Modern distributions of cetaceans both on continent and oceanic islands including Taiwan are largely biased on toothed whales due to severe declines of baleen whales.

#### Baiji

Baiji dolphin reserves

The [baiji](/wiki/Baiji) dolphin's habit historically covered much of the Yangtze River and its tributaries and lakes, from Yichang to Shanghai. It is mentioned in historical records going back 2,000 years. According to legend, the baiji dolphin is the reincarnation of a princess and called the Goddess of the Yangtze. As recently as the 1950s, there were as many as 6,000 baiji dolphins in China, but their number fell to the hundreds by the 1980s, under 100 in the 1990s and fewer than a dozen since 2000.

The Yangtze River catchment area is one of the most densely populated areas in China and the world. The river, China's longest, is also a major highway for ships. Water and noise pollution, commercial fishing, and large propellers of ships are all major threats to the baiji. The building of the [Gezhouba Dam](/wiki/Gezhouba_Dam) in the 1970s and the [Three Gorges Dam](/wiki/Three_Gorges_Dam) in the 1990s blocked the access of the dolphins upstream, altered the seasonal flow of the river, and enabled large oceangoing ships to sail on the river.

By 1997, a survey of the river found only 13 baiji. A [Sino-Swiss joint survey of the river](/wiki/Yangtze_Freshwater_Dolphin_Expedition_2006) from Yichang to Shanghai in 2006 found no animals and declared the species to be [functionally extinct](/wiki/Functionally_extinct), that is, even if a few individuals continued to survive, their numbers are too few to reproduce. The last sighting confirmed by zoologist was in 2004 when a dead baiji dolphin washed ashore near [Nanjing](/wiki/Nanjing).

Nature reserves to protect the baiji dolphin were established along the Yangtze in [Hunan](/wiki/Hunan), [Hubei](/wiki/Hubei) and [Anhui](/wiki/Anhui) province, along with observation and captive centers. The longest living baiji dolphin in captivity, Qiqi, lived in a [dolphinarium](/wiki/Dolphinarium) in [Wuhan](/wiki/Wuhan) from 1980 to 2002. The [Tian-e-Zhou Oxbow Nature Reserve](/wiki/Tian-e-Zhou_Oxbow_Nature_Reserve), created out of an [oxbow](/wiki/Oxbow) bend in the Yangtze was designed as a captive breeding area for the baiji. One baiji was sent there in 1995 but died in 1996. The reserve is now a breeding ground for the [finless porpoise](/wiki/Finless_porpoise).

#### Finless porpoise

[Narrow-ridged finless porpoises](/wiki/Yangtze_finless_porpoise)

Finless porpoises in [Lake Dongting](/wiki/Lake_Dongting)

At least two subspecies of [finless porpoise](/wiki/Finless_porpoise) are known to inhabit coastal waters such as off [Dalian](/wiki/Dalian)< [Nanjing](/wiki/Nanjing), Nanji Islands Marine Sanctuary, Jiushan Chain Islands[Weizhou Island](/wiki/Weizhou_Island), and at [Matsu Islands](/wiki/Matsu_Islands). A freshwater subspecies lives in the Yangtze, [Gan](/wiki/Gan_River_(Jiangxi)) and [Xiang Rivers](/wiki/Xiang_River). Unlike dolphins, they lack a dorsal fin. The freshwater porpoise faces the same threat as the baiji. In April 2012, twelve were found dead in [Dongting Lake](/wiki/Dongting_Lake) in a span of 44 days. Construction of the [Poyang Lake Dam](/wiki/Poyang_Lake_Dam) may cause severe damages on remaining population.

As of 2012, the Tian-e-Zhou Oxbow Nature Reserve had about 40 finless porpoises with another 85 in Dongting Lake and 300–400 in [Poyang Lake](/wiki/Poyang_Lake). The freshwater finless porpoise, a Class II protected species, is rarer than the giant panda. They are also well present in [Gulf of Tonkin](/wiki/Gulf_of_Tonkin).

In recent years, small concentrations have been confirmed at the estuaries on the mouth of [Yellow River](/wiki/Yellow_River) in [Lijin County](/wiki/Lijin_County). Stable numbers of porpoises, two subspecies being involved, have been found recently along [Chongming Island](/wiki/Chongming_Island) where the local waters show drastic recovery thanks to efforts to improve water quality.

Sousa, the [Chinese white dolphin](/wiki/Chinese_white_dolphin) (locally called the [Matsu's](/wiki/Mazu_(goddess)) fish) that was previously considered to be a subspecies of the [Indo-Pacific humpback dolphin](/wiki/Indo-Pacific_humpback_dolphin), lives in the waters off southern China including [Wanshan Archipelago](/wiki/Wanshan_Archipelago), Nanji Islands, the [Pearl River Delta](/wiki/Pearl_River_Delta), and [Hong Kong](/wiki/Hong_Kong), [Gulf of Tonkin](/wiki/Gulf_of_Tonkin) [Hainan Island](/wiki/Hainan_Island) such as around [Sanya Bay](/wiki/Sanya_Bay), [Paracel Islands](/wiki/Paracel_Islands), and on [Penghu](/wiki/Penghu) Islands to the western coast of Taiwan, mainland coast along the [Formosa Strait](/wiki/Formosa_Strait) such as at [Xiamen](/wiki/Xiamen) and [Xiapu County](/wiki/Xiapu_County), and Nánpēng Islands Marine Sanctuary in [Nan'ao County](/wiki/Nan%27ao_County), and Sanniang Bay dolphin sanctuary in [Qingzhou](/wiki/Qingzhou). Risso's dolphins are one of the most common cetaceans along the east coast of Taiwan.

#### Whales

Engraving of a [sperm whale](/wiki/Sperm_whale) and various fish ashore on the Chinese coast

Whales were historically abundant in Chinese and Taiwanese waters especially in the winter and spring whene they come to coastal areas to breed and calve, while especially [baleen whales](/wiki/Baleen_whale) other than those which migrated from the outer Pacific and [Sea of Japan](/wiki/Sea_of_Japan) swam northward to feed in the Yellow and Bohai basins during warmer seasons. Most of the large whales in Taiwan were recorded prior to 1952. In imperial times, villages along the coast of the Leizhou Peninsula in Guangdong hunted whales and made offerings of whale oil to the emperor in Beijing. On the other hand, however, like among other nations such as in Korea, China, [Ebis](/wiki/Ebisu_(mythology)) in Japan, Indonesia, among [Indochina](/wiki/Indochina) including in Vietnam where whales were once well respected, heavenly deities among coastal people, regarded as the "[King of the sea](/wiki/List_of_water_deities)", the "[Dragon emperor in the ocean](/wiki/Dragon_King)", or "Dragon Soldiers" in almost entire coastal regions excluding above mentioned Hainan and Leizhou, as when whales were seen, fishermen and boats had to make ways for them and wait the whales to pass. In Chinese mythology, for example, *Yu-kiang*, the ruler of the sea, is said to be a whale with arms and legs. [Indigenous tribes on Taiwan](/wiki/Taiwanese_aborigines) also recognized the presences of large whales and representing whales in their local myths and folklores.

[Baleen whales](/wiki/Baleen_whales) found in the ocean off China's coast include the [blue whale](/wiki/Blue_whale), the world's largest animal, as well as the [Eden's](/wiki/Eden%27s_whale), [Omura's](/wiki/Omura%27s_whale), [Bryde's](/wiki/Bryde%27s_whale), [common minke](/wiki/Common_minke_whale), [fin](/wiki/Fin_whale), [sei](/wiki/Sei_whale), and [humpback](/wiki/Humpback_whale) whales. Historically, there had been an endemic, resident population of fin whales from the Yellow and Bohai Sea to the East China Sea. Minke whales are also resident in the same regions. Historically, Bryde's whales were resident near Taiwan and the southern coast.

In the Chinese EEZ, critically endangered [North Pacific right whales](/wiki/North_Pacific_right_whale) and western [gray whales](/wiki/Gray_whale) had been sighted in the East China Sea and Yellow Sea only on prior to the 1970s, especially for right whales. There had been records of gray whales and the only record in the 21st century was of a mature female accidentally killed in local fisheries near [Pingtan](/wiki/Pingtan_Island) in the [Taiwan Strait](/wiki/Taiwan_Strait) in 2007.

The following statements focus on right and gray whales because their behavioral patterns (high reliance on shallow waters compared to their size, such that they enter river mouths and estuaries regularly, and their curiosity about humans) made it easier for hunters to kill them and they were wiped out faster than other species, but they also apply to the larger whales. [Rorquals](/wiki/Rorqual)' situations were similar, but their functional local extinction was caused later in the 20th century by modern Japanese whaling.

The biology and natural history of whales in Chinese waters prior to exploitation is unclear because [academic studies or approaches to biology of cetaceans](/wiki/Cetology) was minimal. The local populations of migratory whales appear to have been intensively hunted to the point of near-functional extinction on the main migratory collider (the Japanese archipelago) by [Japanese whaling industries](/wiki/Whaling_in_Japan). The fates of right whales, for example, were threatened by legal, illegal and research whaling, and the most devastatingly, the Soviet Union's mass illegal whaling in the 1960s and 1970s.

Gray whales migrating on the coasts of Japanese were wiped out earlier than their Korean counterparts. Other populations along the Korean Peninsula were targeted later. The most intensive hunts of all times were carried out by Japanese whaling industries in the 20th century; these covered a wide range of east Asian waters including almost entire [EEZ](/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone) of China, North Korea and South Korea. Whaling stations, such as at [Daya Bay](/wiki/Daya_Bay), were established along the Chinese and Korean coastlines causing virtual, functional extinction of almost all species of larger baleen whales in east and southeast Asian nations. The presence of larger cetaceans has not been confirmed.

[Toothed whales](/wiki/Toothed_whale), excluding dolphins, include the [sperm](/wiki/Sperm_whale), [dwarf sperm](/wiki/Dwarf_sperm_whale), [pygmy sperm](/wiki/Pygmy_sperm_whale), [Baird's beaked](/wiki/Baird%27s_beaked_whale), [Longman's beaked](/wiki/Tropical_bottlenose_whale), [Cuvier's beaked](/wiki/Cuvier%27s_beaked_whale), [Blainville's beaked](/wiki/Blainville%27s_beaked_whale), [ginkgo-toothed beaked](/wiki/Ginkgo-toothed_beaked_whale) whales, and the [orca](/wiki/Orca) and [pilot whales](/wiki/Pilot_whale) ([false killer](/wiki/False_killer_whale), [pygmy killer](/wiki/Pygmy_killer_whale), [melon-headed](/wiki/Melon-headed_whale), [short-finned pilot](/wiki/Short-finned_pilot_whale)). False killers still remain along coasts of mainland China, and are known to enter rivers regularly in particular regions.

[Stranding](/wiki/Cetacean_stranding) of toothed whales has been common on Taiwanese coasts.

Large whales have become very rare on today's Chinese coasts where only tiny remnants of minke whales or several more survived. However, [whale watching](/wiki/Whale_watching) industries became popular attractions along the east coast of [Taiwan](/wiki/Taiwan), offering excellent opportunities to observe majestic creatures, especially in the summer. Recently, whale watching has been considered in the Yellow Sea based on recoveries whale populations. Larger rorquals have been sighted in pelagic waters occasionally. Whales migrating through [Tsushima Strait](/wiki/Tsushima_Strait) possibly to Chinese waters are under serious threat of being struck by high-speed vessels.

Older and modern whaling records suggest that there had been historical summering and wintering/calving grounds for baleen whales in various areas along coastal China particularly in several locations. Below is a list showing some of those areas corresponding with baleen and few of larger toothed whales, but excluding undiscovered or unstudied regions and species.

##### Toothed whales

Skeleton of a sperm whale which was stranded on [Liugong Island](/wiki/Liugong_Island)

- [Sperm whales](/wiki/Sperm_whale) – The only large cetacean still common in the nation's waters were one of the main targets of whale-watching industries along the east coast of Taiwan, as well as islands such as the [Xiaoliuqiu](/wiki/Xiaoliuqiu) and [Spratly Islands](/wiki/Spratly_Islands). Occasionally whales strand on mainland shores in the Yellow and Bohai regions. They appear seldom in near-shore waters because they feed mostly in deep [sea canyons](/wiki/Sea_canyon). Sperm whales appear in near-shore waters in some cases; at locations where deep waters approach shores, or some particular individuals or groups have learned to come to rest in shallow bays, straits or along beaches. There were sightings of nine whales in the East China Sea off the Korean Peninsula in 1999, and eight whales off the eastern Korean Peninsula in 2004. The last catches were of five whales off [Ulsan](/wiki/Ulsan) in 1911.
- [Baird's beaked whales](/wiki/Baird%27s_beaked_whale) – The second largest of the Odontoceti are extreme divers second only to sperm whales. Next to nothing about this species' natural history and biology in Chinese waters is clear as the species has been considered not to occur, and the origin of the skeleton at the [Zhejiang](/wiki/Zhejiang) Museum of natural History is unclear. This species could still be within the Chinese EEZ as some groups on the Japanese archipelago survive, but are under serious danger from commercial whaling. Based on archeological reports, these elusive, friendly whales once were regular among the Yellow and Bohai seas notably around Lingshan Island off [Huangdao District](/wiki/Huangdao_District) or the mouth of [Jiaozhou Bay](/wiki/Jiaozhou_Bay) and off [Dalian](/wiki/Dalian) at least until the mid-16th century, but they were seemingly wiped out by Japanese whalers. Southern limits of their distributions in Chinese waters are unclear while a stranding or a catch was recorded in Zhoushan in the 1950s. Twelve whales were caught as [bycatch](/wiki/Bycatch) along the eastern Korean Peninsula between 1996 and 2012.
- [Longman's beaked whales](/wiki/Longman%27s_beaked_whale) and other beaked whales – Being one of newly classified and less known species, their overall distributions have been rather unclear. They are the second largest of beaked whales and third largest of toothed whales that can be seen in the Chinese EEZ. In Chinese waters records of these whales are concentrated on the east coast of Taiwan and surrounding waters including Lanyu and Green Island. Based on studies, presences of other beaked whales, being lesser known as well, have been confirmed to be common around Taiwanese waters, and Taiwan is one of few locations where beaked whales have been observed with regularity during [whale watching](/wiki/Whale_watching) tours. [Stejneger's beaked whales](/wiki/Stejneger%27s_beaked_whale) are resident in the Sea of Japan, and are one of the most commonly recorded Ziphiidae species of the Korean Peninsula although their presence within the Yellow Sea is unclear.
- [Orcas](/wiki/Orca) – The current status of killer whales along the nation's coasts and surrounding areas is unclear. Sightings are more common along the eastern Taiwanese coasts such as off [Chenggong](/wiki/Chenggong,_Taitung) while on the mainland, they occur on almost the entire shoreline from the Bohai and Yellow Sea in the north to [Ningbo](/wiki/Ningbo) and [Zhoushan Archipelago](/wiki/Zhoushan_Archipelago) in the east, and along the southern coasts and islands including Paracel Islands as well. There was a commercial catch newar southern Taiwan in the 1990s. They still occur occasionally in the Korean side of the Yellow Sea or nearby; there was a sighting of pairs in 2001 and five or six whales off [Wando (island)](/wiki/Wando_(island)) within the [Dadohaehaesang National Park](/wiki/Dadohaehaesang_National_Park) in 2016.
- [Short-finned pilot whales](/wiki/Short-finned_pilot_whale) – The so-called the "Southern Form" of the species ranges within the Chinese waters. Most of records concentrate on the eastern coasts of Taiwan. Mainland distributions are rather unclear as there had been only one stranding record in Hainan, including regularities of occurrences within the Yellow Sea, but occasional strandings have been recorded such as at [Taeanhaean National Park](/wiki/Taeanhaean_National_Park) and [Jeju](/wiki/Jeju_Province). There was a mass stranding on the Nanji Islands in 2004.
- [False killer whales](/wiki/False_killer_whale) – One of few species surviving today in descent numbers on mainland coasts, but in peril; any warmer regions such as Taiwan, Nánpēng Islands, Nanji Island Marine Sanctuary, [Matsu Islands](/wiki/Matsu_Islands), Langyatai on Huangdao District, [Dongshan County](/wiki/Dongshan_County), Hong Kong, Paracel Islands, and so on.
  - False killer whales along continental China are known to often enter and swim up large rivers in pods or large numbered schools, reaching more than 30 to 50 km, or individuals have traveled 220 to 300 km. Rivers and canals in [Xiangshui County](/wiki/Xiangshui_County) such as Guanhe, Jiangsu, [Huai](/wiki/Huai_River), and [Tongyu](/wiki/Tongyu) (*通榆河*) rivers have local legends of "鲸拜龙王" (*Worshiped Whale Dragon King*), telling that every spring whales gather at river mouths and swim up. In recent years, especially from earlier 2000s, false killer whales have been observed to swim up rivers rather regularly, showing dramatic recoveries and their numbers are rising once again, up to more than 200 whales. Whales occasionally appear in [Jiaozhou Bay](/wiki/Jiaozhou_Bay) which was part of the regular range for the species until in the 1980s.

### Dugongs

A [dugong](/wiki/Dugong)

[Dugongs](/wiki/Dugong) are marine mammals that feed entirely on vegetation such as [seagrass](/wiki/Seagrass). They are related to [manatees](/wiki/Manatee) in the Western Hemisphere, and are only [sirenian](/wiki/Sirenia) species found in Asian waters. In China, dugongs are found along the coasts of the [Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region](/wiki/Guangxi_Zhuang_Autonomous_Region), where the Hepu Dugong National Nature Reserve, near Beihai, was created in 1992 for their protection, and less frequently in Hainan. Current distributions could be much more restricted than that of pre-exploitation ranges, as once might have been seen in the [Yellow sea](/wiki/Yellow_sea) regions.

They are considered regionally extinct in Taiwan. The dugong is a Class I protected species. They were hunted for their meat in the late 1950s and early 1960s during the [Great Leap Forward](/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward). Dugongs are threatened by the loss of seagrass beds from coastal development. Several areas still possess feasible habitats for dugongs today such as the [Dongsha Atoll](/wiki/Dongsha_Atoll_National_Park) and the west coasts of [Hainan](/wiki/Hainan) and [Leizhou Peninsula](/wiki/Leizhou_Peninsula), and Chinese government funded to establish a sanctuary designed for dugong and [mangrove](/wiki/Mangrove) conservation ranging from [Hepu County](/wiki/Hepu_County) to Shankou in [Guanxi](/wiki/Guanxi), also to secure local [Chinese white dolphins](/wiki/Chinese_white_dolphin). Individuals distributed among the [Beibu Gulf Economic Rim](/wiki/Beibu_Gulf_Economic_Rim) in [Gulf of Tonkin](/wiki/Gulf_of_Tonkin) face threats of busy-becoming ship-lanes and polluted waters.

### Elephant

An [Asian elephant](/wiki/Asian_elephant)

[Asian elephants](/wiki/Asian_elephants) [once roamed a large swath of China](/wiki/Elephants_in_ancient_China), but are now confined to the [Xishuangbanna](/wiki/Xishuangbanna) and [Pu'er](/wiki/Puer_Hani_and_Yi_Autonomous_County) Prefectures of southern Yunnan. Xishuangbana means 12 elephants in the local [Thai language](/wiki/Thai_language). In recent years, Chinese demand for ivory has led to a sharp increase in elephant poaching around the world. Due to strict enforcement of elephant protection laws with capital punishment for poachers and government financed feeding programs, the population of elephants within China from 1994 to 2014 roughly doubled to nearly 300.

### Odd-toed ungulates

#### Rhino

[Indian rhinoceros](/wiki/Indian_rhinoceros), extinct in China since 1920

[Sumatran rhinoceros](/wiki/Sumatran_rhinoceros), extinct in China since 1916

[Southern white rhinoceros](/wiki/Southern_white_rhinoceros) introduced into the wild in 2014

[Northern Sumatran rhinoceros](/wiki/Northern_Sumatran_rhinoceros), declared extinct on several occasions in the 20th century

[Records and artwork from antiquity](/wiki/Rhinoceroses_in_ancient_China) indicate that three species of Asian [rhinoceros](/wiki/Rhinoceros), the [Indian](/wiki/Indian_rhinoceros), [Javan](/wiki/Javan_rhinoceros) and [Sumatran](/wiki/Sumatran_rhinoceros), more specially the [Northern Sumatran rhinoceros](/wiki/Northern_Sumatran_rhinoceros) have lived in China. During the [Shang dynasty](/wiki/Shang_dynasty), some 3,000 years ago, rhinoceros ranged as far north as Inner Mongolia. By the beginning of the [Han dynasty](/wiki/Han_dynasty), 2,200 years ago, they had disappeared from the Central Plains of northern China.

During the [Tang dynasty](/wiki/Tang_dynasty), about 1,200 years ago, rhinos were found across southern China and the imperial zoo had a captive breeding program that returned some animals to the wild. Cooler climate in northern China may have caused rhinoceros habitat to shrink, but it was demand for rhino horns for use in traditional Chinese medicine, documented in as early as the [Song dynasty](/wiki/Song_dynasty) 1,000 years ago, that drove the animal toward extinction.

In the [Ming dynasty](/wiki/Ming_dynasty) about 650 years ago, rhinoceros were confined to Yunnan and Guizhou, and by the [Qing dynasty](/wiki/Qing_dynasty) to only Yunnan. The Qing government limited the hunting of rhinos to only officials, and some 300 horns were harvested between 1900 and 1910. The collapse of the Qing dynasty in 1911 allowed individuals to hunt the animal. The last Sumatran rhino was killed in 1916, the last Indian rhino in 1920 and the last Javan rhino in 1922.

In 2010, a herd of nine southern white rhinoceros were imported from South Africa and shipped to Yunnan, where they were kept in a wild animal park for acclimation. In March 2013, seven of the animals were shipped to the Laiyanghe National Forest Park, a habitat where Asian rhinoceros once lived. Two of the African rhinos began the process of being released into the wild on 13 May 2014.

#### Horses and wild asses

[Przewalski's horse](/wiki/Przewalski%27s_horse)

[Asiatic wild ass (kiang)](/wiki/Kiang)

The [Przewalski's horse](/wiki/Przewalski%27s_horse), the only species of [wild horses](/wiki/Wild_horse) never to have been domesticated, once roamed free in large parts of northwestern China but became locally extinct in 1957. In the 1980s, herds from Europe have been introduced to habitats in Xinjiang and Gansu.

The other [odd-toed ungulates](/wiki/Odd-toed_ungulates) in China are the [Mongolian wild ass](/wiki/Mongolian_wild_ass) and the [Tibetan wild ass (kiang)](/wiki/Kiang). The former is endangered while the latter is not. Both are Class I protected species.

### Even-toed ungulates

#### Deer

China has a great variety of [true deer](/wiki/True_deer) and its close kin the [musk deer](/wiki/Musk_deer). The largest deer species, the [elk](/wiki/Moose) (known as the moose in North America), is found in the [Greater](/wiki/Greater_Khingan) and [Lesser Khingan](/wiki/Lesser_Khingan) ranges of the northeast. The moose stands at 2 m tall and weighs as much 700 kg. In contrast, the [lesser mouse-deer](/wiki/Lesser_mouse-deer) of Yunnan, which is just 45 cm in height and weighs 2 kg, is not much bigger than a rabbit.

China also contains the closely related [elk](/wiki/Elk) and [red deer](/wiki/Red_deer), the second and fourth largest deer species, which until 2004 were considered the same species. The elk (also known as wapiti) has four subspecies in Asia – the [Altai wapiti](/wiki/Altai_wapiti), [Tian Shan wapiti](/wiki/Tian_Shan_wapiti), [Manchurian wapiti](/wiki/Manchurian_wapiti) and [Alashan wapiti](/wiki/Alashan_wapiti) – all of which are present in China. The [red deer](/wiki/Red_deer), though quite common in Europe, has subspecies in China that are endangered. The [red deer](/wiki/Red_deer) are the deer that have been most important to human societies.

The [Yarkand deer](/wiki/Yarkand_deer) lives along the [Tarim River](/wiki/Tarim_River) in Xinjiang south of the Tian Shan. The [Bactrian deer](/wiki/Bactrian_deer) lives north of the Tian Shan in northern Xinjiang and Central Asian Republics. The [Tibetan red deer](/wiki/Tibetan_red_deer), [Gansu red deer](/wiki/Kansu_red_deer), [Sichuan deer](/wiki/Sichuan_deer) have been alternatively categorized as subspecies of the elk or the [Central Asian red deer](/wiki/Central_Asian_red_deer).

The [sambar deer](/wiki/Sambar_deer), the third largest deer species, is found throughout southern China, and on the islands of Hainan and Taiwan. They live near water and are called "water deer" in Chinese. They are not to be confused with the [Chinese water deer](/wiki/Chinese_water_deer), a smaller deer which are found in the Yangtze Delta region. The water deer is the only species of [true deer](/wiki/True_deer) without antlers.

[Tufted deer](/wiki/Tufted_deer)

Water deer, [tufted deer](/wiki/Tufted_deer) and [muntjacs](/wiki/Muntjacs) are small deer with long upper canines that protrude like tusks. Muntjacs are known for their soft hide and tender meat. The [Indian muntjac](/wiki/Indian_muntjac) is found throughout southern China. The range of the [Reeve's muntjac](/wiki/Reeve%27s_muntjac) extends north to Gansu and to Taiwan. [Fea's muntjac](/wiki/Fea%27s_muntjac) are found in eastern Tibet and the [Gongshan muntjac](/wiki/Gongshan_muntjac) in neighboring Yunnan. The [hairy-fronted muntjac](/wiki/Hairy-fronted_muntjac) is endemic to the mountains at the juncture of Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangxi and Fujian and is a protected species. The [tufted deer](/wiki/Tufted_deer), a close relative of the muntjac, is found throughout central China.

Deer is prized in China for the velvet of their antlers. Antler velvet is rich in growth hormone and is used in traditional Chinese medicine. The most valuable antler velvet comes from the [sika deer](/wiki/Sika_deer) which is raised on farms. Several subspecies of the sika deer, including the [Shanxi sika](/wiki/Shanxi_sika_deer) and the [North China sika](/wiki/North_China_sika_deer) may have become extinct in the wild and survive exclusively in captivity. The [Sichuan sika deer](/wiki/Sichuan_sika_deer), another subspecies, was discovered in 1978 and lives in mountains of northern Sichuan and southern Gansu. The [Formosan sika deer](/wiki/Formosan_sika_deer) is endemic to Taiwan.

[Eld's deer](/wiki/Eld%27s_deer)

[Hog deer](/wiki/Hog_deer)

[Thorold's or white-lipped deer](/wiki/Thorold%27s_deer)

[Reindeer](/wiki/Reindeer), which are found in the forests of the Greater Khingan range in northern Inner Mongolia, are domesticated by the ethnic [Ewenki](/wiki/Evenks) and [Oroqen](/wiki/Oroqen_people) people. The Oroqen call themselves, "people who use the reindeer". One branch of the Ewenki rely on reindeer to haul goods through swampy forests. They use reindeer milk and meat for nourishment, hides for clothing and tents, and antlers for medicine and income. The Kyrgyz people, who now reside in Central Asia and western Xinjiang, used to live in northeast Asia and regard the sika deer as a holy animal. According to Kyrgyz legend, the Kyrgyz Bugu tribe descended from a mother deer.

The [sika deer](/wiki/Sika_deer) is protected as a [Class I endangered species](/wiki/List_of_endangered_and_protected_species_of_China) by the state, though it is classified by the  [International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN)](/wiki/International_Union_for_Conservation_of_Nature) as [least concern](/wiki/Least_concern). Another Class I protected deer is the [Thorold's](/wiki/Thorold%27s_deer) or white-lipped deer. This large deer with a population of about 15,000 that is endemic to Qinghai, Gansu, Sichuan, Tibet and Yunnan, is considered [vulnerable](/wiki/Vulnerable_species) by the IUCN. The Chinese population of [Eld's deer](/wiki/Eld%27s_deer), a Class I protected species that is also considered [endangered](/wiki/Endangered_species) by IUCN, is found only on the island of Hainan. For decades, the [Indochinese hog deer](/wiki/Indochinese_hog_deer) was believed to be extinct in China until a fawn was discovered in 2007 in the [Yongde](/wiki/Yongde_County) Daxueshan National Wildlife Reserve. The Indochinese hog deer is also protected by the state.

[Père David's deer](/wiki/P%C3%A8re_David%27s_deer) at Woburn Deer Park, where the species was saved and from which the animal was eventually reintroduced to China

Perhaps the most remarkable endangered deer species in China is [Père David's deer](/wiki/P%C3%A8re_David%27s_deer). This deer, colloquially known as the *sibuxiang* or the "Four-Not-Look-Alike", is said to have the hooves of an ox, antlers of a deer, neck of a camel and tail of a donkey, but does not look like any one animal. According to Chinese legend, this animal helped the ancient sage [Jiang Ziya](/wiki/Jiang_Ziya) overthrow the [tyrant king](/wiki/King_Zhou_of_Shang) of the [Shang dynasty](/wiki/Shang_dynasty) 4,000 years ago and became a symbol of good fortune. Chinese emperors kept the *sibuxiang* also called *milu* in imperial hunting parks, even as the animal became extinct in the wild, perhaps as early as 2,000 years ago. By 1866, when Father [Armand David](/wiki/Armand_David) identified the animal, there were only 200–300 remaining in the [Nanhaizi Royal Park](/wiki/Milu_Yuan) in [Beijing](/wiki/Beijing). A few animals were sold to zoos in Europe before 1894, when the park was flooded and some of the animals escaped only to be hunted and eaten. The last of the animals in China died during the chaos of the [Boxer Rebellion](/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion). In 1898, [Herbrand Russell, 11th Duke of Bedford](/wiki/Herbrand_Russell,_11th_Duke_of_Bedford) assembled a herd of 18 animals from European zoos and bred them at his estate, [Woburn Abbey](/wiki/Woburn_Abbey) in England. In 1985, 22 deer from this herd was reintroduced back to the Nanhaizi Park in Beijing and in 1986 another 39 were sent to [Dafeng](/wiki/Dafeng), in northern Jiangsu on the [Yellow Sea](/wiki/Yellow_Sea). In 1998, eight animals in the latter herd were introduced into wilderness of the Dafeng Milu National Wildlife Reserve. By 2013, the reserve had 196 Père David's deer.

[Siberian roe deer](/wiki/Siberian_roe_deer)

The [Siberian roe deer](/wiki/Siberian_roe_deer), once plentiful in the Northeast and favored as game meat, has also become a protected species. Hunting of roe deer was banned in 2000.

[Siberian musk deer](/wiki/Siberian_musk_deer)

[Lesser mouse-deer](/wiki/Lesser_mouse-deer)

[Musk deer](/wiki/Musk_deer) and [mouse-deer](/wiki/Mouse-deer) resemble small deer but are not [true deer](/wiki/True_deer). They do not have antlers or facial scent glands. Male musk deer have scent glands that secrete [deer musk](/wiki/Deer_musk), which is used for perfume, incense and medicine. Of the seven musk deer species in the world, six are found in China and five are endangered: the [Anhui musk deer](/wiki/Anhui_musk_deer) and [dwarf musk deer](/wiki/Dwarf_musk_deer) of central China, the [alpine musk deer](/wiki/Alpine_musk_deer) of western China, the [white-bellied musk deer](/wiki/White-bellied_musk_deer) and [black musk deer](/wiki/Black_musk_deer) of Tibet. The [Siberian musk deer](/wiki/Siberian_musk_deer) in the northeast is considered vulnerable. The lesser mouse-deer is found in southern Yunnan.

#### Antelope

[Goitered gazelle](/wiki/Goitered_gazelle)

The grasslands, plateau and deserts of northern and western China are home to several species of [antelope](/wiki/Antelope). The [Mongolian gazelle](/wiki/Mongolian_gazelle), also known as the Zeren or yellow sheep, can run at speeds of up to 90 km/h and gather in herds by the thousands. They used to be spread over much of northern China but are now confined largely to Inner Mongolia. The [Tibetan gazelle](/wiki/Tibetan_gazelle) or goa antelope, is slightly smaller than the Mongolian gazelle, and lives on the [Tibetan Plateau](/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau). The [Przewalski's gazelle](/wiki/Przewalski%27s_gazelle), whose males have distinctive horns that curl outward and then inward at the top, are extremely rare and endemic to a small region around [Qinghai Lake](/wiki/Qinghai_Lake) on the northeastern part of the Tibetan Plateau. The [goitered gazelle](/wiki/Goitered_gazelle) is about the same size as the Mongolian gazelle and is found throughout the [Gobi Desert](/wiki/Gobi_Desert).

The [Tibetan antelope](/wiki/Tibetan_antelope), also known as chiru, is taller than the gazelles and has longer horns. It is endemic to the Tibetan Plateau and is endangered. The animal is poached for its fine wool, which is made by [Kashmiri](/wiki/Kashmiri_people) weavers into the [Shahtoosh](/wiki/Shahtoosh) shawl. The film *[Kekexili: Mountain Patrol](/wiki/Kekexili:_Mountain_Patrol)* documents efforts to protect the animal from poaching. The Tibetan antelope was one of the [mascots](/wiki/Fuwa) for the [2008 Summer Olympics](/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics).

The [saiga antelope](/wiki/Saiga_antelope)'s horns are used in traditional Chinese medicine to treat a variety of ailments including the common cold. Despite its status as a Class I protected species, the saiga antelope has been poached to extinction in the [Dzungar Basin](/wiki/Dzungaria) of northern Xinjiang and is critically endangered in Central Asia and Russia. Chinese police routinely interdict large batches of smuggled horns into Xinjiang. Attempts have been made to reintroduce the saiga antelope to habitats in China.

#### Goat antelopes

[Serows](/wiki/Serow), [gorals](/wiki/Goral), and the [takin](/wiki/Takin) are called antelope by the Chinese, and [goat antelope](/wiki/Goat_antelope) by western taxonomists.

The largest of these goat antelope is the [takin](/wiki/Takin), a relative of the musk ox. It lives in highlands from the eastern foothills of the Himalayas to the Qinling and shares habitat with the giant panda in Sichuan and Shaanxi. The takin is a Class I protected species.

Serows are smaller than takins but significantly larger than gorals. Both serows and gorals live in rainy mountainous regions and are excellent climbers. Serows have shorter and coarser wool than gorals. The [mainland serow](/wiki/Mainland_serow) is spread across southern China. #### Mountain sheep and goat

[Himalayan blue sheep or bharal](/wiki/Bharal)

[Siberian ibex](/wiki/Siberian_ibex)

The [argali](/wiki/Argali) or mountain sheep, the Asian cousin of the North American [bighorn sheep](/wiki/Bighorn_sheep) has nine subspecies, seven of which are found in northern and western China, including the [Marco Polo sheep](/wiki/Marco_Polo_sheep), which the [Venetian traveler](/wiki/Marco_Polo) reported observing in the [Pamir mountains](/wiki/Pamir_mountains).

The [Himalayan blue sheep](/wiki/Bharal), with much smaller horns than the argali, are agile climbers on Himalayan cliffs. The [dwarf blue sheep](/wiki/Dwarf_blue_sheep) is found in western Sichuan. The [Himalayan tahr](/wiki/Himalayan_tahr), discovered in China in 1974, is a Class I protected species with perhaps only 500 animals in southern Tibet.

The [Siberian ibex](/wiki/Siberian_ibex), the largest and heaviest goat, is found in the [Tian Shan](/wiki/Tian_Shan) range of Xinjiang.

#### Cattle, camel, pig

There are large numbers of domesticated [gaur](/wiki/Gaur), [yak](/wiki/Yak) and [Bactrian camel](/wiki/Bactrian_camel) in China but in the wild, they are Class I protected species. The gaur or Indian bison is the tallest species of cattle and found in southern Tibet and Yunnan. Domesticated gaur, called [gayal](/wiki/Gayal), is raised by farmers in Yunnan. Yaks are the largest animals on the [Tibetan Plateau](/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau). Wild yaks are larger than domestic yaks and slightly smaller than the gaur. They can tolerate extremely cold climate, climb steep slopes, and ford fierce rapids. Yaks are the imost important animal for Tibetan herders, who eat yak meat and milk for food, burn yak dung as fuel, spin yak hair into fabric, make yak hide leather and use yaks to transport and plow fields. Bactrian camels have two humps and can go a month or longer without drinking water. A thirsty Bactrian camel can drink 135 liters (30 gallons) in only 13 minutes. They can withstand extremely hot and cold weather and have broad hooves that do not sink in desert. Bactrian camels are known as the "boats of desert" – for millennia, they were used to carry goods along the Silk Road. Wild camels are critically endangered and found in the [Gobi](/wiki/Gobi) and [Taklamakan Deserts](/wiki/Taklamakan_Desert).

### Pangolin

[Chinese pangolin](/wiki/Chinese_pangolin)

The [pangolin](/wiki/Pangolin), a scaly [anteater](/wiki/Anteater) that feed on ants and termites and curl into a ball when threatened, is prized in China for its flesh, which is considered a delicacy and scales, which used in traditional Chinese medicine to treat among other ailments, inadequate lactation in breast-feeding mothers. The [Chinese pangolin](/wiki/Chinese_pangolin) is found throughout southern China, Hainan and Taiwan and the [Sunda pangolin](/wiki/Sunda_pangolin) in western Yunnan. In Chinese, the pangolin is called "that which wears mountain armor" and the animal is believed by local shamans to hold magical powers such that hunters must utter incantation before killing them to ward off bad luck. As a Class II protected species, trading of wild pangolins is prohibited, but poaching and illegal trade remains rampant. The pangolin can be farm-raised, but pangolin farms must generally also raise termites to feed the livestock. In recent years, Chinese customs have intercepted large shipments of pangolin from Southeast Asia and Africa.

### Rodents

#### Porcupine

[Indian porcupine](/wiki/Indian_porcupine)

The [porcupine](/wiki/Porcupine), called *haozhu* or "pig with long thin hair" in Chinese, should not be confused with [hedgehog](/wiki/Hedgehog), *ciwei* or the "thorned creature". Porcupines are rodents and hedgehogs belong to a [separate order](/wiki/Erinaceidae). Three species of [Old World porcupine](/wiki/Old_World_porcupine) are found in China: the [Asiatic brush-tailed porcupine](/wiki/Asiatic_brush-tailed_porcupine), [Indian crested porcupine](/wiki/Indian_crested_porcupine), and [Malayan porcupine](/wiki/Malayan_porcupine). Many parts of the porcupine including the brain, organs, fat, quills and even the feces can be used to make traditional Chinese medicine. Porcupines are raised on farms.

#### Beaver

[Eurasian beaver](/wiki/Eurasian_beaver)

In the early 20th century, the [Eurasian beaver](/wiki/Eurasian_beaver) was hunted to near extinction for its fur and [castoreum](/wiki/Castoreum), a scent gland secretion used to make perfume and medicine. Though the global population has rebounded, the animal remains a Class I protected species. The Bulgan Beaver Nature Reserve in [Qinggil County](/wiki/Qinggil_County) of northern [Xinjiang](/wiki/Xinjiang), at the source of the [Irtysh](/wiki/Irtysh_River) and [Ulungur River](/wiki/Ulungur_River) along the border with [Mongolia](/wiki/Mongolia), was created in 1980 to protect the beaver. In 2007, there were 145 beaver colonies with an estimated population of 500–600 beavers in the reserve.

#### Squirrels

[Squirrels](/wiki/Squirrels) are called *songshu* or "pine rodent" in Chinese but not all species live in trees. The squirrel family includes [tree squirrels](/wiki/Tree_squirrel), [flying squirrels](/wiki/Flying_squirrel), [ground squirrels](/wiki/Ground_squirrel), [rock squirrels](/wiki/Rock_squirrel), [marmots](/wiki/Marmot) and [chipmunks](/wiki/Chipmunk), which are all found in China, often in great variety.

[Red squirrel](/wiki/Red_squirrel)

[Himalayan striped squirrel](/wiki/Himalayan_striped_squirrel)

[Black giant squirrel](/wiki/Black_giant_squirrel)

The [red squirrel](/wiki/Red_squirrel) common in Europe and the [black giant squirrel](/wiki/Black_giant_squirrel) of Southeast Asia are found, respectively, in the northern and southern parts of China. Other [tree squirrel](/wiki/Tree_squirrel) species include the [Pallas's](/wiki/Pallas%27s_squirrel), [inornate](/wiki/Inornate_squirrel), [Phayre's](/wiki/Phayre%27s_squirrel), [Irrawaddy](/wiki/Irrawaddy_squirrel), [Anderson's](/wiki/Anderson%27s_squirrel), [orange-bellied Himalayan](/wiki/Orange-bellied_Himalayan_squirrel), [Perny's long-nosed](/wiki/Perny%27s_long-nosed_squirrel), [red-hipped](/wiki/Red-hipped_squirrel), [Asian red-cheeked](/wiki/Asian_red-cheeked_squirrel), [Himalayan striped](/wiki/Himalayan_striped_squirrel), [Maritime striped](/wiki/Maritime_striped_squirrel), and [Swinhoe's striped squirrel](/wiki/Swinhoe%27s_striped_squirrel).

[Flying squirrels](/wiki/Flying_squirrel) are found in almost every part of China, from the Himalayas to the tropical island of Hainan to the rural outskirts of Beijing. Flying squirrel species include the [groove-toothed](/wiki/Groove-toothed_flying_squirrel), [complex-toothed](/wiki/Complex-toothed_flying_squirrel), [hairy-footed](/wiki/Hairy-footed_flying_squirrel), [particolored](/wiki/Particolored_flying_squirrel), [Indochinese](/wiki/Indochinese_flying_squirrel), [red giant](/wiki/Red_giant_flying_squirrel), [red and white giant](/wiki/Red_and_white_giant_flying_squirrel), [spotted giant](/wiki/Spotted_giant_flying_squirrel), [Indian giant](/wiki/Indian_giant_flying_squirrel), [Chinese giant](/wiki/Chinese_giant_flying_squirrel), [Japanese giant](/wiki/Japanese_giant_flying_squirrel), [Bhutan giant](/wiki/Bhutan_giant_flying_squirrel), [Siberian](/wiki/Siberian_flying_squirrel), Yunnan giant (*petaurista yunnanensis*), and [Hodgson's giant](/wiki/Hodgson%27s_giant_flying_squirrel). Several are endemic to China.

[Indian giant flying squirrel](/wiki/Indian_giant_flying_squirrel)

[Red giant flying squirrel](/wiki/Red_giant_flying_squirrel)

[Bhutan giant flying squirrel](/wiki/Bhutan_giant_flying_squirrel)

The fecal pellets of the [Siberian flying squirrel](/wiki/Siberian_flying_squirrel). The pellets are used in traditional Chinese medicine.

Flying squirrels are timid creatures that are active at nighttime and use the [patagium](/wiki/Patagium), a membrane connecting the fore and hind limbs to glide from trees. They do not build nests and live in caves or rock crevices. They also defecate at specific locations, which facilitates the harvest of their fecal pellets. The pellets are made into *wulingzhi*, a traditional Chinese medicine used to facilitate blood flow and ease pain. Flying squirrel pellets can accumulate on the floor of caves for years and not rot. Several species of flying squirrels are farm-raised to produce *wulingzhi*.

The [groove-toothed flying squirrel](/wiki/Groove-toothed_flying_squirrel), also known as the North Chinese flying squirrel, is endemic to eastern Hebei Province and the suburbs of Beijing in [North China](/wiki/North_China) and northern Sichuan. The [complex-toothed flying squirrel](/wiki/Complex-toothed_flying_squirrel) is endemic to southern China.

[Ground squirrels](/wiki/Ground_squirrels), [rock squirrels](/wiki/Rock_squirrel), [marmots](/wiki/Marmot) and [chipmunks](/wiki/Chipmunk) belong to the same tribe within the squirrel family.

[Yellow ground squirrel](/wiki/Yellow_ground_squirrel)

[Siberian chipmunk](/wiki/Siberian_chipmunk)

[Père David's rock squirrel](/wiki/P%C3%A8re_David%27s_rock_squirrel) at the [Summer Palace](/wiki/Summer_Palace) in [Beijing](/wiki/Beijing)

In China, ground squirrels are found in arid regions of the north and west where the animals live in burrows. Ground squirrel species include the [Alashan](/wiki/Alashan_ground_squirrel), [Daurian](/wiki/Daurian_ground_squirrel), [red-cheeked](/wiki/Red-cheeked_ground_squirrel), [long-tailed](/wiki/Long-tailed_ground_squirrel) and [yellow ground squirrel](/wiki/Yellow_ground_squirrel).

Two species of rock squirrels are endemic to China, the [Père David's rock squirrel](/wiki/P%C3%A8re_David%27s_rock_squirrel), which is found across a wide swath of the country from the mountains around Beijing to Gansu and Sichuan, and the [Forrest's rock squirrel](/wiki/Forrest%27s_rock_squirrel), found only in the mountains dividing the [Yangtze](/wiki/Yangtze_River) and [Mekong River](/wiki/Mekong_River) watershed in northwestern Yunnan.

The [Siberian chipmunk](/wiki/Siberian_chipmunk), the only chipmunk species found outside [North America](/wiki/North_America), has six subspecies in China, all in northern parts of the country. The animal is raised as pets and for its tender flesh, fine fur and ingredients for traditional Chinese medicine.

The [marmot](/wiki/Marmot), called *hanta* in Chinese for "land" or "dry otter", is related to ground squirrels but are bigger, have shorter tails and are more social animals. They can grow to be the size of a cat and live in large colonies. Four species are found in China, all along the northern and western periphery of the country: [gray](/wiki/Gray_marmot), [long-tailed](/wiki/Long-tailed_marmot), [Himalayan](/wiki/Himalayan_marmot), and [Tarbagan](/wiki/Tarbagan_marmot). Of these, the tarbagan marmot is an endangered, Class III protected species. Marmots are also farm-raised for food and fur.

#### Jumping rodents

[Mongolian five-toed jerboa](/wiki/Mongolian_five-toed_jerboa)

[Southern birch mouse](/wiki/Southern_birch_mouse)

A wide variety of jumping rodents belonging to the family *[Dipodidae](/wiki/Dipodidae)* can be found in China. These include [jerboas](/wiki/Jerboas) and [jumping mice](/wiki/Jumping_mice), called *tiaoshu*, the "jumping rodent", and the [birch mouse](/wiki/Birch_mouse), called *jueshu*, the "falling rodent" or "stomping rodent". Jerboas, jumping mice, and birch mice all have long hind legs which can be used to make leaps from a bipedal stance.

#### Zokors, bamboo rats

[Lesser bamboo rat](/wiki/Lesser_bamboo_rat)

[Zokors](/wiki/Zokor) and [bamboo rats](/wiki/Bamboo_rat) are chubby and furry rodents with short limbs that burrow underground.

Zokors have strong front limbs for digging. Zokor bones are used in traditional Chinese medicine and can substitute tiger bones. The [Chinese zokor](/wiki/Chinese_zokor), [Rothschild's zokor](/wiki/Rothschild%27s_zokor) and [Smith's zokor](/wiki/Smith%27s_zokor) are endemic to China. The range of the Chinese zokor extends across north China from Qinghai to Beijing while that of the Rothschild's and Smith's zokors are confined to Gansu, Shaanxi, Hubei and Qinghai. The [false zokor](/wiki/False_zokor) and [Transbaikal zokor](/wiki/Transbaikal_zokor) are found along China's border region with Russia and Mongolia.

All four bamboo rat species in the world are found in China: the [Chinese bamboo rat](/wiki/Chinese_bamboo_rat) south of the Yangtze, [hoary bamboo rat](/wiki/Hoary_bamboo_rat) in southwest China, [large bamboo rat](/wiki/Large_bamboo_rat) in [Xishuangbanna](/wiki/Xishuangbanna) in southern Yunnan and [lesser bamboo rat](/wiki/Lesser_bamboo_rat) and western Yunnan. The large bamboo rat can weigh as much as 5 kg. The flesh of the bamboo rat is rich in protein and low in fat. Bamboo rat oil can be used to treat burn wounds.

Both the zokor and bamboo rat are farm-raised for their fur, meat and use in medicine.

#### Hamsters

About half of the world's 25 species of [hamsters](/wiki/Hamster) are found in China. Most live in the deserts of Xinjiang, Gansu and Inner Mongolia. Some are named after the specific region in which they are found, such as the [Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_hamster), [Mongolian](/wiki/Mongolian_hamster), [Gansu](/wiki/Gansu_hamster), [Chinese striped](/wiki/Chinese_striped_hamster), [Tibetan dwarf](/wiki/Tibetan_dwarf_hamster), [Kham dwarf](/wiki/Kam_dwarf_hamster), and [Djungarian hamster](/wiki/Djungarian_hamster), and some by their founder, such as [Campbell's dwarf](/wiki/Campbell%27s_dwarf_hamster), [Roborovski](/wiki/Roborovski_hamster), and [Sokolov's dwarf](/wiki/Sokolov%27s_dwarf_hamster). Others include the [gray dwarf](/wiki/Grey_dwarf_hamster), [long-tailed dwarf](/wiki/Long-tailed_dwarf_hamster), [greater long-tailed hamster](/wiki/Greater_long-tailed_hamster) and [black-bellied hamster](/wiki/Black-bellied_hamster). The Chinese hamster and Roborovski hamster have been bred as pets and found in homes throughout the world.

### Bats

Bats, the only mammals capable of sustained flight, are the second largest order of mammals after rodents. They are divided broadly into [microbats](/wiki/Microbats), which use [echolocation](/wiki/Animal_echolocation) to navigate and hunt insects, and [megabats](/wiki/Megabats), which rely on large eyes and keen smell to feed on fruits and nectar. Bats are found in great abundance and variety throughout China and are considered to be auspicious animals, symbolizing good fortune. Bat feces collected from caves are used in traditional Chinese medicine.

#### Megabats

[Lesser short-nosed fruit bat](/wiki/Lesser_short-nosed_fruit_bat)

[Lyle's flying foxes](/wiki/Lyle%27s_flying_fox)

[Large flying foxes](/wiki/Large_flying_fox)

Megabats, also called fruit bats, include [flying foxes](/wiki/Flying_foxes), which are the largest bat species. Four species are found in China, all in isolated populations: the [large flying fox](/wiki/Large_flying_fox) in Shaanxi, [Indian flying fox](/wiki/Indian_flying_fox) in Qinghai, [Ryukyu flying fox](/wiki/Ryukyu_flying_fox) in Taiwan, and [Lyle's flying fox](/wiki/Lyle%27s_flying_fox) in Yunnan. The large flying fox can weigh 0.65–1.1 kg (1.4–2.4 lb) and has a wingspan of up to 1.5 m (4 ft 11 in).

[Geoffroy's rousette](/wiki/Geoffroy%27s_rousette) and [Leschenault's rousette](/wiki/Leschenault%27s_rousette), both dog-faced fruit bats, are the only megabats in China that can echolocate. Unlike microbats, which generate ultrasound with their larynx, rousettes generate sonar sound waves with tongue clicks.

Other fruit bat species include the [greater](/wiki/Greater_short-nosed_fruit_bat) and [lesser short-nosed fruit bat](/wiki/Lesser_short-nosed_fruit_bat), [Blanford's fruit bat](/wiki/Blanford%27s_fruit_bat) and the [cave nectar bat](/wiki/Cave_nectar_bat). Fruit bats are sometimes considered pests by fruit farmers, and are hunted and eaten in parts of Guangdong, Guangxi and Hainan. They also help pollinate certain species of tropical fruit trees.

#### Microbats

##### Vesper bats

[Vesper](/wiki/Vesper_bat) or evening bats comprise the largest family of bats with at least 45 species in China. Members include [mouse-eared bats](/wiki/Myotis), [long-eared bats](/wiki/Long-eared_bat), [pipistrelles](/wiki/Pipistrelle), [noctules](/wiki/Noctule) and [barbastelles](/wiki/Barbastelle).

[Mouse-eared bats](/wiki/Mouse-eared_bats)

[Pond bat](/wiki/Pond_bat)

[Daubenton's bat](/wiki/Daubenton%27s_bat)

[Whiskered bat](/wiki/Whiskered_bat)

[Hodgson's bats](/wiki/Hodgson%27s_bat)

[Lesser mouse-eared bat](/wiki/Lesser_mouse-eared_bat)

[Common pipistrelle](/wiki/Common_pipistrelle)

[Brown long-eared bat](/wiki/Brown_long-eared_bat)

[*Myotis*](/wiki/Mouse-eared_bat) or mouse-eared bats are delicate and furry bats with pointed ears. Of the 90 or so species in the world, about one-fifth are found in China.

The [lesser mouse-eared bat](/wiki/Lesser_mouse-eared_bat), [pond bat](/wiki/Pond_bat), [Daubenton's bat](/wiki/Daubenton%27s_bat), [Natterer's bat](/wiki/Natterer%27s_bat) and [whiskered bat](/wiki/Whiskered_bat) are spread across Eurasia. Others inhabit either the warmer climes of southern China and Southeast Asia including the [large myotis](/wiki/Large_myotis), [Szechwan myotis](/wiki/Szechwan_myotis), [Burmese whiskered bat](/wiki/Burmese_whiskered_bat) and [Horsfield's bat](/wiki/Horsfield%27s_bat) or the temperate regions of northern China and Northeast Asia including the [Far Eastern myotis](/wiki/Far_Eastern_myotis), [fraternal myotis](/wiki/Fraternal_myotis), and [Ikonnikov's bat](/wiki/Ikonnikov%27s_bat). [Hodgson's bat](/wiki/Hodgson%27s_bat), known for its distinctive golden fur, has unconnected populations in Afghanistan, India, central China, southeastern China, Manchuria, Taiwan, Korea and Indonesia. The [Beijing mouse-eared bat](/wiki/Beijing_mouse-eared_bat) is endemic to eastern China, and the [long-footed myotis](/wiki/Myotis_fimbriatus) is endemic to southern China and Hong Kong.

Most mouse-eared bats are insectivores. [Rickett's big-footed bat](/wiki/Rickett%27s_big-footed_bat), which is distributed across [China proper](/wiki/China_proper) into Laos, lives near water and feeds on fish. The [large-footed bat](/wiki/Large-footed_bat) of Taiwan hunts insects on the surface of the water.

[Pipistrels](/wiki/Pipistrel) and their relatives are tiny bats that flutter like butterflies in flight. The [common pipistrelle](/wiki/Common_pipistrelle) weighs only 3.5 to 8.5 g (0.12 to 0.30 oz) and has a wingspan ranging of 18 to 25 cm (7.1 to 9.8 in). Other pipistrelles found in China include the [least pipistrelle](/wiki/Least_pipistrelle), [Kelaart's](/wiki/Kelaart%27s_pipistrelle), [Mount Popa](/wiki/Mount_Popa_pipistrelle), [Savi's](/wiki/Savi%27s_pipistrelle), [chocolate](/wiki/Chocolate_pipistrelle) [black-gilded](/wiki/Black-gilded_pipistrelle) and the [Chinese pipistrelle](/wiki/Chinese_pipistrelle). In Chinese, pipistrelles are called *fuyi* meaning "hidden wing". The flesh, blood, brain and feces of pipistrelle can be used to make traditional Chinese medicine. The brain is applied to the skin to treat acne and ingested to improve memory.

[Common noctule](/wiki/Common_noctule)

[Noctules](/wiki/Noctule) are closely related to pipistrelles but can be much larger in size. The [Chinese noctule](/wiki/Chinese_noctule), which is endemic to the southern half of the country and Taiwan, weighs three to four times as much as the Chinese pipistrelle. Known as "mountain bats" in Chinese, noctules live in caves and rock croppings as well as the under the eaves of traditional homes. Noctules droppings are collected for medicinal uses. Other noctule bats in China include the [common noctule](/wiki/Common_noctule), [lesser noctule](/wiki/Lesser_noctule), and [birdlike noctule](/wiki/Birdlike_noctule).

Barbastelles are called wide-eared bats in Chinese. The range of the [Asian barbastelle](/wiki/Asian_barbastelle) extends from Egypt through China to Japan. In 2001, a Chinese zoologist discovered a new species of barbastelle in the mountains of rural [Beijing](/wiki/Beijing). This bat was discovered in a cave in [Fangshan District](/wiki/Fangshan_District) where four other bat species—Rickett's big-foot, large mouse-eared, [greater horseshoe](/wiki/Greater_horseshoe_bat) and [greater tube-nosed bats](/wiki/Greater_tube-nosed_bat) also live.
The [Beijing barbastelle](/wiki/Beijing_barbastelle) (*Barbastella beijingensis*) was distinguished by the distinctiveness of its DNA and recognized as a species on 23 May 2007, the 300th birthday of [Carl Linnaeus](/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus). As of 2012, no other populations of this species have been found beyond Beijing.

[Long-eared bats](/wiki/Long-eared_bat) have enormous ears that can grow almost as long as their bodies, and are represented in China by multiple species (e.g. *[Plecotus kozlovi](/wiki/Plecotus_kozlovi)* and *[Plecotus ognevi](/wiki/Plecotus_ognevi)*). The [greater](/wiki/Greater_bamboo_bat) and [lesser bamboo bats](/wiki/Lesser_bamboo_bat) prefer to roost inside the hollow shoots of giant bamboo through holes eaten by beetles. Because the holes are small, bamboo bats are also tiny. An adult lesser bamboo bat that measures 4 cm (1.6 in) in length and weighs 3.5 to 5.8 g (0.12 to 0.20 oz), is not much bigger than a [bumble bee](/wiki/Bumble_bee).

[House bats](/wiki/House_bat) including the [Gobi big brown bat](/wiki/Gobi_big_brown_bat), [northern bat](/wiki/Northern_bat), [thick-eared bat](/wiki/Thick-eared_bat), [serotine bat](/wiki/Serotine_bat) are also closely related to pipistrelles, noctules and barbastelles. Other relatives within [this extensive subfamily](/wiki/Vespertilioninae) include [Tickell's bat](/wiki/Tickell%27s_bat), [great evening bat](/wiki/Great_evening_bat), [harlequin bat](/wiki/Harlequin_bat), [greater Asiatic yellow bat](/wiki/Greater_Asiatic_yellow_bat), [parti-colored bat](/wiki/Parti-colored_bat) and [Asian particolored bat](/wiki/Asian_particolored_bat).

[Tube-nosed bats](/wiki/Murininae) have longer nostrils than other vespers and funnel-shaped ears. Chinese species include the [greater](/wiki/Greater_tube-nosed_bat), [little](/wiki/Little_tube-nosed_bat), [round-eared](/wiki/Round-eared_tube-nosed_bat), [Hutton's](/wiki/Hutton%27s_tube-nosed_bat), and [dusky tube-nosed bat](/wiki/Dusky_tube-nosed_bat). The dusky tube-nosed bat is endemic to Heilongjiang and Jilin in northeastern China. The greater tube-nosed bat of Beijing feeds on aerial beetles.

The [painted bat](/wiki/Painted_bat) and [Hardwicke's woolly bat](/wiki/Hardwicke%27s_woolly_bat), also vesper bats, live in the forests of southern China.

##### Long-winged bats

[Common bent-wing bat](/wiki/Common_bent-wing_bat)

[Long-winged bats](/wiki/Miniopterus) in China include the [common](/wiki/Common_bent-wing_bat) and [western bent-winged bats](/wiki/Western_bent-winged_bat). The common bent-wing bats can form large colonies and migrate hundreds of kilometers.

##### Free-tailed bats

[European free-tailed bat](/wiki/European_free-tailed_bat)

[Free-tailed bats](/wiki/Molossidae), unlike other bats, have tails that are detached from their wing membrances. Species include the [European free-tailed bat](/wiki/European_free-tailed_bat), [La Touche's free-tailed bat](/wiki/La_Touche%27s_free-tailed_bat) and the [wrinkle-lipped free-tailed bat](/wiki/Wrinkle-lipped_free-tailed_bat).

##### False vampire

[Greater false vampire bat](/wiki/Greater_false_vampire_bat)

[Rufous horseshoe bat](/wiki/Rufous_horseshoe_bat)

The [greater false vampire bat](/wiki/Greater_false_vampire_bat) of Guangxi is a carnivorous bat that feeds on rodents, fish, insects and smaller bats. It is smaller than the ["true" vampire bats](/wiki/Spectral_bat) of South America.

##### Sac-winged bats

[Black-bearded tomb bat](/wiki/Black-bearded_tomb_bat)

[Sac-winged bats](/wiki/Sac-winged_bat) have sac-like glands under their wings that carry [pheromones](/wiki/Pheromones), which are released to attract mates. Out of some 51 sac-winged bat species in the world, only the [black-bearded tomb bat](/wiki/Black-bearded_tomb_bat) is found in China.

##### Horseshoe bats

[Horseshoe bats](/wiki/Horseshoe_bat) are called "[chrysanthemum](/wiki/Chrysanthemum) bats" in Chinese because they have horseshoe-shaped folds of skin that unfurl on their faces like the petals of a flower. These noseleaves help the horseshoe bat emit ultrasonic signals for echolocation. Species found in China include the [greater](/wiki/Greater_horseshoe_bat), [least](/wiki/Least_horseshoe_bat), [king](/wiki/King_horseshoe_bat), [big-eared](/wiki/Big-eared_horseshoe_bat), [rufous](/wiki/Rufous_horseshoe_bat), [Chinese rufous](/wiki/Chinese_rufous_horseshoe_bat), [little Japanese](/wiki/Little_Japanese_horseshoe_bat), [Blyth's](/wiki/Blyth%27s_horseshoe_bat), [Osgood's](/wiki/Osgood%27s_horseshoe_bat), [Pearson's](/wiki/Pearson%27s_horseshoe_bat), [Thomas's](/wiki/Thomas%27s_horseshoe_bat), and [Dobson's](/wiki/Dobson%27s_horseshoe_bat). The king and Osgood's horseshoe bats are endemic to southwest China.
Scientists believe that the [SARS coronavirus](/wiki/SARS_coronavirus) may have originated in horseshoe bats in China.

Closely related to the horseshoe bats are the [roundleaf bats](/wiki/Hipposideros), including the [great roundleaf](/wiki/Great_roundleaf_bat), [intermediate roundleaf](/wiki/Intermediate_roundleaf_bat), [Pomona](/wiki/Pomona_roundleaf_bat) and [Pratt's](/wiki/Pratt%27s_roundleaf_bat), the [East Asian tailless leaf-nosed bat](/wiki/East_Asian_tailless_leaf-nosed_bat) and [Stoliczka's trident bat](/wiki/Stoliczka%27s_trident_bat).

## Amphibians

For a more comprehensive list, see [List of amphibians of China](/wiki/List_of_amphibians_of_China).

China is home to 346 species of amphibian. China's amphibian diversity is greater than any other country in the Old World, and it is the 5th in the whole world. China's amphibian fauna includes an important element of widespread, generally non-threatened species though 27.3% of amphibian species are extinct or threatened and because conservation assessments of Chinese amphibians have only started recently, it is likely that the current data on threats to amphibians are insufficient. Several amphibian species in China have very limited geographical distributions.

## Fish

In freshwater alone, China has more than 1,000 fish species. By far the most diverse order are the [cypriniforms](/wiki/Cypriniform), followed by the [siluriforms](/wiki/Siluriform). [Yangtze](/wiki/Yangtze) is the richest river basin in the country and it is home to more than 350 strict freshwater fish species (as well as several also found in [brackish](/wiki/Brackish) or saltwater). A high percentage of these are [endemic](/wiki/Endemism) to the country and many are seriously threatened. Among others, it is feared that the [Chinese paddlefish](/wiki/Chinese_paddlefish), as well as several species from the Yunnan lakes (notably [Dian](/wiki/Dian_Lake), [Erhai](/wiki/Erhai_Lake), [Fuxian](/wiki/Fuxian_Lake) and [Yilong](/wiki/Yilong_Lake)), already are extinct. China has far more [cavefish](/wiki/Cavefish) species than any other country in the world.

With a long coastline that ranges from temperate to tropical oceans, China has many marine fish species such as the [Pacific cod](/wiki/Pacific_cod).

## Invertebrates

### Freshwater crabs

China is home to more than 250 different species of [freshwater crabs](/wiki/Freshwater_crab) (families [Potamidae](/wiki/Potamidae) and [Gecarcinucidae](/wiki/Gecarcinucidae)), many of them endemics. It is thus the country with the highest species richness in freshwater crabs. The most speciose genera are *Sinopotamon*, *Longpotamon*, *[Indochinamon](/wiki/Indochinamon)* and *[Nanhaipotamon](/wiki/Nanhaipotamon)*.
