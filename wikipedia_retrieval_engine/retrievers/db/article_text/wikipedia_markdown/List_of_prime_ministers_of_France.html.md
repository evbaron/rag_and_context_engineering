# List of prime ministers of France

The [head](/wiki/Head_of_government) of the [government of France](/wiki/Government_of_France) has been called the [prime minister of France](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_France) (French: *Premier ministre*) since 1959, when [Michel Debré](/wiki/Michel_Debr%C3%A9) became the first officeholder appointed under the [Fifth Republic](/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic). During earlier periods of [French history](/wiki/History_of_France), the country's head of government was known by different titles. As was common in European democracies of the 1815–1958 period (the [Bourbon Restoration](/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France) and [July Monarchy](/wiki/July_Monarchy), the [Second](/wiki/French_Second_Republic), [Third](/wiki/French_Third_Republic), and [Fourth Republic](/wiki/French_Fourth_Republic), as well as the [Vichy regime](/wiki/Vichy_France)), the head of government was called "President of the Council of Ministers" (*Président du Conseil des ministres*), generally shortened to "President of the Council" (*Président du Conseil*). This should not be confused with the elected office of [president of the French Republic](/wiki/President_of_France), who, as [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state), appoints the prime minister as head of government.

## 16th century – 18th century

### Kingdom of France (843–1792)

Main articles: [Ancien régime](/wiki/Ancien_r%C3%A9gime), [Chief minister of France](/wiki/Chief_minister_of_France), [Kingdom of France](/wiki/Kingdom_of_France), and [Kingdom of France (1791–92)](/wiki/Kingdom_of_France_(1791%E2%80%9392))

Under the [Kingdom of France](/wiki/Kingdom_of_France), there was no official title for the leader of the government as the monarch held [absolute power](/wiki/Absolute_monarchy_in_France). However, conventions developed that the monarch would not act without the advice of [royal council](/wiki/Conseil_du_Roi). The chief ministers (*principaux ministres*) of these councils under certain kings of France exercised enough influence to lead the government *[de facto](/wiki/De_facto)*. This situation ended with the start of the [French Revolution](/wiki/French_Revolution) as the adoption of a constitution in [1791](/wiki/French_Constitution_of_1791) reframed the power dynamics of the monarchy. As the revolution progressed, the power dynamics continued to shift and ultimately resulted in the [execution of the monarch](/wiki/Execution_of_Louis_XVI) and the [establishment of a republic](/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_abolition_of_the_monarchy).

## 18th century – 19th century

### French First Republic (1792–1804)

Main articles: [French Revolution](/wiki/French_Revolution) and [First French Republic](/wiki/First_French_Republic)

During the First Republic, there were three arrangements for governance, the leadership of which changed frequently. The first government was that of the [National Convention](/wiki/National_Convention) (20 September 1792 – 2 November 1795) and did not provide for a formal head of state or a head of government. The [President of the National Convention](/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_National_Convention) would only serve for a term of 14 days at a time. Even though the President was re-eligible to return to the office after another term had passed, this rarely occurred. Notable Presidents during this period included:

- [Jérôme Pétion de Villeneuve](/wiki/J%C3%A9r%C3%B4me_P%C3%A9tion_de_Villeneuve) (20 September – 4 October 1792)
- [Georges Danton](/wiki/Georges_Danton) (25 July – 8 August 1793)
- [Maximilien Robespierre](/wiki/Maximilien_Robespierre) (22 August – 7 September 1793 and 4–19 June 1794)
- [Jean Jacques Régis de Cambacérès](/wiki/Jean-Jacques-R%C3%A9gis_de_Cambac%C3%A9r%C3%A8s) (7–22 October 1794)
- [Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès](/wiki/Emmanuel_Joseph_Siey%C3%A8s) (20 April – 5 May 1795)

Given the dissatisfaction with the National Convention a new Constitution was adopted in [1795](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Year_III) which created the a five member executive called the [Directory](/wiki/French_Directory) (2 November 1795 – 10 November 1799). The members of the Directory were elected by the legislature who themselves in turn elected a ceremonial President whose term lasted only three months. [Paul Barras](/wiki/Paul_Barras), who served two stints as President (4 December 1797 – 25 February 1798 and 26 November 1798 – 26 May 1799), came to be the dominant member of the group.

In 1799, the Directory was superseded when a [coup d'état](/wiki/Coup_of_18_Brumaire) headed by [Napoleon Bonaparte](/wiki/Napoleon) forced the sitting members to resign. A new [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Year_VIII) was written establishing the [Consulate](/wiki/French_Consulate) (10 November 1799 – 18 May 1804). While initially a coequal of the other Consuls, Napoleon quickly set himself up as "First Consul of France" and later "Consul for Life" under yet another [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Year_X).

### French First Empire (1804–1815)

Main article: [First French Empire](/wiki/First_French_Empire)

On May 18, 1804 the [French Senate](/wiki/S%C3%A9nat_conservateur) bestowed the title of "[Emperor of the French](/wiki/Emperor_of_the_French)" on Napoleon and he [crowned himself](/wiki/Coronation_of_Napoleon) on December 2, 1804. As Emperor, Napoleon was both head of state and head of government under the new [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Year_XII).

### First Restoration (1814–1815)

Main article: [First Restoration](/wiki/First_Restoration)

Following Napoleon's defeat in the [War of the Sixth Coalition](/wiki/War_of_the_Sixth_Coalition), he [abdicated](/wiki/Napoleon%27s_first_abdication) and was [exiled](/wiki/Principality_of_Elba) to the island of [Elba](/wiki/Elba). [Louis XVIII](/wiki/Louis_XVIII), brother of the executed King Louis XVI, was placed on the newly restored throne of the French monarchy. Unlike the previous [Ancien régime](/wiki/Ancien_r%C3%A9gime), the prerogative of the new monarch was limited by the [Charter of 1814](/wiki/Charter_of_1814).

### Hundred Days (1815)

Main article: [Hundred Days](/wiki/Hundred_Days)

In March 1815, Napoleon left his exile and returned to France. The [government of the Louis XVIII](/wiki/Ghent_government_in_exile) fled to the city of [Ghent](/wiki/Ghent). Napoleon reinstated his role as both head of state and head of government. Upon [Napoleon's second abdication](/wiki/Napoleon%27s_second_abdication), his son [Napoleon II](/wiki/Napoleon_II) was named Emperor. This rule was nominal, and Napoleon II, then a four-year old child, remained in Austria throughout his nominal reign.

### Bourbon Restoration (1815–1830)

Main article: [Bourbon Restoration in France](/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France)

Following his second defeat at the [Battle of Waterloo](/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo), Napoleon was again [exiled](/wiki/Napoleon%27s_exile_to_St._Helena), this time to the island of [St. Helena](/wiki/Saint_Helena). Louis XVIII returned to Paris and to his role as constitutional monarch.

### July Monarchy (1830–1848)

Main article: [July Monarchy](/wiki/July_Monarchy)

When King [Charles X](/wiki/Charles_X_of_France) was [crowned](/wiki/Coronation_of_Charles_X_of_France) he swore to uphold the constitutional Charter of 1814, but as his reign progressed he was seen to be increasingly in conflict with it. In 1830 the tension came to a climax when Charles dissolved the legislature, suspended certain liberties, and barred the middle class from participating in future elections. These [July Ordinances](/wiki/July_Ordinances) in turn triggered outrage in Paris which resulted in the [July Revolution](/wiki/July_Revolution). The revolution resulted in Charles X abdicating the throne in favor of his cousin [Louis Philippe](/wiki/Louis_Philippe_I), Duke of Orléans who promised to maintain a liberal constitutional monarchy under the [Charter of 1830](/wiki/Charter_of_1830). Unlike the previous Charter, which was self-imposed by the monarch, the 1830 charter emanated from the people. This popular monarchy was reflected in Louis Philippe's title of "King of the French" (*roi des Français*) rather than "King of France" (*roi de France*).

### Second French Republic (1848–1852)

Main article: [Second French Republic](/wiki/Second_French_Republic)

The popularity of the new "Citizen King" declined over concerns that the suffrage of the populace was extremely limited to certain property owners. The desire to expand the electorate to a more [universal male suffrage](/wiki/Universal_manhood_suffrage) was undercut by a 1835 law which prohibited public political assemblies. To circumvent this ban, in 1847 a series of private meeting known as the [campagne des banquets](/wiki/Campagne_des_banquets) were organized. When these banquets were themselves banned popular unrest spilled into the streets in the [February Revolution of 1848](/wiki/French_Revolution_of_1848), which resulted in King Louis Philippe abdicating the throne and triggered a [series of revolutions](/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848) across Europe. A provisional government proclaimed a Second Republic, which would organized under a [new constitution](/wiki/French_Constitution_of_1848) which provided for the election of a [President](/wiki/President_of_France). [Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte](/wiki/Napoleon_III_of_France), nephew of the former Emperor, won the subsequent [election](/wiki/1848_French_presidential_election) in a landslide.

### Second French Empire (1852–1870)

Main article: [Second French Empire](/wiki/Second_French_Empire)

Under the constitution of the Second Republic, the President was to serve only one non-renewable term of four years. Bonaparte attempted to pass a constitutional amendment to make him eligible to run for another term, and while the amendment did receive a majority in the legislature, it was not enough to overcome the 2/3 supermajority needed. Believing he had the support of the people to continue in his policies, Bonaparte staged a [self-coup](/wiki/1851_French_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat) on December 2, 1851. A few weeks later, the action was legitimized through a referendum that voted 92% in favor of Bonaparte remaining in office and authorizing him to draft a [new constitution](/wiki/French_Constitution_of_1852). The conduct of this referendum was considered to be rigged. Under the new form of government, Bonaparte was immediately reelected to a new 10 year term with no limits on reelection. About a year later, Bonaparte held another rigged [referendum](/wiki/1852_French_Second_Empire_referendum) which voted 97% in favor of reestablishing the French Empire. Subsequently, Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte crowned himself Emperor Napoleon III, and as such was both head of state and head of government.

The [legislative elections](/wiki/1869_French_legislative_election) of 1869 resulted in an agreement which saw [Émile Ollivier](/wiki/%C3%89mile_Ollivier) lead an independent government. While this was seen by some as a liberalization of the empire, a [referendum](/wiki/1870_French_constitutional_referendum) in 1870 again showed overwhelming support for the Emperor's regime.

## 19th century – 20th century

### Third French Republic (1870–1940)

Main articles: [Government of National Defense](/wiki/Government_of_National_Defense) and [Third French Republic](/wiki/Third_French_Republic)

In 1870, Napoleon III was facing pressure to combat the growing power of [Prussia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) which was conducting a campaign of [unification](/wiki/Unification_of_Germany). Fearing that a new unified German state would disrupt France's place as a world leader, [war](/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War) was declared. The Prussian army proved to be much more effective and during the [Battle of Sedan](/wiki/Battle_of_Sedan) Napoleon III was captured. When the news reached Paris on September 4, a crowd stormed the [Palais Bourbon](/wiki/Palais_Bourbon) and [proclaimed a new republic](/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_French_Republic_(September_4,_1870)). A new Government of National Defense was formed with the intention of continuing the war against the Prussians, but Paris remained [under siege](/wiki/Siege_of_Paris_(1870%E2%80%931871)) for the remainder of the war. An armistice was ultimately agreed to and French forces surrendered in January 1871.

The defeated French organized a provisional government which negotiated the [Treaty of Frankfurt](/wiki/Treaty_of_Frankfurt_(1871)) ending the war. Post war instability and chaos, including the formation and fall of the [Paris Commune](/wiki/Paris_Commune), led to contentious debates about the formation of this new republic. It was even heavily considered that a third [restoration of the monarchy](/wiki/French_Third_Restoration) was in order. However, disagreements continued and the organization of the provisional government became permanent with the enactment of the [Constitutional Laws of 1875](/wiki/French_constitutional_laws_of_1875).

### French State (1940–1944)

Main article: [Vichy France](/wiki/Vichy_France)

Following the success of the [German military](/wiki/Wehrmacht) in the [Battle of France](/wiki/Battle_of_France), the government under Marshal [Philippe Pétain](/wiki/Philippe_P%C3%A9tain) signed the [Second Armistice at Compiègne](/wiki/Armistice_of_22_June_1940)**.** The armistice transferred the northern territory of the nation into a zone [occupied by the German army](/wiki/German_military_administration_in_occupied_France_during_World_War_II), while the southern portion of the country would remain a [Zone libre](/wiki/Zone_libre). Those opposed to the armistice, led by [Charles de Gaulle](/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle), continued the war effort abroad and formed a [government in exile](/wiki/Free_France). On 10 July 1940, the French parliament [voted to give full powers](/wiki/French_Constitutional_Law_of_1940) to Pétain who days later proclaimed the *État Français* (the "French State"), commonly known as "Vichy France" due to its location. Pétain served as Chief of State and nominal President of the Council of Ministers, until 1942. From 1942, Pétain remained Chief of State, but [Pierre Laval](/wiki/Pierre_Laval) was named Chief of the Government.

When the war concluded, de Gaulle explicitly refused to declare a new republic, insisting that the Third Republic had never ceased to exist. The [Ordinance of 9 August 1944](/wiki/Ordinance_of_9_August_1944) declared the Vichy government unconstitutional, and as such any actions taken by them were [null and void](/wiki/Provisional_Government_of_the_French_Republic#Position_on_the_Vichy_regime).

### Provisional Government of the French Republic (1944–1946)

Main article: [Provisional Government of the French Republic](/wiki/Provisional_Government_of_the_French_Republic)

A Provisional Government of the French Republic was set up by the [Free France resistance leadership](/wiki/French_Committee_of_National_Liberation) on June 3, 1944 in the leadup to the [Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II)' [invasion of Normandy](/wiki/Normandy_landings). The primary goals of this government were to oversee the war effort and secure French national sovereignty following the war and prevent [allied military administration](/wiki/United_States_military_government_in_France). Following the war, a [constitutional referendum](/wiki/1945_French_constitutional_referendum) showed overwhelming support for reorganizing the government in a [constituent assembly](/wiki/National_Constituent_Assembly_(France,_1945)).

### Fourth French Republic (1946–1958)

Main article: [Fourth French Republic](/wiki/Fourth_French_Republic)

A [series of](/wiki/May_1946_French_constitutional_referendum) [referendums](/wiki/October_1946_French_constitutional_referendum) in 1946 resulted in the adoption of a new [constitution](/wiki/French_Constitution_of_27_October_1946). The new constitution split executive power between a President of the Republic and a President of the Council of Ministers. This marked the first time the role of the President of the Council of Ministers was explicitly laid out in a constitution, previously the role existed merely as a convention.

## 20th century – 21st century

### Fifth French Republic (since 1958)

Main article: [Fifth French Republic](/wiki/Fifth_French_Republic)

The Fourth Republic collapsed as the result of the [Algiers Crisis of 1958](/wiki/May_1958_crisis_in_France). Charles de Gaulle returned from retirement and in order to prevent a civil war was granted extraordinary powers to restructure the government. The new [constitution](/wiki/Constitution_of_France) strengthened the powers of the executive, especially those of the President which had previously been mostly a ceremonial role.
