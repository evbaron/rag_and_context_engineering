**New Hampshire** ([/ˈhæmpʃər/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [*HAMP-shər*](/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key)) is a [state](/wiki/U.S._state) in the [New England](/wiki/New_England) region of the [Northeastern United States](/wiki/Northeastern_United_States). It borders [Massachusetts](/wiki/Massachusetts) to the south, [Vermont](/wiki/Vermont) to [the west](/wiki/Vermont_v._New_Hampshire), [Maine](/wiki/Maine) and the [Gulf of Maine](/wiki/Gulf_of_Maine) to the east, and the Canadian province of [Quebec](/wiki/Quebec) to the north. Of the [50 U.S. states](/wiki/List_of_states_and_territories_of_the_United_States), New Hampshire is the [seventh-smallest](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_area) by land area and the [tenth-least populous](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_population), with a population of 1,377,529 residents as of the [2020 census](/wiki/2020_United_States_census). [Concord](/wiki/Concord,_New_Hampshire) is the [state capital](/wiki/List_of_capitals_in_the_United_States) and [Manchester](/wiki/Manchester,_New_Hampshire) is the [most populous city](/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_New_Hampshire). New Hampshire's [motto](/wiki/List_of_U.S._state_mottos), "[Live Free or Die](/wiki/Live_Free_or_Die)", reflects its role in the [American Revolutionary War](/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War); its [nickname](/wiki/State_nickname), "The [Granite](/wiki/Granite) State", refers to its extensive granite formations and [quarries](/wiki/Quarries). It is well known for holding [the first primary](/wiki/New_Hampshire_primary) in the [U.S. presidential election cycle](/wiki/United_States_presidential_election), and its resulting influence on American electoral politics.

New Hampshire was inhabited for thousands of years by [Algonquian](/wiki/Algonquian_languages)-speaking peoples such as the [Abenaki](/wiki/Abenaki). Europeans arrived in the 17th century, with the English establishing some of the earliest non-indigenous settlements. The [Province of New Hampshire](/wiki/Province_of_New_Hampshire) was established in 1629, named after the [English](/wiki/England) [county](/wiki/Counties_of_England) of [Hampshire](/wiki/Hampshire). Up to the time of the [French and Indian War](/wiki/French_and_Indian_War), New Hampshire was on the frontier of British territory and saw action against the French and their Indian allies. Following tensions between [the British colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) and the crown in the 1760s, New Hampshire saw one of the earliest acts of rebellion, with the seizing of [Fort William and Mary](/wiki/Fort_William_and_Mary) from the British in 1774. In 1776, it became the first of the British North American [colonies](/wiki/European_colonization_of_the_Americas) to establish an independent government and state constitution. It signed the [United States Declaration of Independence](/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence) and [contributed troops, ships, and supplies](/wiki/History_of_New_Hampshire#Revolution:_1774–1815) in the war against [Britain](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain). In 1788, it was the 9th state to ratify the [U.S. Constitution](/wiki/United_States_Constitution), bringing it into effect. Through the mid-19th century, New Hampshire was an active center of [abolitionism](/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_States), and fielded close to 32,000 [Union soldiers](/wiki/Union_Army) during the [U.S. Civil War](/wiki/American_Civil_War). Afterwards the state saw rapid industrialization and population growth, becoming a center of [textile manufacturing](/wiki/Textile_manufacturing), [shoemaking](/wiki/Shoemaking), and [papermaking](/wiki/Papermaking); the [Amoskeag Manufacturing Company](/wiki/Amoskeag_Manufacturing_Company) in Manchester was the largest cotton textile plant in the world. [French Canadians](/wiki/French_Canadian) formed the most significant influx of immigrants, and a quarter of New Hampshire residents have [French American](/wiki/French_Americans) ancestry.

Reflecting a nationwide trend, New Hampshire's industrial sector declined after World War II. Since 1950, its economy has diversified to include financial services, real estate, education, transportation and high-tech, with manufacturing still higher than the US average. Its population surged as highways connected it to [Greater Boston](/wiki/Greater_Boston) and led to more [commuter towns](/wiki/Bedroom_communities). New Hampshire is among the wealthiest and most-educated states, tying Massachusetts for the highest [Human Development](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_Human_Development_Index) in the nation. It is one of nine states without an income tax and has no taxes on sales, capital gains, or inheritance while relying heavily on local property taxes to fund education; consequently, its state [tax burden](/wiki/Tax_incidence) is among the lowest in the country. New Hampshire is one of the [least religious states](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_religiosity) and known for its [libertarian](/wiki/Libertarianism_in_the_United_States)-leaning political culture; it is one of the least liberal states in [New England](/wiki/New_England). The New Hampshire Republican Party has held a trifecta majority in state level government since 2017, with the exception of 2019 and 2020, while the Democratic Party has held a majority on federal level representation in Congress.

With its mountainous and heavily forested terrain, New Hampshire has a growing tourism sector centered on recreation. It has some of the [highest ski mountains on the East Coast](/wiki/List_of_New_England_ski_areas_by_vertical_drop) and is a major destination for winter sports; [Mount Monadnock](/wiki/Mount_Monadnock) is among the most climbed mountains in the world. Other activities include [observing the fall foliage](/wiki/Leaf_peeping), summer cottages along lakes and the seacoast, motorsports at the [New Hampshire Motor Speedway](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Motor_Speedway) in [Loudon](/wiki/Loudon,_New_Hampshire), and [Motorcycle Week](/wiki/Laconia_Motorcycle_Week), a motorcycle rally held in [Weirs Beach](/wiki/Weirs_Beach,_New_Hampshire) in [Laconia](/wiki/Laconia,_New_Hampshire). The [White Mountain National Forest](/wiki/White_Mountain_National_Forest) includes most of the [Appalachian Trail](/wiki/Appalachian_Trail) between Vermont and Maine, and has the [Mount Washington Auto Road](/wiki/Mount_Washington_Auto_Road), where visitors may drive to the top of 6,288-foot (1,917 m) [Mount Washington](/wiki/Mount_Washington).

## History

Main article: [History of New Hampshire](/wiki/History_of_New_Hampshire)

The historical coat of arms of New Hampshire, from 1876

Various [Algonquian](/wiki/Algonquian_languages)-speaking [Abenaki](/wiki/Abenaki_people) tribes, largely divided between the [Androscoggin](/wiki/Androscoggin_people), [Cowasuck](/wiki/Cowasuck) and [Pennacook](/wiki/Pennacook) nations, inhabited the area before European colonization. Despite the similar language, they had a very different culture and religion from other Algonquian peoples.
Indigenous people lived near [Keene, New Hampshire](/wiki/Keene,_New_Hampshire) 12,000 years ago, according to 2009 archaeological digs, and the Abenaki were present in New Hampshire in pre-colonial times.

English and French explorers visited New Hampshire in 1600–1605, and [David Thompson](/wiki/David_Thompson_(New_Hampshire_settler)) settled at [Odiorne's Point](/wiki/Odiorne_Point_State_Park) in present-day [Rye](/wiki/Rye,_New_Hampshire) in 1623. The first permanent European settlement was at Hilton's Point (present-day [Dover](/wiki/Dover,_New_Hampshire)). By 1631, the Upper Plantation comprised modern-day Dover, [Durham](/wiki/Durham,_New_Hampshire) and [Stratham](/wiki/Stratham,_New_Hampshire); in 1679, it became the "[Royal Province](/wiki/Province_of_New_Hampshire)". Southern New Hampshire was on the front lines during [King William's War](/wiki/King_William%27s_War) during which both [Dover](/wiki/Raid_on_Dover) and [Durham](/wiki/Raid_on_Oyster_River) were burned in 1689 and 1692, respectively. [Father Rale's War](/wiki/Father_Rale%27s_War) was fought between the colonists and the [Wabanaki Confederacy](/wiki/Wabanaki_Confederacy) throughout New Hampshire.

The [Strawbery Banke](/wiki/Strawbery_Banke) Museum in Portsmouth, New Hampshire includes 37 restored buildings dating from the 17th through 19th centuries.

New Hampshire was one of the [Thirteen Colonies](/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) that rebelled against British rule during the [American Revolution](/wiki/American_Revolution). During the American Revolution, New Hampshire was economically divided. The Seacoast region revolved around sawmills, shipyards, merchants' warehouses, and established village and town centers, where wealthy merchants built substantial homes, furnished them with luxuries, and invested their capital in trade and land speculation. At the other end of the social scale, there developed a permanent class of day laborers, mariners, indentured servants and slaves.

Illustration showing the site of the first house in New Hampshire; present day location of the [Wentworth–Coolidge Mansion](/wiki/Wentworth%E2%80%93Coolidge_Mansion).

In December 1774, [Paul Revere](/wiki/Paul_Revere) warned Patriots that [Fort William and Mary](/wiki/Fort_William_and_Mary) would be reinforced with British troops. The following day, [John Sullivan](/wiki/John_Sullivan_(general)) raided the fort for weapons. During the raid, the British soldiers fired at rebels with cannon and muskets, but there were apparently no casualties. These were among the first shots in the American Revolutionary period, occurring approximately five months before the [Battles of Lexington and Concord](/wiki/Battles_of_Lexington_and_Concord). On January 5, 1776, New Hampshire became the first colony to declare independence from Great Britain, almost six months before the Declaration of Independence was signed by the Continental Congress.

[Josiah Bartlett](/wiki/Josiah_Bartlett), signer of the [Declaration of Independence](/wiki/Declaration_of_Independence) and the [Articles of Confederation](/wiki/Articles_of_Confederation), was the first governor of New Hampshire. [His house](/wiki/Josiah_Bartlett_House) in [Kingston, New Hampshire](/wiki/Kingston,_New_Hampshire) still stands.

The [United States Constitution](/wiki/United_States_Constitution) was ratified by New Hampshire on June 21, 1788, when New Hampshire became the ninth state to do so.

New Hampshire was a Jacksonian stronghold; the state sent [Franklin Pierce](/wiki/Franklin_Pierce) to the White House in the election of 1852. Industrialization took the form of numerous textile mills, which in turn attracted large flows of [immigrants](/wiki/Immigrants) from Quebec (the "French Canadians") and [Ireland](/wiki/Ireland). The northern parts of the state produced lumber, and the mountains provided tourist attractions. After 1960, the textile industry collapsed, but the economy rebounded as a center of high technology and as a service provider.

Starting in 1952, New Hampshire gained national and international attention for its [presidential primary](/wiki/New_Hampshire_primary) held early in every presidential election year. It immediately became an important testing ground for candidates for the Republican and Democratic nominations but did not necessarily guarantee victory. The media gave New Hampshire and [Iowa](/wiki/Iowa) significant attention compared to other states in the primary process, magnifying the state's decision powers and spurring repeated efforts by out-of-state politicians to change the rules.

New Hampshire is part of the six-state [New England](/wiki/New_England) region of the [Northeastern United States](/wiki/Northeastern_United_States). It is bounded by Quebec, Canada, to the north and northwest; Maine and the Gulf of Maine to the east; Massachusetts to the south; and Vermont to the west. New Hampshire's major regions are the [Great North Woods](/wiki/Great_North_Woods_Region_(New_Hampshire)), the [White Mountains](/wiki/White_Mountains_Region), the [Lakes Region](/wiki/Lakes_Region_(New_Hampshire)), the [Seacoast](/wiki/Seacoast_Region_(New_Hampshire)), the [Merrimack Valley](/wiki/Merrimack_Valley), the [Monadnock Region](/wiki/Monadnock_Region), and the [Dartmouth-Lake Sunapee](/wiki/Dartmouth-Lake_Sunapee_Region) area. New Hampshire has the shortest ocean coastline of any U.S. coastal state, with a length of 18 miles (29 km), sometimes measured as only 13 miles (21 km).

The [White Mountains range](/wiki/White_Mountains_(New_Hampshire)) in New Hampshire spans the north-central portion of the state. The range includes [Mount Washington](/wiki/Mount_Washington_(New_Hampshire)), the tallest in the northeastern U.S.—site of the second-highest wind speed ever recorded— as well as [Mount Adams](/wiki/Mount_Adams_(New_Hampshire)) and [Mount Jefferson](/wiki/Mount_Jefferson_(New_Hampshire)). With hurricane-force winds every third day on average, more than a hundred recorded deaths among visitors, and conspicuous [krumholtz](/wiki/Krumholtz) (dwarf, matted trees much like a carpet of [bonsai](/wiki/Bonsai) trees), the climate on the upper reaches of Mount Washington has inspired the weather observatory on the peak to claim that the area has the "World's Worst Weather". The White Mountains were home to the rock formation called the [Old Man of the Mountain](/wiki/Old_Man_of_the_Mountain), a face-like profile in [Franconia Notch](/wiki/Franconia_Notch), until the formation disintegrated in May 2003. Even after its loss, the Old Man remains an enduring symbol for the state, seen on state highway signs, automobile license plates, and many government and private entities around New Hampshire.

In southwestern New Hampshire, the landmark [Mount Monadnock](/wiki/Mount_Monadnock) has given its name to a class of earth-forms—a *[monadnock](/wiki/Monadnock)*—signifying, in geomorphology, any isolated resistant peak rising from a less resistant eroded plain.

New Hampshire has more than 800 lakes and ponds, and approximately 19,000 miles (31,000 km) of rivers and streams. Major rivers include the 110-mile (177 km) [Merrimack River](/wiki/Merrimack_River), which bisects the lower half of the state north–south before passing into Massachusetts and reaching the sea in [Newburyport](/wiki/Newburyport,_Massachusetts). Its tributaries include the [Contoocook River](/wiki/Contoocook_River), [Pemigewasset River](/wiki/Pemigewasset_River), and [Winnipesaukee River](/wiki/Winnipesaukee_River). The 410-mile (660 km) [Connecticut River](/wiki/Connecticut_River), which starts at New Hampshire's [Connecticut Lakes](/wiki/Connecticut_Lakes) and flows south to [Connecticut](/wiki/Connecticut), defines the western border with Vermont. The state border is not in the center of that river, as is usually the case, but at the low-water mark on the Vermont side; meaning the entire river along the Vermont border (save for areas where the water level has been raised by a dam) lies within New Hampshire. Only one town—[Pittsburg](/wiki/Pittsburg,_New_Hampshire)—shares a land border with the state of Vermont. The "northwesternmost headwaters" of the Connecticut also define part of the Canada–U.S. border.

The [Piscataqua River](/wiki/Piscataqua_River) and its several [tributaries](/wiki/Tributary) form the state's only significant ocean port where they flow into the Atlantic at [Portsmouth](/wiki/Portsmouth,_New_Hampshire). The [Salmon Falls River](/wiki/Salmon_Falls_River) and the Piscataqua define the southern portion of the border with Maine. The Piscataqua River boundary was the subject of a [border dispute](/wiki/Piscataqua_River_border_dispute) between New Hampshire and Maine in 2001, with New Hampshire claiming dominion over several islands (primarily [Seavey's Island](/wiki/Seavey%27s_Island)) that include the [Portsmouth Naval Shipyard](/wiki/Portsmouth_Naval_Shipyard). The [U.S. Supreme Court](/wiki/United_States_Supreme_Court) dismissed the case in 2002, leaving ownership of the island with Maine. New Hampshire still claims sovereignty of the base, however.

The largest of [New Hampshire's lakes](/wiki/List_of_lakes_in_New_Hampshire) is [Lake Winnipesaukee](/wiki/Lake_Winnipesaukee), which covers 71 square miles (184 km2) in the east-central part of New Hampshire. [Umbagog Lake](/wiki/Umbagog_Lake) along the Maine border, approximately 12.3 square miles (31.9 km2), is a distant second. [Squam Lake](/wiki/Squam_Lake) is the second largest lake entirely in New Hampshire.

New Hampshire has the [shortest ocean coastline](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_coastline) of any state in the United States, approximately 18 miles (29 km) long. [Hampton Beach](/wiki/Hampton_Beach,_New_Hampshire) is a popular local summer destination. About 7 miles (11 km) offshore are the [Isles of Shoals](/wiki/Isles_of_Shoals), nine small islands (four of which are in New Hampshire) known as the site of a 19th-century art colony founded by poet [Celia Thaxter](/wiki/Celia_Thaxter), and the alleged location of one of the buried treasures of the pirate [Blackbeard](/wiki/Blackbeard).

It is the state with the highest percentage of timberland area in the country. New Hampshire is in the [temperate broadleaf and mixed forests](/wiki/Temperate_broadleaf_and_mixed_forests) [biome](/wiki/Biome). Much of the state, in particular the White Mountains, is covered by the [conifers](/wiki/Conifer) and [northern hardwoods](/wiki/Northern_hardwood_forest) of the [New England-Acadian forests](/wiki/New_England-Acadian_forests). The southeast corner of the state and parts of the Connecticut River along the Vermont border are covered by the mixed [oaks](/wiki/Quercus) of the [Northeastern coastal forests](/wiki/Northeastern_coastal_forests). The state's numerous forests are popular among autumnal [leaf peepers](/wiki/Leaf_peeping) seeking the brilliant foliage of the numerous [deciduous trees](/wiki/Deciduous_tree).

The northern third of the state is locally referred to as the "north country" or "north of the notches", in reference to the White Mountain [passes](/wiki/Mountain_pass) that channel traffic. It contains less than 5% of the state's population, suffers relatively high poverty, and is steadily losing population as the logging and paper industries decline. However, the tourist industry, in particular visitors who go to northern New Hampshire to [ski](/wiki/Skiing), [snowboard](/wiki/Snowboarding), [hike](/wiki/Hiking) and [mountain bike](/wiki/Mountain_biking), has helped offset economic losses from mill closures.

Environmental protection emerged as a key state issue in the early 1900s in response to poor logging practices. In the 1970s, activists defeated a proposal to build an oil refinery along the coast and limited plans for a full-width interstate highway through [Franconia Notch](/wiki/Franconia_Notch) to a parkway.

Winter season lengths are projected to decline at ski areas across New Hampshire due to the [effects of climate change](/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change), which is likely to continue the historic contraction and consolidation of the ski industry and threaten individual ski businesses and communities that rely on ski tourism.

[Black bears](/wiki/American_black_bear), [white-tailed deer](/wiki/White-tailed_deer), and [moose](/wiki/Moose) can be found all over New Hampshire. There are also less-common animals such as the [marten](/wiki/Marten) and the [Canadian lynx](/wiki/Canadian_lynx).

### Climate

New Hampshire experiences a [humid continental climate](/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) ([Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *Dfa* in some southern areas, *Dfb* in most of the state, and *Dfc* subarctic in some northern highland areas), with warm, humid summers, and long, cold, and snowy winters. Precipitation is fairly evenly distributed all year. The climate of the southeastern portion is moderated by the [Atlantic Ocean](/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean) and averages relatively milder winters (for New Hampshire), while the northern and interior portions experience colder temperatures and lower humidity. Winters are cold and snowy throughout the state, and especially severe in the northern and mountainous areas. Average annual snowfall ranges from 60 inches (150 cm) to over 100 inches (250 cm) across the state.

Average daytime highs are in the mid 70s°F to low 80s°F (24–28 °C) throughout the state in July, with overnight lows in the mid 50s°F to low 60s°F (13–15 °C). January temperatures range from an average high of 34 °F (1 °C) on the coast to overnight lows below 0 °F (−18 °C) in the far north and at high elevations. Average annual precipitation statewide is roughly 40 inches (100 cm) with some variation occurring in the [White Mountains](/wiki/White_Mountains_(New_Hampshire)) due to differences in elevation and annual snowfall. New Hampshire's highest recorded temperature was 106 °F (41 °C) in [Nashua](/wiki/Nashua,_New_Hampshire) on July 4, 1911, while the lowest recorded temperature was −47 °F (−44 °C) atop [Mount Washington](/wiki/Mount_Washington_(New_Hampshire)) on January 29, 1934. Mount Washington also saw an unofficial −50 °F (−46 °C) reading on January 22, 1885, which, if made official, would tie the record low for New England (also −50 °F (−46 °C) at [Big Black River](/wiki/Big_Black_River_(Saint_John_River)), Maine, on January 16, 2009, and [Bloomfield, Vermont](/wiki/Bloomfield,_Vermont) on December 30, 1933).

Extreme snow is often associated with a [nor'easter](/wiki/Nor%27easter), such as the [Blizzard of '78](/wiki/Northeastern_United_States_blizzard_of_1978) and the [Blizzard of 1993](/wiki/Blizzard_of_1993), when several feet accumulated across portions of the state over 24 to 48 hours. Lighter snowfalls of several inches occur frequently throughout winter, often associated with an [Alberta Clipper](/wiki/Alberta_Clipper).

New Hampshire, on occasion, is affected by [hurricanes](/wiki/Tropical_cyclone) and tropical storms—although, by the time they reach the state, they are often [extratropical](/wiki/Extratropical)—with most storms striking the southern New England coastline and moving inland or passing by offshore in the [Gulf of Maine](/wiki/Gulf_of_Maine). Most of New Hampshire averages fewer than 20 days of thunderstorms per year and an average of two tornadoes occur annually statewide.

The [National Arbor Day Foundation](/wiki/National_Arbor_Day_Foundation) plant [hardiness zone](/wiki/Hardiness_zone) map depicts zones 3, 4, 5, and 6 occurring throughout the state and indicates the transition from a relatively cooler to warmer climate as one travels southward across New Hampshire. The 1990 [USDA](/wiki/USDA) plant [hardiness zones](/wiki/Hardiness_zone) for New Hampshire range from zone 3b in the north to zone 5b in the south.

### Population

Population density by census tract

As of the [2020 census](/wiki/2020_United_States_census), the resident population of New Hampshire was 1,377,529, a 4.6% increase since the [2010 United States census](/wiki/2010_United_States_census). The [center of population](/wiki/Center_of_population) of New Hampshire is in [Merrimack County](/wiki/Merrimack_County,_New_Hampshire), in the town of [Pembroke](/wiki/Pembroke,_New_Hampshire). The center of population has moved south 12 miles (19 km) since 1950, a reflection of the fact that the state's fastest growth has been along its southern border, which is within commuting range of Boston and other Massachusetts cities.

As indicated in the census, in 2020 88.3% of the population were [White](/wiki/White_Americans); 1.5% were [Black or African American](/wiki/African_Americans); 0.2% were [Native American](/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) or Alaskan Native; 2.6% were [Asian](/wiki/Asian_Americans); 0.0% were [Native Hawaiian](/wiki/Native_Hawaiian) or other [Pacific Islander](/wiki/Pacific_Islander); 1.7% were some other race; and 5.6% were [two or more races](/wiki/Multiracial_Americans). 4.3% of the total population were [Hispanic or Latino](/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans) of any race. 18.6% of the population were under 18 years of age; 19.3% were 65 years and over. The female population was 50.5%.

The most densely populated areas generally lie within 50 miles (80 km) of the Massachusetts border, and are concentrated in two areas: along the [Merrimack River Valley](/wiki/Merrimack_River_Valley) running from [Concord](/wiki/Concord,_New_Hampshire) to [Nashua](/wiki/Nashua,_New_Hampshire), and in the [Seacoast Region](/wiki/Seacoast_Region_(New_Hampshire)) along an axis stretching from [Rochester](/wiki/Rochester,_New_Hampshire) to [Portsmouth](/wiki/Portsmouth,_New_Hampshire). Outside of those two regions, only one community, the city of [Keene](/wiki/Keene,_New_Hampshire), has a population of over 20,000. The four counties covering these two areas account for 72% of the state population, and one ([Hillsborough](/wiki/Hillsborough_County,_New_Hampshire)) has nearly 30% of the state population, as well as the two most populous communities, Manchester and Nashua. The northern portion of the state is very sparsely populated: the largest county by area, [Coos](/wiki/Coos_County,_New_Hampshire), covers the northern one-fourth of the state and has only around 31,000 people, about a third of whom live in a single community ([Berlin](/wiki/Berlin,_New_Hampshire)). Over the past several decades, New Hampshire's population has shifted southward as northern communities faced economic decline while southern regions were increasingly integrated into the [Greater Boston](/wiki/Greater_Boston) metropolitan area.

As of the [2010 census](/wiki/2010_United_States_Census), the population of New Hampshire was 1,316,470. The gender makeup of the state at that time was 49.3% male and 50.7% female. 21.8% of the population were under the age of 18; 64.6% were between the ages of 18 and 64; and 13.5% were 65 years of age or older. Additionally, about 57.3% of the population was born out of state.

According to [HUD](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Housing_and_Urban_Development)'s 2022 [Annual Homeless Assessment Report](/wiki/Annual_Homeless_Assessment_Report_to_Congress), there were an estimated 1,605 [homeless](/wiki/Homelessness) people in New Hampshire.

A Pew survey in 2014 showed that the religious affiliations of the people of New Hampshire was as follows: [nonreligious](/wiki/Nonreligious) 36%, [Protestant](/wiki/Protestantism) 30%, [Catholic](/wiki/Catholicism_in_the_United_States) 26%, Jehovah's Witness 2%, [LDS](/wiki/Latter-day_Saint) ([Mormon](/wiki/Mormon)) 1%, and Jewish 1%.

A survey suggests people in New Hampshire and Vermont are less likely than other Americans to attend weekly services and only 54% say they are "absolutely certain there is a God" compared to 71% in the rest of the nation. New Hampshire and Vermont are also at the lowest levels among states in religious commitment. In 2012, 23% of New Hampshire residents in a Gallup poll considered themselves "very religious", while 52% considered themselves "non-religious". According to the [Association of Religion Data Archives](/wiki/Association_of_Religion_Data_Archives) (ARDA) in 2010, the largest denominations were the [Catholic Church](/wiki/Catholic_Church) with 311,028 members; the [United Church of Christ](/wiki/United_Church_of_Christ) with 26,321 members; and the [United Methodist Church](/wiki/United_Methodist_Church) with 18,029 members.

In 2016, a Gallup Poll found that New Hampshire was the least religious state in the United States. Only 20% of respondents in New Hampshire categorized themselves as "very religious", while the nationwide average was 40%.

According to the 2020 [Public Religion Research Institute](/wiki/Public_Religion_Research_Institute) study, 64% of the population was Christian, dominated by Roman Catholicism and [evangelical Protestantism](/wiki/Evangelicalism). In contrast with varying studies of estimated irreligiosity, the Public Religion Research Institute reported that irreligion declined from 36% at the separate 2014 Pew survey to 25% of the population in 2020. In 2021, the unaffiliated increased to 40% of the population, although Christianity altogether made up 54% of the total population (Catholics, Protestants, and Jehovah's Witnesses).

## Economy

Further information: [New Hampshire locations by per capita income](/wiki/New_Hampshire_locations_by_per_capita_income) and [List of power stations in New Hampshire](/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_New_Hampshire)

[Farmers' market](/wiki/Farmers%27_market) of [Mack's Apples](/wiki/Mack%27s_Apples)

- Total employment : 628,166
- Number of employer establishments: 39,298

According to the [Bureau of Economic Analysis](/wiki/Bureau_of_Economic_Analysis), in 2025, New Hampshire's [gross state product](/wiki/Gross_regional_domestic_product) was $125.5 billion and the state's per capita personal income was $86,959. [Median household income](/wiki/Median_household_income) in 2017 was $74,801, the fourth highest in the country (including [Washington, DC](/wiki/Washington,_DC)). Its agricultural outputs are dairy products, nursery stock, cattle, apples and eggs. Its industrial outputs are machinery, electric equipment, rubber and plastic products, and tourism is a major component of the economy.

New Hampshire experienced a major shift in its economic base during the 20th century. Historically, the base was composed of traditional New England textiles, shoemaking, and small machine shops, drawing upon low-wage labor from nearby small farms and parts of Quebec. Today, of the state's total manufacturing dollar value, these sectors contribute only two percent for textiles, two percent for leather goods, and nine percent for machining. They experienced a sharp decline due to obsolete plants and the lure of cheaper wages in the [Southern United States](/wiki/Southern_United_States).

New Hampshire today has a broad-based and growing economy, with a state GDP growth rate of 2.8% in 2024. The state's largest economic sectors in 2018, based on contribution to GDP, are: 15% real estate and rental and leasing; 13% [professional business services](/wiki/Professional_services); 12% manufacturing; 10% government and government services; and 9% health care and social services. Small businesses make up 99.0% of businesses in the state and employ 50.3% of its work force.

The state's [budget](/wiki/Budget) in FY2018 was $5.97 billion, including $1.79 billion in federal funds. The state has a [property tax](/wiki/Property_tax) (subject to municipal control) but no [sales](/wiki/Sales_tax) or [income](/wiki/Income_tax) tax. The state does have narrower taxes on meals, lodging, vehicles, and business income; it has tolls on some expressways. In 2025, the state was ranked third-best on the Tax Competitiveness Index created by the [Tax Foundation](/wiki/Tax_Foundation).

According to the [Energy Information Administration](/wiki/Energy_Information_Administration), New Hampshire's energy consumption and per capita energy consumption are among the lowest in the country. The [Seabrook Station Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Seabrook_Station_Nuclear_Power_Plant) is the largest individual electrical generating unit on the New England power grid and provided 56% of New Hampshire's electricity generation in 2024. [Natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas) accounted for around 25% of the state's electricity generation, while renewables, including [hydroelectric](/wiki/Hydroelectricity) power, [biomass](/wiki/Biomass_(energy)), [wind](/wiki/Wind_power), and [solar](/wiki/Solar_power) supplied around 17%. Coal and petroleum sources account for around 1% of the total electricity generation in New Hampshire. New Hampshire is a net exporter of electricity and in 2024 it exported 10,913,961 [megawatthours](/wiki/Kilowatt-hour) (MWh) of electricity.

New Hampshire's residential electricity use is low compared with the national average, in part because demand for air conditioning is low during the generally mild summer months and because few households use electricity as their primary energy source for home heating. Nearly half of New Hampshire households use [fuel oil](/wiki/Fuel_oil) for winter heating, which is one of the largest shares in the United States.

The (preliminary) seasonally [unemployment rate](/wiki/Unemployment) in November 2025 was 3.0% based on a 774,900 person civilian workforce with 751,300 people in employment.

## Law and government

Main article: [Government of New Hampshire](/wiki/Government_of_New_Hampshire)

The [New Hampshire State House](/wiki/New_Hampshire_State_House) in [Concord](/wiki/Concord,_New_Hampshire)

The governor of New Hampshire, since January 9, 2025, is Republican [Kelly Ayotte](/wiki/Kelly_Ayotte). New Hampshire's two U.S. senators are [Jeanne Shaheen](/wiki/Jeanne_Shaheen) and [Maggie Hassan](/wiki/Maggie_Hassan), both of whom are Democrats and former governors. New Hampshire's two U.S. representatives as of January 2025 are [Chris Pappas](/wiki/Chris_Pappas_(American_politician)) and [Maggie Goodlander](/wiki/Maggie_Goodlander), both Democrats.

New Hampshire is an [alcoholic beverage control state](/wiki/Alcoholic_beverage_control_state), and through the [State Liquor Commission](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Liquor_Commission) takes in $100 million from the sale and distribution of liquor.

New Hampshire is the only state in the U.S. that does not require adults to wear seat belts in their vehicles. It is one of three states that have no mandatory helmet law.

### Governing documents

Main article: [Law of New Hampshire](/wiki/Law_of_New_Hampshire)

The [New Hampshire State Constitution](/wiki/New_Hampshire_State_Constitution) of 1783 is the supreme law of the state, followed by the [New Hampshire Revised Statutes Annotated](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Revised_Statutes_Annotated) and the [New Hampshire Code of Administrative Rules](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Code_of_Administrative_Rules). These are roughly analogous to the federal [United States Constitution](/wiki/United_States_Constitution), [United States Code](/wiki/United_States_Code) and [Code of Federal Regulations](/wiki/Code_of_Federal_Regulations) respectively.

### Branches of government

New Hampshire has a bifurcated executive branch, consisting of the governor and a five-member [executive council](/wiki/Executive_Council_of_New_Hampshire) which votes on state contracts worth more than $10,000 and "advises and consents" to the governor's nominations to major state positions such as department heads and all judgeships and [pardon](/wiki/Pardon) requests. New Hampshire does not have a [lieutenant governor](/wiki/Lieutenant_governor_(United_States)#New_Hampshire); the Senate president serves as "acting governor" whenever the governor is unable to perform the duties.

The legislature is called the [General Court](/wiki/New_Hampshire_General_Court). It consists of the [House of Representatives](/wiki/New_Hampshire_House_of_Representatives) and the [Senate](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Senate). There are 400 representatives, making it one of the largest elected bodies in the English-speaking world, and 24 senators. Legislators are paid a nominal salary of $200 per two-year term plus travel costs, the lowest in the U.S. by far. Thus most are effectively volunteers, nearly half of whom are [retirees](/wiki/Retirement). (For details, see the article on [Government of New Hampshire](/wiki/Government_of_New_Hampshire#Legislative_Branch).)

The state's sole appellate court is the [New Hampshire Supreme Court](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Supreme_Court). The [Superior Court](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Superior_Court) is the court of general jurisdiction and the only court which provides for jury trials in [civil](/wiki/Civil_law_(common_law)) or [criminal](/wiki/Criminal_law) cases. The other state courts are the [Probate Court](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Probate_Court), [District Court](/wiki/New_Hampshire_District_Court), and the [Family Division](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Family_Division).

### Local government

New Hampshire has [10 counties](/wiki/List_of_counties_in_New_Hampshire) and [234 cities and towns](/wiki/List_of_cities_and_towns_in_New_Hampshire).

New Hampshire is a ["Dillon Rule"](/wiki/John_Forrest_Dillon) state, meaning the state retains all powers not specifically granted to municipalities. Even so, the legislature strongly favors local control, particularly concerning land use regulations. New Hampshire municipalities are classified as [towns](/wiki/New_England_town) or cities, which differ primarily by the form of government. Most towns generally operate on the [town meeting](/wiki/Town_meeting) form of government, where the registered voters in the town act as the town legislature, and a [board of selectmen](/wiki/Board_of_selectmen) acts as the executive of the town. Larger towns and the state's thirteen cities operate either on a [council–manager](/wiki/Council%E2%80%93manager_government) or [council–mayor](/wiki/Mayor%E2%80%93council_government) form of government. There is no difference, from the state government's point of view, between towns and cities besides the form of government. All state-level statutes treat all municipalities identically.

New Hampshire has a small number of [unincorporated areas](/wiki/Unincorporated_area) that are titled as grants, locations, purchases, or townships. These locations have limited to no self-government, and services are generally provided for them by neighboring towns or the county or state where needed. As of the 2000 census, there were 25 of these left in New Hampshire, accounting for a total population of 173 people (as of 2000); several were entirely depopulated. All but two of these unincorporated areas are in Coös County.

### Politics

Main article: [Politics of New Hampshire](/wiki/Politics_of_New_Hampshire)

New Hampshire is known for its [fiscal conservatism](/wiki/Fiscal_conservatism) and [cultural liberalism](/wiki/Cultural_liberalism). The state's politics are cited as [libertarian](/wiki/Libertarianism) leaning. It is the [least religious state](/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_religiosity) in the Union as of a 2016 Gallup poll. The state has long had a great disdain for state taxation and state [bureaucracy](/wiki/Bureaucracy).

The [Democratic Party](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)) and the [Republican Party](/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States)), in that order, are the two largest parties in the state. A plurality of voters are registered as undeclared, and can choose either ballot in the primary and then regain their undeclared status after voting. The [Libertarian Party](/wiki/Libertarian_Party_(United_States)) had official party status from 1990 to 1996 and from 2016 to 2018. A movement known as the [Free State Project](/wiki/Free_State_Project) suggests libertarians move to the state to concentrate their power. As of August 30, 2022, there were 869,863 registered voters, of whom 332,008 (38.17%) did not declare a political party affiliation, 273,921 (31.49%) were Democratic, and 263,934 (30.34%) were Republican.

As of 2025, the Republican Party controls the governorship, the Executive Council, and both houses of the [legislature](/wiki/New_Hampshire_General_Court). However, the Democratic Party controls all four seats in the state's delegation to Congress.

#### New Hampshire primary

[Saint Anselm College](/wiki/Saint_Anselm_College) has held several national debates on campus.

The [New Hampshire primary](/wiki/New_Hampshire_primary) is the first [primary](/wiki/U.S._presidential_primary) election in the quadrennial American presidential election cycle. State law requires that the Secretary of State schedule this election at least one week before any "similar event". While the [Iowa caucus](/wiki/Iowa_caucus) precedes the New Hampshire primary, the New Hampshire election is the nation's first contest that uses the same procedure as the general election. It draws more attention than those in other states and shapes the national contest.

In February 2023, the Democratic National Committee awarded that party's first primary to [South Carolina](/wiki/South_Carolina), to be held on February 3, 2024, directing New Hampshire and Nevada to vote three days later. New Hampshire officials from both parties stood by the state's "first in the nation" law and ignored the DNC.

State law permits a town with fewer than 100 residents to open its polls at midnight and close when all registered citizens have cast their ballots. [Dixville Notch](/wiki/Dixville_Notch,_New_Hampshire) in [Coos County](/wiki/Coos_County,_New_Hampshire) and [Hart's Location](/wiki/Hart%27s_Location,_New_Hampshire) in [Carroll County](/wiki/Carroll_County,_New_Hampshire), among others, have implemented these provisions. Dixville Notch and Hart's Location are thus the first precincts in both New Hampshire and the U.S. to report results in presidential primaries and elections.

Nominations for all other partisan offices are decided in a separate [primary election](/wiki/Partisan_primary). In Presidential election cycles, this is the second primary election held in New Hampshire.

[Saint Anselm College](/wiki/Saint_Anselm_College) in [Goffstown](/wiki/Goffstown,_New_Hampshire) has become a popular campaign spot for politicians as well as several national presidential debates.

#### Elections

Main article: [Elections in New Hampshire](/wiki/Elections_in_New_Hampshire)

Further information: [United States presidential elections in New Hampshire](/wiki/United_States_presidential_elections_in_New_Hampshire)

Dartmouth College before a debate in 2008

In the past, New Hampshire has often voted Republican. Between 1856 and 1988, New Hampshire cast its electoral votes for the Democratic presidential ticket six times: [Woodrow Wilson](/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson) (twice), [Franklin D. Roosevelt](/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) (three times), and [Lyndon B. Johnson](/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) (once).

Beginning in 1992, New Hampshire became a [swing state](/wiki/Swing_state) in national and local elections, and in that time has supported Democrats in all presidential elections except 2000. It was the only state in the country to switch from supporting Republican [George W. Bush](/wiki/George_W._Bush) in the [2000 election](/wiki/2000_United_States_presidential_election) to supporting his Democratic challenger in the [2004 election](/wiki/2004_United_States_presidential_election), when [John Kerry](/wiki/John_Kerry), a senator from neighboring Massachusetts, won the state.

The Democrats dominated elections in New Hampshire in 2006 and 2008. In 2006, Democrats won both congressional seats (electing [Carol Shea-Porter](/wiki/Carol_Shea-Porter) in the first district and [Paul Hodes](/wiki/Paul_Hodes) in the second), re-elected Governor [John Lynch](/wiki/John_Lynch_(New_Hampshire_governor)), and gained a majority on the Executive Council and in both houses for the first time since 1911. Democrats had not held both the legislature and the governorship since 1874. Neither U.S. Senate seat was up for a vote in 2006. In 2008, Democrats retained their majorities, governorship, and Congressional seats; and former governor [Jeanne Shaheen](/wiki/Jeanne_Shaheen) defeated incumbent Republican [John E. Sununu](/wiki/John_E._Sununu) for the U.S. Senate in a rematch of the 2002 contest.

The 2008 elections resulted in women holding a majority, 13 of the 24 seats, in the New Hampshire Senate, a first for any legislative body in the United States.

In the 2010 midterm elections, Republicans made historic gains in New Hampshire, capturing veto-proof majorities in the state legislature, taking all five seats in the Executive Council, electing a new U.S. senator, [Kelly Ayotte](/wiki/Kelly_Ayotte), winning both U.S. House seats, and reducing the margin of victory of incumbent Governor [John Lynch](/wiki/John_Lynch_(New_Hampshire_governor)) compared to his 2006 and 2008 landslide wins.

In the 2012 state legislative elections, Democrats took back the [New Hampshire House of Representatives](/wiki/New_Hampshire_House_of_Representatives) and narrowed the Republican majority in the [New Hampshire Senate](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Senate) to 13–11. In 2012, New Hampshire became the first state in U.S. history to elect an all-female federal delegation: Democratic Congresswomen [Carol Shea-Porter](/wiki/Carol_Shea-Porter) of [Congressional District 1](/wiki/New_Hampshire%27s_1st_congressional_district) and [Ann McLane Kuster](/wiki/Ann_McLane_Kuster) of [Congressional District 2](/wiki/New_Hampshire%27s_2nd_congressional_district) accompanied U.S. Senators [Jeanne Shaheen](/wiki/Jeanne_Shaheen) and [Kelly Ayotte](/wiki/Kelly_Ayotte) in 2013. Further, the state elected its second female governor: Democrat [Maggie Hassan](/wiki/Maggie_Hassan).

In the 2014 elections, Republicans retook the [New Hampshire House of Representatives](/wiki/New_Hampshire_House_of_Representatives) with a 239–160 majority and expanded their majority in the [New Hampshire Senate](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Senate) to 14 of the Senate's 24 seats. On the national level, incumbent Democratic Senator [Jeanne Shaheen](/wiki/Jeanne_Shaheen) defeated her Republican challenger, former Massachusetts senator [Scott Brown](/wiki/Scott_Brown_(politician)). New Hampshire also elected [Frank Guinta](/wiki/Frank_Guinta) (R) for its First Congressional District representative and [Ann Kuster](/wiki/Ann_Kuster) (D) for its Second Congressional District representative.

In the 2016 elections, Republicans held the [New Hampshire House of Representatives](/wiki/New_Hampshire_House_of_Representatives) with a majority of 220–175 and held onto their 14 seats in the [New Hampshire Senate](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Senate). In the gubernatorial race, retiring Governor [Maggie Hassan](/wiki/Maggie_Hassan) was succeeded by Republican [Chris Sununu](/wiki/Chris_Sununu), who defeated Democratic nominee [Colin Van Ostern](/wiki/Colin_Van_Ostern). Sununu became the state's first Republican governor since [Craig Benson](/wiki/Craig_Benson), who left office in 2005 following defeat by [John Lynch](/wiki/John_Lynch_(New_Hampshire_governor)). In the presidential race, the state voted for the Democratic nominee, former [Secretary of State](/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_State) [Hillary Clinton](/wiki/Hillary_Clinton) over the Republican nominee, Donald Trump, by a margin of 2,736 votes, or 0.3%, one of the closest results the state has ever seen in a presidential race, while Libertarian nominee [Gary Johnson](/wiki/Gary_Johnson) received 4.12% of the vote. The Democrats also won a competitive race in the Second Congressional District, as well as a competitive senate race.

Since 2017, Democrats have controlled New Hampshire's entire congressional delegation, while Republicans have controlled the governorship and (except in 2019–2020) both houses of the legislature.

#### Free State Project

The [Free State Project](/wiki/Free_State_Project) (FSP) is a movement founded in 2001 to recruit at least 20,000 libertarians to move to a single low-population state (New Hampshire, was selected in 2003), to concentrate [libertarian](/wiki/Libertarianism) activism around a single region. The Free State Project emphasizes decentralized decision-making, encouraging new movers and prior residents of New Hampshire to participate in a way the individual mover deems most appropriate. For example, as of 2017, there were 17 so-called Free Staters elected to the New Hampshire House of Representatives, and in 2021, the [New Hampshire Liberty Alliance](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Liberty_Alliance), which ranks bills and elected representatives based on their adherence to what they see as libertarian principles, scored 150 representatives as "A−" or above rated representatives. Participants also engage with other like-minded activist groups such as Rebuild New Hampshire, [Young Americans for Liberty](/wiki/Young_Americans_for_Liberty), and [Americans for Prosperity](/wiki/Americans_for_Prosperity). As of April 2022, approximately 6,232 participants have moved to New Hampshire for the Free State Project.

## Transportation

### Highways

Main article: [New Hampshire Highway System](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Highway_System)

New Hampshire has a well-maintained, well-signed network of [Interstate highways](/wiki/Interstate_highways), U.S. highways, and state highways. State highway markers still depict the [Old Man of the Mountain](/wiki/Old_Man_of_the_Mountain) despite that rock formation's demise in 2003. Several route numbers align with the same route numbers in neighboring states. State highway numbering is arbitrary, with no overall system, while the U.S. highway and Interstate systems follow national numbering conventions. Major routes include:

- [Interstate 89](/wiki/Interstate_89) runs northwest from near [Concord](/wiki/Concord,_New_Hampshire) to [Lebanon](/wiki/Lebanon,_New_Hampshire) on the [Vermont](/wiki/Vermont) border.
- [Interstate 93](/wiki/Interstate_93) is the main Interstate highway in New Hampshire and runs north from [Salem](/wiki/Salem,_New_Hampshire) (on the Massachusetts border) to [Littleton](/wiki/Littleton,_New_Hampshire) (on the Vermont border). I-93 connects the more densely populated southern part of the state to the Lakes Region and the White Mountains further to the north.
- [Interstate 95](/wiki/Interstate_95) runs north–south briefly along New Hampshire's seacoast to serve the city of [Portsmouth](/wiki/Portsmouth,_New_Hampshire), before entering [Maine](/wiki/Maine)
- [U.S. Route 1](/wiki/U.S._Route_1) runs north–south briefly along New Hampshire's seacoast to the east of and paralleling I-95.
- [U.S. Route 2](/wiki/U.S._Route_2) runs east–west through [Coos County](/wiki/Coos_County,_New_Hampshire) from Maine, intersecting [Route 16](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Route_16), skirting the [White Mountain National Forest](/wiki/White_Mountain_National_Forest) passing through [Jefferson](/wiki/Jefferson,_New_Hampshire) and into Vermont.
- [U.S. Route 3](/wiki/U.S._Route_3) is the longest numbered route in the state, and the only one to run completely through the state from the Massachusetts border to the Canada–U.S. border. It generally parallels [Interstate 93](/wiki/Interstate_93). South of Manchester, it takes a more westerly route through [Nashua](/wiki/Nashua,_New_Hampshire). North of Franconia Notch, U.S. 3 takes a more easterly route, before terminating at the Canada–U.S. border.
- [U.S. Route 4](/wiki/U.S._Route_4) terminates at the [Portsmouth Traffic Circle](/wiki/Portsmouth_Traffic_Circle) and runs east–west across the southern part of the state connecting [Durham](/wiki/Durham,_New_Hampshire), Concord, [Boscawen](/wiki/Boscawen,_New_Hampshire), and Lebanon.
- [New Hampshire Route 16](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Route_16) is a major north–south highway in the eastern part of the state that generally parallels the border with [Maine](/wiki/Maine), eventually entering Maine as Maine Route 16. The southernmost portion of NH 16 is a four-lane freeway, co-signed with U.S. Route 4.
- [New Hampshire Route 101](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Route_101) is a major east–west highway in the southern part of the state that connects [Keene](/wiki/Keene,_New_Hampshire) with [Manchester](/wiki/Manchester,_New_Hampshire) and the Seacoast region. East of Manchester, NH 101 is a four-lane, limited-access highway that runs to [Hampton Beach](/wiki/Hampton_Beach,_New_Hampshire) and I-95.

### Air

[Manchester–Boston Regional Airport](/wiki/Manchester%E2%80%93Boston_Regional_Airport) from the air

For a more comprehensive list, see [List of airports in New Hampshire](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_New_Hampshire).

New Hampshire has 25 public-use airports, three with some scheduled commercial passenger service. The busiest airport by number of passengers handled is [Manchester-Boston Regional Airport](/wiki/Manchester-Boston_Regional_Airport) in Manchester and [Londonderry](/wiki/Londonderry,_New_Hampshire), which serves the [Greater Boston](/wiki/Greater_Boston) metropolitan area. The closest airport with international service is [Logan International Airport](/wiki/Logan_International_Airport) in [Boston](/wiki/Boston).

### Public transportation

Long-distance intercity passenger rail service is provided by [Amtrak](/wiki/Amtrak)'s *[Vermonter](/wiki/Vermonter_(train))* and *[Downeaster](/wiki/Downeaster_(train))* lines.

[Greyhound](/wiki/Greyhound_Lines), [Concord Coach](/wiki/Concord_Coach_Lines), [Vermont Translines](/wiki/Vermont_Translines), and Dartmouth Coach all provide intercity bus connections to and from points in New Hampshire and to long-distance points beyond and in between.

As of 2013, Boston-centered [MBTA Commuter Rail](/wiki/MBTA_Commuter_Rail) services reach only as far as northern Massachusetts. The [New Hampshire Rail Transit Authority](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Rail_Transit_Authority) is working to extend "Capital Corridor" service from [Lowell, Massachusetts](/wiki/Lowell,_Massachusetts), to Nashua, Concord, and Manchester, including [Manchester-Boston Regional Airport](/wiki/Manchester-Boston_Regional_Airport); and "Coastal Corridor" service from [Haverhill, Massachusetts](/wiki/Haverhill,_Massachusetts), to [Plaistow, New Hampshire](/wiki/Plaistow,_New_Hampshire). Legislation in 2007 created the [New Hampshire Rail Transit Authority](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Rail_Transit_Authority) (NHRTA) with the goal of overseeing the development of commuter rail in the state of New Hampshire. In 2011, Governor John Lynch vetoed HB 218, a bill passed by Republican lawmakers, which would have drastically curtailed the powers and responsibilities of NHRTA. The I-93 Corridor transit study suggested a rail alternative along the [Manchester and Lawrence branch line](/wiki/Manchester_and_Lawrence_Railroad) which could provide freight and passenger service. This rail corridor would also have access to Manchester-Boston Regional Airport.

Eleven public transit authorities operate local and regional bus services around the state, and eight private carriers operate express bus services which link with the national intercity bus network. The [New Hampshire Department of Transportation](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Department_of_Transportation) operates a statewide ride-sharing match service, in addition to independent ride matching and guaranteed ride home programs.

[Tourist railroads](/wiki/Tourist_railroad) include the [Conway Scenic Railroad](/wiki/Conway_Scenic_Railroad), [Hobo-Winnipesaukee Railroad](/wiki/Hobo-Winnipesaukee_Railroad), and the [Mount Washington Cog Railway](/wiki/Mount_Washington_Cog_Railway).

Freight railways in New Hampshire include [Claremont & Concord Railroad](/wiki/Concord_and_Claremont_Railroad) (CCRR), [Pan Am Railways](/wiki/Pan_Am_Railways) via subsidiary Springfield Terminal Railway (ST), the [New England Central Railroad](/wiki/New_England_Central_Railroad) (NHCR), the [St. Lawrence and Atlantic Railroad](/wiki/St._Lawrence_and_Atlantic_Railroad) (SLR), and [New Hampshire Northcoast Corporation](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Northcoast_Corporation) (NHN).

## Education

[Dartmouth College](/wiki/Dartmouth_College)'s [Baker Library](/wiki/Baker_Memorial_Library)

Thompson Hall, at [UNH](/wiki/University_of_New_Hampshire), was built in 1892.

New Hampshire’s education system includes public school districts, private schools, and a variety of school choice initiatives. One significant component of the latter is the [New Hampshire Education Freedom Accounts](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Education_Freedom_Accounts) program, a statewide initiative that allows eligible families to direct a portion of state education funding toward approved educational expenses outside of traditional public schools.

The average cost per pupil for public K-12 education in New Hampshire exceeded $21,000, with most funding coming from local property taxes. In 2025, the state expanded Education Freedom Accounts into a universal school choice program, making most students eligible to participate.

### High schools

See also: [List of high schools in New Hampshire](/wiki/List_of_high_schools_in_New_Hampshire) and [List of school districts in New Hampshire](/wiki/List_of_school_districts_in_New_Hampshire)

The first public high schools in the state were the Boys' High School and the Girls' High School of [Portsmouth](/wiki/Portsmouth,_New_Hampshire), established either in 1827 or 1830, depending on the source.

New Hampshire has more than 80 public high schools, many of which serve more than one town. The largest is [Pinkerton Academy](/wiki/Pinkerton_Academy) in [Derry](/wiki/Derry,_New_Hampshire), which is owned by a private non-profit organization and serves as the public high school of several neighboring towns. There are at least 30 private high schools in the state.

New Hampshire is also the home of several prestigious [university-preparatory](/wiki/University-preparatory_school) schools, such as [Phillips Exeter Academy](/wiki/Phillips_Exeter_Academy), [St. Paul's School](/wiki/St._Paul%27s_School_(New_Hampshire)), [Proctor Academy](/wiki/Proctor_Academy), [Brewster Academy](/wiki/Brewster_Academy), and [Kimball Union Academy](/wiki/Kimball_Union_Academy).

Traditionally, school funding has been decided by municipalities, often at [town meeting](/wiki/Town_meeting). In 1993, the [*Claremont* litigation](/wiki/Claremont_School_District_v._Governor_of_New_Hampshire) began when that town's school district secured a verdict that Part 2, Article 83 of the state constitution required the state to fund education adequately and uniformly. This was never achieved despite additional rounds of litigation. In 2002, New Hampshire enacted a [statewide property tax](/wiki/Government_of_New_Hampshire#Funding) to be forwarded to school districts to address the problem. In 2008, the Court ended its supervisory role because the original laws had been replaced, but it did not reverse its earlier finding. In 2023, a district judge set a new, higher amount for educational "adequacy". In June 2025, the legislature enacted language asserting its right to determine funding. The New Hampshire Supreme Court upheld the 2023 verdict but declined to force the legislature to comply.

The sport of [paintball](/wiki/Paintball) was invented in [Henniker](/wiki/Henniker,_New_Hampshire) in 1981. [Sutton](/wiki/Sutton,_New_Hampshire) was the home of the world's first commercial paintball facility.

The [New Hampshire Motor Speedway](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Motor_Speedway) in [Loudon](/wiki/Loudon,_New_Hampshire) is an oval track and road course that has been visited by national motorsport championship series such as the [NASCAR Cup Series](/wiki/NASCAR_Cup_Series), the NASCAR [Xfinity Series](/wiki/Xfinity_Series), the NASCAR [Craftsman Truck Series](/wiki/Craftsman_Truck_Series), NASCAR [Whelen Modified Tour](/wiki/Whelen_Modified_Tour), [American Canadian Tour](/wiki/American_Canadian_Tour) (ACT), the [Champ Car](/wiki/Champ_Car) and the [IndyCar Series](/wiki/IndyCar_Series). Other motor racing venues include [Star Speedway](/wiki/Star_Speedway) and [New England Dragway](/wiki/New_England_Dragway) in [Epping](/wiki/Epping,_New_Hampshire), [Lee USA Speedway](/wiki/Lee_USA_Speedway) in [Lee](/wiki/Lee,_New_Hampshire), Twin State Speedway in [Claremont](/wiki/Claremont,_New_Hampshire), [Monadnock Speedway](/wiki/Monadnock_Speedway) in [Winchester](/wiki/Winchester,_New_Hampshire) and [Canaan Fair Speedway](/wiki/Canaan_Motor_Club) in [Canaan](/wiki/Canaan,_New_Hampshire).

New Hampshire has two universities competing at the [NCAA Division I](/wiki/NCAA_Division_I) in all collegiate sports: the [Dartmouth Big Green](/wiki/Dartmouth_Big_Green) ([Ivy League](/wiki/Ivy_League)) and the [New Hampshire Wildcats](/wiki/New_Hampshire_Wildcats) ([America East Conference](/wiki/America_East_Conference)), as well as three [NCAA Division II](/wiki/NCAA_Division_II) teams: Franklin Pierce Ravens, Saint Anselm Hawks, and Southern New Hampshire Penmen ([Northeast-10 Conference](/wiki/Northeast-10_Conference)). Most other schools compete in [NCAA Division III](/wiki/NCAA_Division_III) or the [NAIA](/wiki/National_Association_of_Intercollegiate_Athletics).

Annually since 2002, high-school statewide all-stars compete against Vermont in 10 sports during "Twin State" playoffs.

## Culture

Detail of a reproduction of *[Diana](/wiki/Diana_(Saint-Gaudens))* at the [Saint-Gaudens National Historical Park](/wiki/Saint-Gaudens_National_Historical_Park). New Hampshire's sole [national park](/wiki/National_Park_Service) is one of only two dedicated to a [visual artist](/wiki/Visual_arts).

In the spring, New Hampshire's many [sap houses](/wiki/Sugar_house) hold sugaring-off open houses. In summer and early autumn, New Hampshire is home to many [county fairs](/wiki/Fair), the largest being the [Hopkinton State Fair](/wiki/Hopkinton_State_Fair), in [Contoocook](/wiki/Contoocook,_New_Hampshire). New Hampshire's [Lakes Region](/wiki/Lakes_Region_(New_Hampshire)) is home to many summer camps, especially around [Lake Winnipesaukee](/wiki/Lake_Winnipesaukee), and is a popular tourist destination. The Peterborough Players have performed every summer in [Peterborough](/wiki/Peterborough,_New_Hampshire) since 1933. [The Barnstormers Theatre](/wiki/The_Barnstormers_Theatre) in [Tamworth](/wiki/Tamworth,_New_Hampshire), founded in 1931, is one of the longest-running professional summer theaters in the United States. The [Saint-Gaudens National Historical Park](/wiki/Saint-Gaudens_National_Historical_Park) is the sole [National Park Unit](/wiki/National_Park_Service) in the state, and a section of the [Appalachian Trail](/wiki/Appalachian_Trail) passes through the north.

In September, New Hampshire is host to the *New Hampshire Highland Games*. New Hampshire has also registered an official [tartan](/wiki/Tartan) with the proper authorities in [Scotland](/wiki/Scotland), used to make kilts worn by the [Lincoln](/wiki/Lincoln,_New_Hampshire) Police Department while its officers serve during the games. The [fall foliage](/wiki/Autumn_leaf_color) peaks in mid-October. In the winter, [New Hampshire's ski areas](/wiki/List_of_ski_areas_and_resorts_in_the_United_States#New_Hampshire) and [snowmobile](/wiki/Snowmobile) trails attract visitors from a wide area. After the lakes freeze over they become dotted with [ice fishing](/wiki/Ice_fishing) ice houses, known locally as bobhouses.

[Funspot](/wiki/Funspot_Family_Fun_Center), the world's largest video arcade (now termed a museum), is in [Laconia](/wiki/Laconia,_New_Hampshire).

### In fiction

Many [novels](/wiki/Category:Novels_set_in_Hampshire), [plays](/wiki/Category:Plays_set_in_New_Hampshire) and [screenplays](/wiki/Category:Films_set_in_New_Hampshire) have been set in New Hampshire.

#### Theater

- The fictional New Hampshire town of Grover's Corners serves as the setting of the [Thornton Wilder](/wiki/Thornton_Wilder) play *[Our Town](/wiki/Our_Town)*. Grover's Corners is based, in part, on the real town of [Peterborough](/wiki/Peterborough,_New_Hampshire). Several local landmarks and nearby towns are mentioned in the text of the play, and Wilder himself spent some time in Peterborough at the [MacDowell Colony](/wiki/MacDowell_Colony), writing at least some of the play while in residence there.

#### Comics

- Al Capp, creator of the comic strip *[Li'l Abner](/wiki/Li%27l_Abner)*, used to joke that [Dogpatch](/wiki/Dogpatch), the setting for the strip, was based on [Seabrook](/wiki/Seabrook,_New_Hampshire), where he would vacation with his wife.

#### Television

Many [television programs](/wiki/Category:Television_shows_set_in_New_Hampshire) have been set in New Hampshire. Notable recent examples include:

- In the AMC drama *[Breaking Bad](/wiki/Breaking_Bad)* ("[Granite State](/wiki/Granite_State_(Breaking_Bad))") series lead [Walter White](/wiki/Walter_White_(Breaking_Bad)) escapes to a cabin in a fictional county in northern New Hampshire.
- An episode of the NBC drama *[The West Wing](/wiki/The_West_Wing)* takes place in the fictional [Hartsfield's Landing](/wiki/Hartsfield%27s_Landing), New Hampshire.
- In the sixth season of [HBO](/wiki/HBO) series *[The Sopranos](/wiki/The_Sopranos)*, in an episode named for New Hampshire's slogan "[Live Free or Die](/wiki/Live_Free_or_Die)", a character flees to the fictional town of Dartford, New Hampshire, because of inadvertently being [outed](/wiki/Outing) as a gay man.
