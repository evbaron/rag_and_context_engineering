# Macaque

"Macaca" redirects here. For a racial slur for dark-skinned people, see [Macaca (term)](/wiki/Macaca_(term)).

The **macaques** ([/məˈkɑːk, -ˈkæk/](/wiki/Help:IPA/English)) constitute a [genus](/wiki/Genus) (***Macaca***) of [gregarious](/wiki/Sociality) [Old World monkeys](/wiki/Old_World_monkey) of the subfamily [Cercopithecinae](/wiki/Cercopithecinae). The 23 species of macaques inhabit [ranges](/wiki/Species_distribution) throughout Asia, [North Africa](/wiki/North_Africa), and Europe (in [Gibraltar](/wiki/Barbary_macaques_in_Gibraltar)). Macaques are principally [frugivorous](/wiki/Frugivore) (preferring fruit), although their diet also includes seeds, leaves, flowers, and tree bark. Some species such as the long-tailed macaque (*M. fascicularis*; also called the [crab-eating macaque](/wiki/Crab-eating_macaque)) will supplement their diets with small amounts of meat from shellfish, insects, and small mammals. On average, a [southern pig-tailed macaque](/wiki/Southern_pig-tailed_macaque) (*M. nemestrina*) in [Malaysia](/wiki/Malaysia) eats about 70 large rats each year. All macaque [social groups](/wiki/Social_group) are arranged around dominant [matriarchs](/wiki/Matriarchy).

Macaques are found in a variety of habitats throughout the Asian continent and are highly [adaptable](/wiki/Adaptation). Certain species are [synanthropic](/wiki/Synanthrope), having learned to live alongside humans, but they have become problematic in urban areas in [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia) and are not suitable to live with, as they can carry transmittable diseases.

Most macaque species are listed as vulnerable to critically endangered on the [International Union of the Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List](/wiki/IUCN_Red_List_endangered_species_(Animalia)).

## Description

Aside from [humans](/wiki/Human) (genus *[Homo](/wiki/Homo_(genus))*), the macaques are the most widespread [primate](/wiki/Primate) genus, ranging from [Japan](/wiki/Japan) to the [Indian subcontinent](/wiki/Indian_subcontinent), and in the case of the [Barbary macaque](/wiki/Barbary_macaque) (*Macaca sylvanus*), to [North Africa](/wiki/Maghreb) and [Southern Europe](/wiki/Southern_Europe). Twenty-three macaque species are currently recognized. Macaques are robust primates whose arms and legs are about the same in length. The fur of these animals is typically varying shades of brown or black and their muzzles are rounded in profile with nostrils on the upper surface. The tail varies among each species, which can be long, moderate, short or totally absent. Although several species lack tails, and their common names refer to them as [apes](/wiki/Ape), these are true monkeys, with no greater relationship to the true apes than any other [Old World monkeys](/wiki/Old_World_monkeys). Instead, this comes from an earlier definition of 'ape' that included primates generally.

In some species, skin folds join the second through fifth toes, almost reaching the first [metatarsal](/wiki/Metatarsus) joint. The monkey's size differs depending on sex and species. Males from all species can range from 41 to 70 cm (16 to 27.5 in) in head and body length, and in weight from 5.5 to 18 kg (12 to 40 lb). Females can range from a weight of 2.4 to 13 kg (5.3 to 28.7 lb). These primates live in troops that vary in size, where males dominate, however the order of dominance frequently shifts. Female dominance lasts longer and depends upon their genealogical position. Macaques are able to swim and spend most of their time on the ground and spend some time in trees. They have large pouches in their cheeks where they carry extra food. They are considered highly intelligent and are often used in the medical field for experimentation due to their remarkable similarity to humans in emotional and cognitive development. Extensive experimentation has led to the long-tailed macaque being listed as endangered.

## Distribution and habitat

Macaques are highly [adaptable](/wiki/Adaptability) to different habitats and climates and can tolerate a wide fluctuation of temperatures and live in varying landscape settings. They easily adapt to human-[built environments](/wiki/Built_environment) and can survive well in [urban settings](/wiki/Urban_wildlife) if they are able to obtain food. They can also survive in completely [natural settings](/wiki/Natural_environment) absent of humans.

The ecological and geographic ranges of the macaque are the widest of any non-human primate. Their habitats include the tropical rainforests of [Southeast Asia](/wiki/Southeast_Asia), [Sri Lanka](/wiki/Sri_Lanka), [India](/wiki/India), arid mountains of [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan) and [Afghanistan](/wiki/Afghanistan), and temperate mountains in [Algeria](/wiki/Algeria), [Japan](/wiki/Japan), [China](/wiki/China), [Morocco](/wiki/Morocco), and [Nepal](/wiki/Nepal). Some species also inhabit villages and towns in cities in Asia. There is also an introduced population of [rhesus macaques](/wiki/Rhesus_macaque) in the [US](/wiki/United_States) state of [Florida](/wiki/Florida) consisting, essentially, of monkeys abandoned when a failed boat ride-safari was shut down in the mid-20th century.

A probable Early Pliocene macaque [molar](/wiki/Molar_(tooth)) from the [Red Crag Formation](/wiki/Red_Crag_Formation) ([Waldringfield](/wiki/Waldringfield), United Kingdom), represents one of the oldest and northernmost records of the genus in Europe reported to date.

## Ecology and behavior

### Diet

Macaques are mainly [frugivorous](/wiki/Frugivore), although some species have been observed feeding on insects. In natural habitats, they have been observed to consume certain parts of over one hundred species of plants including the buds, fruit, young leaves, bark, roots, and flowers. When macaques [live amongst people](/wiki/Synanthrope), they raid agricultural [crops](/wiki/Crop) such as wheat, rice, or [sugarcane](/wiki/Sugarcane); and garden crops like tomatoes, bananas, melons, mangos, or [papayas](/wiki/Papaya). In human settings, they also [rely heavily](/wiki/Begging_in_animals) on direct handouts from people. This includes peanuts, rice, [legumes](/wiki/Legume), or even prepared food.

### Group structure

Macaques live in established social groups that can range from a few individuals to several hundred, as they are social animals. A typical social group possess between 20 and 50 individuals of all ages and of both sexes. The typical composition consists of 15% adult males, 35% adult females, 20% infants, and 30% juveniles, though there exists variation in structure and size of groups across populations.

The [premotor cortex](/wiki/Premotor_cortex) of macaques is widely studied.

Macaques have a very intricate social structure and hierarchy, with different classifications of despotism depending on species. If a macaque of a lower level in the social chain has eaten berries and none are left for a higher-ranking macaque, then the one higher in status can, within this social organization, remove the berries from the other monkey's mouth.

### Reproduction and mortality

The reproductive potential of each species differs. Populations of the rhesus macaque can grow at rates of 10% to 15% per year if the environmental conditions are favorable. However, some forest-dwelling species are [endangered](/wiki/Endangered_species) with much lower reproductive rates. After one year of age, macaques move from being dependent on their mother during infancy, to the juvenile stage, where they begin to associate more with other juveniles through rough tumble and playing activities. They sexually mature between three and five years of age. Females will usually stay with the social group in which they were born; however, young adult males tend to disperse and attempt to enter other social groups. Not all males succeed in joining other groups and may become solitary, attempting to join other social groups for many years. Macaques have a typical lifespan of 20 to 30 years.

### As invasive species

*M. fascicularis* on a [scooter](/wiki/Scooter_(motorcycle)) at [Ko Chang](/wiki/Ko_Chang), Thailand

Certain species under the genus *Macaca* have been transplanted to different parts of the world, where they become [invasive](/wiki/Invasive_species), while others remain [threatened](/wiki/Threatened_species). The [long-tailed macaque](/wiki/Crab-eating_macaque) (*M. fascicularis*) is listed as an invasive alien species in [Mauritius](/wiki/Mauritius), along with the [rhesus macaques](/wiki/Rhesus_macaque) (*M. mulatta*) in Florida. Despite this, the former is listed as [endangered](/wiki/Endangered_species).

The long-tailed macaque causes severe damage to parts of its range where it has been [introduced](/wiki/Introduced_species) because the populations grow unchecked due to a lack of predators. On the island of Mauritius, they have created serious conservation concerns for other [endemic species](/wiki/Endemism). They consume seeds of native plants and aid in the spread of exotic weeds throughout the forests. This changes the composition of the habitats and allows them to be rapidly overrun by invasive plants.

Long-tailed macaques are also responsible for the near extinction of several bird species on Mauritius by destroying the nests of the birds and eating the eggs of [critically endangered](/wiki/Critically_endangered) species, such as the [pink pigeon](/wiki/Pink_pigeon) and Mauritian green parrot. They have been known to eat crops from human farms and gardens, sparking [human-wildlife conflicts](/wiki/Human%E2%80%93wildlife_conflict).

In Florida, a group of rhesus macaques inhabit [Silver Springs State Park](/wiki/Silver_Springs_State_Park). Humans often feed them, which may alter their movement and keep them close to the river on weekends where high human traffic is present. The monkeys can become aggressive toward humans, largely due to human ignorance of macaque behavior. They carry potentially fatal human diseases, including the [herpes B virus](/wiki/Herpes_B_virus).

## Relations with humans

Several species of macaque are used extensively in [animal testing](/wiki/Animal_testing), particularly in the [neuroscience](/wiki/Neuroscience) of [visual perception](/wiki/Visual_perception) and the [visual system](/wiki/Visual_system).

Nearly all (73–100%) captive rhesus macaques are [carriers](/wiki/Asymptomatic_carrier) of the [herpes B virus](/wiki/Herpes_B_virus). This [virus](/wiki/Virus) is harmless to macaques, but infections of humans, while rare, are potentially fatal, a risk that makes macaques [unsuitable as pets](/wiki/Pet_monkey).

Urban performing macaques also carried [simian foamy virus](/wiki/Simian_foamy_virus), suggesting they could be involved in the [species-to-species jump](/wiki/Zoonosis) of similar retroviruses to humans.

### Population control

Management techniques have historically been controversial, and public disapproval can hinder control efforts. Previously, efforts to remove macaque individuals were met with public resistance. One management strategy that is currently being explored is that of [sterilization](/wiki/Neutering). Natural resource managers are being educated by scientific studies in the proposed strategy. Effectiveness of this strategy is estimated to succeed in keeping populations in check. For example, if 80% of females are sterilized every five years, or 50% every two years, it could effectively reduce the population. Other control strategies include planting specific trees to provide protection to native birds from macaque predation, live trapping, and the vaccine [porcine zona pellucida](/wiki/Porcine_zona_pellucida) (PZP), which causes infertility in females.

### Cloning

See also: [List of animals that have been cloned](/wiki/List_of_animals_that_have_been_cloned)

In January 2018, scientists in China reported in the journal *[Cell](/wiki/Cell_(journal))* the first creation of two [crab-eating macaque](/wiki/Crab-eating_macaque) [clones](/wiki/Cloning), named [Zhong Zhong and Hua Hua](/wiki/Zhong_Zhong_and_Hua_Hua), using [somatic cell nuclear transfer](/wiki/Somatic_cell_nuclear_transfer) – the same method that produced [Dolly the sheep](/wiki/Dolly_(sheep)).
