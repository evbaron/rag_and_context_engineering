# Insect

For other uses, see [Insect (disambiguation)](/wiki/Insect_(disambiguation)).

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dc/Insect_world_of_motion.webm/250px--Insect_world_of_motion.webm.jpg)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/dc/Insect_world_of_motion.webm)

Insects live in a world of motion. This [leaf-footed bug](/wiki/Acanthocephala_terminalis) climbs windblown grass and flies off.

**Insects** (from [Latin](/wiki/Latin) **insectum**) are [hexapod](/wiki/Hexapoda) [invertebrates](/wiki/Invertebrate) of the [class](/wiki/Class_(biology)) **Insecta**. They are the largest group within the [arthropod](/wiki/Arthropod) [phylum](/wiki/Phylum). Insects have a [chitinous](/wiki/Chitin) [exoskeleton](/wiki/Exoskeleton), a three-part body ([head](/wiki/Insect_morphology#Head), [thorax](/wiki/Thorax_(insect_anatomy)) and [abdomen](/wiki/Abdomen_(insect_anatomy))), three pairs of jointed [legs](/wiki/Arthropod_leg), [compound eyes](/wiki/Compound_eye), and a pair of [antennae](/wiki/Antenna_(biology)). Insects are the most diverse group of animals, with more than a million described [species](/wiki/Species); they represent more than half of all animal species.

The insect [nervous system](/wiki/Nervous_system) consists of a [brain](/wiki/Insect_brain) and a [ventral nerve cord](/wiki/Ventral_nerve_cord). Most insects reproduce [by laying eggs](/wiki/Oviparous). Insects [breathe air](/wiki/Respiratory_system_of_insects) through a system of [paired openings](/wiki/Spiracle_(arthropods)) along their sides, connected to [small tubes](/wiki/Trachea#Invertebrates) that take air directly to the tissues. The blood therefore does not carry oxygen; it is only partly contained in vessels, and some circulates in an open [hemocoel](/wiki/Hemocoel). Insect vision is mainly through their [compound eyes](/wiki/Compound_eye), with additional small [ocelli](/wiki/Ocelli). Many insects can hear, using [tympanal organs](/wiki/Tympanal_organ), which may be on the legs or other parts of the body. Their [sense of smell](/wiki/Insect_olfaction) is via receptors, usually on the antennae and the mouthparts.

Nearly all insects hatch from [eggs](/wiki/Egg). Insect growth is constrained by the inelastic exoskeleton, so development involves a series of [molts](/wiki/Moulting). The immature stages often differ from the adults in structure, habit, and habitat. Groups that undergo [four-stage metamorphosis](/wiki/Holometabolism) often have a nearly immobile [pupa](/wiki/Pupa). Insects that undergo [three-stage metamorphosis](/wiki/Hemimetabolism) lack a pupa, developing through a series of increasingly adult-like [nymphal](/wiki/Nymph_(biology)) stages. The higher level relationship of the [insects](/wiki/Hexapoda) is unclear. Fossilized insects of enormous size have been found from the [Paleozoic](/wiki/Paleozoic) Era, including [giant dragonfly-like insects](/wiki/Meganisoptera) with wingspans of 55 to 70 cm (22 to 28 in). The most diverse insect groups appear to have [coevolved](/wiki/Coevolution) with [flowering plants](/wiki/Flowering_plant).

Adult insects typically move about by walking and flying; some can swim. Insects are the only invertebrates that can achieve sustained powered flight; [insect flight](/wiki/Insect_flight) evolved just once. Many insects are at least partly [aquatic](/wiki/Aquatic_insect), and have [larvae](/wiki/Larva) with gills; in some species, the adults too are aquatic. Some species, such as [water striders](/wiki/Gerridae), can walk on the surface of water. Insects are mostly solitary, but some, such as [bees](/wiki/Bee), [ants](/wiki/Ant) and [termites](/wiki/Termite), are [social](/wiki/Eusociality) and live in large, well-organized [colonies](/wiki/Colony_(biology)). Others, such as [earwigs](/wiki/Earwig), provide maternal care, guarding their eggs and young. Insects can communicate with each other in a variety of ways. Male [moths](/wiki/Moth) can sense the [pheromones](/wiki/Pheromone) of female moths over great distances. Other species communicate with sounds: [crickets](/wiki/Cricket_(insect)) [stridulate](/wiki/Stridulation), or rub their wings together, to attract a mate and repel other males. [Lampyrid](/wiki/Firefly) [beetles](/wiki/Beetle) communicate with light.

Humans regard many insects as [pests](/wiki/Pest_(organism)), especially those that damage crops, and attempt to control them using [insecticides](/wiki/Insecticide) and other techniques. Others are [parasitic](/wiki/Parasitism), and may act as [vectors](/wiki/Vector_(epidemiology)) of [diseases](/wiki/List_of_diseases_caused_by_insects). Insect [pollinators](/wiki/Pollination) are essential to the reproduction of many flowering plants and so to their ecosystems. Many insects are ecologically beneficial as predators of pest insects, while a few provide direct economic benefit. Two species in particular are economically important and were domesticated many centuries ago: [silkworms](/wiki/Silkworm) for [silk](/wiki/Silk) and [honey bees](/wiki/Honey_bee) for [honey](/wiki/Honey). Insects are intentionally consumed as food in 80% of the world's nations, by people in roughly 3,000 ethnic groups. Human activities are having serious effects on [insect biodiversity](/wiki/Insect_biodiversity).

## Etymology

The word *insect* comes from the [Latin](/wiki/Latin_language) word *insectum* from *in* + *sĕco*, "cut up", as insects appear to be cut into three parts. The Latin word was introduced by [Pliny the Elder](/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder) who [calqued](/wiki/Calque) the [Ancient Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greek) word ἔντομον *éntomon* "insect" (as in [entomology](/wiki/Entomology)) from [ἔντομος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BC%94%CE%BD%CF%84%CE%BF%CE%BC%CE%BF%CF%82) *éntomos* "cut in pieces"; this was [Aristotle](/wiki/Aristotle)'s term for this [class of life in his biology](/wiki/Aristotle%27s_biology#Classification), also in reference to their notched bodies. The English word *insect* first appears in 1601 in [Philemon Holland](/wiki/Philemon_Holland)'s translation of Pliny.

## Insects and other "bugs"

### Distinguishing features

In common speech, insects and other terrestrial [arthropods](/wiki/Arthropod) are often called **bugs**  or [creepy crawlies](/wiki/Minibeast). Entomologists to some extent reserve the name "bugs" for a narrow category of "[true bugs](/wiki/True_bugs)", insects of the order [Hemiptera](/wiki/Hemiptera), such as [cicadas](/wiki/Cicada) and [shield bugs](/wiki/Shield_bug). Other terrestrial arthropods, such as [centipedes](/wiki/Centipede), [millipedes](/wiki/Millipede), [woodlice](/wiki/Woodlouse), [spiders](/wiki/Spider), [mites](/wiki/Mite) and [scorpions](/wiki/Scorpion), are sometimes confused with insects, since they have a jointed exoskeleton. Adult insects are the only arthropods that ever have wings, with up to two pairs on the thorax. Whether winged or not, adult insects can be distinguished by their three-part body plan, with head, thorax, and abdomen; they have three pairs of legs on the thorax.

### Diversity

Main article: [Insect biodiversity](/wiki/Insect_biodiversity)

About half of all [eukaryotes](/wiki/Eukaryote) are insects (left side of diagram).

Estimates of the total number of insect species vary considerably, suggesting that there are perhaps some 5.5 million insect species in existence, of which about one million have been described and named. These constitute around half of all [eukaryote](/wiki/Eukaryote) species, including [animals](/wiki/Animal), [plants](/wiki/Plant), and [fungi](/wiki/Fungus). The most diverse insect [orders](/wiki/Order_(biology)) are the Hemiptera (true bugs), Lepidoptera (butterflies and moths), Diptera (true flies), Hymenoptera (wasps, ants, and bees), and Coleoptera (beetles), each with more than 100,000 described species.

### Distribution and habitats

- Insects occur in habitats as varied as snow, freshwater, the tropics, desert, and even the sea.
- The snow scorpionfly *[Boreus hyemalis](/wiki/Boreus_hyemalis)* on snow
- The great diving beetle *[Dytiscus marginalis](/wiki/Dytiscus_marginalis)* larva in a pond
- The green orchid bee *[Euglossa dilemma](/wiki/Euglossa_dilemma)* of Central America
- The desert locust *[Schistocerca gregaria](/wiki/Schistocerca_gregaria)* laying eggs in sand
- Sea skater *[Halobates](/wiki/Halobates)* on a Hawaii beach

Insects are distributed over every continent and almost every terrestrial habitat. There are many more species in the [tropics](/wiki/Tropics), especially in [rainforests](/wiki/Tropical_rainforest), than in temperate zones. The world's regions have received widely differing amounts of attention from entomologists. The British Isles have been thoroughly surveyed, so that Gullan and Cranston 2014 state that the total of around 22,500 species is probably within 5% of the actual number there; they comment that Canada's list of 30,000 described species is surely over half of the actual total. They add that the 3,000 species of the American Arctic must be broadly accurate. In contrast, a large majority of the insect species of the tropics and the [southern hemisphere](/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere) are probably undescribed. Some 30–40,000 species [inhabit freshwater](/wiki/Aquatic_insect); very few insects, perhaps a hundred species, are marine. Insects such as [snow scorpionflies](/wiki/Snow_scorpionfly) flourish in cold habitats including the [Arctic](/wiki/Boreal_ecosystem) and at high altitude. Insects such as [desert locusts](/wiki/Desert_locust), ants, beetles, and termites are adapted to some of the hottest and driest environments on earth, such as the [Sonoran Desert](/wiki/Sonoran_Desert).

## Phylogeny and evolution

### External phylogeny

Insects form a [clade](/wiki/Clade), a natural group with a common ancestor, among the [arthropods](/wiki/Arthropod). A [phylogenetic](/wiki/Phylogenetic) analysis by Kjer et al. (2016) places the insects among the [Hexapoda](/wiki/Hexapoda), six-legged animals with segmented bodies; their closest relatives are the [Diplura](/wiki/Diplura) (bristletails).

[Aristotle](/wiki/Aristotle) was the first to describe the insects as a distinct group. He placed them as the second-lowest level of animals on his *[scala naturae](/wiki/Scala_naturae)*, above the [spontaneously generating](/wiki/Spontaneous_generation) sponges and worms, but below the hard-shelled marine snails. His classification remained in use for many centuries.

In 1758, in his *[Systema Naturae](/wiki/Systema_Naturae)*, [Carl Linnaeus](/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus) divided the animal kingdom into six classes including [Insecta](/wiki/Insecta_in_the_10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae). He created seven orders of insect according to the structure of their wings. These were the wingless Aptera, the two-winged Diptera, and five four-winged orders: the Coleoptera with fully-hardened forewings; the Hemiptera with partly-hardened forewings; the Lepidoptera with scaly wings; the Neuroptera with membranous wings but no [sting](/wiki/Stinger); and the Hymenoptera, with membranous wings and a sting.

[Jean-Baptiste de Lamarck](/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_de_Lamarck), in his 1809 *[Philosophie Zoologique](/wiki/Philosophie_Zoologique)*, treated the insects as one of nine invertebrate [phyla](/wiki/Phylum). In his 1817 *[Le Règne Animal](/wiki/Le_R%C3%A8gne_Animal)*, [Georges Cuvier](/wiki/Georges_Cuvier) grouped all animals into four *embranchements* ("branches" with different body plans), one of which was the articulated animals, containing arthropods and annelids. This arrangement was followed by the embryologist [Karl Ernst von Baer](/wiki/Karl_Ernst_von_Baer) in 1828, the zoologist [Louis Agassiz](/wiki/Louis_Agassiz) in 1857, and the comparative anatomist [Richard Owen](/wiki/Richard_Owen) in 1860. In 1874, [Ernst Haeckel](/wiki/Ernst_Haeckel) divided the animal kingdom into two subkingdoms, one of which was Metazoa for the multicellular animals. It had five phyla, including the articulates.

#### Modern

See also: [List of insect orders](/wiki/List_of_insect_orders)

Traditional morphology-based [systematics](/wiki/Systematics) have usually given the [Hexapoda](/wiki/Hexapoda) the rank of [superclass](/wiki/Superclass_(biology)), and identified four groups within it: insects (Ectognatha), [Collembola](/wiki/Springtail), [Protura](/wiki/Protura), and [Diplura](/wiki/Diplura), the latter three being grouped together as the [Entognatha](/wiki/Entognatha) on the basis of internalized mouth parts.

The use of phylogenetic data has brought about numerous changes in relationships above the level of [orders](/wiki/Order_(biology)). Insects can be divided into two groups historically treated as subclasses: wingless insects or [Apterygota](/wiki/Apterygota), and winged insects or [Pterygota](/wiki/Pterygota). The Apterygota traditionally consisted of the primitively wingless orders [Archaeognatha](/wiki/Archaeognatha) (jumping bristletails) and [Zygentoma](/wiki/Zygentoma) (silverfish). However, Apterygota is not [monophyletic](/wiki/Monophyletic), as Archaeognatha are sister to all other insects, based on the arrangement of their [mandibles](/wiki/Mandible_(insect_mouthpart)), while the Pterygota, the winged insects, emerged from within the [Dicondylia](/wiki/Dicondylia), alongside the Zygentoma.

The Pterygota ([Palaeoptera](/wiki/Palaeoptera) and [Neoptera](/wiki/Neoptera)) are winged and have [hardened plates](/wiki/Sclerite) on the outside of their body segments; the Neoptera have muscles that allow their wings to fold flat over the abdomen. Neoptera can be divided into groups with incomplete metamorphosis ([Polyneoptera](/wiki/Polyneoptera) and [Paraneoptera](/wiki/Paraneoptera)) and those with complete metamorphosis ([Holometabola](/wiki/Holometabola)). The molecular finding that the traditional [louse](/wiki/Louse) orders [Mallophaga](/wiki/Mallophaga) and [Anoplura](/wiki/Anoplura) are within [Psocoptera](/wiki/Psocoptera) has led to the new taxon [Psocodea](/wiki/Psocodea). [Phasmatodea](/wiki/Phasmatodea) and [Embiidina](/wiki/Embiidina) have been suggested to form the Eukinolabia. Mantodea, Blattodea, and Isoptera form a monophyletic group, [Dictyoptera](/wiki/Dictyoptera). Fleas are now thought to be closely related to boreid mecopterans.

### Evolutionary history

Main article: [Evolution of insects](/wiki/Evolution_of_insects)

The oldest fossil that may be a primitive wingless insect is *[Leverhulmia](/wiki/Leverhulmia)* from the Early [Devonian](/wiki/Devonian) [Windyfield chert](/wiki/Windyfield_chert). The oldest known flying insects are from the mid-[Carboniferous](/wiki/Carboniferous), around 328–324 million years ago. The group subsequently underwent a rapid [explosive diversification](/wiki/Evolutionary_radiation). Claims that they originated substantially earlier, during the [Silurian](/wiki/Silurian) or [Devonian](/wiki/Devonian) (some 400 million years ago) based on [molecular clock](/wiki/Molecular_clock) estimates, are unlikely to be correct, given the fossil record.

Four [large-scale radiations](/wiki/Evolutionary_radiation) of insects have occurred: [beetles](/wiki/Beetle) (from about 300 million years ago), [flies](/wiki/Fly) (from about 250 million years ago), [moths](/wiki/Moth) and [wasps](/wiki/Wasp) (both from about 150 million years ago).

The extremely successful [Hymenoptera](/wiki/Hymenoptera) (wasps, bees, and ants) appeared some 200 million years ago in the [Triassic](/wiki/Triassic) Period, but achieved their wide diversity more recently in the [Cenozoic](/wiki/Cenozoic) era, which began 66 million years ago. Some highly successful insect groups evolved in conjunction with [flowering plants](/wiki/Flowering_plant), a powerful illustration of [coevolution](/wiki/Coevolution). Insects were among the earliest terrestrial [herbivores](/wiki/Herbivore) and acted as major selection agents on plants. Plants evolved chemical [defenses against this herbivory](/wiki/Plant_defense_against_herbivory) and the insects, in turn, evolved mechanisms to deal with plant toxins. Many insects make use of these toxins to protect themselves from their predators. Such insects often advertise their toxicity using [warning colors](/wiki/Warning_colors).

- The giant dragonfly-like insect *[Meganeura monyi](/wiki/Meganeura_monyi)* grew to wingspans of 75 cm (2 ft 6 in) in the late [Carboniferous](/wiki/Carboniferous), around 300 million years ago.
- [Beetle](/wiki/Beetle) *[Moravocoleus permianus](/wiki/Tshekardocoleidae)*, fossil and reconstruction, from the Early [Permian](/wiki/Permian)
- [Hymenoptera](/wiki/Hymenoptera) such as this *[Iberomaimetsha](/wiki/Maimetshidae)* from the Early [Cretaceous](/wiki/Cretaceous), around 100 million years ago.

#### Three-part body

Insects have a [segmented](/wiki/Segmentation_(biology)) body supported by an [exoskeleton](/wiki/Arthropod_exoskeleton), the hard outer covering made mostly of [chitin](/wiki/Chitin). The body is organized into three [interconnected units](/wiki/Tagma_(biology)): the [head](/wiki/Insect_morphology#Head), [thorax](/wiki/Thorax_(insect_anatomy)) and [abdomen](/wiki/Abdomen_(insect_anatomy)). The head supports a pair of sensory [antennae](/wiki/Antenna_(biology)), a pair of [compound eyes](/wiki/Compound_eye), zero to three simple eyes (or [ocelli](/wiki/Ocelli)) and three sets of variously modified appendages that form the [mouthparts](/wiki/Insect_mouthparts). The thorax carries the three pairs of legs and up to two pairs of [wings](/wiki/Insect_wing). The abdomen contains most of the digestive, respiratory, excretory and reproductive structures.

#### Segmentation

Further information: [Insect morphology](/wiki/Insect_morphology)

The head is enclosed in a hard, heavily [sclerotized](/wiki/Sclerotin), unsegmented [head capsule](/wiki/Head_capsule), which contains most of the sensing organs, including the antennae, compound eyes, ocelli, and mouthparts. The thorax is composed of three sections named (from front to back) the [prothorax](/wiki/Prothorax), [mesothorax](/wiki/Mesothorax) and [metathorax](/wiki/Metathorax). The prothorax carries the first pair of legs. The mesothorax carries the second pair of legs and the front wings. The metathorax carries the third pair of legs and the hind wings. The abdomen is the largest part of the insect, typically with 11–12 segments, and is less strongly sclerotized than the head or thorax. Each segment of the abdomen has sclerotized upper and lower plates (the tergum and sternum), connected to adjacent sclerotized parts by membranes. Each segment carries a pair of [spiracles](/wiki/Spiracle_(arthropods)).

#### Exoskeleton

Main article: [Arthropod cuticle](/wiki/Arthropod_cuticle)

The outer skeleton, the [cuticle](/wiki/Arthropod_cuticle), is made up of two layers: the epicuticle, a thin and waxy water-resistant outer layer without [chitin](/wiki/Chitin), and a lower layer, the thick chitinous procuticle. The procuticle has two layers: an outer exocuticle and an inner endocuticle. The tough and flexible endocuticle is built from numerous layers of fibrous chitin and proteins, criss-crossing each other in a sandwich pattern, while the exocuticle is rigid and sclerotized. As an adaptation to life on land, insects have an [enzyme](/wiki/Enzyme) that uses atmospheric oxygen to harden their cuticle, unlike crustaceans which use heavy calcium compounds for the same purpose. This makes the insect exoskeleton a lightweight material.

### Internal systems

Main article: [Insect physiology](/wiki/Insect_physiology)

#### Nervous

The [nervous system](/wiki/Nervous_system) of an insect consists of a [brain](/wiki/Insect_brain) and a [ventral nerve cord](/wiki/Ventral_nerve_cord). The head capsule is made up of six fused segments, each with either a pair of [ganglia](/wiki/Ganglion), or a cluster of nerve cells outside of the brain. The first three pairs of ganglia are fused into the brain, while the three following pairs are fused into a structure of three pairs of ganglia under the insect's [esophagus](/wiki/Esophagus), called the [subesophageal ganglion](/wiki/Subesophageal_ganglion). The [thoracic](/wiki/Thoracic) segments have one ganglion on each side, connected into a pair per segment. This arrangement is also seen in the first eight segments of the abdomen. Many insects have fewer ganglia than this. Insects are capable of learning.

#### Digestive

An insect uses its digestive system to extract nutrients and other substances from the food it consumes. There is extensive variation among different [orders](/wiki/Taxonomic_order), [life stages](/wiki/Metamorphosis), and even [castes](/wiki/Caste_(insect)) in the digestive system of insects. The [gut](/wiki/Alimentary_canal) runs lengthwise through the body. It has three sections, with paired [salivary glands](/wiki/Salivary_gland) and salivary reservoirs. By moving its mouthparts the insect mixes its food with saliva. Some insects, like [flies](/wiki/Diptera), expel [digestive enzymes](/wiki/Digestive_enzyme) onto their food to break it down, but most insects digest their food in the gut. The [foregut](/wiki/Foregut) is lined with cuticule as protection from tough food. It includes the [mouth](/wiki/Buccal_cavity), pharynx, and [crop](/wiki/Crop_(anatomy)) which stores food. Digestion starts in the mouth with enzymes in the saliva. Strong muscles in the pharynx pump fluid into the mouth, lubricating the food, and enabling certain insects to feed on blood or from the [xylem](/wiki/Xylem) and [phloem](/wiki/Phloem) transport vessels of plants. Once food leaves the crop, it passes to the [midgut](/wiki/Stomach), where the majority of digestion takes place. Microscopic projections, [microvilli](/wiki/Microvillus), increase the surface area of the wall to absorb nutrients. In the [hindgut](/wiki/Hindgut), undigested food particles are joined by [uric acid](/wiki/Uric_acid) to form fecal pellets; most of the water is absorbed, leaving a dry pellet to be eliminated. Insects may have one to hundreds of [Malpighian tubules](/wiki/Malpighian_tubule_system). These remove nitrogenous wastes from the hemolymph of the insect and regulate osmotic balance. Wastes and solutes are emptied directly into the alimentary canal, at the junction between the midgut and hindgut.

#### Reproductive

Main article: [Insect reproductive system](/wiki/Insect_reproductive_system)

The [reproductive system of female insects](/wiki/Insect_reproductive_system) consist of a pair of [ovaries](/wiki/Ovaries), accessory glands, one or more [spermathecae](/wiki/Spermatheca) to store sperm, and ducts connecting these parts. The ovaries are made up of a variable number of egg tubes, [ovarioles](/wiki/Ovarioles). Female insects make eggs, receive and store sperm, manipulate sperm from different males, and lay eggs. Accessory glands produce substances to maintain sperm and to protect the eggs. They can produce glue and protective substances for coating eggs, or tough coverings for a batch of eggs called [oothecae](/wiki/Ootheca).

For males, the reproductive system consists of one or two [testes](/wiki/Testis), suspended in the body cavity by [tracheae](/wiki/Invertebrate_trachea). The testes contain sperm tubes or follicles in a membranous sac. These connect to a duct that leads to the outside. The terminal portion of the duct may be sclerotized to form the [intromittent organ](/wiki/Intromittent_organ), the [aedeagus](/wiki/Aedeagus).

#### Respiratory

Main article: [Respiratory system of insects](/wiki/Respiratory_system_of_insects)

The tube-like heart (green) of the mosquito *[Anopheles gambiae](/wiki/Anopheles_gambiae)* extends horizontally across the body, interlinked with the diamond-shaped [wing muscles](/wiki/Insect_wing#Muscles) (also green) and surrounded by [pericardial cells](/wiki/Pericardium) (red). Blue depicts [cell nuclei](/wiki/Cell_nuclei).

[Insect respiration](/wiki/Respiratory_system_of_insects) is accomplished without [lungs](/wiki/Lung). Instead, insects have a system of internal tubes and sacs through which gases either diffuse or are actively pumped, delivering oxygen directly to tissues that need it via their [tracheae](/wiki/Invertebrate_trachea) and tracheoles. In most insects, air is taken in through paired [spiracles](/wiki/Spiracle_(arthropods)), openings on the sides of the abdomen and thorax. The respiratory system limits the size of insects. As insects get larger, [gas exchange](/wiki/Gas_exchange#Invertebrates) via spiracles becomes less efficient, and thus the heaviest insect currently weighs less than 100 g. However, with increased atmospheric oxygen levels, as were present in the late [Paleozoic](/wiki/Paleozoic), larger insects were possible, such as dragonflies with wingspans of more than two feet (60 cm). Gas exchange patterns in insects range from continuous and [diffusive](/wiki/Diffusion) ventilation, to [discontinuous](/wiki/Discontinuous_gas_exchange).

#### Circulatory

Further information: [Insect physiology § Circulatory system](/wiki/Insect_physiology#Circulatory_system)

Because oxygen is delivered directly to tissues via tracheoles, the circulatory system is not used to carry oxygen, and is therefore greatly reduced. The insect circulatory system is open; it has no [veins](/wiki/Vein) or [arteries](/wiki/Artery), and instead consists of little more than a single, perforated dorsal tube that pulses [peristaltically](/wiki/Peristalsis). This dorsal blood vessel is divided into two sections: the heart and aorta. The dorsal blood vessel circulates the [hemolymph](/wiki/Hemolymph), arthropods' fluid analog of [blood](/wiki/Blood), from the rear of the body cavity forward. Hemolymph is composed of plasma in which [hemocytes](/wiki/Hemocytes) are suspended. Nutrients, hormones, wastes, and other substances are transported throughout the insect body in the hemolymph. Hemocytes include many types of cells that are important for immune responses, wound healing, and other functions. Hemolymph pressure may be increased by muscle contractions or by swallowing air into the digestive system to aid in molting.

#### Sensory

Further information: [Insect physiology § Sensory organs](/wiki/Insect_physiology#Sensory_organs)

Most insects have a pair of large [compound eyes](/wiki/Compound_eye) and other sensory organs such as antennae able to detect movements and chemical stimuli on their heads.

Many insects possess numerous specialized [sensory organs](/wiki/Sensilla) able to detect stimuli including limb position ([proprioception](/wiki/Proprioception)) by [campaniform sensilla](/wiki/Campaniform_sensilla), light, [water](/wiki/Hygroreception), chemicals (senses of [taste](/wiki/Taste) and [smell](/wiki/Sense_of_smell)), sound, and heat. Some insects such as [bees](/wiki/Bees) can perceive [ultraviolet](/wiki/Ultraviolet) wavelengths, or detect [polarized light](/wiki/Polarized_light), while the [antennae](/wiki/Antenna_(biology)) of male moths can detect the [pheromones](/wiki/Pheromone) of female moths over distances of over a kilometer. There is a trade-off between visual acuity and chemical or tactile acuity, such that most insects with well-developed eyes have reduced or simple antennae, and vice versa. Insects perceive sound by different mechanisms, such as thin vibrating membranes ([tympana](/wiki/Tympanal_organ)). Insects were the earliest organisms to produce and sense sounds. Hearing has evolved independently at least 19 times in different insect groups.

Most insects, except some [cave crickets](/wiki/Rhaphidophoridae), are able to perceive light and dark. Many have acute vision capable of detecting small and rapid movements. The eyes may include simple eyes or [ocelli](/wiki/Ocellus) as well as larger [compound eyes](/wiki/Compound_eye). Many species can detect light in the [infrared](/wiki/Infrared), [ultraviolet](/wiki/Ultraviolet) and [visible light](/wiki/Visible_light) wavelengths, with color vision. Phylogenetic analysis suggests that UV-green-blue [trichromacy](/wiki/Trichromacy) existed from at least the [Devonian](/wiki/Devonian) Period, some 400 million years ago.

The individual lenses in compound eyes are immobile, but fruit flies have photoreceptor cells underneath each lens which move rapidly in and out of focus, in a series of movements called photoreceptor microsaccades. This gives them, and possibly many other insects, a much clearer image of the world than previously assumed.

An insect's [sense of smell](/wiki/Insect_olfaction) is via [chemical receptors](/wiki/Chemoreceptor), usually on the antennae and the mouthparts. These detect both airborne [volatile compounds](/wiki/Volatile_organic_compound) and odorants on surfaces, including pheromones from other insects and compounds released by food plants. Insects use olfaction to locate mating partners, food, and places to lay eggs, and to avoid predators. It is thus an extremely important sense, enabling insects to discriminate between thousands of volatile compounds.

Some insects are capable of [magnetoreception](/wiki/Magnetoreception); ants and bees navigate using it both locally (near their nests) and when migrating. The [Brazilian stingless bee](/wiki/Schwarziana_quadripunctata) detects magnetic fields using the hair-like [sensilla](/wiki/Sensillum) on its antennae.

## Reproduction and development

Main article: [Insect reproduction](/wiki/Insect_reproduction)

[Butterflies](/wiki/Butterfly) mating

Male insects produce sperm, which is delivered to the female via the [aedeagus](/wiki/Aedeagus). In some insects, such as [bed bugs](/wiki/Bed_bug), the male can inject his sperm directly into the abdomen through the [cuticle](/wiki/Cuticle_(arthropod)) of the female, a process known as [traumatic insemination](/wiki/Traumatic_insemination). With the exception of [parthenogenetic](/wiki/Parthenogenesis) insects, insects are most often sexually [dimorphic](/wiki/Sexual_dimorphism#Insects), with the males and females of most insects differing in appearance. Insects demonstrate a variety of strategies for locating mates, including the use of sex pheromones, mass swarming (referred to as [leks](/wiki/Lek_mating)), and [nuptial gifts](/wiki/Nuptial_gift).

### Life-cycles

The majority of insects hatch from [eggs](/wiki/Egg_(biology)). The fertilization and development takes place inside the egg, which is usually drought resistant. Many insects lay their eggs in large masses, while others favour greater dispersion and lay single eggs in different locations. Some insects are [parthenogenetic](/wiki/Parthenogenesis), meaning that the female can reproduce and give birth without having the eggs [fertilized](/wiki/Fertilization) by a [male](/wiki/Male). Many aphids alternate between generations of asexual and sexual reproduction.

Since most insects are [poikilothermic](/wiki/Poikilotherm), a rise in temperature will speed up the metabolism of an insect and consequently increase its rate of development. For this reason the development stage of an insect can be more accurately predicted when temperature over time is taken into account, rather than just the calendar age of the insect. Most insects develop in a year or less, exhibiting varying degrees of [voltinism](/wiki/Voltinism). Most insects in temperate climates do not develop continuously throughout the year, but slow or arrest their development during unfavourable times of the year. During seasonal extremes in summer ([aestivation](/wiki/Aestivation)) or cold winters ([hibernation](/wiki/Hibernation)) insects will enter [diapause](/wiki/Diapause). Some insects will enter diapause every year regardless of conditions, where as others will only enter diapause during seasonal extremes.

### Metamorphosis

[Incomplete metamorphosis](/wiki/Hemimetabolism) in a [locust](/wiki/Locust) with multiple [instars](/wiki/Instar). Egg is not shown. The largest specimen is adult.

[Metamorphosis](/wiki/Metamorphosis) in insects is the process of development that converts young to adults. There are two forms of metamorphosis: incomplete ([hemimetabolism](/wiki/Hemimetabolism)) and complete ([holometabolism](/wiki/Holometabolism)).

[Hemimetabolous](/wiki/Hemimetabolism) insects, those with incomplete metamorphosis, change gradually after hatching from the [egg](/wiki/Egg_(biology)) by undergoing a series of [molts](/wiki/Ecdysis) through stages called [instars](/wiki/Instar), until the final, [adult](/wiki/Adult), stage is reached.

Life-cycle of butterfly, undergoing [complete metamorphosis](/wiki/Holometabolism) from egg through [caterpillar](/wiki/Caterpillar) larvae to [pupa](/wiki/Pupa) and adult

[Holometabolism](/wiki/Holometabolism), or complete metamorphosis, is where the insect changes in four stages, an egg or [embryo](/wiki/Embryo), a [larva](/wiki/Insect_larvae), a [pupa](/wiki/Pupa) and the adult or [imago](/wiki/Imago). In these species, an egg hatches to produce a worm-like larva. The larva grows and eventually becomes a pupa, a stage marked by reduced movement. Insects such as butterflies undergo considerable change in form during the pupal stage, and emerge as adults.

## Communication

Insects that produce sound can generally hear it. Most [insects can hear](/wiki/Animal_hearing) only a [narrow range](/wiki/Hearing_range) of [frequencies](/wiki/Frequency) related to the frequency of the sounds they can produce. Mosquitoes can hear up to 2 [kilohertz](/wiki/Hertz). Certain predatory and parasitic insects can detect the characteristic sounds made by their prey or hosts, respectively. Likewise, some nocturnal moths can perceive the [ultrasonic](/wiki/Ultrasound) emissions of [bats](/wiki/Bat), which helps them [avoid predation](/wiki/Ultrasound_avoidance).

### Light production

A few insects, such as [Mycetophilidae](/wiki/Mycetophilidae) (Diptera) and the beetle families [Lampyridae](/wiki/Lampyridae), [Phengodidae](/wiki/Phengodidae), [Elateridae](/wiki/Elateridae) and [Staphylinidae](/wiki/Staphylinidae) are [bioluminescent](/wiki/Bioluminescent). The most familiar group are the [fireflies](/wiki/Fireflies), beetles of the family Lampyridae. Some species are able to control this light generation to produce flashes. The function varies with some species using them to attract mates, while others use them to lure prey. Cave dwelling larvae of *[Arachnocampa](/wiki/Arachnocampa)* (Mycetophilidae, fungus gnats) glow to lure small flying insects into sticky strands of silk. Some fireflies of the genus *[Photuris](/wiki/Photuris)* [mimic](/wiki/Mimicry) the flashing of female *[Photinus](/wiki/Photinus_(beetle))* species to attract males of that species, which are then captured and devoured. The colors of emitted light vary from dull blue (*Orfelia fultoni*, Mycetophilidae) to the familiar greens and the rare reds (*Phrixothrix tiemanni*, Phengodidae).

### Sound production

Insects make sounds mostly by mechanical action of appendages. In [grasshoppers](/wiki/Grasshopper) and crickets, this is achieved by [stridulation](/wiki/Stridulation). [Cicadas](/wiki/Cicada) make the loudest sounds among the insects by producing and amplifying sounds with special modifications to their body to form [tymbals](/wiki/Tymbal) and associated musculature. The African [cicada](/wiki/Cicada) *[Brevisana brevis](/wiki/Brevisana_brevis)* has been measured at 106.7 [decibels](/wiki/Decibel) at a distance of 50 cm (20 in). Some insects, such as the *[Helicoverpa zea](/wiki/Helicoverpa_zea)* moths, [hawk moths](/wiki/Hawk_moth) and [Hedylid](/wiki/Hedylidae) butterflies, can hear [ultrasound](/wiki/Ultrasound) and [take evasive action](/wiki/Ultrasound_avoidance) when they sense that they have been detected by bats. Some moths produce ultrasonic clicks that warn predatory bats of their unpalatability (acoustic [aposematism](/wiki/Aposematism)), while some palatable moths have evolved to mimic these calls (acoustic [Batesian mimicry](/wiki/Batesian_mimicry)). The claim that some moths can [jam bat sonar](/wiki/Echolocation_jamming#Jamming_by_prey) has been revisited. Ultrasonic recording and high-speed infrared videography of bat-moth interactions suggest the palatable tiger moth really does defend against attacking big brown bats using ultrasonic clicks that jam bat sonar.

Very low sounds are produced in various species of [Coleoptera](/wiki/Coleoptera), [Hymenoptera](/wiki/Hymenoptera), [Lepidoptera](/wiki/Lepidoptera), [Mantodea](/wiki/Mantodea) and [Neuroptera](/wiki/Neuroptera). These low sounds are produced by the insect's movement, amplified by stridulatory structures on the insect's muscles and joints; these sounds can be used to warn or communicate with other insects. Most sound-making insects also have [tympanal organs](/wiki/Tympanal_organ) that can perceive airborne sounds. Some [hemipterans](/wiki/Hemiptera), such as the [water boatmen](/wiki/Corixidae), communicate via underwater sounds.

Cricket in garage with familiar call

Communication using surface-borne vibrational signals is more widespread among insects because of size constraints in producing air-borne sounds. Insects cannot effectively produce low-frequency sounds, and high-frequency sounds tend to disperse more in a dense environment (such as [foliage](/wiki/Foliage)), so insects living in such environments communicate primarily using substrate-borne vibrations.

Some species use vibrations for communicating, such as to attract mates as in the songs of the [shield bug](/wiki/Shield_bug) *[Nezara viridula](/wiki/Nezara_viridula)*. Vibrations can also be used to communicate between species; [lycaenid](/wiki/Lycaenid) caterpillars, which [form a mutualistic association with ants](/wiki/Myrmecophile) communicate with ants in this way. The [Madagascar hissing cockroach](/wiki/Madagascar_hissing_cockroach) has the ability to press air through its spiracles to make a hissing noise as a sign of aggression; the [death's-head hawkmoth](/wiki/Death%27s-head_hawkmoth) makes a squeaking noise by forcing air out of their pharynx when agitated, which may also reduce aggressive worker honey bee behavior when the two are close.

### Chemical communication

Main articles: [Chemical communication in insects](/wiki/Chemical_communication_in_insects) and [Insect olfaction](/wiki/Insect_olfaction)

Social insects such as ants have multiple types of [pheromonal](/wiki/Pheromone) glands, producing different [semiochemicals](/wiki/Semiochemical) for [communication with other insects](/wiki/Chemical_communication_in_insects).

Many insects have evolved [chemical means for communication](/wiki/Chemical_communication_in_insects). These [semiochemicals](/wiki/Semiochemical) are often derived from plant metabolites including those meant to attract, repel and provide other kinds of information. [Pheromones](/wiki/Pheromone) are used for attracting mates of the opposite sex, for aggregating [conspecific](/wiki/Conspecificity) individuals of both sexes, for deterring other individuals from approaching, to mark a trail, and to trigger aggression in nearby individuals. [Allomones](/wiki/Allomone) benefit their producer by the effect they have upon the receiver. [Kairomones](/wiki/Kairomone) benefit their receiver instead of their producer. Synomones benefit the producer and the receiver. While some chemicals are targeted at individuals of the same species, others are used for communication across species. The use of scents is especially well-developed in social insects. Cuticular hydrocarbons  are nonstructural materials produced and secreted to the cuticle surface to fight [desiccation](/wiki/Desiccation) and [pathogens](/wiki/Pathogen). They are important, too, as pheromones, especially in social insects.

## Social behavior

Main article: [Eusociality](/wiki/Eusociality)

A [cathedral mound](/wiki/Nasutitermes_triodiae) created by [eusocial](/wiki/Eusociality) [mound-building termites](/wiki/Mound-building_termites).

[Honey bee](/wiki/Honey_bee)'s figure-eight [waggle dance](/wiki/Waggle_dance). An orientation 45° to the right of 'up' on the comb indicates food 45° to the right of the sun. The dancer's rapid waggling blurs her abdomen.

[Social insects](/wiki/Social_insect), such as [termites](/wiki/Termite), [ants](/wiki/Ant) and many [bees](/wiki/Bee) and [wasps](/wiki/Wasp), are [eusocial](/wiki/Eusociality). They live together in such large well-organized colonies of genetically similar individuals that they are sometimes considered [superorganisms](/wiki/Superorganism). In particular, reproduction is largely limited to a [queen caste](/wiki/Gyne); other females are [workers](/wiki/Worker_caste), prevented from reproducing by [worker policing](/wiki/Worker_policing). [Honey bees](/wiki/Honey_bee) have evolved a system of abstract symbolic communication where a behavior is used to represent and convey specific information about the environment. In this communication system, called [dance language](/wiki/Bee_learning_and_communication), the angle at which a bee dances represents a direction relative to the sun, and the length of the dance represents the distance to be flown. [Bumblebees](/wiki/Bumblebee) too have some social communication behaviors. *[Bombus terrestris](/wiki/Bombus_terrestris)*, for example, more rapidly learns about visiting unfamiliar, yet rewarding flowers, when they can see a conspecific foraging on the same species.

Only insects that live in nests or colonies possess fine-scale spatial orientation. Some can [navigate](/wiki/Animal_navigation) unerringly to a single hole a few millimeters in diameter among thousands of similar holes, after a trip of several kilometers. In [philopatry](/wiki/Philopatry), insects that [hibernate](/wiki/Hibernation) are able to recall a specific location up to a year after last viewing the area of interest. A few insects seasonally [migrate](/wiki/Insect_migration) large distances between different geographic regions, as in the continent-wide [monarch butterfly migration](/wiki/Monarch_butterfly_migration).

### Care of young

[Eusocial](/wiki/Eusocial) insects build nests, guard eggs, and provide food for offspring full-time. Most insects, however, lead short lives as adults, and rarely interact with one another except to mate or compete for mates. A small number provide [parental care](/wiki/Parental_care), where they at least guard their eggs, and sometimes guard their offspring until adulthood, possibly even feeding them. Many wasps and bees construct a nest or burrow, [store provisions](/wiki/Mass_provisioning) in it, and lay an egg upon those provisions, providing no further care.

## Locomotion

### Flight

Main article: [Insect flight](/wiki/Insect_flight)

Insects such as [hoverflies](/wiki/Hoverflies) are capable of rapid and agile [flight](/wiki/Insect_flight).

Insects are the only group of [invertebrates](/wiki/Invertebrate) to have developed flight. The ancient groups of insects in the Palaeoptera, the dragonflies, damselflies and mayflies, operate their wings directly by paired muscles attached to points on each wing base that raise and lower them. This can only be done at a relatively slow rate. All other insects, the Neoptera, have [indirect flight](/wiki/Indirect_flight), in which the flight muscles cause rapid oscillation of the thorax: there can be more wingbeats than nerve impulses commanding the muscles. One pair of flight muscles is aligned vertically, contracting to pull the top of the thorax down, and the wings up. The other pair runs longitudinally, contracting to force the top of the thorax up and the wings down. Most insects gain [aerodynamic lift](/wiki/Aerodynamic_lift) by creating a spiralling [vortex](/wiki/Vortex) at the [leading edge](/wiki/Leading_edge) of the wings. Small insects like thrips with tiny feathery wings gain lift using the [clap and fling](/wiki/Clap_and_fling) mechanism; the wings are clapped together and pulled apart, flinging vortices into the air at the leading edges and at the wingtips.

The evolution of [insect wings](/wiki/Insect_wing) has been [a subject of debate](/wiki/Insect_wing#Hypotheses); it has been suggested they came from modified gills, flaps on the spiracles, or an appendage, the epicoxa, at the base of the legs. More recently, entomologists have favored evolution of wings from lobes of the [notum](/wiki/Notum), of the [pleuron](/wiki/Pleuron_(insect_anatomy)), or more likely both.
In the [Carboniferous](/wiki/Carboniferous) age, the dragonfly-like *[Meganeura](/wiki/Meganeura)* had as much as a 50 cm (20 in) wide wingspan. The appearance of gigantic insects is consistent with high atmospheric oxygen at that time, as the respiratory system of insects constrains their size. The largest flying insects today are much smaller, with the largest wingspan belonging to the white witch moth (*[Thysania agrippina](/wiki/Thysania_agrippina)*), at approximately 28 cm (11 in).

Unlike [birds](/wiki/Bird), small insects are swept along by the [prevailing winds](/wiki/Prevailing_winds) although many larger insects [migrate](/wiki/Insect_migration). [Aphids](/wiki/Aphid) are transported long distances by low-level [jet streams](/wiki/Jet_stream).

### Walking

Further information: [Walking § Insects](/wiki/Walking#Insects)

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/61/Alternating_Tripod_Gait.webm/330px--Alternating_Tripod_Gait.webm.jpg)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/6/61/Alternating_Tripod_Gait.webm/Alternating_Tripod_Gait.webm.480p.vp9.webm)

Spatial and temporal stepping pattern of walking desert ants performing an alternating tripod gait. Recording rate: 500 fps, Playback rate: 10 fps.

Many adult insects use six legs for walking, with an alternating [tripod gait](/wiki/Tripod_gait). This allows for rapid walking with a stable stance; it has been studied extensively in [cockroaches](/wiki/Cockroach) and [ants](/wiki/Ant). For the first step, the middle right leg and the front and rear left legs are in contact with the ground and move the insect forward, while the front and rear right leg and the middle left leg are lifted and moved forward to a new position. When they touch the ground to form a new stable triangle, the other legs can be lifted and brought forward in turn. The purest form of the tripedal gait is seen in insects moving at high speeds. However, this type of locomotion is not rigid and insects can adapt a variety of gaits. For example, when moving slowly, turning, avoiding obstacles, climbing or slippery surfaces, four (tetrapodal) or more feet ([wave-gait](/wiki/Walking#Insects)) may be touching the ground. Cockroaches are among the fastest insect runners and, at full speed, adopt a bipedal run. More sedate locomotion is seen in the well-[camouflaged](/wiki/Camouflage) stick insects ([Phasmatodea](/wiki/Phasmatodea)). A small number of species such as [Water striders](/wiki/Water_strider) can move on the surface of water; their claws are recessed in a special groove, preventing the claws from piercing the water's surface film. The ocean-skaters in the genus *[Halobates](/wiki/Halobates)* even live on the surface of open oceans, a habitat that has few insect species.

### Swimming

Main article: [Aquatic insects](/wiki/Aquatic_insects)

The [backswimmer](/wiki/Backswimmer) *[Notonecta glauca](/wiki/Notonecta_glauca)* underwater, showing its paddle-like hindleg adaptation

A large number of insects live either part or the whole of their lives underwater. In many of the more primitive orders of insect, the immature stages are aquatic. In some groups, such as [water beetles](/wiki/Water_beetle), the adults too are aquatic.

Many of these species are adapted for under-water locomotion. Water beetles and water bugs have legs adapted into paddle-like structures. Dragonfly [naiads](/wiki/Nymph_(biology)) use jet propulsion, forcibly expelling water out of their rectal chamber. Other insects such as the [rove beetle](/wiki/Staphylinidae) *[Stenus](/wiki/Stenus)* emit [pygidial](/wiki/Pygidium#Insects) gland [surfactant](/wiki/Surfactant) secretions that reduce surface tension; this enables them to move on the surface of water by [Marangoni propulsion](/wiki/Marangoni_effect).

## Ecology

Main article: [Insect ecology](/wiki/Insect_ecology)

Insects play many critical roles in [ecosystems](/wiki/Ecosystem), including soil turning and aeration, dung burial, pest control, pollination and wildlife nutrition. For instance, termites modify the environment around their nests, encouraging grass growth; many [beetles](/wiki/Beetle) are [scavengers](/wiki/Scavenger); dung beetles [recycle](/wiki/Recycling) biological materials into forms useful to other [organisms](/wiki/Organism). Insects are responsible for much of the process by which [topsoil](/wiki/Topsoil) is created.

### Defense

Main article: [Defense in insects](/wiki/Defense_in_insects)

*[Reduvius personatus](/wiki/Reduvius_personatus)*, the masked hunter bug [nymph](/wiki/Nymph_(biology)), [camouflages](/wiki/Camouflage) itself with sand grains to avoid [predators](/wiki/Predation).

Insects are mostly small, soft bodied, and fragile compared to larger lifeforms. The immature stages are small, move slowly or are immobile, and so all stages are exposed to [predation](/wiki/Predation) and [parasitism](/wiki/Parasitism). Insects accordingly employ multiple [defensive strategies](/wiki/Defense_in_insects), including [camouflage](/wiki/Camouflage), [mimicry](/wiki/Mimicry), toxicity and active defense.
Many [insects rely on camouflage](/wiki/Defense_in_insects#Hiding) to avoid being noticed by their predators or prey. It is common among [leaf beetles](/wiki/Leaf_beetle) and [weevils](/wiki/Weevil) that feed on wood or vegetation. [Stick insects](/wiki/Stick_insect) mimic the forms of sticks and leaves.
Many insects use [mimicry](/wiki/Mimicry) to deceive predators into avoiding them. In [Batesian mimicry](/wiki/Batesian_mimicry), edible species, such as of [hoverflies](/wiki/Hoverflies) (the mimics), gain a survival advantage by resembling inedible species (the models). In [Müllerian mimicry](/wiki/M%C3%BCllerian_mimicry), inedible species, such as of wasps and bees, resemble each other so as to reduce the sampling rate by predators who need to learn that those insects are inedible. *[Heliconius](/wiki/Heliconius)* butterflies, many of which are toxic, form Müllerian complexes, advertising their inedibility.
[Chemical defense](/wiki/Chemical_defense#Terrestrial_arthropods) is common among Coleoptera and Lepidoptera, usually being advertised by bright warning colors ([aposematism](/wiki/Aposematism)), as in the [monarch butterfly](/wiki/Monarch_butterfly). As larvae, they obtain their [toxicity](/wiki/Caterpillar#Chemical_defences) by sequestering chemicals from the plants they eat into their own tissues. Some manufacture their own toxins. Predators that eat poisonous butterflies and moths may vomit violently, learning not to eat insects with similar markings; this is the basis of Müllerian mimicry.
Some [ground beetles](/wiki/Ground_beetle) of the family Carabidae actively defend themselves, spraying chemicals from their abdomen with great accuracy, to repel predators.

### Pollination

Main article: [Entomophily](/wiki/Entomophily)

[European honey bee](/wiki/Apis_mellifera) carrying pollen in a [pollen basket](/wiki/Pollen_basket) back to the hive

Pollination is the process by which [pollen](/wiki/Pollen) is transferred in the reproduction of plants, thereby enabling [fertilisation](/wiki/Fertilisation) and [sexual reproduction](/wiki/Sexual_reproduction). Most flowering plants require an animal to do the transportation. The majority of [pollination is by insects](/wiki/Entomophily). Because insects usually receive benefit for the pollination in the form of energy rich nectar it is a [mutualism](/wiki/Mutualism_(biology)). The various flower traits, such as bright colors and [pheromones](/wiki/Insect_pheromones) that [coevolved](/wiki/Coevolution) with their pollinators, have been called [pollination syndromes](/wiki/Pollination_syndrome), though around one third of flowers cannot be assigned to a single syndrome.

### Parasitism

Further information: [Parasitism](/wiki/Parasitism) and [Parasitoid wasp](/wiki/Parasitoid_wasp)

Many insects are [parasitic](/wiki/Parasitism). The largest group, with over 100,000 species and perhaps over a million, consists of a single [clade](/wiki/Clade) of [parasitoid wasps](/wiki/Parasitoid_wasp) among the Hymenoptera. These are parasites of other insects, eventually killing their hosts. Some are hyper-parasites, as their hosts are other parasitoid wasps. Several groups of insects can be considered as either [micropredators](/wiki/Micropredator) or [external parasites](/wiki/Ectoparasites); for example, many [hemipteran](/wiki/Hemiptera) bugs have piercing and sucking mouthparts, adapted for feeding on plant sap, while species in groups such as [fleas](/wiki/Flea), [lice](/wiki/Lice), and [mosquitoes](/wiki/Mosquito) are [hematophagous](/wiki/Hematophagy), feeding on the [blood](/wiki/Blood) of animals.

- A [parasitoid wasp](/wiki/Parasitoid_wasp) ovipositing into an [aphid](/wiki/Aphid)
- Plant parasite or [micropredator](/wiki/Micropredator): a [coreid](/wiki/Coreidae) bug sucking plant sap
- [Human head-lice](/wiki/Pediculus_humanus_capitis) are directly transmitted [obligate](/wiki/Obligate_parasite) ectoparasites.

## Relationship to humans

Main article: [Human interactions with insects](/wiki/Human_interactions_with_insects)

### As pests

*[Aedes aegypti](/wiki/Aedes_aegypti)*, the [yellow fever](/wiki/Yellow_fever) mosquito, is a [vector](/wiki/Disease_vector) of several [diseases](/wiki/Mosquito-borne_disease).

Main article: [Pest insect](/wiki/Pest_insect)

Many insects are considered [pests](/wiki/Pest_(organism)) by humans. These include parasites of people and livestock, such as [lice](/wiki/Lice) and [bed bugs](/wiki/Cimex); [mosquitoes](/wiki/Mosquito) act as [vectors](/wiki/Disease_vector) of [several diseases](/wiki/Mosquito-borne_disease). Other pests include insects like [termites](/wiki/Termite) that damage wooden structures; herbivorous insects such as [locusts](/wiki/Locust), aphids, and [thrips](/wiki/Thrips) that destroy agricultural crops, or like [wheat weevils](/wiki/Wheat_weevil) damage stored agricultural produce. Farmers have often attempted to control insects with chemical [insecticides](/wiki/Insecticide), but increasingly rely on [biological pest control](/wiki/Biological_pest_control). This uses one organism to reduce the population density of a pest organism; it is a key element of [integrated pest management](/wiki/Integrated_pest_management). Biological control is favored because insecticides can cause harm to ecosystems far beyond the intended pest targets.

### In beneficial roles

See also: [Economic entomology § Beneficial insects](/wiki/Economic_entomology#Beneficial_insects)

[Silkworms](/wiki/Silkworm) were [domesticated](/wiki/Domestication) for [silk](/wiki/Silk) over 5,000 years ago. Here, silk cocoons are being unrolled.

[Pollination](/wiki/Pollination) of flowering plants by insects including [bees](/wiki/Bee), [butterflies](/wiki/Butterfly), [flies](/wiki/Diptera), and [beetles](/wiki/Beetle), is economically important. The value of insect pollination of crops and fruit trees was estimated in 2021 to be about $34 billion in the US alone.

Insects produce useful substances such as [honey](/wiki/Honey), [wax](/wiki/Wax), [lacquer](/wiki/Lacquer) [silk](/wiki/Silk), and [carmine](/wiki/Carmine). [Honey bees](/wiki/Honey_bee) have been cultured by humans for thousands of years for honey. Beekeeping in pottery vessels began about 9,000 years ago in North Africa. The [silkworm](/wiki/Silkworm) has greatly affected human history, as [silk-driven trade](/wiki/Silk_Road) established relationships between China and the rest of the world.

Insects that feed on or parasitise other insects are beneficial to humans if they thereby reduce damage to agriculture and human structures. For example, [aphids](/wiki/Aphid) feed on crops, causing economic loss, but [ladybugs](/wiki/Ladybug) feed on aphids, and can be used [to control them](/wiki/Biological_pest_control). Insects account for the vast majority of insect consumption.

Fly larvae ([maggots](/wiki/Maggot)) were formerly [used to treat wounds](/wiki/Maggot_therapy) to prevent or stop [gangrene](/wiki/Gangrene), as they would only consume dead flesh. This treatment is finding modern usage in some hospitals. Insects have gained attention as potential sources of drugs and other medicinal substances. Adult insects, such as crickets and insect larvae of various kinds, are commonly used as fishing bait.

### Population declines

Main article: [Decline in insect populations](/wiki/Decline_in_insect_populations)

At least 66 insect species extinctions have been recorded since 1500, many of them on oceanic islands. [Declines in insect abundance](/wiki/Decline_in_insect_populations) have been attributed to human activity in the form of artificial lighting, land use changes such as urbanization or farming, pesticide use, and invasive species. A 2019 research review suggested that a large proportion of insect species is threatened with extinction in the 21st century, though the details have been disputed. A larger 2020 meta-study, analyzing data from 166 long-term surveys, suggested that populations of terrestrial insects are indeed decreasing rapidly, by about 9% per decade.

### In research

The fruit fly *[Drosophila melanogaster](/wiki/Drosophila_melanogaster)* is a widely used [model organism](/wiki/Model_organism).

Insects play important roles in biological research. For example, because of its small size, short generation time and high [fecundity](/wiki/Fecundity), the common fruit fly *[Drosophila melanogaster](/wiki/Drosophila_melanogaster)* is a [model organism](/wiki/Model_organism) for studies in the [genetics](/wiki/Genetics) of [eukaryotes](/wiki/Eukaryote), including [genetic linkage](/wiki/Genetic_linkage), [interactions between genes](/wiki/Epistasis), [chromosomal](/wiki/Chromosome) genetics, [development](/wiki/Evo-devo), behavior and [evolution](/wiki/Evolution). Because genetic systems are well conserved among eukaryotes, understanding basic cellular processes like [DNA replication](/wiki/DNA_replication) or [transcription](/wiki/Transcription_(genetics)) in fruit flies can help to understand those processes in other eukaryotes, including humans. The [genome](/wiki/Genome) of *D. melanogaster* was [sequenced](/wiki/Genome_project) in 2000, reflecting the organism's important role in biological research. It was found that 70% of the fly genome is similar to the [human genome](/wiki/Human_genome), supporting the theory of [evolution](/wiki/Evolution).

### As food

Main article: [Insects as food](/wiki/Insects_as_food)

[Witchetty grubs](/wiki/Witchetty_grub) are prized as high-protein foods by Aboriginal Australians.

Insects are consumed as food in 80% of the world's nations, by people in roughly 3,000 ethnic groups. In Africa, locally abundant species of [locusts](/wiki/Locust) and [termites](/wiki/Termite) are a common traditional human food source. Some, especially [deep-fried](/wiki/Deep-fried) [cicadas](/wiki/Cicada), are considered to be [delicacies](/wiki/Delicacy). Insects have a high protein content for their mass, and some authors suggest their potential as a major source of [protein](/wiki/Protein) in human [nutrition](/wiki/Nutrition). In most first-world countries, however, [entomophagy](/wiki/Entomophagy) (the eating of insects), is [taboo](/wiki/Taboo). They are also recommended by [armed forces](/wiki/Military) as a [survival](/wiki/Survival_skills) food for troops in adversity. Because of the abundance of insects and a worldwide concern of food shortages, the [Food and Agriculture Organization](/wiki/Food_and_Agriculture_Organization) of the [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) considers that people throughout the world may have to eat insects as a food staple. Insects are noted for their nutrients, having a high content of protein, minerals and fats and are already regularly eaten by one-third of the world's population.

### In other products

[Black soldier fly](/wiki/Black_soldier_fly) larvae can provide [protein](/wiki/Protein) and fats for use in [cosmetics](/wiki/Cosmetics). Insect cooking oil, insect butter and [fatty alcohols](/wiki/Fatty_alcohol) [can be made from](/wiki/Biorefinery) such insects as the superworm (*[Zophobas morio](/wiki/Zophobas_morio)*). Insect species including the black soldier fly or the [housefly](/wiki/Housefly) in their [maggot](/wiki/Maggot_farming) forms, and beetle larvae such as [mealworms](/wiki/Mealworms), can be processed and [used as feed](/wiki/Insects_as_feed) for farmed animals including chicken, fish and pigs. Many species of insects are sold and kept as [pets](/wiki/Pet).

### In religion and folklore

Further information: [Insects in mythology](/wiki/Insects_in_mythology)

[Ancient Egyptian](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) [scarab](/wiki/Scarab_(artifact)) with separate wings, c. 712-342 BC

[Scarab beetles](/wiki/Scarabaeidae) held religious and cultural symbolism in [ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt), [Greece](/wiki/Greece) and some [shamanistic](/wiki/Shamanism) Old World cultures. The ancient [Chinese](/wiki/China) regarded [cicadas](/wiki/Cicada) as symbols of rebirth or immortality. In [Mesopotamian](/wiki/Mesopotamia) literature, the epic poem of [Gilgamesh](/wiki/Gilgamesh) has allusions to [Odonata](/wiki/Odonata) that signify the impossibility of immortality. In the case of the ['San' bush-men](/wiki/San_people) of the [Kalahari](/wiki/Kalahari), it is the [praying mantis](/wiki/Praying_mantis) that holds much cultural significance including creation and [zen](/wiki/Zen)-like patience in waiting.
