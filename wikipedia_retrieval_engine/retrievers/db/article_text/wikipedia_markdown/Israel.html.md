**Israel**, officially the **State of Israel**, is a country in the [Southern Levant](/wiki/Southern_Levant) region of [West Asia](/wiki/West_Asia). It is [bordered](/wiki/Borders_of_Israel) by [Lebanon](/wiki/Lebanon) to the north, [Syria](/wiki/Syria) to the northeast, [Jordan](/wiki/Jordan) to the east, and [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt) to the southwest. Israel [illegally](/wiki/Legality_of_the_Israeli_occupation_of_Palestine) [occupies the West Bank](/wiki/Israeli_occupation_of_the_West_Bank) and the [Gaza Strip](/wiki/Israeli_occupation_of_the_Gaza_Strip) of the [Palestinian territories](/wiki/Palestinian_territories), as well as the Syrian [Golan Heights](/wiki/Golan_Heights). [Israel's western coast](/wiki/Israeli_coastal_plain) lies on the [Mediterranean Sea](/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea), its southern tip reaches the [Red Sea](/wiki/Red_Sea), and to the east is [Earth's lowest point](/wiki/Extremes_on_Earth) near the [Dead Sea](/wiki/Dead_Sea). [Jerusalem](/wiki/Jerusalem) is the government seat and [proclaimed capital](/wiki/Status_of_Jerusalem), while [Tel Aviv](/wiki/Tel_Aviv) is Israel's [largest urban area](/wiki/Gush_Dan) and [economic centre](/wiki/Economy_of_Israel).

The [Land of Israel](/wiki/Land_of_Israel), also called [Palestine](/wiki/Palestine_(region)) or the [Holy Land](/wiki/Holy_Land), was home to the ancient [Canaanites](/wiki/Canaan) and later the kingdoms of [Israel and Judah](/wiki/Ancient_Israel_and_Judah) and [Hasmonean Judea](/wiki/Hasmonean_Judea). Located near [continental crossroads](/wiki/Levantine_corridor), its [demographics shifted](/wiki/Demographic_history_of_Palestine_(region)) under various empires. 19th-century [European antisemitism](/wiki/European_antisemitism) fuelled the [Zionist](/wiki/Zionist) movement for a [Jewish homeland](/wiki/Jewish_homeland), which gained [British](/wiki/British_Empire) support with the 1917 [Balfour Declaration](/wiki/Balfour_Declaration). After [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I), Britain occupied the region and established [Mandatory Palestine](/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine). British rule and [Jewish immigration](/wiki/Aliyah) in the leadup to the [Holocaust](/wiki/The_Holocaust) intensified [Arab-Jewish tensions](/wiki/Intercommunal_conflict_in_Mandatory_Palestine), which escalated into [a civil war](/wiki/1947%E2%80%931948_civil_war_in_Mandatory_Palestine) after the 1947 [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations) (UN) [Partition Plan](/wiki/United_Nations_Partition_Plan_for_Palestine).

Israel [declared independence](/wiki/Israeli_Declaration_of_Independence) as the [British Mandate ended](/wiki/End_of_the_British_Mandate_for_Palestine) on 14 May 1948, followed by an [invasion](/wiki/1948_Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_War) by [Arab states](/wiki/Arab_world). The [1949 armistice](/wiki/1949_Armistice_Agreements) expanded Israel beyond the UN plan, while no new Arab state was created, leaving [Gaza under Egyptian control](/wiki/Occupation_of_the_Gaza_Strip_by_the_United_Arab_Republic) and the [West Bank ruled by Jordan](/wiki/Jordanian_annexation_of_the_West_Bank). Most [Palestinian Arabs](/wiki/Palestinians) [fled or were expelled](/wiki/1948_Palestinian_expulsion_and_flight) during the [Nakba](/wiki/Nakba), while Israeli independence prompted [antisemitism in the Arab world](/wiki/Antisemitism_in_the_Arab_world) and [Jewish exodus therefrom](/wiki/Jewish_exodus_from_the_Muslim_world), mainly to Israel. After the 1967 [Six-Day War](/wiki/Six-Day_War), Israel occupied the West Bank, Gaza and Egyptian [Sinai](/wiki/Israeli_occupation_of_the_Sinai_Peninsula), and annexed [East Jerusalem](/wiki/Israeli_annexation_of_East_Jerusalem) and the Syrian [Golan Heights](/wiki/Golan_Heights_Law). Peace treaties were signed with [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt%E2%80%93Israel_peace_treaty) in 1979 (the Sinai being returned in 1982) and [Jordan](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Jordan_peace_treaty) in 1994. The 1993 [Oslo Accords](/wiki/Oslo_Accords) introduced limited [Palestinian self-rule](/wiki/Palestinian_Authority) in the West Bank and Gaza, while the 2020 [Abraham Accords](/wiki/Abraham_Accords) [normalised](/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_normalization) ties with more Arab states, but the [Israeli–Palestinian conflict](/wiki/Israeli%E2%80%93Palestinian_conflict) persists. Israeli [occupation of the Palestinian territories](/wiki/Israeli-occupied_territories) has drawn international criticism, with experts calling its actions [war crimes](/wiki/Israeli_war_crimes) and crimes against humanity. After the [Hamas](/wiki/Hamas)-led [October 7 attacks](/wiki/October_7_attacks), Israel began committing [genocide against Palestinians in Gaza](/wiki/Gaza_genocide). Israel and its allies, including the [United States](/wiki/United_States), [reject](/wiki/Gaza_genocide_denial) that Israel's actions constitute genocide.

The [Basic Laws of Israel](/wiki/Basic_Laws_of_Israel) establish the [Knesset](/wiki/Knesset) as a [proportionally](/wiki/Proportional_representation) elected [parliament](/wiki/Parliamentary_system). It shapes the [government](/wiki/Cabinet_of_Israel), led by the [prime minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Israel), and elects the largely ceremonial [president](/wiki/President_of_Israel). Israel has one of the Middle East's largest economies, [one of Asia's highest living standards](/wiki/List_of_countries_in_Asia_and_Oceania_by_Human_Development_Index), and globally ranks [25th in nominal GDP](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)) and [14th in nominal GDP per capita](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)_per_capita). One of the world's most [technologically advanced](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_Israel) countries, Israel allocates a larger share of its economy to research and development [than any other state](/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states_by_research_and_development_spending) and is believed to [possess nuclear weapons](/wiki/Israel_and_nuclear_weapons). The only country with a [revived](/wiki/Revival_of_the_Hebrew_language) official language, [Hebrew](/wiki/Modern_Hebrew), the [culture of Israel](/wiki/Culture_of_Israel) combines [Jewish traditions](/wiki/Jewish_culture) with [Arab](/wiki/Arab_culture) influences.

## Etymology

Further information: [Israel (name)](/wiki/Israel_(name)) and [Names of the Levant § Israel and Judea](/wiki/Names_of_the_Levant#Israel_and_Judea)

The names [Land of Israel](/wiki/Land_of_Israel) and Children of Israel have historically been used to refer to the biblical [Kingdom of Israel](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_(united_monarchy)) and the entire [Jewish](/wiki/Jews) people respectively. The [name *Israel*](/wiki/Israel_(name)) (Hebrew: *Yīsrāʾēl*; [Septuagint](/wiki/Septuagint) [Ancient Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language): Ἰσραήλ, *Israēl*, "[El (God)](/wiki/El_(deity)) persists/rules") refers to the patriarch [Jacob](/wiki/Jacob) who, according to the [Hebrew Bible](/wiki/Hebrew_Bible), was given the name after he successfully [wrestled](/wiki/Jacob_wrestling_with_the_angel) with the [Angel of the Lord](/wiki/Angel_of_the_Lord). The earliest known archaeological artefact to mention the word *Israel* as a collective is the [Merneptah Stele](/wiki/Merneptah_Stele) of [ancient Egypt](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) (dated to the late-13th century BCE).

Under the [British Mandate](/wiki/Mandate_for_Palestine) (1920–1948), the entire region was known as *Palestine*. Upon [establishment in 1948](/wiki/Israeli_Declaration_of_Independence), the country formally adopted the name *State of Israel* ([Hebrew](/wiki/Hebrew_language): מְדִינַת יִשְׂרָאֵל, [**Medīnat Yisrā'el**](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/9/93/He-Medinat_Israel.ogg/He-Medinat_Israel.ogg.mp3)[ⓘ](/wiki/File:He-Medinat_Israel.ogg) [[mediˈnat jisʁaˈʔel]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Hebrew); [Arabic](/wiki/Arabic_language): دَوْلَة إِسْرَائِيل, **Dawlat Isrāʼīl**, [[dawlat ʔisraːˈʔiːl]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Arabic)) after other [proposed names](/wiki/Israeli_Declaration_of_Independence#Name) including *[Land of Israel](/wiki/Land_of_Israel)* (*Eretz Israel*), *Ever* (from ancestor [Eber](/wiki/Eber)), *[Zion](/wiki/Zion)*, and *[Judea](/wiki/Judea)*, were considered but rejected. The name *Israel* was suggested by [David Ben-Gurion](/wiki/David_Ben-Gurion) and passed by a vote of 6–3. In the early weeks after establishment, the government chose the term *[Israeli](/wiki/Israelis)* to denote a citizen of the state.

## History

Further information: [History of Israel (1948–present)](/wiki/History_of_Israel_(1948%E2%80%93present))

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of Israeli history](/wiki/Timeline_of_Israeli_history).

### Prehistory

Further information: [Prehistory of the Levant](/wiki/Prehistory_of_the_Levant)

The [Ubeidiya prehistoric site](/wiki/Ubeidiya_prehistoric_site) in northern Israel shows the [presence of archaic humans](/wiki/Early_expansions_of_hominins_out_of_Africa) around 1.5 million years ago. The second-oldest evidence of [anatomically modern humans](/wiki/Anatomically_modern_human) [outside Africa](/wiki/Recent_African_origin_of_modern_humans) is a 200,000-year-old fossil from [Misliya Cave](/wiki/Misliya_Cave) on Mount Carmel. The [Natufian culture](/wiki/Natufian_culture) (c. 10,000 BCE) may be linked to the [Proto-Afroasiatic language](/wiki/Proto-Afroasiatic_language) and is notable for adopting [sedentism](/wiki/Sedentism) before the [advent of agriculture](/wiki/Origins_of_agriculture_in_West_Asia) and the [Neolithic Revolution](/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution).

### Bronze and Iron Ages

Further information: [Ancient Israel and Judah](/wiki/Ancient_Israel_and_Judah)

[Tel Megiddo](/wiki/Tel_Megiddo), the ruins of a [Canaanite](/wiki/Canaan) and later [Israelite](/wiki/Israelites) city

Early references to "[Canaan](/wiki/Canaan)" and "Canaanites" appear in ancient [Near Eastern](/wiki/Ancient_Near_East) and [Egyptian](/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) texts (c. 2000 BCE); these populations were structured as politically independent [city-states](/wiki/City-state). During the [Late Bronze Age](/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age) (1550–1200 BCE), large parts of Canaan formed [vassal states](/wiki/Vassal_state) of the [New Kingdom of Egypt](/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt). As a result of the [Late Bronze Age collapse](/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse), Canaan fell into chaos, and Egyptian control over the region collapsed. Ancestors of the [Israelites](/wiki/Israelites) are thought to have included [ancient Semitic-speaking peoples](/wiki/Ancient_Semitic-speaking_peoples) native to this area. Modern archaeological accounts suggest that the Israelites and their culture branched out of the Canaanite peoples through the development of a distinct [monolatristic](/wiki/Monolatry)—and later [monotheistic](/wiki/Monotheism)—religion centered on [Yahweh](/wiki/Yahweh). They spoke an archaic form of [Hebrew](/wiki/Hebrew_language), known as [Biblical Hebrew](/wiki/Biblical_Hebrew). Around the same time, the [Philistines](/wiki/Philistines) settled on the southern [coastal plain](/wiki/Israeli_coastal_plain).

The [Yavne-Yam ostracon](/wiki/Yavne-Yam_ostracon), a [Paleo-Hebrew](/wiki/Paleo-Hebrew_alphabet) inscription documenting administration in [Judah](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah)

Most modern scholars agree that the [Exodus](/wiki/The_Exodus) narrative in the [Torah](/wiki/Torah) and [Old Testament](/wiki/Old_Testament) did not take place as depicted; however, some elements of these traditions do have [historical roots](/wiki/Historicity_of_the_Bible). There is debate about the earliest existence of the [Kingdoms of Israel and Judah](/wiki/History_of_ancient_Israel_and_Judah) and their extent and power. While it is unclear if there was a [United Kingdom of Israel](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_(united_monarchy)), historians and archaeologists agree that the northern [Kingdom of Israel](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_(Samaria)) existed by ca. 900 BCE and the [Kingdom of Judah](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah) by ca. 850 BCE. The Kingdom of Israel was the more prosperous of the two and soon developed into a regional power, with a capital at [Samaria](/wiki/Samaria_(ancient_city)); during the [Omride dynasty](/wiki/Omrides), it controlled [Samaria](/wiki/Samaria), [Galilee](/wiki/Galilee), the upper [Jordan Valley](/wiki/Jordan_Valley), the [plain of Sharon](/wiki/Sharon_plain) and large parts of [Transjordan](/wiki/Transjordan_(region)). The Kingdom of Israel [was conquered](/wiki/Samerina) around 720 BCE by the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire). The Kingdom of Judah, under [Davidic](/wiki/Davidic_line) rule with its capital in [Jerusalem](/wiki/Jerusalem), later became a [client state](/wiki/Client_state) of first the Neo-Assyrian Empire and then the [Neo-Babylonian Empire](/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire). It is estimated that [the region's population](/wiki/Demographic_history_of_Palestine_(region)) was around 400,000 in the [Iron Age II](/wiki/Iron_Age_II). In 587/6 BCE, following a [revolt in Judah](/wiki/Judah%27s_revolts_against_Babylon), King [Nebuchadnezzar II](/wiki/Nebuchadnezzar_II) [besieged and destroyed Jerusalem](/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(587_BC)) and [Solomon's Temple](/wiki/Solomon%27s_Temple), dissolved the kingdom and [exiled much of the Judean elite to Babylon](/wiki/Babylonian_captivity).

### Classical antiquity

Further information: [Second Temple period](/wiki/Second_Temple_period)

[Hasmonean coin](/wiki/Hasmonean_coinage) of [Antigonus II Mattathias](/wiki/Antigonus_II_Mattathias), depicting the [Temple menorah](/wiki/Temple_menorah)

After [capturing Babylon](/wiki/Fall_of_Babylon) in 539 BCE, [Cyrus the Great](/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great), founder of the [Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire), issued [a proclamation](/wiki/Edict_of_Cyrus) allowing the exiled Judean population to return. The construction of the [Second Temple](/wiki/Second_Temple) was completed c. 520 BCE. The Achaemenids ruled the region as the province of [Yehud Medinata](/wiki/Yehud_Medinata). In 332 BCE, [Alexander the Great](/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) conquered the region as part of his [campaign against the Achaemenid Empire](/wiki/Wars_of_Alexander_the_Great). After his death, the area [was controlled](/wiki/Hellenistic_Palestine) by the [Ptolemaic](/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom) and [Seleucid](/wiki/Seleucid_Empire) empires as a part of [Coele-Syria](/wiki/Coele-Syria). Under the Hellenistic kingdoms, ongoing [Hellenisation](/wiki/Hellenization) generated cultural tensions among the Jewish population that culminated under [Antiochus IV](/wiki/Antiochus_IV_Epiphanes), whose decrees outlawed Jewish practices and triggered the [Maccabean Revolt](/wiki/Maccabean_Revolt) in 167 BCE. The revolt weakened Seleucid control over Judea; by 142/141 BCE the [Hasmoneans](/wiki/Hasmonean_dynasty) had secured autonomy and soon established an [independent Jewish kingdom](/wiki/Hasmonean_Judea) that, in the late 2nd–early 1st century BCE, expanded into neighbouring territories. The [Hasmonean civil war](/wiki/Hasmonean_civil_war) ended with the [Roman](/wiki/Roman_Republic) [siege of Jerusalem](/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(63_BC)) in 63 BCE.

View of the [Masada](/wiki/Masada) fortress overlooking the [Dead Sea](/wiki/Dead_Sea), which is the location of a [1st-century Roman siege](/wiki/Siege_of_Masada)

In 37 BCE, [Herod the Great](/wiki/Herod_the_Great) was installed as a [dynastic vassal](/wiki/Herodian_dynasty) of [Rome](/wiki/Ancient_Rome) following the [Roman–Parthian Wars](/wiki/Roman%E2%80%93Parthian_Wars). In 6 CE, the area was annexed as the [Roman province of Judaea](/wiki/Judaea_(Roman_province)); tensions with Roman rule led to a series of [Jewish–Roman wars](/wiki/Jewish%E2%80%93Roman_wars), resulting in widespread destruction. The [First Jewish–Roman War](/wiki/First_Jewish%E2%80%93Roman_War) (66–73 CE) resulted in the [destruction of Jerusalem and the Second Temple](/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(70_CE)) and a sizable portion of the population being killed or displaced. A second uprising known as the [Bar Kokhba revolt](/wiki/Bar_Kokhba_revolt) (132–136 CE) initially allowed the Jews to form an independent state, but the Romans brutally crushed the rebellion, devastating and depopulating Judea's countryside. Jerusalem was rebuilt as a [Roman colony](/wiki/Colonia_(Roman)) ([Aelia Capitolina](/wiki/Aelia_Capitolina)), and the province of Judea was renamed [Syria Palaestina](/wiki/Syria_Palaestina). Jews were expelled from the districts surrounding Jerusalem. Nevertheless, there was a continuous small Jewish presence, and Galilee became its religious center.

### Late antiquity and the medieval period

Further information: [Byzantine Palestine](/wiki/Byzantine_Palestine), [Bilad al-Sham](/wiki/Bilad_al-Sham), and [Kingdom of Jerusalem](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jerusalem)

3rd-century [Kfar Bar'am synagogue](/wiki/Kfar_Bar%27am_synagogue) in the Galilee

During the [Byzantine period](/wiki/Byzantine_Palestine), [Early Christianity](/wiki/Early_Christianity) displaced [Roman paganism](/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Rome) in the 4th century CE, with [Constantine](/wiki/Constantine_the_Great) embracing and promoting the Christian religion and [Theodosius I](/wiki/Theodosius_I) making it [the state religion](/wiki/Christianity_as_the_Roman_state_religion). A series of laws were passed that discriminated against Jews and Judaism, and Jews were persecuted by both the church and the authorities. Many Jews had emigrated to flourishing [diaspora](/wiki/Jewish_diaspora) communities, while locally there was both Christian immigration and local conversion. By the middle of the 5th century, there was a Christian majority. Towards the end of the 5th century, [Samaritan revolts](/wiki/Samaritan_revolts) erupted, continuing until the late 6th century and resulting in a large decrease in the Samaritan population. After the [Sasanian conquest of Jerusalem](/wiki/Sasanian_conquest_of_Jerusalem) and the short-lived [Jewish revolt against Heraclius](/wiki/Jewish_revolt_against_Heraclius) in 614 CE, the Byzantine Empire [reconsolidated control of the area](/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_602%E2%80%93628) in 628.

In 634–641 CE, the [Rashidun Caliphate](/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate) [conquered the Levant](/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_the_Levant). Caliph [Umar ibn al-Khattab](/wiki/Umar_ibn_al-Khattab) (r. 634–644) lifted the Christian ban on Jews entering [Jerusalem](/wiki/Jerusalem) and permitted them to worship there. Over the next six centuries, control of the region transferred between the [Umayyad](/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate), [Abbasid](/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate), and [Fatimid](/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate) caliphates, and subsequently the [Seljuk](/wiki/Seljuk_dynasty) and [Ayyubid](/wiki/Ayyubid_dynasty) dynasties. The population drastically decreased during the following several centuries, dropping from an estimated 1 million during Roman and Byzantine periods to about 300,000 by the early [Ottoman period](/wiki/Ottoman_Empire), and there was steady [Arabisation](/wiki/Arabization) and [Islamisation](/wiki/Islamization). The end of the 11th century brought the [Crusades](/wiki/Crusades), [papally](/wiki/Pope)-sanctioned incursions of Christian crusaders intent on wresting Jerusalem and the [Holy Land](/wiki/Holy_Land) from Muslim control and establishing [crusader states](/wiki/Crusader_states). The Ayyubids pushed back the crusaders before Muslim rule was fully restored by the [Mamluk sultans of Egypt](/wiki/Mamluk_Sultanate_(Cairo)) in 1291.

### Modern period and the emergence of Zionism

Main articles: [Jerusalem Sanjak](/wiki/Jerusalem_Sanjak) and [Mutasarrifate of Jerusalem](/wiki/Mutasarrifate_of_Jerusalem)

Further information: [Old Yishuv](/wiki/Old_Yishuv) and [Zionism](/wiki/Zionism)

Jews at the [Western Wall](/wiki/Western_Wall) in the 1870s, a holy site in [Judaism](/wiki/Judaism)

In 1516, the Ottoman Empire conquered the region and ruled it as part of [Ottoman Syria](/wiki/Ottoman_Syria). Two violent incidents took place against Jews, the [1517 Safed attacks](/wiki/1517_Safed_attacks) and the [1517 Hebron attacks](/wiki/1517_Hebron_attacks), after the Turkish Ottomans ousted the [Mamluks](/wiki/Mamluk) during the [Ottoman–Mamluk War](/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Mamluk_War_(1516%E2%80%931517)). Under the Ottoman Empire, the Levant was fairly cosmopolitan, with religious freedoms for [Christians, Muslims, and Jews](/wiki/People_of_the_Book). In 1561 the [Ottoman sultan](/wiki/Suleiman_the_Magnificent) invited [Sephardic Jews](/wiki/Sephardic_Jews) escaping the [Spanish Inquisition](/wiki/Spanish_Inquisition) to settle in and rebuild the city of [Tiberias](/wiki/Tiberias).

Under the Ottoman Empire's [millet system](/wiki/Millet_(Ottoman_Empire)), Christians and Jews were considered *[dhimmi](/wiki/Dhimmi)* ("protected") under [Ottoman law](/wiki/Ottoman_law) in exchange for loyalty to the state and payment of the *[jizya](/wiki/Jizya)* tax. Non-Muslim Ottoman subjects faced geographic and lifestyle restrictions, though these were not always enforced. The millet system organised non-Muslims into autonomous communities on the basis of religion.

The [First Zionist Congress](/wiki/First_Zionist_Congress) (1897) in [Basel](/wiki/Basel), Switzerland

The [concept of an eventual return to Zion](/wiki/Return_to_Zion) remained a symbol within religious Jewish belief which emphasised that their return should be determined by [Divine Providence](/wiki/Divine_providence_in_Judaism) rather than human action. The Jewish population of Palestine from the Ottoman rule to the beginning of the Zionist movement, known as the [Old Yishuv](/wiki/Old_Yishuv), comprised a minority and fluctuated in size. During the 16th century, Jewish communities struck roots in the [Four Holy Cities](/wiki/Four_Holy_Cities)—Jerusalem, Tiberias, [Hebron](/wiki/Hebron), and [Safed](/wiki/Safed)—and in 1697, Rabbi Yehuda Hachasid led 1,500 Jews to Jerusalem. A 1660 [Druze revolt](/wiki/Druze_power_struggle_(1658%E2%80%931667)#Lebanon_and_Galilee_campaign) against the Ottomans destroyed [Safed](/wiki/1660_destruction_of_Safed) and [Tiberias](/wiki/1660_destruction_of_Tiberias). In the second half of the 18th century, Eastern European Jews who were [opponents](/wiki/Misnagdim) of [Hasidism](/wiki/Hasidic_Judaism), known as the [Perushim](/wiki/Perushim), settled in Palestine.

In the late 18th century, local Arab Sheikh [Daher al-Umar](/wiki/Daher_al-Umar) created a de facto independent emirate in the Galilee. Ottoman attempts to subdue the sheikh failed. After Daher's death the Ottomans regained control of the area. In 1799, governor [Jazzar Pasha](/wiki/Jazzar_Pasha) repelled an [assault on Acre](/wiki/Siege_of_Acre_(1799)) by [Napoleon](/wiki/Napoleon)'s troops, prompting the French to abandon the Syrian campaign. In 1834, a [revolt by Palestinian Arab peasants](/wiki/Peasants%27_revolt_in_Palestine) against Egyptian conscription and taxation policies under [Muhammad Ali](/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt) was suppressed; Muhammad Ali's army retreated and Ottoman rule was restored with British support in 1840. The [Tanzimat](/wiki/Tanzimat) reforms were implemented across the Ottoman Empire.

The first wave of modern Jewish migration to [Ottoman-ruled Palestine](/wiki/Southern_Syria), known as the [First Aliyah](/wiki/First_Aliyah), began in 1881, as Jews fled [pogroms](/wiki/Pogrom) in Eastern Europe. The 1882 [May Laws](/wiki/May_Laws) increased economic discrimination against Jews, and restricted where they could live. In response, political [Zionism](/wiki/Zionism) took form, a movement that sought to establish a [Jewish state](/wiki/Jewish_state) in Palestine, thus offering a solution to the [Jewish question](/wiki/Jewish_question) of the European states. Antisemitism, pogroms and official policies in tsarist Russia led to the emigration of three million Jews in the years between 1882 and 1914, only 1% of whom went to Palestine. Those who went to Palestine were driven primarily by ideas of self-determination and Jewish identity, rather than as a response to pogroms or economic insecurity.

The [Second Aliyah](/wiki/Second_Aliyah) (1904–1914) began after the [Kishinev pogrom](/wiki/Kishinev_pogrom); some 40,000 Jews settled in Palestine, although nearly half left eventually. Both the first and second waves of migrants were mainly [Orthodox Jews](/wiki/Orthodox_Judaism). The Second Aliyah included [Zionist socialist](/wiki/Labor_Zionism) groups who established the *[kibbutz](/wiki/Kibbutz)* movement based on the idea of establishing a separate Jewish economy based exclusively on Jewish labour. Those of the Second Aliyah who became leaders of the [Yishuv](/wiki/Yishuv) in the coming decades believed that the Jewish settler economy should not depend on Arab labour. This would be a dominant source of antagonism with the Arab population, with the new Yishuv's nationalist ideology overpowering its socialist one. Though the immigrants of the Second Aliyah largely sought to create communal Jewish agricultural settlements, [Tel Aviv](/wiki/Tel_Aviv) was established as the first planned Jewish town in 1909. Jewish armed militias emerged during this period, the first being [Bar-Giora](/wiki/Bar-Giora_(organization)) in 1907. Two years later, the larger [Hashomer](/wiki/Hashomer) organisation was founded as its replacement.

### British Mandate for Palestine

Main article: [Mandatory Palestine](/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine)

Further information: [Yishuv](/wiki/Yishuv), [Intercommunal conflict in Mandatory Palestine](/wiki/Intercommunal_conflict_in_Mandatory_Palestine), and [1948 Palestine war](/wiki/1948_Palestine_war)

See also: [Jewish land purchase in Palestine](/wiki/Jewish_land_purchase_in_Palestine)

[Chaim Weizmann](/wiki/Chaim_Weizmann)'s efforts to garner British support for the Zionist movement eventually secured the [Balfour Declaration](/wiki/Balfour_Declaration) of 1917, stating Britain's support for the creation of a Jewish "[national home](/wiki/Homeland_for_the_Jewish_people)" in Palestine. Weizmann's interpretation of the declaration was that negotiations on the future of the country were to happen directly between Britain and the Jews, excluding Arabs. [Jewish-Arab relations](/wiki/Islamic%E2%80%93Jewish_relations) in Palestine deteriorated dramatically in the following years.

Tel Aviv in the 1930s

In 1918, the [Jewish Legion](/wiki/Jewish_Legion), primarily Zionist volunteers, assisted in the British [conquest of Palestine](/wiki/Sinai_and_Palestine_campaign). In 1920, the territory was divided between Britain and [France](/wiki/France) under the [mandate system](/wiki/League_of_Nations_mandate), and the British-administered area (including modern Israel) was named [Mandatory Palestine](/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine). Arab opposition to British rule and Jewish immigration led to the [1920 Palestine riots](/wiki/1920_Nebi_Musa_riots) and the formation of a Jewish militia known as the [Haganah](/wiki/Haganah) as an outgrowth of Hashomer, from which the [Irgun](/wiki/Irgun) and [Lehi](/wiki/Lehi_(group)) paramilitaries later split. In 1922, the [League of Nations](/wiki/League_of_Nations) granted Britain the [Mandate for Palestine](/wiki/Mandate_for_Palestine) under terms which included the Balfour Declaration with its promise to the Jews and with similar provisions regarding the Arab Palestinians. The [population of the area](/wiki/Demographic_history_of_Palestine_(region)) was predominantly Arab and Muslim, with Jews accounting for about 11% and Arab Christians about 9.5% of the population.

The [Third](/wiki/Third_Aliyah) (1919–1923) and [Fourth Aliyahs](/wiki/Fourth_Aliyah) (1924–1929) brought an additional 100,000 Jews to Palestine. The [rise of Nazism](/wiki/Adolf_Hitler%27s_rise_to_power) and the increasing persecution of Jews in 1930s Europe led to the [Fifth Aliyah](/wiki/Fifth_Aliyah), with an influx of a quarter of a million Jews. This was a major cause of the [Arab revolt of 1936–39](/wiki/1936%E2%80%931939_Arab_revolt_in_Palestine), which was suppressed by British security forces and Zionist militias. Several hundred British security personnel and Jews were killed; 5,032 Arabs were killed, 14,760 wounded, and 12,622 detained. An estimated ten per cent of the adult male [Palestinian Arab](/wiki/Palestinian_people) population was killed, wounded, imprisoned, or exiled.

"Jews and Arabs in Grim Struggle for Holy Land", article from 1938

The British introduced restrictions on Jewish immigration to Palestine with the [White Paper of 1939](/wiki/White_Paper_of_1939). With countries around the world turning away Jewish refugees fleeing [the Holocaust](/wiki/The_Holocaust), a clandestine movement known as [Aliyah Bet](/wiki/Aliyah_Bet) was organised to bring Jews to Palestine. During [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), Palestine was [repeatedly bombed](/wiki/Italian_bombing_of_Mandatory_Palestine_in_World_War_II) by Axis aircraft, causing casualties among both Jews and Arabs. About 30,000 Jews from Palestine served in the British military during the war, of whom around 700 were killed. Some 12,000 Palestinian Arabs also served in the British military during the war. At the end of the war, about 31% of the population of Palestine was Jewish. The UK found itself facing a [Jewish insurgency](/wiki/Jewish_insurgency_in_Mandatory_Palestine) over immigration restrictions from 1944, which intensified following the end of the war, and continued conflict with the Arab community over limit levels. The Haganah joined Irgun and Lehi in an armed struggle against British rule. The Haganah attempted to bring tens of thousands of Jewish refugees and [Holocaust survivors](/wiki/Holocaust_survivors) to Palestine by ship. Most of the ships were intercepted by the [Royal Navy](/wiki/Royal_Navy) and the refugees placed in detention camps in [Atlit](/wiki/Atlit_detainee_camp) and [Cyprus](/wiki/Cyprus_internment_camps).

On 22 July 1946, Irgun [bombed the British administrative headquarters](/wiki/King_David_Hotel_bombing) for Palestine, killing 91. The attack was a response to [Operation Agatha](/wiki/Operation_Agatha) (a series of raids, including one on the [Jewish Agency](/wiki/Jewish_Agency_for_Israel), by the British) and was the deadliest directed at the British during the Mandate era. The Jewish insurgency continued throughout 1946 and 1947 despite concerted efforts by the British military and [Palestine Police Force](/wiki/Palestine_Police_Force) to suppress it.

British efforts to mediate with Jewish and Arab representatives were not successful as the Jews were unwilling to accept any solution that did not involve a Jewish state and suggested a partition of Palestine into Jewish and Arab states, while the Arabs were adamant that a Jewish state in any part of Palestine was unacceptable and that the only solution was a unified Palestine under Arab rule. In February 1947, the British referred the Palestine issue to the newly formed [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations). On 15 May 1947, the [UN General Assembly](/wiki/General_Assembly_of_the_United_Nations) resolved that a [Special Committee](/wiki/United_Nations_Special_Committee_on_Palestine) be created "to prepare ... a report on the question of Palestine". The Report of the Committee [proposed a plan](/wiki/United_Nations_Partition_Plan_for_Palestine) to replace the British Mandate with "an independent Arab State, an independent Jewish State, and the City of Jerusalem [...] the last to be under an International Trusteeship System".

Meanwhile, the Jewish insurgency continued and peaked in July 1947, with a series of widespread guerrilla raids culminating in [the Sergeants affair](/wiki/The_Sergeants_affair), in which the Irgun took two British sergeants hostage as attempted leverage against the planned execution of three Irgun operatives. After the executions were carried out, the Irgun killed the two British soldiers, hanged their bodies from trees, and left a booby trap at the scene which injured a British soldier. The incident caused widespread outrage in the UK. In September 1947, the British cabinet decided to evacuate Palestine as the Mandate was no longer tenable.

[1947 partition plan](/wiki/United_Nations_Partition_Plan_for_Palestine) of Palestine between Israel and the [State of Palestine](/wiki/State_of_Palestine), as proposed by the [United Nations](/wiki/United_Nations)

On 29 November 1947, the General Assembly adopted [Resolution 181 (II)](/wiki/United_Nations_Partition_Plan_for_Palestine). The plan attached to the resolution was essentially that proposed in the report of 3 September. The Jewish Agency, the recognised representative of the Jewish community, accepted the plan, which assigned 55–56% of Mandatory Palestine to the Jews. At the time, the Jews were about a third of the population and owned around 6–7% of the land. Arabs constituted the majority and owned about 20% of the land, with the remainder held by the Mandate authorities or foreign landowners.

The [Arab League](/wiki/Arab_League) and [Arab Higher Committee](/wiki/Arab_Higher_Committee) of Palestine rejected the partition plan on the basis that the partition plan privileged European interests over those of the Palestinians, and indicated that they would reject any other plan of partition. On 1 December 1947, the Arab Higher Committee proclaimed a three-day strike, and [riots broke out in Jerusalem](/wiki/1947_Jerusalem_riots). The situation spiralled into [a civil war](/wiki/1947%E2%80%931948_civil_war_in_Mandatory_Palestine). Colonial Secretary [Arthur Creech Jones](/wiki/Arthur_Creech_Jones) announced that the British Mandate would end on 15 May 1948, at which point the British would evacuate. As Arab militias and gangs attacked Jewish areas, they were faced mainly by the Haganah as well as the smaller Irgun and Lehi. In April 1948, the Haganah moved onto the offensive.

### Independence and early years

Further information: [Israeli Declaration of Independence](/wiki/Israeli_Declaration_of_Independence)

[David Ben-Gurion](/wiki/David_Ben-Gurion) declaring the [establishment of Israel](/wiki/Israeli_Declaration_of_Independence) on 14 May 1948

On 14 May 1948, the day before the expiration of the British Mandate, [David Ben-Gurion](/wiki/David_Ben-Gurion), the head of the Jewish Agency, [declared "the establishment of a Jewish state in Eretz-Israel"](/wiki/Israeli_Declaration_of_Independence). The following day, the armies of four Arab countries—Egypt, Syria, Transjordan, and Iraq—entered what had been Mandatory Palestine, launching the [1948 Arab–Israeli War](/wiki/1948_Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_War); contingents from [Yemen](/wiki/Mutawakkilite_Kingdom_of_Yemen), [Morocco](/wiki/Morocco), [Saudi Arabia](/wiki/Saudi_Arabia), and [Sudan](/wiki/Sudan) joined the war. The purpose of the invasion was to prevent the establishment of the Jewish state. The Arab League stated the invasion was to restore order and prevent further bloodshed.

Raising of the [Ink Flag](/wiki/Ink_Flag) on 10 March 1949, marking the end of the [1948 war](/wiki/1948_Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_War)

After a year of fighting, a [ceasefire was declared](/wiki/1949_Armistice_Agreements) and temporary borders, known as the [Green Line](/wiki/Green_Line_(Israel)), were established. [Jordan annexed](/wiki/Jordanian_annexation_of_the_West_Bank) what became known as the [West Bank](/wiki/West_Bank), including [East Jerusalem](/wiki/East_Jerusalem), and [Egypt occupied](/wiki/Occupation_of_the_Gaza_Strip_by_the_United_Arab_Republic) the [Gaza Strip](/wiki/Gaza_Strip). Over 700,000 Palestinians [fled or were expelled](/wiki/1948_Palestinian_expulsion_and_flight) by [Zionist militias](/wiki/Zionist_political_violence) and the [Israeli military](/wiki/Israel_Defence_Forces)—what would become known in Arabic as the *[Nakba](/wiki/Nakba)* ('catastrophe'). The events also led to the destruction of most of Palestine's Arab [culture](/wiki/Culture_of_Palestine), [identity](/wiki/Palestinian_identity), and [national aspirations](/wiki/Palestinian_nationalism). Some 156,000 Arabs remained and became [Arab citizens of Israel](/wiki/Arab_citizens_of_Israel).

By [United Nations General Assembly Resolution 273](/wiki/United_Nations_General_Assembly_Resolution_273), Israel was admitted as a member of the UN on 11 May 1949. In the early years of the state, the [Labour Zionist](/wiki/Labor_Zionism) movement led by Prime Minister Ben-Gurion dominated [Israeli politics](/wiki/Politics_of_Israel). Immigration to Israel during the late 1940s and early 1950s was aided by the Israeli Immigration Department and the non-government sponsored [Mossad LeAliyah Bet](/wiki/Mossad_LeAliyah_Bet) (lit. "Institute for [Immigration B](/wiki/Aliyah_Bet)"). The latter engaged in clandestine operations in countries, particularly in the Middle East and Eastern Europe, where the lives of Jews were in danger and exit was difficult. Mossad LeAliyah Bet was disbanded in 1953. The immigration was in accordance with the [One Million Plan](/wiki/One_Million_Plan). Some immigrants held Zionist beliefs or came for the promise of a better life, while others moved to escape persecution or were expelled from their homes.

An [influx of Holocaust survivors](/wiki/Aliyah#Early_statehood_(1948–1960)) and [Jews from Arab and Muslim countries](/wiki/Jewish_exodus_from_the_Muslim_world) to Israel during the first three years increased the number of Jews from 700,000 to 1,400,000. By 1958, the population had risen to two million. Between 1948 and 1970, approximately 1,150,000 Jewish refugees relocated to Israel. Some immigrants arrived as refugees and were housed in temporary camps known as *[ma'abarot](/wiki/Ma%27abarot)*; by 1952, over 200,000 people were living in these tent cities. [Jews of European background](/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews) were often treated more favourably than Jews from [Middle Eastern](/wiki/Mizrahi_Jews) and [North African](/wiki/Sephardi_Jews) countries—housing units reserved for the latter were often re-designated for the former, so Jews newly arrived from Arab lands generally ended up staying longer in transit camps. During this period, food, clothes and furniture were rationed in what became known as the [austerity period](/wiki/Austerity_in_Israel). The need to solve the crisis led Ben-Gurion to sign a [reparations agreement with West Germany](/wiki/Reparations_Agreement_between_Israel_and_the_Federal_Republic_of_Germany) that triggered mass protests by Jews angered at the idea that Israel could accept monetary compensation for the Holocaust.

### Arab–Israeli conflict

Main article: [Arab–Israeli conflict](/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_conflict)

There were [further expulsions of Palestinians](/wiki/1949%E2%80%931956_Palestinian_expulsions) after the establishment of Israel. During the 1950s, Israel was [frequently attacked](/wiki/List_of_attacks_against_Israeli_civilians_before_1967) by [Palestinian fedayeen](/wiki/Palestinian_fedayeen), nearly always against civilians, mainly from the Egyptian-occupied Gaza Strip, leading to several Israeli [reprisal operations](/wiki/Reprisal_operations). In 1956, the UK and France aimed at regaining control of the [Suez Canal](/wiki/Suez_Canal), which Egypt had nationalised. The continued blockade of the Suez Canal and [Straits of Tiran](/wiki/Straits_of_Tiran) to Israeli shipping, together with increasing fedayeen attacks against Israel's southern population and recent Arab threatening statements, prompted Israel to attack Egypt. Israel joined [a secret alliance](/wiki/Protocol_of_S%C3%A8vres) with the UK and France and overran the [Sinai Peninsula](/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula) in the [Suez Crisis](/wiki/Suez_Crisis) but was pressured to withdraw by the UN in return for guarantees of Israeli shipping rights. The war resulted in significant reduction of Israeli border infiltration.

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/34/1961-04-13_Tale_Of_Century_-_Eichmann_Tried_For_War_Crimes.ogv/250px--1961-04-13_Tale_Of_Century_-_Eichmann_Tried_For_War_Crimes.ogv.jpg)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/34/1961-04-13_Tale_Of_Century_-_Eichmann_Tried_For_War_Crimes.ogv)

U.S. newsreel on the trial of [Adolf Eichmann](/wiki/Adolf_Eichmann)

In the early 1960s, Israel captured Nazi war criminal [Adolf Eichmann](/wiki/Adolf_Eichmann) in Argentina and brought him to Israel [for trial](/wiki/Eichmann_trial). Eichmann remains the only person executed in Israel by conviction in an Israeli civilian court. In 1963, Israel was engaged in a diplomatic standoff with the United States in relation to the Israeli [nuclear programme](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_and_Israel).

Since 1964 Arab countries, concerned over Israeli plans to divert waters of the [Jordan River](/wiki/Jordan_River) into the [coastal plain](/wiki/Israeli_coastal_plain), had been trying to divert the headwaters to deprive Israel of water resources, [provoking tensions](/wiki/War_over_Water_(Jordan_River)) between Israel on the one hand, and Syria and Lebanon on the other. [Arab nationalists](/wiki/Arab_nationalist) led by Egyptian President [Gamal Abdel Nasser](/wiki/Gamal_Abdel_Nasser) refused to recognise Israel and called for its destruction. By 1966, Israeli-Arab relations had deteriorated to the point of battles taking place between Israeli and Arab forces.

In May 1967, Egypt massed its army near the border with Israel, expelled [UN peacekeepers stationed in the Sinai Peninsula](/wiki/United_Nations_Emergency_Force) since 1957, and blocked Israel's access to the Red Sea. Other Arab states mobilised their forces. Israel reiterated that these actions were a *[casus belli](/wiki/Casus_belli)* and launched a pre-emptive strike ([Operation Focus](/wiki/Operation_Focus)) against Egypt in June. Jordan, Syria and Iraq attacked Israel.

The [Sinai Peninsula](/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula) was returned to Egypt in 1982.

In the ensuing [Six-Day War](/wiki/Six-Day_War), Israel captured and occupied the West Bank from Jordan, the Gaza Strip and Sinai Peninsula from Egypt, and the Golan Heights from Syria. Israeli forces [expelled ~300,000 Palestinians](/wiki/Naksa) from the Gaza Strip and the West Bank. Jerusalem's boundaries were enlarged, incorporating [East Jerusalem](/wiki/East_Jerusalem). The 1949 Green Line became the administrative boundary between Israel and the [occupied territories](/wiki/Israeli-occupied_territories).

Following the 1967 war and the "[Three Nos](/wiki/Khartoum_Resolution)" resolution of the Arab League, Israel faced attacks from the Egyptians in the Sinai Peninsula during the 1967–1970 [War of Attrition](/wiki/War_of_Attrition), and from Palestinian groups targeting Israelis in the occupied territories, globally, and in Israel. Most important among the Palestinian and Arab groups was the [Palestine Liberation Organisation](/wiki/Palestine_Liberation_Organisation) (PLO), established in 1964, which initially committed itself to "armed struggle as the only way to liberate the homeland". In the late 1960s and early 1970s, [Palestinian groups launched attacks](/wiki/Palestinian_political_violence) against Israeli and Jewish targets around the world, including [a massacre of Israeli athletes](/wiki/Munich_massacre) at the [1972 Summer Olympics](/wiki/1972_Summer_Olympics) in Munich. The Israeli government responded with an [assassination campaign](/wiki/Mossad_assassinations_following_the_Munich_massacre) against the organisers of the massacre, [a bombing](/wiki/1972_Israeli_air_raid_in_Syria_and_Lebanon) and a [raid on the PLO headquarters in Lebanon](/wiki/1973_Israeli_raid_in_Lebanon).

On 6 October 1973, the Egyptian and Syrian armies launched [a surprise attack](/wiki/Operation_Badr_(1973)) against Israeli forces in the Sinai Peninsula and Golan Heights, opening the [Yom Kippur War](/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War). The war ended on 25 October with Israel repelling Egyptian and Syrian forces but suffering great losses. An [internal inquiry](/wiki/Agranat_Commission) exonerated [the government](/wiki/Fifteenth_government_of_Israel) of responsibility for failures before and during the war, but public anger forced Prime Minister [Golda Meir](/wiki/Golda_Meir) to resign. On 27 June 1976, [Air France Flight 139](/wiki/Air_France_Flight_139) was hijacked in flight from Israel to France by Palestinian guerrillas; Israeli commandos rescued 102 of 106 Israeli hostages days later.

### Peace process

Main article: [Israeli–Palestinian peace process](/wiki/Israeli%E2%80%93Palestinian_peace_process)

The [1977 Knesset elections](/wiki/1977_Israeli_legislative_election) marked a major turning point in Israeli political history as [Menachem Begin](/wiki/Menachem_Begin)'s [Likud](/wiki/Likud) party took control from the [Labor Party](/wiki/Israeli_Labor_Party). Later that year, Egyptian President [Anwar El Sadat](/wiki/Anwar_El_Sadat) made a trip to Israel and spoke before the [Knesset](/wiki/Knesset) in what was the first recognition of Israel by an Arab head of state. Sadat and Begin signed the [Camp David Accords](/wiki/Camp_David_Accords) (1978) and the [Egypt–Israel peace treaty](/wiki/Egypt%E2%80%93Israel_peace_treaty) (1979). In return, Israel withdrew from the Sinai Peninsula and agreed to enter negotiations over autonomy for Palestinians in the West Bank and the Gaza Strip.

On 11 March 1978, a PLO guerilla raid from Lebanon led to the [Coastal Road massacre](/wiki/Coastal_Road_massacre). Israel responded by launching an [invasion of southern Lebanon](/wiki/1978_South_Lebanon_conflict) to destroy PLO bases. Begin's government meanwhile provided incentives for [Israelis to settle](/wiki/Israeli_settlement) in the [occupied West Bank](/wiki/Israeli_occupation_of_the_West_Bank), increasing friction with the Palestinians there.

The 1980 [Jerusalem Law](/wiki/Jerusalem_Law) was believed by some to reaffirm Israel's 1967 annexation of Jerusalem by government decree and [reignited international controversy](/wiki/UN_Security_Council_Resolution_478) over the [status of the city](/wiki/Status_of_Jerusalem). No Israeli legislation has defined the territory of Israel, and no act specifically included East Jerusalem therein. In 1981 Israel [effectively annexed](/wiki/Golan_Heights_Law) the Golan Heights. The international community largely rejected these moves, with the UN Security Council declaring both the Jerusalem Law and the Golan Heights Law null and void. Several waves of [Ethiopian Jews](/wiki/Beta_Israel) [immigrated to Israel](/wiki/Aliyah_from_Ethiopia) since the 1980s, while between 1990 and 1994, [immigration from the post-Soviet states](/wiki/1990s_post-Soviet_aliyah) increased Israel's population by twelve per cent.

On 7 June 1981, during the [Iran–Iraq War](/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War), the [Israeli air force destroyed](/wiki/Operation_Opera) Iraq's sole nuclear reactor, then under construction, in order to impede the Iraqi nuclear weapons programme. Following a series of PLO attacks in 1982, Israel [invaded Lebanon](/wiki/1982_Lebanon_War) to destroy the PLO bases. In the first six days, Israel destroyed the military forces of the PLO in Lebanon and decisively defeated the Syrians. An Israeli government inquiry (the [Kahan Commission](/wiki/Kahan_Commission)) held Begin and several Israeli generals indirectly responsible for the [Sabra and Shatila massacre](/wiki/Sabra_and_Shatila_massacre) and held [defence minister](/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_(Israel)) [Ariel Sharon](/wiki/Ariel_Sharon) as bearing "personal responsibility". Sharon was forced to resign.

In 1985, Israel responded to a Palestinian [terrorist attack in Cyprus](/wiki/Larnaca_yacht_killings) by [bombing the PLO headquarters](/wiki/Operation_Wooden_Leg) in Tunisia. Israel withdrew from most of Lebanon in 1986 but continued to [occupy a borderland buffer zone](/wiki/Israeli_occupation_of_Southern_Lebanon) in southern Lebanon until 2000, from where Israeli forces [engaged in conflict](/wiki/South_Lebanon_conflict_(1985%E2%80%932000)) with [Hezbollah](/wiki/Hezbollah). The [First Intifada](/wiki/First_Intifada), a Palestinian uprising against Israeli rule, broke out in 1987, with waves of uncoordinated demonstrations and violence in the occupied West Bank and Gaza. Over the following six years, the intifada became more organised and included economic and cultural measures aimed at disrupting the Israeli occupation. Over 1,000 people were killed. During the 1991 [Gulf War](/wiki/Gulf_War), the PLO supported [Saddam Hussein](/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) and Iraqi missile [attacks against Israel](/wiki/Iraqi_rocket_attacks_on_Israel). Despite public outrage, Israel heeded American calls to refrain from hitting back.

[Shimon Peres](/wiki/Shimon_Peres) (left) with [Yitzhak Rabin](/wiki/Yitzhak_Rabin) (center) and King [Hussein of Jordan](/wiki/Hussein_of_Jordan) (right), prior to signing the [Israel–Jordan peace treaty](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Jordan_peace_treaty) in 1994

In 1992, [Yitzhak Rabin](/wiki/Yitzhak_Rabin) became prime minister following [an election](/wiki/1992_Israeli_legislative_election) in which his party called for compromise with Israel's neighbours. The following year, [Shimon Peres](/wiki/Shimon_Peres) on behalf of Israel and [Yasser Arafat](/wiki/Yasser_Arafat) for the PLO signed the [Oslo Accords](/wiki/Oslo_Accords), which gave the [Palestinian National Authority](/wiki/Palestinian_Authority) (PNA) the right to govern [parts of the West Bank](/wiki/West_Bank_areas_in_the_Oslo_II_Accord) and the Gaza Strip. The PLO also recognised Israel's right to exist and pledged an end to terrorism. In 1994, the [Israel–Jordan peace treaty](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Jordan_peace_treaty) was signed, making Jordan the second Arab country to normalise relations with Israel. Arab public support for the Accords was damaged by the continuation of Israeli settlements and [checkpoints](/wiki/Israeli_checkpoint), and the deterioration of economic conditions. Israeli public support for the Accords waned after [Palestinian suicide attacks](/wiki/List_of_Palestinian_suicide_attacks). In November 1995, Rabin [was assassinated](/wiki/Assassination_of_Yitzhak_Rabin) by [Yigal Amir](/wiki/Yigal_Amir), a far-right Jew who opposed the Accords.

During [Benjamin Netanyahu](/wiki/Benjamin_Netanyahu)'s premiership at the end of the 1990s, Israel [agreed to withdraw](/wiki/Protocol_Concerning_the_Redeployment_in_Hebron) from [Hebron](/wiki/Hebron), though this was never ratified or implemented, and he signed the [Wye River Memorandum](/wiki/Wye_River_Memorandum). The agreement dealt with further redeployments in the West Bank and security issues. The memorandum was criticised by major international human rights organisations for its "encouragement" of human rights abuses.
[Ehud Barak](/wiki/Ehud_Barak), [elected](/wiki/1999_Israeli_general_election) prime minister in 1999, withdrew forces from southern Lebanon and conducted negotiations with PNA Chairman Yasser Arafat and U.S. President [Bill Clinton](/wiki/Bill_Clinton) at the [2000 Camp David Summit](/wiki/2000_Camp_David_Summit). Barak offered a plan for the establishment of a [Palestinian state](/wiki/Palestinian_state), including the entirety of the Gaza Strip and over 90% of the West Bank with Jerusalem as a shared capital. Each side blamed the other for the failure of the talks.

### 21st century

Further information: [Iran–Israel proxy conflict](/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Israel_proxy_conflict), [Gaza–Israel conflict](/wiki/Gaza%E2%80%93Israel_conflict), and [Middle Eastern crisis (2023–present)](/wiki/Middle_Eastern_crisis_(2023%E2%80%93present))

In late 2000, after a controversial visit by Sharon to the [Temple Mount](/wiki/Temple_Mount), the [Second Intifada](/wiki/Second_Intifada) began. The popular uprising faced disproportionate repression from the Israeli state. [Palestinian suicide bombings](/wiki/Palestinian_suicide_attacks) eventually developed into a recurrent feature of the intifada. Some commentators contend that the intifada was pre-planned by Arafat after the collapse of peace talks. Sharon became prime minister in a [2001 election](/wiki/2001_Israeli_prime_ministerial_election); he carried out his plan to [unilaterally withdraw](/wiki/Israeli_disengagement_from_the_Gaza_Strip) from the Gaza Strip and spearheaded the construction of the [West Bank barrier](/wiki/West_Bank_barrier), ending the intifada. Between 2000 and 2008, 1,063 Israelis, 5,517 Palestinians and 64 foreign citizens were killed.

In July 2006, a Hezbollah artillery assault on Israel's northern border communities and a [cross-border abduction](/wiki/2006_Hezbollah_cross-border_raid) of two Israeli soldiers precipitated the month-long [Second Lebanon War](/wiki/2006_Lebanon_War), including an Israeli invasion of Lebanon. The war wound down in August 2006 after the passage of [United Nations Security Council Resolution 1701](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_1701); Israeli forces mostly withdrew from Lebanon by October 2006 but continued to occupy the Lebanese portion of [Ghajar](/wiki/Ghajar) village. In 2007 the Israeli Air Force [destroyed a nuclear reactor](/wiki/Operation_Outside_the_Box) in Syria.

In 2008, [a ceasefire](/wiki/2008_Israel%E2%80%93Hamas_ceasefire) between [Hamas](/wiki/Hamas) and Israel collapsed, resulting in the three-week [Gaza War](/wiki/Gaza_War_(2008%E2%80%932009)). In what Israel described as a response to [over a hundred Palestinian rocket attacks](/wiki/List_of_Palestinian_rocket_attacks_on_Israel_in_2012) on southern Israeli cities, Israel began [an operation in the Gaza Strip](/wiki/2012_Gaza_War) in 2012, lasting eight days. Israel started another [operation in Gaza](/wiki/2014_Gaza_War) following an escalation of rocket attacks by Hamas in July 2014. In May 2021, another [round of fighting](/wiki/2021_Israel%E2%80%93Palestine_crisis) took place in Gaza and Israel, lasting eleven days. By the 2010s, [increasing regional cooperation](/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_alliance) between Israel and [Arab League](/wiki/Arab_League) countries have been established, culminating in the signing of the [Abraham Accords](/wiki/Abraham_Accords). The Israeli security situation shifted from the traditional [Arab–Israeli conflict](/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_conflict) towards the [Iran–Israel proxy conflict](/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Israel_proxy_conflict) and [direct confrontation with Iran during the Syrian civil war](/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Israel_conflict_during_the_Syrian_civil_war).

Satellite view of fires in the [Gaza envelope](/wiki/Gaza_envelope) on 7 October 2023

On 7 October 2023, Palestinian militant groups from [Gaza](/wiki/Gaza_Strip), led by Hamas, launched [a series of coordinated attacks](/wiki/October_7_attacks) on Israel, leading to the start of the [Gaza war](/wiki/Gaza_war). On that day, approximately 1,300 Israelis, predominantly civilians, were killed in communities near the Gaza Strip border and [during a music festival](/wiki/Re%27im_music_festival_massacre). [Over 200 hostages](/wiki/Gaza_war_hostage_crisis) were kidnapped and taken to the Gaza Strip. Studies modeling trauma exposure and assessing mental health outcomes estimated that approximately 5.3% of Israelis may develop [PTSD](/wiki/PTSD), with national data showing that probable PTSD nearly doubled from 16.2% to 29.8% and rates of anxiety and depression also rising sharply.

After clearing militants from its territory, Israel launched [one of the most destructive bombing campaigns in modern history](/wiki/Israeli_bombing_of_the_Gaza_Strip) and [invaded Gaza](/wiki/Israeli_invasion_of_the_Gaza_Strip) on 27 October with the stated objectives of destroying Hamas and freeing hostages. The fifth war of the [Gaza–Israel conflict](/wiki/Gaza%E2%80%93Israel_conflict) since 2008, it has been the deadliest for Palestinians in the entire [Israeli–Palestinian conflict](/wiki/Israeli%E2%80%93Palestinian_conflict) and the most significant military engagement in the region since the [Yom Kippur War](/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War) in 1973. A [United Nations Special Committee](/wiki/Special_Committee_to_Investigate_Israeli_Practices_Affecting_the_Human_Rights_of_the_Palestinian_People), [multiple governments](/wiki/South_Africa%27s_genocide_case_against_Israel), and various experts and human rights organisations have concluded that Israel is committing [genocide against the Palestinian people](/wiki/Gaza_genocide) due to the harm and loss of life inflicted on civilians during the [Gaza War](/wiki/Gaza_war).

In April 2024, Israel initiated a wave of airstrikes on Iran, after Iranian strikes targeted Israel, marking the [2024 Iran–Israel conflict](/wiki/2024_Iran%E2%80%93Israel_conflict) the first time in which the two countries have ever directly exchanged fire. In October 2024, Israel [invaded Lebanon](/wiki/2024_Israeli_invasion_of_Lebanon) and exchanged missile barrages with Iran three weeks later, in response of Iranian strikes earlier in that month. After nearly a year of the [Israel–Hezbollah conflict](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Hezbollah_conflict_(2023%E2%80%93present)) from October 2023 due to Hezbollah shooting rockets at Israel to support Hamas in Gaza, Israel [assassinated](/wiki/2024_Hezbollah_headquarters_strike) Hezbollah secretary general [Hassan Nasrallah](/wiki/Hassan_Nasrallah) in September 2024. A November 2024 [ceasefire agreement](/wiki/2024_Israel%E2%80%93Lebanon_ceasefire_agreement) instructed Israel to withdraw from Lebanon, which Israel mostly did by February 2025, but against the agreement, Israeli forces stayed in five military outposts on highlands in Southern Lebanon. In June 2025, Israel launched a renewed series of airstrikes on Iran, targeting Iran's air defence systems, missile launchers, their military leadership, and their [nuclear programme](/wiki/Nuclear_programme_of_Iran), which escalated into a [full-scale war](/wiki/Twelve-Day_War). In February 2026, Israel and the United States launched extensive airstrikes on Iran, [assassinating](/wiki/Assassination_of_Ali_Khamenei) Iranian Supreme Leader [Ali Khamenei](/wiki/Ali_Khamenei) and sparking the [2026 Iran war](/wiki/2026_Iran_war) and the [2026 Lebanon war](/wiki/2026_Lebanon_war). The strikes aimed to destroy the Iranian military, nuclear programme, and [achieve regime change](/wiki/Regime_change_efforts_in_the_2026_Iran_war).

Israel is located in the [Levant](/wiki/Levant) area of the [Fertile Crescent](/wiki/Fertile_Crescent). At the [eastern end](/wiki/Eastern_Mediterranean) of the [Mediterranean Sea](/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea), it is bounded by Lebanon to the north, Syria to the north-east, Jordan and the West Bank to the east, and Egypt and the Gaza Strip to the south-west. It lies between latitudes [29°](/wiki/29th_parallel_north) and [34° N](/wiki/34th_parallel_north), and longitudes [34°](/wiki/34th_meridian_east) and [36° E](/wiki/36th_meridian_east).

The sovereign territory of Israel (according to the demarcation lines of the [1949 Armistice Agreements](/wiki/1949_Armistice_Agreements) and excluding all territories captured by Israel during the 1967 Six-Day War) is approximately 20,770 square kilometres (8,019 sq mi), of which two per cent is water. However Israel is so narrow (100 km at its widest, compared to 400 km from north to south) that the [exclusive economic zone](/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone) in the Mediterranean is double the land area of the country. The total area under Israeli law, including East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights, is 22,072 square kilometres (8,522 sq mi), and the total area under Israeli control, including the military-controlled and partially Palestinian-governed territory of the West Bank, is 27,799 square kilometres (10,733 sq mi).

Despite its small size, Israel is home to a variety of geographic features, from the [Negev](/wiki/Negev) desert in the south to the inland fertile [Jezreel Valley](/wiki/Jezreel_Valley), with mountain ranges of the [Galilee](/wiki/Galilee), [Carmel](/wiki/Mount_Carmel) and towards the [Golan](/wiki/Golan_Heights) in the north. The [Israeli coastal plain](/wiki/Israeli_coastal_plain) on the shores of the Mediterranean is home to most of the population. East of the central highlands lies the [Jordan Rift Valley](/wiki/Jordan_Rift_Valley), a small part of the 6,500-kilometre (4,039 mi) [Great Rift Valley](/wiki/Great_Rift_Valley). The [Jordan River](/wiki/Jordan_River) runs along the Jordan Rift Valley, from [Mount Hermon](/wiki/Mount_Hermon) through the [Hulah Valley](/wiki/Hulah_Valley) and the [Sea of Galilee](/wiki/Sea_of_Galilee) to the [Dead Sea](/wiki/Dead_Sea), the [lowest point](/wiki/Extreme_points_of_Earth) on the surface of the Earth. Further south is the [Arabah](/wiki/Arabah), ending with the [Gulf of Eilat](/wiki/Gulf_of_Aqaba), part of the [Red Sea](/wiki/Red_Sea). [Makhtesh](/wiki/Makhtesh), or "erosion cirques" are unique to the Negev and the [Sinai Peninsula](/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula), the largest being the [Makhtesh Ramon](/wiki/Makhtesh_Ramon) at 38 km in length. Israel has the largest number of plant species per square metre of the countries in the Mediterranean Basin and contains four terrestrial ecoregions: [Eastern Mediterranean conifer–sclerophyllous–broadleaf forests](/wiki/Eastern_Mediterranean_conifer%E2%80%93sclerophyllous%E2%80%93broadleaf_forests), [Southern Anatolian montane conifer and deciduous forests](/wiki/Southern_Anatolian_montane_conifer_and_deciduous_forests), [Arabian Desert](/wiki/Arabian_Desert), and [Mesopotamian shrub desert](/wiki/Mesopotamian_shrub_desert). Forests accounted for 8.5% of the area in 2016, up from 2% in 1948, as the result of a large-scale forest planting programme by the [Jewish National Fund](/wiki/Jewish_National_Fund).

### Tectonics and seismicity

Further information: [List of earthquakes in the Levant](/wiki/List_of_earthquakes_in_the_Levant)

The Jordan Rift Valley is the result of tectonic movements within the [Dead Sea Transform](/wiki/Dead_Sea_Transform) (DST) fault system. The DST forms the [transform boundary](/wiki/Transform_fault) between the [African Plate](/wiki/African_Plate) to the west and the [Arabian Plate](/wiki/Arabian_Plate) to the east. The Golan Heights and all of Jordan are part of the Arabian Plate, while the Galilee, West Bank, Coastal Plain, and Negev along with the Sinai Peninsula are on the African Plate. This tectonic disposition leads to a relatively high seismic activity. The entire Jordan Valley segment is thought to have ruptured repeatedly, for instance during the last two major earthquakes along this structure in [749](/wiki/749_Galilee_earthquake) and [1033](/wiki/1033_Jordan_Valley_earthquake). The deficit in [slip](/wiki/Fault_(geology)#Slip,_heave,_throw) that has built up since 1033 is sufficient to cause an earthquake of Mw ~7.4.

The most catastrophic known earthquakes occurred in 31 BCE, [363](/wiki/363_Galilee_earthquake), 749, and 1033 CE, that is every ca. 400 years on average. Destructive earthquakes strike about every 80 years, leading to serious loss of life . While stringent construction regulations are in place and recently built structures are earthquake resistant, as of 2007 many public buildings as well as 50,000 residential buildings did not meet the new standards and were "expected to collapse" if exposed to a strong earthquake.

### Climate

Further information: [Climate change in Israel](/wiki/Climate_change_in_Israel)

The projections of the [IPCC Sixth Assessment Report](/wiki/IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report) show clearly the impacts of climate change on Israel even at 2 degrees of warming.

Temperatures vary widely, especially during the winter. Coastal areas, such as those of Tel Aviv and Haifa, have a typical [Mediterranean climate](/wiki/Mediterranean_climate) with cool, rainy winters and long, hot summers. The area of Beersheba and the northern Negev have a [semi-arid climate](/wiki/Semi-arid_climate) with hot summers, cool winters, and fewer rainy days. The southern Negev and the Arabah areas have a [desert climate](/wiki/Desert_climate) with very hot, dry summers, and mild winters with few days of rain. The highest temperature of 54 °C (129 °F) was recorded in 1942 in the [Tirat Zvi](/wiki/Tirat_Zvi) kibbutz. Mountainous regions can be windy and cold, and areas at elevation of 750 metres (2,460 ft) or more (same elevation as Jerusalem) usually receive at least one [snowfall](/wiki/Snow_in_Israel) each year. From May to September, rain is rare.

There are four different [phytogeographic](/wiki/Phytogeography) regions, due to its location between the temperate and tropical zones. For this reason, the flora and fauna are extremely diverse. There are 2,867 known [species of plants in Israel](/wiki/List_of_endemic_flora_of_Israel). Of these, at least 253 species are [introduced and non-native](/wiki/List_of_adventive_wild_plants_in_Israel). There are 380 [Israeli nature reserves](/wiki/National_parks_and_nature_reserves_of_Israel).

Israel was the thirteenth most water-stressed country in the world in 2022.

With scarce water resources, Israel has developed various water-saving technologies, including [drip irrigation](/wiki/Drip_irrigation). The considerable sunlight available for [solar energy](/wiki/Solar_energy) makes [Israel the leading nation in solar energy](/wiki/Solar_power_in_Israel) use per capita—practically every house uses solar panels for water heating. The [Ministry of Environmental Protection](/wiki/Ministry_of_Environmental_Protection_(Israel)) has reported that [climate change](/wiki/Climate_change_in_Israel) "will have a decisive impact on all areas of life", particularly for vulnerable populations.

## Government and politics

Main articles: [Israeli system of government](/wiki/Israeli_system_of_government) and [Politics of Israel](/wiki/Politics_of_Israel)

The [Knesset](/wiki/Knesset) chamber, home to the Israeli parliament

Israel has a [parliamentary system](/wiki/Parliamentary_system), [proportional representation](/wiki/Proportional_representation) and [universal suffrage](/wiki/Universal_suffrage). A member of parliament supported by a parliamentary majority becomes the [prime minister](/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Israel)—usually this is the chair of the largest party. The prime minister is the [head of government](/wiki/Head_of_government) and of [cabinet](/wiki/Cabinet_of_Israel). The [president](/wiki/President_of_Israel) is [head of state](/wiki/Head_of_state), with largely ceremonial duties.

Israel is governed by a 120-member parliament, known as the [Knesset](/wiki/Knesset). Membership of the Knesset is based on proportional representation of [political parties](/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Israel), with a 3.25% electoral threshold, which in practice has resulted in coalition governments. Residents of Israeli settlements in the West Bank are eligible to vote, and after the [2015 election](/wiki/2015_Israeli_legislative_election), 10 of the 120 members of the Knesset (8%) were settlers. Parliamentary [elections](/wiki/Elections_in_Israel) are scheduled every four years, but unstable coalitions or a [no-confidence vote](/wiki/Motion_of_no_confidence) can dissolve a government earlier. The first Arab-led party was established in 1988, and as of 2022, Arab-led parties hold about 10% of seats. A party cannot run for election to the Knesset if its objectives or actions include the "negation of the existence of the State of Israel as the state of the Jewish people".

The [Basic Laws of Israel](/wiki/Basic_Laws_of_Israel) function as an [uncodified constitution](/wiki/Uncodified_constitution). These define Israel as a [Jewish and democratic state](/wiki/Jewish_and_democratic_state) and the nation-state of exclusively the Jewish people. In 2003, the Knesset began to draft an official constitution based on these laws.

Israel has no official religion, but the definition of the state as "Jewish and democratic" creates a strong connection with [Judaism](/wiki/Judaism). On 19 July 2018, the Knesset passed a Basic Law that characterises Israel as principally a "[Nation State of the Jewish People](/wiki/Basic_Law:_Israel_as_the_Nation-State_of_the_Jewish_People)" and Hebrew as its official language. The bill ascribes an undefined "special status" to the Arabic language. The same bill gives Jews a unique right to national self-determination and views the developing of Jewish settlement in the country as "a national interest", empowering the government to "take steps to encourage, advance and implement this interest".

### Israeli citizenship law

Main article: [Israeli citizenship law](/wiki/Israeli_citizenship_law)

The 1950 [Law of Return](/wiki/Law_of_Return) grants Jews the unrestricted [right to immigrate](/wiki/Jus_soli#Restricted_jus_soli) to Israel and obtain Israeli citizenship. Individuals born within the country receive [birthright citizenship](/wiki/Jus_sanguinis) if at least one parent is a citizen. Israeli law defines Jewish nationality as distinct from Israeli nationality, and the [Supreme Court of Israel](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Israel) has ruled that an Israeli nationality does not exist. A Jewish national is defined as any person practicing Judaism and their descendants.

In 1967, as a result of the Six-Day War, Israel captured and occupied the West Bank (including East Jerusalem), the Gaza Strip and the Golan Heights. Israel also captured the Sinai Peninsula but returned it to Egypt as part of the 1979 Egypt–Israel peace treaty. Between 1982 and 2000, Israel occupied [part of southern Lebanon](/wiki/Israeli_occupation_of_Southern_Lebanon), in what was known as the [Security Belt](/wiki/South_Lebanon_security_belt_administration). Since capture of these territories, Israeli settlements and military installations have been built within each of them, except Lebanon.

The Golan Heights and East Jerusalem have been fully incorporated under Israeli law but not under international law. Israel has applied civilian law to both areas and granted their inhabitants permanent residency status and the ability to apply for citizenship. The UN Security Council has declared the annexation of the Golan Heights and East Jerusalem to be "null and void" and continues to view the territories as occupied. The status of East Jerusalem in any future peace settlement has at times been a difficult issue in negotiations between Israeli governments and representatives of the Palestinians.

The [Israeli West Bank barrier](/wiki/Israeli_West_Bank_barrier) is a separation barrier built by Israel along the Green Line and inside parts of the West Bank.

The West Bank area, excluding East Jerusalem, is known as [the Judea and Samaria Area.](/wiki/Judea_and_Samaria_Area)The almost 400,000 Israeli settlers residing in the area are considered part of Israel's population, have Knesset representation, are subject to a [large part of Israel's civil and criminal laws](/wiki/Enclave_law), and their output is considered part of Israel's economy. The land is not considered part of Israel under Israeli law, as Israel has consciously refrained from annexing the territory, without ever relinquishing its legal claim to the land or defining a border. Israeli political opposition to annexation primarily stems from the perceived "demographic threat" of incorporating the West Bank's Palestinian population into Israel. Outside of the Israeli settlements, the West Bank remains under direct Israeli military rule, and Palestinians in the area cannot become Israeli citizens.

The international community maintains that Israel does not have sovereignty in the West Bank and considers Israel's control of the area to be the longest military occupation in modern history. The West Bank was occupied and annexed by Jordan in 1950, following the 1949 Armistice Agreements. Only Britain recognised this annexation, and Jordan has since [ceded its claim](/wiki/Jordanian_disengagement_from_the_West_Bank) to the territory to the PLO. The population is mainly Palestinians, including refugees of the 1948 Arab–Israeli War. From their occupation in 1967 until 1993, the Palestinians living in these territories were under [Israeli military administration](/wiki/Coordinator_of_Government_Activities_in_the_Territories). Since the [Israel–PLO letters of recognition](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Palestine_Liberation_Organization_letters_of_recognition), most of the Palestinian population and [cities](/wiki/List_of_cities_administered_by_the_State_of_Palestine) have been under the internal jurisdiction of the Palestinian Authority, and only partial Israeli military control, although Israel has redeployed its troops and reinstated full military administration during periods of unrest. Israel's claim of universal suffrage has been questioned due to its blurred territorial boundaries, its simultaneous extension of voting rights to Israeli settlers in the occupied territories and denial of voting rights to their Palestinian neighbours, as well as the alleged [ethnocratic](/wiki/Ethnocracy) nature of the state.

The Gaza Strip is considered to be a "foreign territory" under Israeli law. Israel and Egypt operate a land, air, and sea [blockade of the Gaza Strip](/wiki/Blockade_of_the_Gaza_Strip). The Gaza Strip was occupied by Israel after 1967. In 2005, as part of a [unilateral disengagement plan](/wiki/Israel%27s_unilateral_disengagement_plan), Israel removed its settlers and forces from the territory but continues to maintain control of its airspace and waters. The international community, including numerous international humanitarian organisations and UN bodies, consider Gaza to remain occupied. Following the 2007 Battle of Gaza, when [Hamas assumed power in the Gaza Strip](/wiki/Hamas_government_in_the_Gaza_Strip), Israel tightened control of the Gaza crossings along [its border](/wiki/Gaza%E2%80%93Israel_barrier), as well as by sea and air, and prevented persons from entering and exiting except for isolated cases it deemed humanitarian. Gaza has a [border with Egypt](/wiki/Egypt%E2%80%93Gaza_border), and an agreement between Israel, the EU, and the PA governs how border crossings take place. The application of democracy to its Palestinian citizens and the selective application of Israeli democracy in the Israeli-controlled Palestinian territories have been criticised.

#### International opinion

See also: [Israeli war crimes](/wiki/Israeli_war_crimes)

The [International Court of Justice](/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice) said, in its [2004 advisory opinion](/wiki/International_law_and_the_Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_conflict#2004_advisory_opinion_of_the_ICJ) on the legality of the construction of the West Bank barrier, that the lands captured by Israel in the Six-Day War, including East Jerusalem, are occupied territory and found that the construction of the wall within the occupied Palestinian territory violates international law. Most negotiations relating to the territories have been on the basis of [UN Security Council Resolution 242](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_242), which emphasises "the inadmissibility of the acquisition of territory by war", and calls on Israel to withdraw from occupied territories in return for normalisation of relations with Arab states ("[Land for peace](/wiki/Land_for_peace)"). Israel has been criticised for engaging in systematic and widespread violations of [human rights in the occupied territories](/wiki/Human_rights_in_the_Israeli-occupied_territories), including occupation and war crimes against civilians. The allegations include violations of international humanitarian law by the [UN Human Rights Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Human_Rights_Council). The [U.S. State Department](/wiki/U.S._State_Department) has called reports of [abuses of significant human rights of Palestinians](/wiki/Human_rights_violations_against_Palestinians_by_Israel) "credible" both within Israel and the occupied territories. [Amnesty International](/wiki/Amnesty_International) and other NGOs have documented mass arbitrary arrests, torture, unlawful killings, systemic abuses and impunity in tandem with a denial of the right to [Palestinian self-determination](/wiki/Palestinian_self-determination). Prime Minister Netanyahu has defended the country's security forces for protecting the innocent from terrorists and expressed contempt for what he describes as a lack of concern about the human rights violations committed by "criminal killers".

The [international community](/wiki/International_community) widely regards Israeli settlements in the occupied territories [illegal under international law](/wiki/International_law_and_Israeli_settlements). [United Nations Security Council Resolution 2334](/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_2334) (passed 2016) states that Israel's settlement activity constitutes a "flagrant violation" of [international law](/wiki/International_law) and demands that Israel stop such activity and fulfill its obligations as an [occupying power](/wiki/Military_occupation#The_occupying_power) under the [Fourth Geneva Convention](/wiki/Fourth_Geneva_Convention). A [United Nations special rapporteur](/wiki/United_Nations_special_rapporteur) concluded that the settlement programme was a war crime under the [Rome Statute](/wiki/Rome_Statute), and [Amnesty International](/wiki/Amnesty_International) found that the settlement programme constitutes an illegal transfer of civilians into occupied territory and "pillage", which is prohibited by the [Hague Conventions](/wiki/Hague_Conventions_of_1899_and_1907) and [Geneva Conventions](/wiki/Geneva_Conventions) as well as being a war crime under the Rome Statute.

In a [2024 advisory opinion](/wiki/International_law_and_the_Arab%E2%80%93Israeli_conflict#2024_advisory_opinion_of_the_ICJ), the International Court of Justice stated that occupation of the Palestinian territories violated international law; Israel should end its occupation as quickly as possible and pay reparations. In addition, the court found that Israel was in breach of article 3 of the [International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination](/wiki/International_Convention_on_the_Elimination_of_All_Forms_of_Racial_Discrimination), which requires states to prevent, prohibit and eradicate all practices of racial segregation and apartheid.

#### Accusations of apartheid

Main article: [Israeli apartheid](/wiki/Israeli_apartheid)

Treatment of Palestinians within the occupied territories and to a lesser extent in Israel itself have drawn widespread accusations that it is guilty of [apartheid](/wiki/Crime_of_apartheid), a [crime against humanity](/wiki/Crime_against_humanity) under the Rome Statute and the [International Convention on the Suppression and Punishment of the Crime of Apartheid](/wiki/International_Convention_on_the_Suppression_and_Punishment_of_the_Crime_of_Apartheid). *The Washington Post'*s 2021 survey of scholars and academic experts on the Middle East found an increase from 59% to 65% of these scholars describing Israel as a "one-state reality akin to apartheid". The claim that Israel's policies for Palestinians [within Israel](/wiki/Palestinian_citizens_of_Israel) or [within Israeli-occupied territories](/wiki/Israeli-occupied_territories) amount to apartheid has been affirmed by Israeli human rights organisation [B'tselem](/wiki/B%27tselem), University Network for Human Rights, and international human rights organisations such as Amnesty International and [Human Rights Watch](/wiki/Human_Rights_Watch). Israeli human rights organisation [Yesh Din](/wiki/Yesh_Din) has also accused Israel of apartheid. Amnesty's claim was criticised by politicians and representatives from Israel and its closest allies such as, the US, the UK, the [European Commission](/wiki/European_Commission), Australia, Netherlands and Germany, while said accusations were welcomed by Palestinians and the [Arab League](/wiki/Arab_League). In 2022, Michael Lynk, a Canadian law professor [appointed by the U.N. Human Rights Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Special_Rapporteur_on_the_occupied_Palestinian_territories) said that the situation met the legal definition of apartheid, and concluded: "Israel has imposed upon Palestine an apartheid reality in a post-apartheid world". Subsequent reports from his successor, [Francesca Albanese](/wiki/Francesca_Albanese) and from [Permanent United Nations Fact Finding Mission on the Israel Palestine conflict](/wiki/Permanent_United_Nations_Fact_Finding_Mission_on_the_Israel_Palestine_conflict) chair Navi Pillay echoed the opinion.

In February 2024, the [ICJ held public hearings](/wiki/ICJ_case_on_Israel%27s_occupation_of_the_Palestinian_territories) in regards to the legal consequences arising from the policies and practices of Israel in the occupied Palestinian territory including East Jerusalem. During the hearings, 24 states and three international organisations said that Israeli practices amount to a breach of the prohibition of apartheid and/or amount to prohibited acts of racial discrimination. The [International Court of Justice](/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice) in its [2024 advisory opinion](/wiki/ICJ_case_on_Israel%27s_occupation_of_the_Palestinian_territories) found that Israel's occupation of the Palestinian territories constitutes systemic discrimination and is in breach of Article 3 of the [International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination](/wiki/International_Convention_on_the_Elimination_of_All_Forms_of_Racial_Discrimination), which prohibits racial segregation and apartheid. The opinion is silent as to whether the discrimination amounts to apartheid; individual judges were split on the question.

### Foreign relations

Main articles: [Foreign relations of Israel](/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Israel) and [International recognition of Israel](/wiki/International_recognition_of_Israel)

Israel maintains diplomatic relations with 165 [UN member states](/wiki/United_Nations_member_states), the [Holy See](/wiki/Holy_See), [Kosovo](/wiki/Kosovo), the [Cook Islands](/wiki/Cook_Islands) and [Niue](/wiki/Niue). It has 107 [diplomatic missions](/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_of_Israel); countries with which it has no diplomatic relations include most Muslim countries. Six out of 22 nations in the [Arab League](/wiki/Arab_League) have normalised relations with Israel. Israel remains formally in a [state of war with Syria](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Syria_relations), dating back uninterrupted to 1948. It has been in a similarly [formal state of war with Lebanon](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Lebanon_relations) since the end of the [Lebanese Civil War](/wiki/Lebanese_Civil_War) in 2000, with the Israel–Lebanon border remaining unagreed by treaty.

Despite the peace treaty between Israel and Egypt, Israel is still widely considered an enemy country among Egyptians. Iran withdrew its recognition of Israel during the [Islamic Revolution](/wiki/Islamic_Revolution). Israeli citizens may not visit Syria, Lebanon, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, and Yemen without permission from the [Ministry of the Interior](/wiki/Ministry_of_Interior_(Israel)). As a result of the 2008–09 Gaza War, Mauritania, Qatar, Bolivia, and Venezuela suspended political and economic ties with Israel, though Bolivia renewed ties in 2019.

Yitzhak Rabin and Yasser Arafat at the signing ceremony of the [Oslo Accords peace agreements](/wiki/Oslo_Accords), with then US President [Bill Clinton](/wiki/Bill_Clinton) (center)

The [United States](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93United_States_relations) and the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Russia_relations) were the first two countries to recognise the State of Israel, having declared recognition roughly simultaneously. Diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union were broken in 1967 following the Six-Day War and renewed in 1991. The United States regards Israel as its "most reliable partner in the Middle East", based on "common democratic values, religious affinities, and security interests". The US has provided $68 billion [in military assistance](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93United_States_military_relations) and $32 billion in grants to Israel since 1967, under the [Foreign Assistance Act](/wiki/Foreign_Assistance_Act) (period beginning 1962), more than any other country for that period until 2003. Most surveyed Americans have held consistently favourable views of Israel. The United Kingdom is seen as having a "natural" [relationship](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93United_Kingdom_relations) with Israel because of the Mandate for Palestine. By 2007, [Germany](/wiki/Germany%E2%80%93Israel_relations) had paid 25 billion euros in [reparations to Israel](/wiki/Reparations_Agreement_between_Israel_and_West_Germany) and individual Israeli Holocaust survivors. Israel [is included](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93European_Union_relations) in the European Union's [European Neighbourhood Policy](/wiki/European_Neighbourhood_Policy).

Although [Turkey and Israel](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93Turkey_relations) did not establish full diplomatic relations until 1991, Turkey has cooperated with the Jewish state since its recognition of Israel in 1949. Turkey's ties to other Muslim-majority nations in the region have at times resulted in pressure from Arab and Muslim states to temper its relationship with Israel. Relations took a downturn after the 2008–09 Gaza War and Israel's [raid of the Gaza flotilla](/wiki/2010_Gaza_flotilla_raid). Relations between [Greece and Israel](/wiki/Greece%E2%80%93Israel_relations) have improved since 1995 after decline of Israeli–Turkish relations. The two countries have a defence cooperation agreement and in 2010, the [Israeli Air Force](/wiki/Israeli_Air_Force) hosted Greece's [Hellenic Air Force](/wiki/Hellenic_Air_Force) in a joint exercise. The joint Cyprus-Israel oil and gas explorations centred on the [Leviathan gas field](/wiki/Leviathan_gas_field) are an important factor for Greece, given its [strong links](/wiki/Cyprus%E2%80%93Greece_relations) with Cyprus. Cooperation in the world's longest [submarine power cable](/wiki/Submarine_power_cable), the [EuroAsia Interconnector](/wiki/EuroAsia_Interconnector), has strengthened [Cyprus–Israel relations](/wiki/Cyprus%E2%80%93Israel_relations).

Azerbaijan is one of the few majority Muslim countries to develop strategic and economic [relations with Israel](/wiki/Azerbaijan%E2%80%93Israel_relations). Kazakhstan also has an economic and strategic partnership with Israel. India established full [diplomatic ties](/wiki/India%E2%80%93Israel_relations) with Israel in 1992 and has fostered a strong military, technological and cultural partnership with the country since then. India is the largest customer of the [Israeli military equipment](/wiki/Israeli_military_equipment), and Israel is the second-largest military partner of India after Russia. [Ethiopia](/wiki/Ethiopia%E2%80%93Israel_relations) is Israel's main ally in Africa due to common political, religious and security interests.

As of 2025, Israel is the only UN member state to recognise the [Republic of Somaliland](/wiki/Republic_of_Somaliland).

#### Foreign aid

Israel has a history of providing emergency foreign aid and humanitarian response to disasters across the world. In 1955 Israel began its [foreign aid programme](/wiki/Israeli_foreign_aid) in Burma and then shifted to Africa. Israel's humanitarian efforts officially began in 1957 with the establishment of [Mashav](/wiki/Mashav), the Israel's Agency for International Development Cooperation. In this early period, whilst Israel's aid represented only a small percentage of total aid to Africa, its programme was effective in creating goodwill; however, following the 1967 war relations soured. Israel's foreign aid programme subsequently shifted its focus to Latin America.

Since the late 1970s Israel's foreign aid has gradually decreased, although in recent years Israel has tried to reestablish aid to Africa. There are additional Israeli humanitarian and emergency response groups that work with the government, including [IsraAid](/wiki/IsraAid), a joint programme run by Israeli organisations and North American Jewish groups, [ZAKA](/wiki/ZAKA), The Fast Israeli Rescue and Search Team, Israeli Flying Aid, [Save a Child's Heart](/wiki/Save_a_Child%27s_Heart) and [Latet](/wiki/Latet). Between 1985 and 2015, Israel sent 24 delegations of their search and rescue unit the [Home Front Command](/wiki/Home_Front_Command) to 22 countries. Currently Israeli foreign aid [ranks](/wiki/List_of_development_aid_country_donors) low among [OECD](/wiki/OECD) nations, spending less than 0.1% of its [GNI](/wiki/Gross_national_income) on development assistance. The country ranked 38th in the 2018 [World Giving Index](/wiki/World_Giving_Index).

### Military

Further information: [List of wars involving Israel](/wiki/List_of_wars_involving_Israel), [List of the Israel Defense Forces operations](/wiki/List_of_the_Israel_Defense_Forces_operations), and [Israel and weapons of mass destruction](/wiki/Israel_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction)

[F-35](/wiki/F-35) fighter jets of the [Israeli Air Force](/wiki/Israeli_Air_Force)

The [Israel Defence Forces](/wiki/Israel_Defence_Forces) (IDF) is the sole military wing of the [Israeli security forces](/wiki/Israeli_security_forces) and is headed by its [Chief of the General Staff](/wiki/Chief_of_the_General_Staff_(Israel)), the *Ramatkal*, subordinate to the Cabinet. The IDF consists of the [army](/wiki/GOC_Army_Headquarters), [air force](/wiki/Israeli_Air_Force) and [navy](/wiki/Israeli_Navy). It was founded during the 1948 Arab–Israeli War by consolidating paramilitary organisations, chiefly the Haganah. The IDF also draws upon the resources of the [Military Intelligence Directorate](/wiki/Military_Intelligence_Directorate_(Israel)) (*Aman*).

Most [Israelis are conscripted](/wiki/Conscription_in_Israel) at age 18. Men serve two years and eight months, and women serve two years. Following mandatory service, Israeli men join the reserve forces and usually do up to several weeks of [reserve duty](/wiki/Reserve_duty_(Israel)) every year until their forties. Most women are exempt from reserve duty. [Arab citizens of Israel](/wiki/Arab_citizens_of_Israel) (except the [Druze](/wiki/Druze_in_Israel)) and those engaged in full-time religious studies [are exempt](/wiki/Exemption_from_military_service_in_Israel), although the [exemption of yeshiva students](/wiki/Tal_committee) has been a source of contention. An alternative for those who receive exemptions on various grounds is *[Sherut Leumi](/wiki/Sherut_Leumi)*, or national service, which involves a programme of service in social welfare frameworks. A small minority of Israeli Arabs also volunteer in the army. As a result of its conscription programme, the IDF maintains approximately 176,500 active troops and 465,000 reservists, giving Israel one of the world's highest [percentage of citizens with military training](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_military_and_paramilitary_personnel).

[Iron Dome](/wiki/Iron_Dome) is the world's first operational anti-[artillery rocket](/wiki/Rocket_artillery) defence system.

The military relies heavily on high-tech [weapons](/wiki/Military_equipment_of_Israel) systems [designed and manufactured in Israel](/wiki/Defense_industry_of_Israel) as well as some foreign imports. The [Arrow](/wiki/Arrow_(Israeli_missile)) missile is one of the world's few operational [anti-ballistic missile](/wiki/Anti-ballistic_missile) systems. The [Python](/wiki/Python_(missile)) air-to-air missile series is often considered one of the most crucial weapons in its military history. Israel's [Spike](/wiki/Spike_(missile)) missile is one of the most widely exported [anti-tank guided missiles](/wiki/Anti-tank_guided_missile) in the world. Israel's [Iron Dome](/wiki/Iron_Dome) anti-missile air defence system gained worldwide acclaim after intercepting hundreds of [rockets fired by Palestinian militants](/wiki/Palestinian_rocket_attacks_on_Israel) from the Gaza Strip. Since the [Yom Kippur War](/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War), Israel has developed a network of [reconnaissance satellites](/wiki/Reconnaissance_satellites). The *[Ofeq](/wiki/Ofeq)* programme has made Israel [one of seven countries](/wiki/Timeline_of_first_orbital_launches_by_country) capable of launching such satellites.

Israel is widely believed to [possess nuclear weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_and_Israel) and per a 1993 report, chemical and biological [weapons of mass destruction](/wiki/Israel_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction). Israel has not signed the [Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons](/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Non-Proliferation_of_Nuclear_Weapons) and maintains a [policy of deliberate ambiguity](/wiki/Policy_of_deliberate_ambiguity) towards its nuclear capabilities. The Israeli Navy's [Dolphin submarines](/wiki/Dolphin-class_submarine) are believed to be armed with nuclear missiles offering [second-strike](/wiki/Second_strike) capability. Since the [Gulf War](/wiki/Gulf_War) in 1991, all homes in Israel are required to have a reinforced security room, [Merkhav Mugan](/wiki/Merkhav_Mugan), impermeable to chemical and biological substances.

Since Israel's establishment, military expenditure constituted a significant portion of the country's [gross domestic product](/wiki/Gross_domestic_product), with peak of 30.3% of GDP in 1975. In 2021, Israel ranked 15th in the world [by total military expenditure](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_military_expenditures), with $24.3 billion, and 6th by defence spending as a percentage of GDP, with 5.2%. Since 1974, the United States has been a particularly notable contributor of [military aid](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93United_States_military_relations#Military_aid_and_procurement). Under a [memorandum of understanding](/wiki/Memorandum_of_understanding) signed in 2016, the U.S. is expected to provide the country with $3.8 billion per year, or around 20% of Israel's defence budget, from 2018 to 2028. Israel ranked 8th globally for [arms exports](/wiki/Arms_industry) in 2020–2024. The majority of Israel's arms exports are unreported for security reasons. Israel is consistently rated low in the [Global Peace Index](/wiki/Global_Peace_Index), ranking 134th out of 163 nations in 2022.

### Legal system

Main articles: [Judiciary of Israel](/wiki/Judiciary_of_Israel) and [Israeli law](/wiki/Israeli_law)

[Supreme Court of Israel](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Israel), Givat Ram, Jerusalem

Israel has a [three-tier court system](/wiki/Israeli_judicial_system). At the lowest level are [magistrate](/wiki/Magistrate) courts, situated in most cities across the country. Above them are [district courts](/wiki/District_court), serving as both [appellate](/wiki/Appeal) courts and [courts of first instance](/wiki/Trial_court); they are situated in five of Israel's six [districts](/wiki/Districts_of_Israel). The third and highest tier is the [Supreme Court](/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Israel), located in Jerusalem; it serves a dual role as the highest court of appeals and the [High Court of Justice](/wiki/High_Court_of_Justice_(Israel)). In the latter role, the Supreme Court rules as a court of first instance, allowing both citizens and non-citizens to petition against the decisions of state authorities.

The legal system combines three legal traditions: [English common law](/wiki/English_law), [civil law](/wiki/Civil_law_(legal_system)), and [Jewish law](/wiki/Halakha). It is based on the principle of *[stare decisis](/wiki/Stare_decisis)* (precedent) and is an [adversarial system](/wiki/Adversarial_system). Court cases are decided by professional judges. [Marriage](/wiki/Marriage_in_Israel) and divorce are under the jurisdiction of the religious courts: [Jewish](/wiki/Beth_din), [Muslim](/wiki/Sharia), Druze, and Christian. The election of judges is carried out by a [selection committee](/wiki/Judicial_Selection_Committee_(Israel)) chaired by the [justice minister](/wiki/Ministry_of_Justice_(Israel)) (currently [Yariv Levin](/wiki/Yariv_Levin)). Israel's [Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty](/wiki/Basic_Law:_Human_Dignity_and_Liberty) seeks to defend [human rights and liberties in Israel](/wiki/Human_rights_in_Israel). The [United Nations Human Rights Council](/wiki/United_Nations_Human_Rights_Council) and Israeli human rights organisation [Adalah](/wiki/Adalah_(legal_center)) have highlighted that this law does not in fact contain a general provision for equality and non-discrimination. As a result of "[Enclave law](/wiki/Enclave_law)", large portions of Israeli civil law are applied to Israeli settlements and Israeli residents in the occupied territories. Israeli law is seldom enforced in the occupied territories, with 96% of police investigations into settler violence in the West Bank from 2020 to 2025 ending without an indictment. No one has been charged in the killings of at least 1,100 Palestinian civilians by Israeli soldiers and settlers since 2020.

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Israel](/wiki/Economy_of_Israel)

The [Diamond Exchange District](/wiki/Diamond_Exchange_District) in [Ramat Gan](/wiki/Ramat_Gan)

Israel is considered the most advanced country in [West Asia](/wiki/West_Asia) and the Middle East in economic and industrial development. As of October 2023, the IMF estimated its GDP at 521.7 billion dollars and GDP per capita at 53.2 thousand ([ranking 13th worldwide](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)_per_capita)). It is the third richest country in Asia [by nominal per capita](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)_per_capita) income and has the highest average [wealth per adult](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_wealth_per_adult) in the Middle East. *[The Economist](/wiki/The_Economist)* ranked Israel as the fourth most successful economy among the developed countries for 2022. It has the [most billionaires](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_billionaires) in the Middle East and the 18th most in the world. In recent years, Israel had one of the highest growth rates in the developed world. In 2010, it joined the [OECD](/wiki/OECD). The country is ranked 35th on the [World Bank](/wiki/World_Bank)'s [*Ease of Doing Business* index](/wiki/Ease_of_doing_business_index). Economic data covers the economic territory of Israel, including the Golan Heights, East Jerusalem and Israeli settlements in the West Bank.

Despite limited natural resources, intensive development of the [agricultural](/wiki/Agriculture_in_Israel) and industrial sectors over the past decades has made Israel largely self-sufficient in food production, apart from grains and beef. Imports, totalling $96.5 billion in 2020, include raw materials, military equipment, investment goods, rough diamonds, fuels, grain, and consumer goods. Leading exports include machinery, equipment, software, [cut diamonds](/wiki/Diamond_industry_in_Israel), agricultural products, chemicals, textiles, and apparel; in 2020, exports reached $114 billion. The [Bank of Israel](/wiki/Bank_of_Israel) holds $201 billion of foreign-exchange reserves, the 17th highest in the world. Since the 1970s, Israel has received [military aid](/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93United_States_military_relations) from the United States, as well as loan guarantees, which account for roughly half of Israel's external debt. Israel has [one of the lowest](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_external_debt) external debts in the developed world, and is a lender in terms of net external debt ([assets vs. liabilities abroad](/wiki/Net_international_investment_position)), which in 2015 stood at a surplus of $69 billion.

Israel has the second-largest number of startup companies after the United States and the third-largest number of [NASDAQ-listed companies](/wiki/List_of_Israeli_companies_quoted_on_the_Nasdaq). It is the world leader for number of start-ups per capita and has been dubbed the "[Start-Up Nation](/wiki/Start-up_Nation)". [Intel](/wiki/Intel) and [Microsoft](/wiki/Microsoft) built their first overseas [research and development](/wiki/Research_and_development) facilities in Israel, and other high-tech multinational corporations have opened [research and development centres in the country](/wiki/List_of_multinational_companies_with_research_and_development_centres_in_Israel).

The days which are allocated to working times are Sunday through Thursday (for a five-day workweek), or Friday (for a six-day workweek). In observance of [Shabbat](/wiki/Shabbat), in places where Friday is a work day and the majority of population is Jewish, Friday is a "short day". Several proposals have been raised to adjust the work week with the majority of the world.

### Science and technology

Main articles: [Science and technology in Israel](/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_Israel) and [List of Israeli inventions and discoveries](/wiki/List_of_Israeli_inventions_and_discoveries)

[Matam](/wiki/Matam,_Haifa) high-tech park in Haifa

Israel's development of cutting-edge technologies in software, communications and the life sciences have [evoked comparisons](/wiki/Silicon_Wadi) with [Silicon Valley](/wiki/Silicon_Valley). Israel is first in the world in [expenditure on research and development](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_research_and_development_spending) as a percentage of GDP. It is ranked 14th in the [Global Innovation Index](/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2025, and 5th in the 2019 [Bloomberg Innovation Index](/wiki/Bloomberg_Innovation_Index). Israel has 140 scientists, technicians, and engineers per 10,000 employees, the highest number in the world and has produced six [Nobel Prize-winning](/wiki/List_of_Israeli_Nobel_laureates) scientists, mostly in chemistry, since 2004 and has been frequently ranked as one of the countries with the highest ratios of [scientific papers](/wiki/Scientific_papers) per capita. [Israeli universities](/wiki/List_of_Israeli_universities_and_colleges) are ranked among the top 50 world universities in computer science ([Technion](/wiki/Technion) and [Tel Aviv University](/wiki/Tel_Aviv_University)), mathematics ([Hebrew University of Jerusalem](/wiki/Hebrew_University_of_Jerusalem)) and chemistry ([Weizmann Institute of Science](/wiki/Weizmann_Institute_of_Science)).

In 2012, Israel was ranked ninth in the world by the Futron's [Space Competitiveness Index](/wiki/Space_Competitiveness_Index). The [Israel Space Agency](/wiki/Israel_Space_Agency) coordinates all space research programmes with scientific and commercial goals, and have designed and built at least 13 commercial, research and spy satellites. Some satellites are ranked among the world's most advanced space systems. [Shavit](/wiki/Shavit_2) is a space [launch vehicle](/wiki/Launch_vehicle) produced by Israel to launch small satellites into [low Earth orbit](/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit). It was first launched in 1988, making Israel the [eighth nation](/wiki/Timeline_of_first_orbital_launches_by_country) to have a space launch capability. In 2003, [Ilan Ramon](/wiki/Ilan_Ramon) became Israel's first astronaut, serving on the [fatal mission](/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Columbia_disaster) of [Space Shuttle *Columbia*](/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Columbia).

The [ongoing water shortage](/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Israel) has spurred innovation in water conservation techniques, and a substantial [agricultural modernisation](/wiki/Agricultural_research_in_Israel), [drip irrigation](/wiki/Drip_irrigation), was invented in Israel. Israel is also at the technological forefront of desalination and water recycling. The Sorek desalination plant is the largest seawater [reverse osmosis](/wiki/Reverse_osmosis) desalination facility in the world. By 2014, desalination programmes provided roughly 35% of the drinking water, and it is expected to supply 70% by 2050. As of 2015, over 50 per cent of the water for households, agriculture and industry is artificially produced. In 2011, Israel's water technology industry was worth around $2 billion per year with annual exports of products and services in the tens of millions of dollars. As a result of innovations in reverse osmosis technology, Israel is set to become a net [exporter of water](/wiki/Water_export).

The world's largest [solar parabolic dish](/wiki/Parabolic_reflector) at the [Ben-Gurion National Solar Energy Center](/wiki/Ben-Gurion_National_Solar_Energy_Center)

Israel has embraced [solar energy](/wiki/Solar_power_in_Israel); its engineers are on the cutting edge of solar energy technology, and its solar companies work on projects around the world. Over 90% of homes use solar energy for hot water, the highest per capita. According to government figures, the country saves 8% of its electricity consumption per year because of its solar energy use in heating. The high annual incident solar irradiance at its geographic latitude creates ideal conditions for what is an internationally renowned solar research and development industry in the Negev. Israel had a modern electric car infrastructure involving a countrywide network of charging stations; however, its electric car company [Better Place](/wiki/Better_Place_(company)) shut down in 2013.

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in Israel](/wiki/Energy_in_Israel)

Israel [began producing natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas_in_Israel) from its own offshore gas fields in 2004. In 2009 [Tamar gas field](/wiki/Tamar_gas_field) was discovered near the coast, and [Leviathan gas field](/wiki/Leviathan_gas_field) was discovered in 2010. The natural gas reserves in these two fields could make Israel energy-secure for more than 50 years. Commercial production of natural gas from the Tamar field began in 2013, with over 7.5 billion cubic metres (bcm) produced annually. Israel had 199 billion bcm of proven reserves of natural gas as of 2016. The Leviathan gas field started production in 2019.

[Ketura Sun](/wiki/Ketura_Sun) is Israel's first commercial solar field. Built in 2011 by the [Arava Power Company](/wiki/Arava_Power_Company), the field will produce about 9 gigawatt-hours (32 TJ) of electricity per year, sparing the production of some 125,000 tonnes (123,000 long tons; 138,000 short tons) of carbon dioxide over 20 years.

### Transport

Main article: [Transport in Israel](/wiki/Transport_in_Israel)

[Ben Gurion International Airport](/wiki/Ben_Gurion_International_Airport), the main international airport of Israel

As of 2016, Israel has 19,224 kilometres (11,945 mi) of paved [roads](/wiki/Roads_in_Israel) and 3 million motor vehicles. The [number of motor vehicles per 1,000 persons](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_vehicles_per_capita) is 365, relatively low among developed countries. The country aims to have 30% of vehicles on its roads powered by electricity by 2030.

Israel has 5,715 buses on scheduled routes, operated by several carriers, the largest and oldest of which is [Egged](/wiki/Egged_(company)), serving most of the country. [Railways](/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Israel) stretch across 1,277 kilometres (793 mi) and are operated by government-owned [Israel Railways](/wiki/Israel_Railways). Following major investments beginning in the early to mid-1990s, the number of train passengers per year has grown from 2.5 million in 1990, to 53 million in 2015; railways transport 7.5 million tonnes (8.3 million short tons; 7.4 million long tons) of cargo per year.

Israel is served by three international [airports](/wiki/List_of_airports_in_Israel): [Ben Gurion Airport](/wiki/Ben_Gurion_Airport), the country's main hub for international air travel; [Ramon Airport](/wiki/Ramon_Airport); and [Haifa Airport](/wiki/Haifa_Airport). Ben Gurion handled over 21.1 million passengers in 2023. There are three main ports: the [Port of Haifa](/wiki/Port_of_Haifa), the oldest and largest; [Ashdod Port](/wiki/Port_of_Ashdod); and the [Port of Eilat](/wiki/Port_of_Eilat) on the [Red Sea](/wiki/Red_Sea).

### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in Israel](/wiki/Tourism_in_Israel)

See also: [List of archaeological sites in Israel and Palestine](/wiki/List_of_archaeological_sites_in_Israel_and_Palestine)

[Ein Bokek](/wiki/Ein_Bokek) resort on the shore of the [Dead Sea](/wiki/Dead_Sea)

Tourism, especially [religious tourism](/wiki/Religious_tourism), is an important industry, with [beaches](/wiki/List_of_beaches_in_Israel), [archaeological](/wiki/Archaeology_of_Israel), other [historical](/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Israel) and [biblical](/wiki/List_of_biblical_places) sites, and unique geography also drawing tourists. In 2017, a record 3.6 million tourists visited Israel, yielding a 25 per cent growth since 2016 and contributed NIS 20 billion to the economy.

### Real estate

Main article: [Housing in Israel](/wiki/Housing_in_Israel)

Housing prices are listed in the top third of all countries, with an average of 150 salaries required to buy an apartment. As of 2022, there are about 2.7 million properties in Israel, with an annual increase of over 50,000. However, demand for housing exceeds supply, with a shortage of about 200,000 apartments as of 2021. As a result, by 2021 housing prices rose by 5.6%. In 2021, Israelis took a record of NIS 116.1 billion in mortgages, an increase of 50% from 2020.

## Demographics

Main articles: [Demographics of Israel](/wiki/Demographics_of_Israel) and [Israelis](/wiki/Israelis)

[Aliyah](/wiki/Aliyah) in the years 1948–2015. The two peaks were in 1949 and 1990.

Israel has the largest Jewish population in the world and is the only country where Jews are the majority, and the only country in which Jews make up more than 2% of the total national population. As of April 2025, the population was an estimated 10,094,000. In 2025, the government recorded 72% of the population as [Jews](/wiki/Israeli_Jews), 21% as [Arabs](/wiki/Arab_citizens_of_Israel), and 7% as "Others" (non-Arab Christians and people who have no religion listed). Over the last decade, large numbers of migrant workers from Romania, Thailand, China, Africa, and South America have settled in Israel. Exact figures are unknown, as many of them are living in the country illegally, but estimates run from 166,000 to 203,000. By June 2012, approximately 60,000 [African migrants](/wiki/Illegal_immigration_from_Africa_to_Israel) had entered Israel.

About 93% of Israelis live in urban areas. 90% of Palestinian Israelis reside in 139 densely populated towns and villages concentrated in the Galilee, [Triangle](/wiki/Triangle_(Israel)) and Negev regions, with the remaining 10% in [mixed cities](/wiki/Mixed_cities) and neighbourhoods. The OECD in 2016 estimated the average life expectancy at 82.5 years, the [6th-highest in the world](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_life_expectancy). Israeli Arab life expectancy lags by 3 to 4 years and is higher than in most Arab and Muslim countries. The country has the highest [fertility rate](/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) in the OECD and the only one which is above the replacement figure of 2.1. Retention of Israel's population since 1948 is about even or greater, when compared to other countries with mass immigration. Jewish emigration from Israel (called *[yerida](/wiki/Yerida)*), primarily to the United States and Canada, is described by demographers as modest, but is often cited by Israeli government ministries as a major threat to Israel's future.

Approximately 80% of [Israeli Jews](/wiki/Israeli_Jews) are [born in Israel](/wiki/Sabra_(person)), 14% are immigrants from Europe and the Americas, and 6% are immigrants from Asia and Africa. Jews from Europe and the former Soviet Union and their descendants born in Israel, including Ashkenazi Jews, constitute approximately 44% of Jewish Israelis. Jews from Arab and Muslim countries and their descendants, including both Mizrahi and Sephardi Jews, form most of the rest of the Jewish population. Jewish intermarriage rates run at over 35% and recent studies suggest that the percentage of Israelis descended from both Sephardi and Ashkenazi Jews increases by 0.5 per cent yearly, with over 25% of schoolchildren now originating from both. Around 4% of Israelis (300,000), ethnically defined as "others", are Russian descendants of Jewish origin or family who are not Jewish according to rabbinical law, but were eligible for citizenship under the Law of Return.

Israeli settlers beyond the Green Line number over 600,000 (≈10% of the Jewish Israeli population). In 2016, [399,300 Israelis lived](/wiki/Population_statistics_for_Israeli_settlements_in_the_West_Bank) in West Bank settlements, including those that predated the establishment of the State of Israel and which were re-established after the Six-Day War. Additionally there were more than 200,000 Jews living in East Jerusalem and 22,000 in the Golan Heights. Approximately 7,800 Israelis [lived in settlements](/wiki/Population_statistics_for_Israeli_Gaza_Strip_settlements) in the Gaza Strip, known as [Gush Katif](/wiki/Gush_Katif), until they were evacuated by the government as part of its 2005 [disengagement plan](/wiki/Israel%27s_unilateral_disengagement_plan).

Israeli Arabs (including the Arab population of East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights) comprise 21.1% of the population or 1,995,000 people. In a 2017 poll, 40% of Arab citizens of Israel identified as "Arab in Israel" or "Arab citizen of Israel", 15% identified as "Palestinian", 8.9% as "Palestinian in Israel" or "Palestinian citizen of Israel", and 8.7% as "Arab"; a poll found that 60% of Israeli Arabs have a positive view of the state.

### Language

Main article: [Languages of Israel](/wiki/Languages_of_Israel)

[Road sign](/wiki/Road_signs_in_Israel) in [Hebrew](/wiki/Hebrew_language), [Arabic](/wiki/Arabic_language_in_Israel), and [English](/wiki/English_language)

The official language is [Hebrew](/wiki/Hebrew_language). Hebrew is the primary language of the state and is spoken daily by the majority of the population. Prior to 1948, [opposition](/wiki/Anti-Yiddish_sentiment) to [Yiddish](/wiki/Yiddish), the historical language of the Ashkenazi Jews, was common among supporters of the Zionist movement, including the Yishuv, who sought to promote [Hebrew's revival](/wiki/Revival_of_the_Hebrew_language) as a unifying national language. These sentiments were reflected in the early policies of the Israeli government, which largely banned [Yiddish theatre](/wiki/Yiddish_theatre) and publications. Until 2018, [Arabic](/wiki/Arabic_language_in_Israel) was also an official language; in 2018 it was downgraded to having a "special status in the state". Arabic is spoken by the Arab minority, with Arabic and Hebrew taught in Arab schools. Arabic is studied in most Jewish schools and is often used on signage and in transport announcements.

Due to mass immigration from the former Soviet Union and [Ethiopia](/wiki/Aliyah_from_Ethiopia) (some 130,000 [Ethiopian Jews live in Israel](/wiki/Ethiopian_Jews_in_Israel)), [Russian](/wiki/Russian_language_in_Israel) and [Amharic](/wiki/Amharic) are widely spoken. Over one million Russian-speaking immigrants arrived in Israel between 1990 and 2004. French is spoken by around 700,000 Israelis, mostly originating [from France](/wiki/French_Jews_in_Israel) and North Africa (see [Maghrebi Jews](/wiki/Maghrebi_Jews)). English was an official language during the Mandate period; it lost this status after the establishment of Israel, but retains a role comparable to that of an official language. Many Israelis communicate reasonably well in English, as many television programmes are broadcast in English with subtitles and the language is taught in elementary school. Israeli universities offer courses in English.

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in Israel](/wiki/Religion_in_Israel)

See also: [Abrahamic religions](/wiki/Abrahamic_religions)

The [Dome of the Rock](/wiki/Dome_of_the_Rock) and the [Western Wall](/wiki/Western_Wall), Jerusalem

The estimated religious affiliation as of 2022 was 73.5% Jewish, 18.1% [Muslim](/wiki/Muslims), 1.9% [Christian](/wiki/Christians), 1.6% [Druze](/wiki/Druze), and 4.9% other. The [religious affiliation](/wiki/Jewish_religious_movements) of [Israeli Jews](/wiki/Israeli_Jews) varies widely: a 2016 survey by [Pew Research](/wiki/Pew_Research) indicates that 49% self-identify as [Hiloni](/wiki/Hiloni) (secular), 29% as [Masorti](/wiki/Masortim) (traditional), 13% as [Dati](/wiki/Dati) (religious) and 9% as [Haredi](/wiki/Haredi_Judaism) (ultra-Orthodox). Haredi Jews are expected to represent over 20% of the Jewish population by 2028. [Muslims](/wiki/Islam_in_Israel) constitute the largest religious minority, making up about 18.1% of the population. About 1.9% of the population is [Christian](/wiki/Christianity_in_Israel), and 1.6% is [Druze](/wiki/Druze_in_Israel). The Christian population comprises primarily [Arab Christians](/wiki/Arab_Christians) and [Aramean Christians](/wiki/Arameans_in_Israel) but also includes post-Soviet immigrants, foreign labourers, [Armenian Christians](/wiki/Armenians_in_Israel_and_Palestine), and followers of [Messianic Judaism](/wiki/Messianic_Judaism), considered by most Christians and Jews to be a form of Christianity. Members of many other religious groups, including [Buddhists](/wiki/Buddhism) and [Hindus](/wiki/Hinduism_in_Israel), maintain a presence in Israel, albeit in small numbers. Out of over one million immigrants from the former Soviet Union, about 300,000 are considered not Jewish by the [Chief Rabbinate of Israel](/wiki/Chief_Rabbinate_of_Israel).

Israel comprises a major part of the [Holy Land](/wiki/Holy_Land), a region of significant importance to all [Abrahamic religions](/wiki/Abrahamic_religions). Jerusalem is of [special importance](/wiki/Religious_significance_of_Jerusalem) to Jews, Muslims, and Christians, as it is the home of [sites](/wiki/List_of_places_in_Jerusalem) that are pivotal to their religious beliefs, such as the [Old City](/wiki/Old_City_(Jerusalem)) that incorporates the [Western Wall](/wiki/Western_Wall) and the [Temple Mount](/wiki/Temple_Mount) (Al-Aqsa Mosque compound) and the [Church of the Holy Sepulchre](/wiki/Church_of_the_Holy_Sepulchre). Other locations of religious importance are [Nazareth](/wiki/Nazareth) (site of the [Annunciation](/wiki/Annunciation) of [Mary](/wiki/Mary_(mother_of_Jesus))), [Tiberias](/wiki/Tiberias) and [Safed](/wiki/Safed) (two of the [Four Holy Cities](/wiki/Four_Holy_Cities) in Judaism), the [White Mosque](/wiki/White_Mosque,_Ramla) in [Ramla](/wiki/Ramla) (shrine of the prophet [Saleh](/wiki/Salih)), and the [Church of Saint George and Mosque of Al-Khadr, Lod](/wiki/Church_of_Saint_George_and_Mosque_of_Al-Khadr,_Lod) (tomb of [Saint George](/wiki/Saint_George) or [Al Khidr](/wiki/Al_Khidr)). A number of other religious landmarks are located in the [West Bank](/wiki/West_Bank), including [Joseph's Tomb](/wiki/Joseph%27s_Tomb), the [birthplace of Jesus](/wiki/Church_of_the_Nativity), [Rachel's Tomb](/wiki/Rachel%27s_Tomb), and the [Cave of the Patriarchs](/wiki/Cave_of_the_Patriarchs). The [administrative center](/wiki/Arc_(Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD)) of the [Baháʼí Faith](/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith) and the [Shrine of the Báb](/wiki/Shrine_of_the_B%C3%A1b) are located at the [Baháʼí World Centre](/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_World_Centre) in [Haifa](/wiki/Haifa); the leader of the faith is [buried](/wiki/Shrine_of_Bah%C3%A1%27u%27ll%C3%A1h) in [Acre](/wiki/Acre,_Israel). The [Mahmood Mosque](/wiki/Mahmood_Mosque,_Haifa) is affiliated with the reformist [Ahmadiyya](/wiki/Ahmadiyya_in_Israel) movement. [Kababir](/wiki/Kababir), Haifa's mixed neighbourhood of Jews and Ahmadi Arabs, is one of a few of its kind in the country.

### Education

Main article: [Education in Israel](/wiki/Education_in_Israel)

[Multidisciplinary Brain Research Center](/wiki/Bar-Ilan_University#Leslie_and_Susan_Gonda_Multidisciplinary_Brain_Research_Center) at [Bar-Ilan University](/wiki/Bar-Ilan_University)

In 2015, Israel [ranked](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_tertiary_education_attainment) third among OECD members for the percentage of 25–64-year-olds that have attained tertiary education with 49% compared with the OECD average of 35%. In 2012, the country ranked third in the number of academic degrees per capita (20 per cent of the population).

Israel has a [school life expectancy](/wiki/School_life_expectancy) of 16 years and a [literacy rate](/wiki/List_of_countries_by_literacy_rate) of 97.8%. The State Education Law (1953) established five types of schools: state secular, state religious, ultra orthodox, communal settlement schools, and Arab schools. The public secular is the largest school group and is attended by the majority of Jewish and non-Arab pupils. Most Arabs send their children to schools where Arabic is the language of instruction. Education is compulsory for children between the ages of three and eighteen. Schooling is divided into three tiers—primary school (grades 1–6), middle school (grades 7–9), and high school (grades 10–12)—culminating with *[Bagrut](/wiki/Bagrut)* matriculation exams. Proficiency in core subjects such as mathematics, the Hebrew language, Hebrew and general literature, the English language, history, Biblical scripture and civics is necessary to receive a Bagrut certificate.

The Jewish population maintains a relatively high level of educational attainment where just under half of all Israeli Jews (46%) hold post-secondary degrees. Israeli Jews 25 and older have an average 11.6 years of schooling, making them one of the most highly educated of all major religious groups in the world. In Arab, Christian and Druze schools, the exam on Biblical studies is replaced by an exam on Muslim, Christian or Druze heritage, respectively. In 2020, 68.7% of 12th graders earned a matriculation certificate.

[Mount Scopus](/wiki/Mount_Scopus) Campus of the [Hebrew University of Jerusalem](/wiki/Hebrew_University_of_Jerusalem)

Israel has a tradition of higher education where its university education has been largely responsible in spurring modern economic development. Israel has [nine public universities subsidised by the state and 49 private colleges](/wiki/List_of_Israeli_universities_and_colleges). The [Hebrew University of Jerusalem](/wiki/Hebrew_University_of_Jerusalem) houses the [National Library of Israel](/wiki/National_Library_of_Israel), the world's largest repository of Judaica and Hebraica. The [Technion](/wiki/Technion) and the Hebrew University consistently ranked among world's 100 top universities by [ARWU](/wiki/Academic_Ranking_of_World_Universities) ranking. Other major universities include the [Weizmann Institute of Science](/wiki/Weizmann_Institute_of_Science), [Tel Aviv University](/wiki/Tel_Aviv_University), [Ben-Gurion University of the Negev](/wiki/Ben-Gurion_University_of_the_Negev), [Bar-Ilan University](/wiki/Bar-Ilan_University), the [University of Haifa](/wiki/University_of_Haifa), and the [Open University of Israel](/wiki/Open_University_of_Israel).

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Israel](/wiki/Culture_of_Israel)

Cultural diversity stems from its diverse population: Jews from various diaspora communities brought their cultural and religious traditions with them. [Arab influences](/wiki/Arab_citizens_of_Israel#Culture) are found in [architecture](/wiki/Architecture_of_Israel), [music](/wiki/Music_of_Israel), and [cuisine](/wiki/Israeli_cuisine), among others. Israel is the only country where life revolves around the [Hebrew calendar](/wiki/Hebrew_calendar), which is aligned to the seasons in the Levant. [Public holidays](/wiki/Public_holidays_in_Israel) are determined by the [Jewish holidays](/wiki/Jewish_holidays), and except for [Yom HaAtzma'ut](/wiki/Yom_HaAtzma%27ut) (Independence Day) there are no annual civil holidays. The official day of rest is Saturday, the [Jewish Sabbath](/wiki/Shabbat).

### Literature

[Israeli literature](/wiki/Israeli_literature) is primarily [poetry](/wiki/Modern_Hebrew_poetry) and prose written in Hebrew, as part of the [renaissance](/wiki/Revival_of_the_Hebrew_language) of Hebrew as a spoken language since the mid-19th century, although a small body of literature is published in other languages. By law, two copies of all works published in Israel must be deposited in the [National Library](/wiki/National_Library_of_Israel). In 2016, 89 per cent of the 7,300 books transferred to the library were in Hebrew.

In 1966, [Shmuel Yosef Agnon](/wiki/Shmuel_Yosef_Agnon) shared the [Nobel Prize in Literature](/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature) with German Jewish author [Nelly Sachs](/wiki/Nelly_Sachs). Leading poets include [Yehuda Amichai](/wiki/Yehuda_Amichai), [Nathan Alterman](/wiki/Nathan_Alterman), [Leah Goldberg](/wiki/Leah_Goldberg), and [Rachel Bluwstein](/wiki/Rachel_Bluwstein). Internationally famous contemporary novelists include [Amos Oz](/wiki/Amos_Oz), [Etgar Keret](/wiki/Etgar_Keret) and [David Grossman](/wiki/David_Grossman).

### Music and dance

Further information: [Music of Israel](/wiki/Music_of_Israel) and [Dance in Israel](/wiki/Dance_in_Israel)

[Israel Philharmonic Orchestra](/wiki/Israel_Philharmonic_Orchestra) conducted by [Zubin Mehta](/wiki/Zubin_Mehta)

[Israeli music](/wiki/Music_of_Israel) includes [Mizrahi](/wiki/Mizrahi_music) and [Sephardic music](/wiki/Sephardic_music), [Hasidic](/wiki/Hasidic_Judaism) melodies, [Greek music](/wiki/Greek_music_in_Israel), [jazz](/wiki/Jazz), and [pop rock](/wiki/Pop_rock). The [Israel Philharmonic Orchestra](/wiki/Israel_Philharmonic_Orchestra) has been in operation for over seventy years and performs more than two hundred concerts each year. [Itzhak Perlman](/wiki/Itzhak_Perlman), [Pinchas Zukerman](/wiki/Pinchas_Zukerman) and [Ofra Haza](/wiki/Ofra_Haza) are among the internationally acclaimed musicians born in Israel. The country [has participated](/wiki/Israel_in_the_Eurovision_Song_Contest) in the [Eurovision Song Contest](/wiki/Eurovision_Song_Contest) nearly every year since 1973, winning it four times and hosting three times. [Eilat](/wiki/Eilat) has hosted its own international music festival, the [Red Sea Jazz Festival](/wiki/Red_Sea_Jazz_Festival), every summer since 1987. The nation's canonical [folk songs](/wiki/Folk_music) are known as "Songs of the Land of Israel".

### Cinema and theatre

Main article: [Cinema of Israel](/wiki/Cinema_of_Israel)

Ten Israeli films [have been final nominees](/wiki/List_of_Israeli_submissions_for_the_Academy_Award_for_Best_Foreign_Language_Film) for [Best Foreign Language Film](/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Foreign_Language_Film) at the [Academy Awards](/wiki/Academy_Awards). Palestinian Israeli filmmakers have made films dealing with the Arab-Israeli conflict and status of Palestinians within Israel, such as [Mohammad Bakri](/wiki/Mohammad_Bakri)'s 2002 film *[Jenin, Jenin](/wiki/Jenin,_Jenin)* and *[The Syrian Bride](/wiki/The_Syrian_Bride)*.

Continuing the strong theatrical traditions of the [Yiddish theatre](/wiki/Yiddish_theatre) in Eastern Europe, Israel maintains a vibrant theatre scene. Founded in 1918, [Habima Theatre](/wiki/Habima_Theatre) in Tel Aviv is Israel's oldest [repertory theatre](/wiki/Repertory_theatre) company and national theatre. Other theatres include [Ohel](/wiki/Ohel_Theater), [the Cameri](/wiki/Cameri_Theatre) and [Gesher](/wiki/Gesher_Theater).

### Visual arts

Main article: [Visual arts in Israel](/wiki/Visual_arts_in_Israel)

*[The Roaring Lion](/wiki/The_Roaring_Lion_(sculpture))* sculpture by [Avraham Melnikov](/wiki/Avraham_Melnikov)

Israeli Jewish art has been particularly influenced by the [Kabbalah](/wiki/Kabbalah), the [Talmud](/wiki/Talmud) and the [Zohar](/wiki/Zohar). Another art movement that held a prominent role in the 20th century was the [School of Paris](/wiki/School_of_Paris). In the late 19th and early 20th century, the Yishuv's art was dominated by art trends emanating [Bezalel](/wiki/Bezalel_Academy_of_Arts_and_Design). Beginning in the 1920s, the local art scene was heavily influenced by modern French art, first introduced by [Isaac Frenkel Frenel](/wiki/Yitzhak_Frenkel). Jewish masters of the [school of Paris](/wiki/School_of_Paris), such as [Soutine](/wiki/Cha%C3%AFm_Soutine), [Kikoine](/wiki/Michel_Kikoine), [Frenkel](/wiki/Yitzhak_Frenkel), [Chagall](/wiki/Marc_Chagall) heavily influenced the development of Israeli art. Israeli sculpture took inspiration from modern [European sculpture](/wiki/Art_of_Europe) as well [Mesopotamian](/wiki/Art_of_Mesopotamia), [Assyrian](/wiki/Assyrian_sculpture) and local art. [Avraham Melnikov](/wiki/Avraham_Melnikov)'s [The Roaring Lion](/wiki/The_Roaring_Lion_(sculpture)), David Polus' Alexander Zaid and [Ze'ev Ben Zvi](/wiki/Zeev_Ben-Zvi)'s cubist sculpture exemplify some of the different streams in sculpture.

Common themes in art are the mystical cities of Safed and Jerusalem, the bohemian café culture of Tel Aviv, agricultural landscapes, biblical stories and war. Today Israeli art has delved into [optical art](/wiki/Optical_art), [AI art](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_art), [digital art](/wiki/Digital_art) and the use of salt in sculpture.

### Architecture

Main article: [Architecture of Israel](/wiki/Architecture_of_Israel)

[Bauhaus Museum Tel Aviv](/wiki/Bauhaus_Foundation_Tel_Aviv)

Due to the immigration of Jewish architects, architecture has come to reflect different styles. In the early 20th century Jewish architects sought to combine Occidental and Oriental architecture producing buildings that showcase a myriad of infused styles. The [eclectic](/wiki/Eclecticism_in_architecture) style gave way to the modernist [Bauhaus](/wiki/Bauhaus) style with the influx of German Jewish architects (among them [Erich Mendelsohn](/wiki/Erich_Mendelsohn)) fleeing [Nazi persecution](/wiki/Nazi_persecution_of_Jews). The [White City of Tel Aviv](/wiki/White_City,_Tel_Aviv) is a [UNESCO heritage site](/wiki/UNESCO_Heritage_site). Following independence, multiple government projects were commissioned, a grand part built in a brutalist style with heavy emphasis on the use of concrete and acclimatisation to the desert climate.

Several novel ideas such as the [Garden City](/wiki/Garden_city_movement) were implemented in Israeli cities; the [Geddes plan](/wiki/Geddes_Plan_for_Tel_Aviv) of Tel Aviv became renowned internationally for its revolutionary design and adaptation to the local climate. The design of kibbutzim also came to reflect ideology, such as the planning of the circular kibbutz [Nahalal](/wiki/Nahalal) by [Richard Kauffmann](/wiki/Richard_Kauffmann).

### Museums

For a more comprehensive list, see [List of Israeli museums](/wiki/List_of_Israeli_museums).

[Shrine of the Book](/wiki/Shrine_of_the_Book), repository of the [Dead Sea Scrolls](/wiki/Dead_Sea_Scrolls) in Jerusalem

The [Israel Museum](/wiki/Israel_Museum) in Jerusalem is one of Israel's most important cultural institutions, and houses the [Dead Sea Scrolls](/wiki/Dead_Sea_Scrolls), along with an extensive collection of [Judaica](/wiki/Judaica) and [European art](/wiki/European_art). [Yad Vashem](/wiki/Yad_Vashem) ([Hebrew](/wiki/Hebrew_language): יָד וַשֵׁם, lit. 'a memorial and a name') is the world's central [Holocaust](/wiki/Holocaust) memorial institution and archive of Holocaust-related information. [ANU - Museum of the Jewish People](/wiki/ANU_-_Museum_of_the_Jewish_People) is an interactive museum devoted to the history of Jewish communities around the world.

Israel has the highest number of museums per capita. Several museums are devoted to Islamic culture, including the [Rockefeller Archaeological Museum](/wiki/Rockefeller_Museum) and the [L. A. Mayer Institute for Islamic Art](/wiki/L._A._Mayer_Institute_for_Islamic_Art), both in Jerusalem. The Rockefeller specialises in archaeological remains from Middle East history. It is also the home of the first hominid fossil skull found in West Asia, called [Galilee Man](/wiki/Galilee_Man).

### Mass media

Main article: [Mass media in Israel](/wiki/Mass_media_in_Israel)

Media is diverse. Notable newspapers include the left-wing *[Haaretz](/wiki/Haaretz)*, centrist *[Yedioth Ahronoth](/wiki/Yedioth_Ahronoth)*, and center-right *[Israel Hayom](/wiki/Israel_Hayom)*. There are several major television channels which cater to different audiences, from Russian-language [Channel 9](/wiki/Israel_Plus) to Arabic-language [Kan 33](/wiki/Kan_33). The 2024 Freedom House report found Israeli media is "vibrant and free to criticise government policy". In the 2024 [Press Freedom Index](/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index) by [Reporters Without Borders](/wiki/Reporters_Without_Borders), Israel was placed 112th of 180 countries, second in the Middle East and North Africa.

Reporters Without Borders reported that the Israel Defence Forces had killed more than 100 Palestinian journalists in Gaza. During the 2023 Gaza war, Israel has been alleged to be "trying to suppress the reporting coming out of the besieged enclave while disinformation infiltrates its own media ecosystem". In May 2024, Israel shut down the local offices of [Al Jazeera](/wiki/Al_Jazeera). In 2024, according to the [Committee to Protect Journalists](/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists), Israel was the second leading country in jailing journalists, and responsible for the majority of journalists killed in the world.

### Cuisine

Main article: [Israeli cuisine](/wiki/Israeli_cuisine)

A meal including [falafel](/wiki/Falafel), [hummus](/wiki/Hummus), [French fries](/wiki/French_fries) and [Israeli salad](/wiki/Israeli_salad)

Israeli cuisine includes local dishes as well as [Jewish cuisine](/wiki/Jewish_cuisine) brought to the country by immigrants. Particularly since the late 1970s, a [fusion cuisine](/wiki/Fusion_cuisine) has developed. The cuisine has adapted elements of the [Mizrahi](/wiki/Mizrahi_Jewish_cuisine), [Sephardi](/wiki/Sephardic_Jewish_cuisine), and [Ashkenazi](/wiki/Ashkenazi_cuisine) styles of cooking. It incorporates many foods traditionally eaten in the [Levantine](/wiki/Levantine_cuisine), [Arab](/wiki/Arab_cuisine), [Middle Eastern](/wiki/Middle_Eastern_cuisine) and [Mediterranean](/wiki/Mediterranean_cuisine) cuisines, such as [falafel](/wiki/Falafel), [hummus](/wiki/Hummus), [shakshouka](/wiki/Shakshouka), [couscous](/wiki/Couscous), and [za'atar](/wiki/Za%27atar). [Schnitzel](/wiki/Schnitzel), [pizza](/wiki/Pizza), [hamburgers](/wiki/Hamburger), [French fries](/wiki/French_fries), [rice](/wiki/Rice) and [salad](/wiki/Salad) are common. [Ptitim](/wiki/Israeli_couscous) (Israeli couscous) is a notable Israeli food invented in the 1950s due to rice shortages during the [austerity period](/wiki/Austerity_in_Israel).

Roughly half of the Jewish population attests to keeping [kosher](/wiki/Kosher) at home. [Kosher restaurants](/wiki/Kosher_restaurant) make up around a quarter of the total as of 2015. Pork—often called "white meat" in Israel—is produced and consumed despite attempts to ban it; [it is forbidden](/wiki/Religious_restrictions_on_the_consumption_of_pork) by both Judaism and Islam but is permitted by Christianity and mostly produced in traditionally Christian areas of northern Israel. Other non-kosher foods produced and eaten in Israel include rabbits, ostriches, and non-kosher fish.

### Sports

Main article: [Sport in Israel](/wiki/Sport_in_Israel)

[Maccabi Haifa F.C.](/wiki/Maccabi_Haifa_F.C.) fans at [Sammy Ofer Stadium](/wiki/Sammy_Ofer_Stadium) in the city of Haifa

The most popular spectator sports in Israel are association football and basketball. The [Israeli Premier League](/wiki/Israeli_Premier_League) is the country's premier football league, and the [Israeli Basketball Premier League](/wiki/Israeli_Basketball_Premier_League) is the premier basketball league. [Maccabi Haifa](/wiki/Maccabi_Haifa_F.C.), [Maccabi Tel Aviv](/wiki/Maccabi_Tel_Aviv_F.C.), [Hapoel Tel Aviv](/wiki/Hapoel_Tel_Aviv_F.C.) and [Beitar Jerusalem](/wiki/Beitar_Jerusalem_F.C.) are the largest [football clubs](/wiki/List_of_football_clubs_in_Israel). Maccabi Tel Aviv, Maccabi Haifa and Hapoel Tel Aviv have competed in the [UEFA Champions League](/wiki/UEFA_Champions_League) and Hapoel Tel Aviv reached the [UEFA Cup](/wiki/UEFA_Cup) quarter-finals. Israel hosted and won the [1964 AFC Asian Cup](/wiki/1964_AFC_Asian_Cup); in 1970 the [Israel national football team](/wiki/Israel_national_football_team) qualified for the [FIFA World Cup](/wiki/1970_FIFA_World_Cup), the only time it participated. The [1974 Asian Games](/wiki/1974_Asian_Games), held in Tehran, were the last Asian Games in which Israel [participated](/wiki/Israel_at_the_Asian_Games), plagued by Arab countries that [refused to compete](/wiki/Boycotts_of_Israel_in_sports) with Israel. Israel was excluded from the [1978 Asian Games](/wiki/1978_Asian_Games) and since then has not competed in Asian sport events. In 1994, [UEFA](/wiki/UEFA) agreed to admit Israel, and its football teams now compete in Europe. [Maccabi Tel Aviv B.C.](/wiki/Maccabi_Tel_Aviv_B.C.) has won the [European championship](/wiki/FIBA_European_Champions_Cup_and_EuroLeague_records_and_statistics) in basketball six times.

Israel has won [20 Olympic medals](/wiki/Israel_at_the_Olympics) since its first win [in 1992](/wiki/1992_Summer_Olympics), including a gold medal in [windsurfing](/wiki/Sailing_at_the_2004_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Men%27s_Mistral_One_Design) at the [2004 Summer Olympics](/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics), and seven medals at the 2024 Paris Olympics alone. Israel has won [over 100](/wiki/Israel_at_the_Paralympics) gold medals in the [Paralympic Games](/wiki/Paralympic_Games) and is ranked 20th in the [all-time medal count](/wiki/All-time_Paralympic_Games_medal_table). The [1968 Summer Paralympics](/wiki/1968_Summer_Paralympics) were hosted by Israel. The [Maccabiah Games](/wiki/Maccabiah_Games), an Olympic-style event for [Jewish](/wiki/List_of_Jews_in_sports) and Israeli athletes, was inaugurated in the 1930s, and has been held every four years since. [Krav Maga](/wiki/Krav_Maga), a martial art developed by Jewish ghetto defenders, is used by the Israeli security forces and police.

[Chess](/wiki/Chess) is a leading sport. There are many Israeli grandmasters and [Israeli chess players](/wiki/List_of_Israeli_chess_players) have won a number of youth world championships. Israel stages an annual international [championship](/wiki/Israeli_Chess_Championship) and hosted the [World Team Chess Championship](/wiki/World_Team_Chess_Championship) in 2005.
