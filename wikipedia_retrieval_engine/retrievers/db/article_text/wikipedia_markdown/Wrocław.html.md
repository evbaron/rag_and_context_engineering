**Wrocław** (Polish: [[ˈvrɔt͡swaf]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Polish) [ⓘ](/wiki/File:Pl-Wroc%C5%82aw-2.ogg); [German](/wiki/German_language): *Breslau* [[ˈbʁɛslaʊ]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German) [ⓘ](/wiki/File:De-Breslau2.ogg); also known by [other names](#Names_and_etymology)) is a [city](/wiki/City) in southwestern [Poland](/wiki/Poland), and the capital of the [Lower Silesian Voivodeship](/wiki/Lower_Silesian_Voivodeship). It is the largest city and historical capital of the region of [Silesia](/wiki/Silesia). It lies on the banks of the [Oder](/wiki/Oder) (Odra) River in the [Silesian Lowlands](/wiki/Silesian_Lowlands). In 2025, the official population of Wrocław was 672,545, making it the third-largest city in Poland. The population of the [Wrocław metropolitan area](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_metropolitan_area) is around 1.25 million.

Wrocław is the historical capital of Silesia and [Lower Silesia](/wiki/Lower_Silesia). The history of the city dates back over 1,000 years; throughout history it has been under Polish, [Bohemian](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Bohemia), [Austrian](/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy), [Prussian](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) and [German](/wiki/German_Reich) rule, until it became again part of Poland in 1945 immediately after [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II).

Wrocław is a [university city](/wiki/College_town) with a student population of over 130,000, making it one of the most youth-oriented cities in the country. Wrocław has numerous historical landmarks, including the [Main Market Square](/wiki/Market_Square,_Wroc%C5%82aw), [Cathedral Island](/wiki/Ostr%C3%B3w_Tumski,_Wroc%C5%82aw), [Bridge of Love](/wiki/Tumski_Bridge), [Wrocław Opera](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Opera), the [National Museum](/wiki/National_Museum,_Wroc%C5%82aw) and the [Centennial Hall](/wiki/Hala_Stulecia_(Wroc%C5%82aw)), which is listed as a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](/wiki/UNESCO_World_Heritage_Site). The [Wrocław's dwarfs](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw%27s_dwarfs) are a major tourist attraction and have become a symbol of Wrocław. The city is home to the [Wrocław Zoo](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Zoo), the oldest zoological garden in Poland.

Wrocław is classified as a [Sufficiency global city](/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network#Sufficiency) by [GaWC](/wiki/Globalization_and_World_Cities_Research_Network). It was ranked 1st among all medium and small cities in the 2021 European Cities and Regions of the Future ranking by [fDi Intelligence](/wiki/FDi_Intelligence). The city is home to [Śląsk Wrocław](/wiki/%C5%9Al%C4%85sk_Wroc%C5%82aw) football club and hosted the [2012 European Football Championship](/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2012). In 2016, the city was a [European Capital of Culture](/wiki/European_Capital_of_Culture) and the [World Book Capital](/wiki/World_Book_Capital), and hosted the [Theatre Olympics](/wiki/Theatre_Olympics) and the [European Film Awards](/wiki/European_Film_Awards). In 2017, the city was host to the [World Games](/wiki/World_Games). In 2019, it was named a [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) [City of Literature](/wiki/City_of_Literature).

## Names and etymology

See also: [Names of Wrocław in different languages](/wiki/Names_of_Wroc%C5%82aw_in_different_languages)

The [Coat of arms of Wrocław](/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Wroc%C5%82aw), with the inscription *Civitas Wratislaviensis*, in [Lauf Castle](/wiki/Lauf_Castle), c. 1360

The origin of the city's name is debated. It was long believed that the city was named after Duke [Vratislav I of Bohemia](/wiki/Vratislaus_I,_Duke_of_Bohemia) from the [Czech](/wiki/Czechs) [Přemyslid dynasty](/wiki/P%C5%99emyslid_dynasty), who was ruling between 915 and 921. This thesis is contradicted, however, by the fact that the Czechs only took control of the Wrocław [gród](/wiki/Gr%C3%B3d) around the year 945.

The earliest recorded mentions of the city's name, found in [Thietmar's Chronicle](/wiki/Thietmar%27s_Chronicle) (*Wrotizla*, *Wordisclavia*, *Wortizlania*, *Vaurtizlau*), written around the year 1000, indicate the phonetic form of the original name of the city as *Wrocisław*, derived from the Old Polish given name Wrocisław/Warcisław. According to researchers, this was the name of the city's founder, whom Edmund Małachowicz identified as a duke from the Włost family of Niemcza.

As a result of phonetic changes, the name was later shortened to *Wrocław*, first recorded in 1175 in the Latinized form *Wrezlaw*, and it became widespread in the 13th century. Also in the 12th century, a Czech-influenced and Latinized form, *Vratislavia*, began to appear. The city's first municipal seal was inscribed with *Sigillum civitatis Wratislavie*.

By the 15th century, the [Early New High German](/wiki/Early_New_High_German) variations of the name, *Breslau*, first began to be used. Despite the noticeable differences in spelling, the numerous German forms were still based on the original [West Slavic](/wiki/West_Slavic_languages) name of the city, with the *Vr-* sound being replaced over time by *Br-*, and the suffix *-slav* replaced with *-slau*. These variations included *Wrotizla*, *Vratizlau*, *Wratislau*, *Wrezlau*, *Breßlau* or *Bresslau* among others. A [Prussian](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) description from 1819 mentions two names of the city – Polish and German – stating *"Breslau (polnisch Wraclaw)".*

In other languages, the city's name is: [German](/wiki/German_language): *Breslau* [[ˈbʁɛslaʊ]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German) [ⓘ](/wiki/File:De-Breslau2.ogg); [Silesian German](/wiki/Silesian_German): *Brassel*; [Yiddish](/wiki/Yiddish_language): ברעסלוי, [romanized](/wiki/Romanization_of_Yiddish): *Bresloy*; [Silesian](/wiki/Silesian_language): *Wrocław*; modern [Czech](/wiki/Czech_language): *Vratislav* [[ˈvracɪslaf]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Czech); [Hungarian](/wiki/Hungarian_language): *Boroszló* [[ˈborosloː]](/wiki/Help:IPA/Hungarian); and [Latin](/wiki/Latin_language): *Wratislavia* or *Vratislavia*.

People born or resident in the city are known as "[Wrocławians](/wiki/List_of_people_from_Wroc%C5%82aw)" or "Vratislavians" ([Polish](/wiki/Polish_language): *wrocławianie*). The now little-used German equivalent is "[Breslauer](/wiki/List_of_people_from_Breslau)".

## History

Main articles: [History of Wrocław](/wiki/History_of_Wroc%C5%82aw) and [Timeline of Wrocław](/wiki/Timeline_of_Wroc%C5%82aw)

In [ancient](/wiki/Ancient_history) times, there was a place called Budorigum at or near the site of Wrocław. It was already mapped on [Claudius Ptolemy](/wiki/Ptolemy)'s map of AD 142–147. Settlements in the area existed from the 6th century onward during the [migration period](/wiki/Migration_period). The [Ślężans](/wiki/%C5%9Al%C4%99%C5%BCanie), a [West Slavic](/wiki/West_Slavs) tribe, settled on the [Oder river](/wiki/Oder_river) and erected a fortified [gord](/wiki/Gord_(archaeology)) on [Ostrów Tumski](/wiki/Ostr%C3%B3w_Tumski_in_Wroc%C5%82aw).

Wrocław originated at the intersection of two [trade routes](/wiki/Trade_route), the [Via Regia](/wiki/Via_Regia) and the [Amber Road](/wiki/Amber_Road). Archeological research conducted in the city indicates that it was founded around 940. In 985, Duke [Mieszko I of Poland](/wiki/Mieszko_I_of_Poland) conquered Silesia, and constructed new fortifications on Ostrów. The town was mentioned by Thietmar explicitly in the year [1000 AD](/wiki/AD_1000) in connection with its promotion to an [episcopal see](/wiki/Episcopal_see) during the [Congress of Gniezno](/wiki/Congress_of_Gniezno).

### Middle Ages

[St Martin's Church](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamek_na_Ostrowie_Tumskim_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), the only remaining part of a medieval [Piast](/wiki/Piast) castle

During Wrocław's early history, control over it changed hands between the [Duchy of Bohemia](/wiki/Duchy_of_Bohemia) (1038–1054), the [Duchy of Poland](/wiki/Duchy_of_Poland_(c._960%E2%80%931025)) and the [Kingdom of Poland](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland_(1025%E2%80%931385)) (985–1038 and 1054–1320). Following the [fragmentation of the Kingdom of Poland](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(966%E2%80%931385)), the [Piast dynasty](/wiki/Piast_dynasty) ruled the [Duchy of Silesia](/wiki/Duchies_of_Silesia). One of the most important events during this period was the foundation of the [Diocese of Wrocław](/wiki/Diocese_of_Wroc%C5%82aw) in 1000. Along with the [Bishoprics](/wiki/Diocese) of [Kraków](/wiki/Bishopric_of_Krak%C3%B3w) and [Kołobrzeg](/wiki/Bishopric_of_Ko%C5%82obrzeg), Wrocław was placed under the [Archbishopric of Gniezno](/wiki/Archbishopric_of_Gniezno) in [Greater Poland](/wiki/Greater_Poland), founded by [Pope Sylvester II](/wiki/Pope_Sylvester_II) through the intercession of Polish duke (and later king) [Bolesław I the Brave](/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_I_the_Brave) and Emperor [Otto III](/wiki/Otto_III), during the Gniezno Congress. In the years 1034–1038 the city was affected by the [pagan reaction in Poland](/wiki/Pagan_reaction_in_Poland).

The city became a commercial centre and expanded to [Wyspa Piasek](/wiki/Wyspa_Piasek) (Sand Island), and then onto the left bank of the [River Oder](/wiki/River_Oder). Around 1000, the town had about 1,000 inhabitants. In 1109 during the [Polish-German war](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wojna_polsko-niemiecka_1109), Prince [Bolesław III Wrymouth](/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_III_Wrymouth) defeated the King of Germany [Henry V](/wiki/Henry_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) at the [Battle of Hundsfeld](/wiki/Battle_of_Hundsfeld), stopping the German advance into Poland. The medieval chronicle, *[Gesta principum Polonorum](/wiki/Gesta_principum_Polonorum)* (1112–1116) by [Gallus Anonymus](/wiki/Gallus_Anonymus), named Wrocław, along with [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w) and [Sandomierz](/wiki/Sandomierz), as one of three capitals of the Polish Kingdom. Also, the *[Tabula Rogeriana](/wiki/Tabula_Rogeriana)*, a book written by the Arab geographer Muhammad al-Idrisi in 1154, describes Wrocław as one of the Polish cities, alongside Kraków, [Gniezno](/wiki/Gniezno), [Sieradz](/wiki/Sieradz), [Łęczyca](/wiki/%C5%81%C4%99czyca) and [Santok](/wiki/Santok). By 1139, a settlement belonging to Governor [Piotr Włostowic](/wiki/Piotr_W%C5%82ostowic) (also known as Piotr Włast [Dunin](/wiki/Dunin_(surname))) was built, and another on the left bank of the River Oder, near the present site of the university. While the city was largely Polish, it also had communities of Bohemians (Czechs), [Germans](/wiki/Germany), [Walloons](/wiki/Walloons) and [Jews](/wiki/Jews).

[Church of Saint Giles (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko%C5%9Bci%C3%B3%C5%82_%C5%9Bw._Idziego_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu) erected in the 1220s at [Ostrów Tumski](/wiki/Ostr%C3%B3w_Tumski,_Wroc%C5%82aw) (Cathedral Island), the city's oldest section

In the 13th century, Wrocław was the political centre of the divided Polish kingdom. In April 1241, during the [first Mongol invasion of Poland](/wiki/First_Mongol_invasion_of_Poland), the city was abandoned by its inhabitants and burnt down for strategic reasons. During the battles with the Mongols [Wrocław Castle](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamek_na_Ostrowie_Tumskim_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu) was successfully defended by [Henry II the Pious](/wiki/Henry_II_the_Pious). In 1245, in Wrocław, [Franciscan](/wiki/Franciscans) friar [Benedict of Poland](/wiki/Benedict_of_Poland), considered one of the first Polish explorers, joined Italian diplomat [Giovanni da Pian del Carpine](/wiki/Giovanni_da_Pian_del_Carpine), on his journey to the seat of the Mongol Khan near [Karakorum](/wiki/Karakorum), the capital of the [Mongol Empire](/wiki/Mongol_Empire), in what is considered the first such journey by Europeans.

After the Mongol invasion, the town was partly populated by [German settlers](/wiki/Ostsiedlung) who, in the ensuing centuries, gradually became its dominant population. The city, however, retained its multi-ethnic character, a reflection of its importance as a trading post on the junction of the Via Regia and the Amber Road. With the influx of settlers, the town expanded and in 1242 came under [German town law](/wiki/German_town_law). The [city council](/wiki/City_council) used both Latin and [German](/wiki/German_language), and the early forms of the name *Breslau*, the German name of the city, appeared for the first time in its written records. Polish gradually ceased to be used in the town books, while it survived in the courts until 1337, when it was banned by the new rulers, the German-speaking [House of Luxembourg](/wiki/House_of_Luxembourg).

The oldest printed text in the [Polish language](/wiki/Polish_language)–*Statuta Synodalia Episcoporum Wratislaviensis*, printed in Wrocław, 1475

The enlarged town covered around 60 hectares (150 acres), and the new [main market square](/wiki/Market_Square,_Wroc%C5%82aw), surrounded by timber-frame houses, became the trade centre of the town. The original foundation, [Ostrów Tumski](/wiki/Ostr%C3%B3w_Tumski,_Wroc%C5%82aw), became its religious centre. The city gained [Magdeburg rights](/wiki/Magdeburg_rights) in 1261. While the Polish Piast dynasty remained in control of the region, the city council's ability to [govern](/wiki/Self-Government) independently had increased. The main source of prosperity was trade between Poland and Ruthenia and Western Europe, and crafts also contributed to the city's wealth. In 1274 prince [Henry IV Probus](/wiki/Henry_IV_Probus) gave the city its [staple right](/wiki/Staple_right). In the 13th century, two Polish monarchs were buried in Wrocław churches founded by them, Henry II the Pious in the [St. Vincent church](/wiki/Cathedral_of_St._Vincent_and_St._James,_Wroc%C5%82aw) and Henryk IV Probus in the [Holy Cross church](/wiki/Collegiate_Church_of_the_Holy_Cross_and_St_Bartholomew,_Wroc%C5%82aw).

Wrocław, which for 350 years had been mostly under Polish [hegemony](/wiki/Hegemony), fell in 1335, after the death of [Henry VI the Good](/wiki/Henry_VI_the_Good), to [John of Luxembourg](/wiki/John_of_Bohemia). His son Emperor [Charles IV](/wiki/Charles_IV,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) in 1348 formally incorporated the city into the [Holy Roman Empire](/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire). Between 1342 and 1344, two fires destroyed large parts of the city. Despite the annexation by Bohemia, trade still took place mainly between Poland and Western Europe. In 1387 the city joined the [Hanseatic League](/wiki/Hanseatic_League). On 5 June 1443, the city was rocked by an earthquake, estimated at magnitude 6, which destroyed or seriously damaged many of its buildings.

Between 1469 and 1490, Wrocław was ruled by [Matthias Corvinus](/wiki/Matthias_Corvinus), who claimed the title [King of Bohemia](/wiki/King_of_Bohemia) and ruled parts of the [Bohemian Crown Lands](/wiki/Bohemian_Crown_Lands). Corvinus was said to have had a Vratislavian mistress who bore him a son. In 1474, after almost a century, the city left the Hanseatic League. Also in 1474, the city was besieged by combined Polish-Czech forces. However, in November 1474, Kings [Casimir IV of Poland](/wiki/Casimir_IV_Jagiellon), his son [Vladislaus II of Bohemia](/wiki/Vladislaus_II_of_Hungary), and Matthias Corvinus of Hungary met in the nearby village of [Muchobór Wielki](/wiki/Muchob%C3%B3r_Wielki) (present-day a district of Wrocław), and in December 1474 a [ceasefire](/wiki/Ceasefire) was signed according to which the city remained under the rule of Corvinus. The following year was marked by the publication in Wrocław of the *Statuta Synodalia Episcoporum Wratislaviensium* (1475) by Kasper Elyan, the first ever [incunable](/wiki/Incunable) in Polish, containing the proceedings and prayers of the Wrocław bishops.

### Renaissance and the Reformation

A map of the city from 1562, with its fortifications on the [Oder River](/wiki/Oder_River)

In the 16th century, the Breslauer Schöps [beer style](/wiki/Beer_style) was created in Breslau.

The [Protestant Reformation](/wiki/Protestant_Reformation) reached the city in 1518 and it converted to the new rite. However, starting in 1526 [Silesia](/wiki/Silesia) was ruled by the Catholic [House of Habsburg](/wiki/House_of_Habsburg). In 1618, it supported the [Bohemian Revolt](/wiki/Bohemian_Revolt) out of fear of losing the right to [religious freedom](/wiki/Freedom_of_religion). During the ensuing [Thirty Years' War](/wiki/Thirty_Years%27_War), the city was occupied by [Saxon](/wiki/Electorate_of_Saxony) and [Swedish](/wiki/Swedish_Empire) troops and lost thousands of inhabitants to the [plague](/wiki/Plague_(disease)).

The [Emperor](/wiki/Holy_Roman_Emperor) brought in the [Counter-Reformation](/wiki/Counter-Reformation) by encouraging Catholic orders to settle in the city, starting in 1610 with the [Franciscans](/wiki/Franciscan), followed by the [Jesuits](/wiki/Jesuit), then [Capuchins](/wiki/Order_of_Friars_Minor_Capuchin), and finally [Ursuline nuns](/wiki/Ursulines) in 1687. These orders erected buildings that shaped the city's appearance until 1945. At the end of the Thirty Years' War, however, it was one of only a few Silesian cities to stay Protestant.

The Polish Municipal school opened in 1666 and lasted until 1766. Precise record-keeping of births and deaths by the city fathers led to the use of their data for analysis of mortality, first by [John Graunt](/wiki/John_Graunt) and then, based on data provided to him by Breslau professor [Caspar Neumann](/wiki/Caspar_Neumann), by [Edmond Halley](/wiki/Edmond_Halley). Halley's tables and analysis, published in 1693, are considered to be the first true actuarial tables, and thus the foundation of modern [actuarial science](/wiki/Actuarial_science). During the Counter-Reformation, the intellectual life of the city flourished, as the Protestant [bourgeoisie](/wiki/Bourgeois) lost some of its dominance to the Catholic orders as patrons of the arts.

### Enlightenment period

The [Battle of Breslau](/wiki/Battle_of_Breslau_(1757)) during the [Seven Years' War](/wiki/Seven_Years%27_War) ([Third Silesian War](/wiki/Third_Silesian_War) 1756–1763)

One of two main routes connecting [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw) and [Dresden](/wiki/Dresden) ran through the city in the 18th century and Kings [Augustus II the Strong](/wiki/Augustus_II_the_Strong) and [Augustus III of Poland](/wiki/Augustus_III_of_Poland) often traveled that route. The city became the centre of German [Baroque literature](/wiki/Baroque_literature) and was home to the First and Second Silesian school of poets. In 1742, the [Schlesische Zeitung](/wiki/Schlesische_Zeitung) was founded in Breslau.

In the 1740s the [Kingdom of Prussia](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) annexed the city and most of Silesia during the [War of the Austrian Succession](/wiki/War_of_the_Austrian_Succession). [Habsburg](/wiki/House_of_Habsburg) Empress [Maria Theresa](/wiki/Maria_Theresa) ceded most of the territory in the [Treaty of Breslau](/wiki/Treaty_of_Breslau) in 1742 to Prussia. Austria attempted to recover Silesia during the [Seven Years' War](/wiki/Seven_Years%27_War) at the [Battle of Breslau](/wiki/Battle_of_Breslau_(1757)), but they were unsuccessful. The Venetian Italian adventurer, [Giacomo Casanova](/wiki/Giacomo_Casanova), stayed in Breslau in 1766.

### Napoleonic Wars

The entry of Prince [Jérôme Bonaparte](/wiki/J%C3%A9r%C3%B4me_Bonaparte) into the city, 7 January 1807

During the [Napoleonic Wars](/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars), it was occupied by the [Confederation of the Rhine](/wiki/Confederation_of_the_Rhine) army. The fortifications of the city were levelled, and monasteries and cloisters were seized. The Protestant [Viadrina European University](/wiki/Viadrina_European_University) at [Frankfurt an der Oder](/wiki/Frankfurt_an_der_Oder) was relocated to Breslau in 1811, and united with the local Jesuit University to create the new Silesian Frederick-William University ([German](/wiki/German_language): *Schlesische Friedrich-Wilhelm-Universität*, now the [University of Wrocław](/wiki/University_of_Wroc%C5%82aw)). The city became a centre of the German Liberation movement against Napoleon, and a gathering place for volunteers from all over Germany. The city was the centre of Prussian mobilisation for the campaign which ended at the [Battle of Leipzig](/wiki/Battle_of_Leipzig).

### Industrial age

The [Confederation of the Rhine](/wiki/Confederation_of_the_Rhine) had increased prosperity in Silesia and in the city. The removal of fortifications opened room for the city to expand beyond its former limits. Breslau became an important railway hub and industrial centre, notably for linen and [cotton](/wiki/Cotton) manufacture and the metal industry. The reconstructed university served as a major centre of science; [Johannes Brahms](/wiki/Johannes_Brahms) later wrote his [Academic Festival Overture](/wiki/Academic_Festival_Overture) to thank the university for an honorary doctorate awarded in 1879.

Archbishop's Palace in Wrocław, built in 1794

In 1821, the [(Arch)Diocese of Breslau](/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Wroc%C5%82aw) withdrew from dependence on the Polish [archbishopric of Gniezno](/wiki/Archbishopric_of_Gniezno), and became an exempt see. In 1822, the Prussian police discovered the *Polonia* Polish youth resistance organisation and carried out arrests of its members and searches of their homes. After the unsuccessful Polish [November Uprising](/wiki/November_Uprising), the city was an important contact point between partitioned Poland and the [Great Emigration](/wiki/Great_Emigration) in Western Europe. It also remained an important center of Polish printing. In the 1840s, a local branch of the secret Polish organization Związek Plebejuszy (transl. Union of Plebeians) was active. In 1848, many local Polish students joined the [Greater Poland uprising](/wiki/Greater_Poland_uprising_(1848)) against Prussia. On 5 May 1848, a convention of Polish activists from the Prussian and Austrian partitions of Poland was held in the city. On 10 October 1854, the [Jewish Theological Seminary](/wiki/Jewish_Theological_Seminary_of_Breslau) opened. The institution was the first modern rabbinical seminary in Central Europe. In 1863 the brothers [Carl](/wiki/Carl_Stangen) and Louis Stangen founded the travel agency Stangen, the second travel agency in the world. The city was subjected to Germanisation policies, yet it retained a sizeable Polish population.

The city was an important centre of the [Polish secret resistance movement](/wiki/Resistance_movements_in_partitioned_Poland_(1795%E2%80%931918)) and the seat of a Polish uprising committee before and during the [January Uprising](/wiki/January_Uprising) of 1863–1864 in the [Russian Partition](/wiki/Russian_Partition) of Poland. Local Poles took part in Polish national mourning after the Russian massacre of Polish protesters in [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw) in February 1861, and also organised several patriotic Polish church services throughout 1861. Secret Polish correspondence, weapons, and insurgents were transported through the city.

After the outbreak of the uprising in 1863, the Prussian police carried out mass searches of Polish homes, especially those of Poles who had recently come to the city. The city's inhabitants, both Poles and Germans, excluding the German aristocracy, largely sympathised with the uprising, and some Germans even joined local Poles in their secret activities. In June 1863 the city was officially confirmed as the seat of secret Polish insurgent authorities. In January 1864, the Prussian police arrested a number of members of the Polish insurgent movement.

[Old Town Hall](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Town_Hall), 1900

The [Unification of Germany](/wiki/Unification_of_Germany) in 1871 turned Breslau into the sixth-largest city in the [German Empire](/wiki/German_Empire). Its population more than tripled to over half a million between 1860 and 1910. The 1900 census listed 422,709 residents.

In 1890, construction began of Breslau Fortress as the city's defences. Important landmarks were inaugurated in 1910, the *Kaiser bridge* (today [Grunwald Bridge](/wiki/Grunwald_Bridge)) and the *Technical University*, which now houses the [Wrocław University of Technology](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_University_of_Technology).
The 1910 census listed 95.7% of the population as German-speakers, with 15,107 Polish-speakers (3%), and 3,431 (0.7%) as bilingual in Polish and German, although some estimates put the number of [Poles](/wiki/Polish_people) in the city at the time at 20,000 to 30,000. The population was 58% Protestant, 37% Catholic (including at least 2% Polish) and 5% Jewish (totaling 20,536 in the 1905 census). The [Jewish community](/wiki/Jewish_community) of Breslau was among the most important in Germany, producing several distinguished artists and scientists.

From 1912, the head of the university's Department of Psychiatry and director of the Clinic of Psychiatry (*Königlich Psychiatrischen und Nervenklinik*) was [Alois Alzheimer](/wiki/Alois_Alzheimer) and, that same year, professor [William Stern](/wiki/William_Stern_(psychologist)) introduced the concept of [IQ](/wiki/Intelligence_quotient).

[Market Square](/wiki/Market_Square,_Wroc%C5%82aw), 1890–1900

[Feniks Department Store](/wiki/Barasch_Brothers%27_Department_Store), built in 1902–1904

In 1913, the newly built [Centennial Hall](/wiki/Hala_Stulecia_(Wroc%C5%82aw)) housed an exhibition commemorating the 100th anniversary of the historical [German Wars of Liberation](/wiki/War_of_the_Sixth_Coalition) against [Napoleon](/wiki/Napoleon_I_of_France) and the first award of the [Iron Cross](/wiki/Iron_Cross). The Centennial Hall was built by [Max Berg](/wiki/Max_Berg) (1870–1947), since 2006 it is part of the world heritage of UNESCO. The central station (by [Wilhelm Grapow](/wiki/Wilhelm_Grapow), 1857) was one of the biggest in Germany and one of the first stations with electrified railway services. Since 1900 modern department stores like Barasch (today "Feniks") or Petersdorff (built by architect [Erich Mendelsohn](/wiki/Erich_Mendelsohn)) were erected.

### First World War and interwar period

During [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I), in 1914, a branch of the *Organizacja Pomocy Legionom* ("Legion Assistance Organisation") operated in the city with the goal of gaining support and recruiting volunteers for the [Polish Legion](/wiki/Polish_Legions_in_World_War_I), but three Legions' envoys were arrested by the Germans in November 1914 and deported to Austria, and the organisation soon ended its activities in the city. During the war, the German administration operated seven forced labour camps for [Allied](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I) [prisoners of war](/wiki/World_War_I_prisoners_of_war_in_Germany) in the city.

Following the war, Breslau became the capital of the newly created Prussian [Province of Lower Silesia](/wiki/Province_of_Lower_Silesia) of the [Weimar Republic](/wiki/Weimar_Republic) in 1919. After the war the Polish community began holding masses in Polish at the Church of Saint Anne, and, as of 1921, at St. Martin's and a Polish School was founded by Helena Adamczewska. In 1920 a Polish [consulate](/wiki/Consulate) was opened on the Main Square. In August 1920, during the Polish [Silesian Uprising](/wiki/Silesian_Uprisings) in [Upper Silesia](/wiki/Upper_Silesia), the Polish Consulate and School were destroyed, while the Polish Library was burned down by a mob. The number of Poles as a percentage of the total population fell to just 0.5% after the re-emergence of [Poland](/wiki/History_of_Poland_(1918%E2%80%931939)) as a state in 1918, when many moved to Poland. [Antisemitic](/wiki/Antisemitism) riots occurred in 1923.

The city boundaries were expanded between 1925 and 1930 to include an area of 175 km2 (68 sq mi) with a population of 600,000. In 1929, the [Werkbund](/wiki/Deutscher_Werkbund) opened *[WuWa](/wiki/WUWA_(Breslau))* ([German](/wiki/German_language): *Wohnungs- und Werkraumausstellung*) in Breslau-Scheitnig, an international showcase of [modern architecture](/wiki/Modern_architecture) by architects of the Silesian branch of the Werkbund. In June 1930, Breslau hosted the *[Deutsche Kampfspiele](/wiki/German_Combat_Games)*, a [sporting](/wiki/Sport) event for German athletes after Germany was excluded from the [Olympic Games](/wiki/Olympic_Games) after World War I. The number of Jews remaining in Breslau fell from 23,240 in 1925 to 10,659 in 1933. Up to the beginning of World War II, Breslau was the largest city in Germany east of [Berlin](/wiki/Berlin).

A pre-war aerial view of the city, 1920

Known as a stronghold of [left wing liberalism](/wiki/Left-wing_politics) during the German Empire, Breslau eventually became one of the strongest support bases of the [Nazi Party](/wiki/Nazi_Party), which in the 1932 elections received 44% of the city's vote, their third-highest total in all Germany.

After [Hitler](/wiki/Adolf_Hitler)'s [appointment as German Chancellor](/wiki/NSDAP#After_taking_power:_intertwining_of_party_and_state) in 1933, political enemies of the Nazis were persecuted, and their institutions closed or destroyed. [KZ Dürrgoy](/wiki/KZ_D%C3%BCrrgoy), one of the first concentration camps in [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany), was set up in the city in 1933. The [Gestapo](/wiki/Gestapo) began actions against Polish and Jewish students (see: [Jewish Theological Seminary of Breslau](/wiki/Jewish_Theological_Seminary_of_Breslau)), [Communists](/wiki/KPD), [Social Democrats](/wiki/Social_Democratic_Party_of_Germany), and [trade unionists](/wiki/Trade_unionists). Arrests were made for speaking Polish in public, and in 1938 the Nazi-controlled police destroyed the Polish cultural centre.

In June 1939, Polish students were expelled from the university. Also many other people seen as "undesirable" by Nazi Germany were sent to [concentration camps](/wiki/Nazi_concentration_camps). A network of [concentration camps](/wiki/Internment) and [forced labour camps](/wiki/Arbeitslager) was established around Breslau to serve industrial concerns, including [FAMO](/wiki/FAMO), [Junkers](/wiki/Junkers), and [Krupp](/wiki/Krupp). Tens of thousands of forced labourers were imprisoned there.

The last big event organised by the [National Socialist League of the Reich for Physical Exercise](/wiki/National_Socialist_League_of_the_Reich_for_Physical_Exercise), called [Deutsches Turn-und-Sportfest](/wiki/Deutsches_Turn-_und_Sportfest_1938) (Gym and Sports Festivities), took place in Breslau from 23 to 31 July 1938. The Sportsfest was held to commemorate the 125th anniversary of the German Wars of Liberation against Napoleon's invasion.

### Second World War

Memorials to the [Olimp](/wiki/Olimp_(organization)) resistance organization and to victims of a local [forced labour](/wiki/Forced_labour_under_German_rule_during_World_War_II) camp

During the [invasion of Poland](/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland), which started [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), in September 1939, the Germans carried out mass arrests of local Polish activists and banned Polish organisations, and the city was made the headquarters of the southern district of the *[Selbstschutz](/wiki/Selbstschutz)*, whose task was to [persecute Poles](/wiki/Nazi_crimes_against_the_Polish_nation). In November 1939, the Germans imprisoned 173 Polish professors and lecturers from [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w)'s universities in the city, and then deported them to the [Sachsenhausen concentration camp](/wiki/Sachsenhausen_concentration_camp) (see *[Sonderaktion Krakau](/wiki/Sonderaktion_Krakau)*). For most of the war, the fighting did not affect the city. During the war, the Germans opened the graves of medieval Polish monarchs and local dukes to carry out [anthropological](/wiki/Anthropology) research for [propaganda](/wiki/Propaganda_in_Nazi_Germany) purposes, wanting to demonstrate German "[racial purity](/wiki/Racial_policy_of_Nazi_Germany)". The remains were transported to other places, and they have not been found to this day.

In 1941, the remnants of the pre-war Polish minority in the city, as well as Polish slave labourers, organised a resistance group called [Olimp](/wiki/Olimp_(organization)). The organisation gathered intelligence, carrying out sabotage and organising aid for Polish slave workers. In September 1941 the city's 10,000 Jews were expelled from their homes and soon deported to concentration camps. Few survived the [Holocaust](/wiki/The_Holocaust). As the war continued, refugees from bombed-out German cities, and later refugees from farther east, swelled the population to nearly one million, including 51,000 forced labourers in 1944, and 9,876 Allied PoWs. At the end of 1944 an additional 30,000–60,000 Poles were moved into the city after the Germans crushed the [Warsaw Uprising](/wiki/Warsaw_Uprising).

During the war the German administration operated four [subcamps](/wiki/List_of_subcamps_of_Gross-Rosen) of the [Gross-Rosen concentration camp](/wiki/Gross-Rosen_concentration_camp) in the city. Approximately 3,400–3,800 men were imprisoned in three subcamps, among them Poles, [Russians](/wiki/Russians), [Italians](/wiki/Italians), [Frenchmen](/wiki/French_people), [Ukrainians](/wiki/Ukrainians), [Czechs](/wiki/Czechs), [Belgians](/wiki/Belgians), [Yugoslavs](/wiki/Yugoslavs), [Dutchmen](/wiki/Dutch_people), [Chinese](/wiki/Chinese_people), and about 1,500 [Jewish](/wiki/Jews) women were imprisoned in the fourth camp. Many prisoners died, and the remaining were evacuated to the main camp of Gross-Rosen in January 1945. There were also three subcamps of the [Stalag VIII-B/344](/wiki/Stalag_VIII-B) [prisoner-of-war camp](/wiki/German_prisoner-of-war_camps_in_World_War_II), three subcamps of the [Stalag VIII-C](/wiki/Stalag_VIII-C) POW camp, and two Nazi prisons in the city, including a youth prison, with multiple forced labour subcamps.

Wartime destruction around the cathedral, 1945

In 1945, the city became part of the front lines and was the site of the brutal [Siege of Breslau](/wiki/Siege_of_Breslau). Adolf Hitler had in 1944 declared Breslau to be a fortress ([Festung](/wiki/Festung)), to be held at all costs. An attempted evacuation of the city took place in January 1945, with 18,000 people freezing to death in icy snowstorms of −20 °C (−4 °F) weather. In February 1945, the [Soviet](/wiki/Soviet_Union) [Army](/wiki/Red_Army) approached the city and the German [Luftwaffe](/wiki/Luftwaffe) began an [airlift](/wiki/Airlift) to the besieged garrison. A large area of the city centre was demolished and turned into an airfield by the defenders. By the end of the three-month siege in May 1945, half the city had been destroyed. Breslau was the last major city in Germany to surrender, capitulating only two days before the end of the war in Europe. Civilian deaths amounted to as many as 80,000. In August the Soviets placed the city under the control of German [communists](/wiki/Communists).

Following the [Yalta Conference](/wiki/Yalta_Conference) held in February 1945, where the new [geopolitics](/wiki/Geopolitics) of [Central Europe](/wiki/Central_Europe) were decided, the terms of the [Potsdam Conference](/wiki/Potsdam_Conference) decreed that along with almost all of Lower Silesia, the city would become again part of [Poland](/wiki/Polish_People%27s_Republic) in exchange for Poland's loss of the city of [Lwów](/wiki/Lw%C3%B3w) along with the massive territory of [Kresy](/wiki/Kresy) in the east, which was annexed by the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union). The Polish name of Wrocław was declared official. There had been discussion among the [Western Allies](/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) to place the southern Polish-German boundary on the [Eastern Neisse](/wiki/Eastern_Neisse), which meant post-war Germany would have been allowed to retain approximately half of Silesia, including those parts of Breslau that lay on the west bank of the Oder. However, the Soviet government insisted the border be drawn at the [Lusatian Neisse](/wiki/Lusatian_Neisse) farther west.

### 1945–present

The city's German inhabitants who were not forced to flee, or could not return to their home city after the war had ended, [were expelled](/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_from_Poland_during_and_after_World_War_II) between 1945 and 1949 and were settled in the [Soviet occupation zone](/wiki/Soviet_occupation_zone) or in the [Allied Occupation Zones](/wiki/Allied-occupied_Germany) in the remainder of Germany. The city's last pre-war German school was closed in 1963.

The Polish population was dramatically increased by the resettlement of Poles, partly due to postwar [population transfers](/wiki/Polish_population_transfers_(1944%E2%80%931946)) during the forced [deportations](/wiki/Deportation) from [Polish lands annexed by the Soviet Union](/wiki/Territories_of_Poland_annexed_by_the_Soviet_Union) in [the east](/wiki/Kresy) region, some of whom came from [Lviv](/wiki/Lviv) (*Lwów*), [Volhynia](/wiki/Volhynia), and the [Vilnius Region](/wiki/Vilnius_Region). However, despite the prime role given to re-settlers from the [Kresy](/wiki/Kresy), in 1949, only 20% of the new Polish population actually were refugees themselves. A small German minority (about 1,000 people, or 0.2% of the population) remains in the city, so that today the proportion of the Polish population compared to that of the Germans is the reverse of what it was a hundred years ago. Traces of the German past, such as inscriptions and signs, were removed. In 1948, Wrocław organised the [Recovered Territories](/wiki/Recovered_Territories) Exhibition and the [World Congress of Intellectuals in Defence of Peace](/wiki/World_Congress_of_Intellectuals_in_Defense_of_Peace). [Picasso](/wiki/Picasso)'s lithograph, *La Colombe* (The Dove), a traditional, realistic picture of a pigeon, without an olive branch, was created on a napkin at the [Monopol Hotel](/wiki/Monopol_Hotel) in Wrocław during the [World Congress of Intellectuals in Defence of Peace](/wiki/World_Congress_of_Intellectuals_in_Defense_of_Peace).

In 1963, Wrocław was declared a [closed city](/wiki/Closed_city) because of [a smallpox epidemic](/wiki/1963_smallpox_epidemic_in_Wroc%C5%82aw).

[Fighting Solidarity](/wiki/Fighting_Solidarity) logo

In 1982, during [martial law in Poland](/wiki/Martial_law_in_Poland), the [anti-communist](/wiki/Anti-communism) underground organisations [Fighting Solidarity](/wiki/Fighting_Solidarity) and [Orange Alternative](/wiki/Orange_Alternative) were founded in Wrocław. [Wrocław's dwarves](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw%27s_dwarfs), made of bronze, famously grew out of and commemorate Orange Alternative.

In 1983 and 1997, [Pope John Paul II](/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II) visited the city.

PTV Echo, the first non-state television station in Poland and in the post-communist countries, began to broadcast in Wrocław on 6 February 1990.

A [Wrocław dwarf](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw%27s_dwarfs)

In May 1997, Wrocław hosted the 46th International [Eucharistic Congress](/wiki/Eucharistic_Congress).

In July 1997, the city was heavily affected by the [Millennium Flood](/wiki/1997_Central_European_flood_in_Wroc%C5%82aw), the worst flooding in post-war Poland, Germany, and the [Czech Republic](/wiki/Czech_Republic). About one-third of the area of the city was flooded. The smaller [Widawa River](/wiki/Widawa) also flooded the city simultaneously, worsening the damage. An earlier, equally devastating flood of the Oder river had taken place in 1903.
A small part of the city was also flooded during the [flood of 2010](/wiki/2010_Central_European_floods). From 2012 to 2015, the [Wrocław water node](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wroc%C5%82awski_w%C4%99ze%C5%82_wodny) was renovated and redeveloped to prevent further flooding.

[Municipal Stadium](/wiki/Municipal_Stadium_(Wroc%C5%82aw)) in Wrocław, opened in 2011, hosted three matches in Group A of the [UEFA Euro 2012](/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2012) championship.

In 2016, Wrocław was declared the [European Capital of Culture](/wiki/European_Capital_of_Culture).

In 2017, Wrocław hosted the [2017 World Games](/wiki/2017_World_Games).

Wrocław won the *European Best Destination* title in 2018.

Wrocław is now a unique European city of mixed heritage, with architecture influenced by Polish, [Bohemian](/wiki/Bohemia), [Austrian](/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy), and [Prussian](/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) traditions, such as Silesian [Gothic](/wiki/Gothic_architecture) and its [Baroque](/wiki/Baroque) style of court builders of Habsburg Austria ([Fischer von Erlach](/wiki/Johann_Bernhard_Fischer_von_Erlach)). Wrocław has a number of notable buildings by German [modernist](/wiki/Modernism) architects including the famous [Centennial Hall](/wiki/Hala_Stulecia_(Wroc%C5%82aw)) (1911–1913) designed by [Max Berg](/wiki/Max_Berg).

## Geography

Wrocław is located in the three [mesoregions](/wiki/Mesoregion_(geomorphology)) of the [Silesian Lowlands](/wiki/Silesian_Lowlands) ([Wrocław Plain](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Plain), [Wrocław Valley](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Valley), Oleśnica Plain) at an elevation of around 105–156 metres (Gajowe Hill and Maślickie Hill) above sea level. The city lies on the [Oder](/wiki/Oder) River and its four tributaries, which supply it within the city limits – [Bystrzyca](/wiki/Bystrzyca_(Odra)), [Oława](/wiki/O%C5%82awa_(river)), [Ślęza](/wiki/%C5%9Al%C4%99za) and [Widawa](/wiki/Widawa). The Dobra River and many streams flow through the city. The city has a [sewage treatment](/wiki/Sewage_treatment) plant on the Janówek estate.

### Climate

Within the [Köppen climate classification](/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification), Wrocław has an [oceanic climate](/wiki/Oceanic_climate) (*Cfb*), bordering on a [humid continental climate](/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) (*Dfb*) using the 0 °C (32 °F) isotherm. The position of Wrocław in the [Silesian Lowlands](/wiki/Silesian_Lowlands), which are located just north of the [Sudetes](/wiki/Sudetes) and to the southwest of the [Trzebnickie Hills](/wiki/Trzebnickie_Hills), creates a favourable environment for accumulation of heat in the [Oder](/wiki/Oder) river valley between Wrocław and [Opole](/wiki/Opole). Wrocław is therefore the warmest [city in Poland](/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Poland), among those tracked by the [Institute of Meteorology and Water Management](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instytut_Meteorologii_i_Gospodarki_Wodnej) (IMGW), with a mean annual temperature of 9.7 °C (49 °F).

The city experiences relatively mild and dry winters, but with the skies frequently overcast. Summers are warm and generally sunny, however, that is the period when most precipitation occurs, which often falls during thunderstorms. The city sometimes experiences [foehn](/wiki/Foehn_wind)-like conditions, particularly when the wind blows from the south or the south-west. The temperatures in the city centre often tend to be higher than on the outskirts due to the [urban heat island](/wiki/Urban_heat_island) effect.

Snow may fall in any month from October to May but normally does so in winter. The snow cover of at least 1 cm (0.39 in) stays on the ground for an average of 27.5 days per year – one of the lowest in Poland. The highest temperature in Wrocław recognised by IMGW was noted on 8 August 2015 (37.9 °C (100 °F)), though thermometers at the meteorological station managed by the [University of Wrocław](/wiki/University_of_Wroc%C5%82aw) indicated 38.9 °C (102 °F) on that day. The lowest temperature was recorded on 11 February 1956 (−32 °C (−26 °F)).

## Government and politics

Main article: [List of mayors of Wrocław](/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Wroc%C5%82aw)

Wrocław New City Hall – the seat of the city mayor

Wrocław is the capital city of [Lower Silesian Voivodeship](/wiki/Lower_Silesian_Voivodeship), a province ([voivodeship](/wiki/Voivodeships_of_Poland)) created in 1999. It was previously the capital of [Wrocław Voivodeship](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Voivodeship). The city is a separate urban [gmina](/wiki/Gmina) and [city-county](/wiki/City_with_powiat_rights). It is also the seat of [Wrocław County](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_County), which adjoins but does not include the city.

### Districts

Main article: [Districts of Wrocław](/wiki/Districts_of_Wroc%C5%82aw)

Wrocław was previously subdivided into five [boroughs](/wiki/Dzielnica) ([Polish](/wiki/Polish_language): *dzielnice*): [Old Town](/wiki/Old_Town,_Wroc%C5%82aw_(former_district)), [Downtown](/wiki/Downtown,_Wroc%C5%82aw), [Krzyki](/wiki/Krzyki), [Fabryczna](/wiki/Fabryczna), and [Psie Pole](/wiki/Psie_Pole). Although they were abolished in 1991 and have not existed as public administration units since then, areas of borders and names similar/identical to the former districts exist in the practice of operation of various types of authorities and administrations (e.g., as divisions of territorial competencies of courts, prosecutors' offices, tax offices, etc.).

The present Wrocław [districts](/wiki/Osiedle) ([Polish](/wiki/Polish_language): *osiedla*) were created in 1991, and are a type of local government district.

### Municipal government

Wrocław is governed by the [city's mayor](/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Wroc%C5%82aw) and a municipal legislature known as the city council. The city council is made up of 39 councilors and is directly elected by the city's inhabitants. The remit of the council and president extends to all areas of municipal policy and development planning, up to and including development of local infrastructure, transport and planning permission. It is not able to draw taxation directly from its citizens, and instead receives its budget from the Polish national government, whose seat is in Warsaw.

The city's current mayor is [Jacek Sutryk](/wiki/Jacek_Sutryk), who has served in this position since 2018. The first mayor of Wrocław after the war was [Bolesław Drobner](/wiki/Boles%C5%82aw_Drobner), appointed to the position on 14 March 1945, even before the [surrender of Festung Breslau](/wiki/Siege_of_Breslau).

## Economy

[Sky Tower](/wiki/Sky_Tower_(Wroc%C5%82aw)) is one of the [tallest buildings in Poland](/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Poland). It has office, commercial, residential and recreational space.

See also: [Economy of Poland](/wiki/Economy_of_Poland)

Wrocław is the second-wealthiest of the large cities in Poland after [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw). The city is home to the largest number of [leasing](/wiki/Leasing) and debt collection companies in the country, including the largest European Leasing Fund as well as numerous banks. Due to the proximity of the borders with Germany and the Czech Republic, Wrocław and the region of [Lower Silesia](/wiki/Lower_Silesia) is a large import and export partner with these countries.

Wrocław is one of the most innovative cities in Poland with the largest number of R&D centres, due to the cooperation between the municipality, business sector and numerous universities. There are many organisations dealing with innovation–research institutions and technology transfer offices, incubators, technology and business parks, business support organisations, companies, start-ups and co-working spaces. The complex and varied infrastructure available in Wrocław facilitates the creation of innovative products and services and enables conducting research projects.

Wrocław's industry manufactures [buses](/wiki/Bus), [railroad cars](/wiki/Railroad_car), [home appliances](/wiki/Home_appliance), chemicals, and electronics. The city houses factories and development centres of many foreign and domestic corporations, such as [WAGO Kontakttechnik](/wiki/WAGO_Kontakttechnik), [Siemens](/wiki/Siemens), [Bosch](/wiki/Robert_Bosch_GmbH), [Whirlpool Corporation](/wiki/Whirlpool_Corporation), [Nokia Networks](/wiki/Nokia_Networks), [Volvo](/wiki/Volvo), [HP](/wiki/Hewlett-Packard), [IBM](/wiki/IBM), [Google](/wiki/Google), [Opera Software](/wiki/Opera_Software), [Bombardier Transportation](/wiki/Bombardier_Transportation), [WABCO](/wiki/WABCO_Vehicle_Control_Systems) and others. Wrocław is the location of offices for large Polish companies including [Getin Holding](/wiki/Getin_Holding), [AmRest](/wiki/AmRest), [Polmos](/wiki/Polmos), and [MCI Management SA](/wiki/MCI_Management_SA). [Kaufland](/wiki/Kaufland) Poland has its main headquarters in the city.

The [Crédit Agricole](/wiki/Cr%C3%A9dit_Agricole) Poland headquarters in Wrocław

Since the beginning of the 21st century, the city has had a developing [high-tech](/wiki/Information_technology) sector.
Many high-tech companies are located in the Wrocław Technology Park, such as Baluff, CIT Engineering, Caisson Elektronik, ContiTech, Ericsson, Innovative Software Technologies, [IBM](/wiki/IBM), IT-MED, IT Sector, [LiveChat Software](/wiki/Livechat_Software), Mitsubishi Electric, Maas, PGS Software, Technology Transfer Agency Techtra and Vratis. In [Biskupice Podgórne](/wiki/Biskupice_Podg%C3%B3rne) ([Community Kobierzyce](/wiki/Gmina_Kobierzyce)) there are factories of [LG](/wiki/LG) (LG Display, LG Electronics, LG Chem, LG Innotek), Dong Seo Display, Dong Yang Electronics, [Toshiba](/wiki/Toshiba), and many other companies, mainly from the electronics and home appliances sectors, while the [Nowa Wieś Wrocławska](/wiki/Nowa_Wie%C5%9B_Wroc%C5%82awska) factory and distribution centre of [Nestlé](/wiki/Nestl%C3%A9) Purina and factories a few other enterprises.

The city is the seat of Wrocław Research Centre [EIT](/wiki/European_Institute_of_Innovation_and_Technology)+, which contains, inter alia, geological research laboratories to the unconventional and Lower Silesian Cluster of Nanotechnology. The logistics centres [DHL](/wiki/DHL), [FedEx](/wiki/FedEx) and [UPS](/wiki/United_Parcel_Service) are based in Wrocław. It is a major centre for the pharmaceutical industry (U.S. Pharmacia, Hasco-Lek, Galena, Avec Pharma, [3M](/wiki/3M), Labor, S-Lab, Herbapol, and Cezal).

Wrocław is home to Poland's largest [shopping mall](/wiki/Shopping_mall) – [Bielany Avenue](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleja_Bielany) (pl. Aleja Bielany) and Bielany Trade Centre, located in [Bielany Wrocławskie](/wiki/Bielany_Wroc%C5%82awskie) where stores such as [Auchan](/wiki/Auchan), [Decathlon](/wiki/Decathlon_(retailer)), [Leroy Merlin](/wiki/Leroy_Merlin), [Makro](/wiki/Makro), [IKEA](/wiki/IKEA), Jula, [OBI](/wiki/Obi_(store)), [Castorama](/wiki/Castorama), [Black Red White](/wiki/Black_Red_White), Poco, [E. ## Transport

A map of Wrocław, illustrating the [A8 bypass](/wiki/A8_autostrada_(Poland)) and surrounding arterial roads

Wrocław is a major [transport hub](/wiki/Transport_hub), situated at the crossroad of many routes linking Western and Central Europe with the rest of Poland. The city is skirted on the south by the [A4 highway](/wiki/A4_autostrada_(Poland)), which is part of the [European route E40](/wiki/European_route_E40), extending from the Polish-German to the Polish-Ukrainian border across southern Poland. The 672-kilometre highway beginning at [Jędrzychowice](/wiki/J%C4%99drzychowice,_Zgorzelec_County) connects Lower Silesia with [Opole](/wiki/Opole) and the industrial [Upper Silesian](/wiki/Upper_Silesia) metropolis, [Kraków](/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w), [Tarnów](/wiki/Tarn%C3%B3w) and [Rzeszów](/wiki/Rzesz%C3%B3w). It also provides easy access to German cities such as [Dresden](/wiki/Dresden), [Leipzig](/wiki/Leipzig), [Magdeburg](/wiki/Magdeburg) and with the [A18 highway](/wiki/A18_autostrada_(Poland)) [Berlin](/wiki/Berlin), [Hamburg](/wiki/Hamburg).

The toll-free [A8 bypass](/wiki/A8_autostrada_(Poland)) (Wrocław [ring road](/wiki/Beltway)) around the west and north of the city connects the A4 highway with three major routes – [S5 expressway](/wiki/Expressway_S5_(Poland)) leading to [Poznań](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84), [Bydgoszcz](/wiki/Bydgoszcz); the [S8 express road](/wiki/Expressway_S8_(Poland)) towards [Oleśnica](/wiki/Ole%C5%9Bnica), [Łódź](/wiki/%C5%81%C3%B3d%C5%BA), [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw), [Białystok](/wiki/Bia%C5%82ystok); and the [National Road 8](/wiki/National_road_8_(Poland)) to [Prague](/wiki/Prague), [Brno](/wiki/Brno) and other townships in the [Czech Republic](/wiki/Czech_Republic).

[Traffic congestion](/wiki/Traffic_congestion) is a significant issue in Wrocław as in most Polish cities. In 2020 it was ranked as the fifth-most congested city in Poland, and 41st in the world. On average, a car driver in Wrocław annually spends seven days and two hours in a traffic jam. [Roadblocks](/wiki/Roadblock), [gridlocks](/wiki/Gridlock) and narrow [cobblestone](/wiki/Cobblestone) streets around the Old Town are considered obstacles by drivers. The lack of parking space is also a major setback; private lots or on-street pay bays are the most common means of parking. A 2019 study revealed that there are approximately 130 vehicles per each parking spot, and the search for an unoccupied bay takes on average eight minutes.

### Aviation

[Wrocław Airport](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Airport) in [Strachowice](/wiki/Strachowice,_Wroc%C5%82aw)

The city is served by [Wrocław Airport](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Airport) ([IATA](/wiki/IATA_airport_code): WRO [ICAO](/wiki/ICAO_airport_code): EPWR) situated around 10 kilometres southwest from the city centre. The airport handles passenger flights with [LOT Polish Airlines](/wiki/LOT_Polish_Airlines), [Buzz](/wiki/Buzz_(Polish_airline)), [Ryanair](/wiki/Ryanair), [Wizz Air](/wiki/Wizz_Air), [Lufthansa](/wiki/Lufthansa), [Eurowings](/wiki/Eurowings), [Air France](/wiki/Air_France), [KLM](/wiki/KLM), [Scandinavian Airlines](/wiki/Scandinavian_Airlines), [Swiss International Air Lines](/wiki/Swiss_International_Air_Lines) and air [cargo](/wiki/Cargo) connections. In 2019, over a 3.5 million passengers passed through the airport, placing it fifth on the list of busiest airports in Poland.

Among the permanent and traditional destinations are [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw), [Amsterdam](/wiki/Amsterdam), [Düsseldorf](/wiki/D%C3%BCsseldorf), [Frankfurt am Main](/wiki/Frankfurt_am_Main), [Zürich](/wiki/Z%C3%BCrich) and [Budapest](/wiki/Budapest). [Low-cost flights](/wiki/Low-cost_carrier) are common among British, Italian, Spanish and Ukrainian travellers, based on the number of destinations. Seasonal charter flights are primarily targeted at Polish holidaymakers travelling to [Southern Europe](/wiki/Southern_Europe) and [North Africa](/wiki/North_Africa).

### Rail and bus

A [Lower Silesian Railways](/wiki/Lower_Silesian_Railways) train at [Wrocław Główny railway station](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_G%C5%82%C3%B3wny_railway_station)

The main rail station is [Wrocław Główny](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_G%C5%82%C3%B3wny_railway_station), which is the largest railway station in Poland by the number of passengers served (21.2 million passengers a year), and perhaps the most important railroad junction alongside [Warsaw Central station](/wiki/Warszawa_Centralna). The station is supported by [PKP Intercity](/wiki/PKP_Intercity), [Polregio](/wiki/Polregio), [Lower Silesian Railways](/wiki/Lower_Silesian_Railways) and [Leo Express](/wiki/Leo_Express). There are direct connections to [Szczecin](/wiki/Szczecin_G%C5%82%C3%B3wny_railway_station), [Poznań](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84_G%C5%82%C3%B3wny), and to Warsaw Central through [Łódź Fabryczna railway station](/wiki/%C5%81%C3%B3d%C5%BA_Fabryczna_railway_station). There is also a regular connection to [Berlin Hauptbahnhof](/wiki/Berlin_Hauptbahnhof) and [Wien Hauptbahnhof](/wiki/Wien_Hauptbahnhof) (Vienna), as well as indirect to [Praha hlavní nádraží](/wiki/Praha_hlavn%C3%AD_n%C3%A1dra%C5%BE%C3%AD) (Prague) and [Budapest-Nyugati](/wiki/Budapest_Nyugati_railway_station) with one transfer depending on the carrier.

Bus transport in Wrocław

Adjacent to the railway station, is a central bus station located in the basement of the shopping mall [Wroclavia](/wiki/Wroclavia), with services offered by [PKS](/wiki/Przedsi%C4%99biorstwo_Komunikacji_Samochodowej), Neobus, [Flixbus](/wiki/Flixbus), [Sindbad](/wiki/Sindbad_(company)), and others.

### Public transport

The public transport in Wrocław comprises 99 bus lines and a well-developed network of 25 [tram lines](/wiki/Tramways_in_Wroc%C5%82aw), with a length of over 200 kilometres, operated by the Municipal Transport Company MPK (*Miejskie Przedsiębiorstwo Komunikacyjne*). Rides are paid for, tickets can be purchased in vending machines, which are located at bus stops, as well as in the vending machines located in the vehicle. Payment is via contactless [payment card](/wiki/Payment_card), and the ticket is saved on the card. The tickets are available for purchase in the electronic form via mobile app: [mPay](/wiki/MPay), [Apple Pay](/wiki/Apple_Pay), SkyCash, Mobill, [Google Pay](/wiki/Google_Pay_(payment_method)). Tickets are one-ride or temporary (0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 24, 48, 72, or 168 hours).

All buses and a significant portion of the trams have low-floors.

[Moderus Gamma LF07AC](/wiki/Moderus_Gamma) tram

Over a dozen traditional [taxicab](/wiki/Taxicab) firms operate in the city as well as online ridesharing services [Uber](/wiki/Uber), iTaxi, [Bolt](/wiki/Bolt_(company)) and [Free Now](/wiki/Free_Now_(service)).

### Other

Wrocław City Bike

There are 1,200 km (746 mi) of [cycling paths](/wiki/Cycling_infrastructure), including about 100 km (62 mi) paths on flood embankments. Wrocław has a bike rental network called the City Bike (*Wrocławski Rower Miejski*). It has 2,000 bicycles and 200 self-service stations. In addition to regular bicycles, tandem, cargo, electric, folding, tricycles, children's, and handbikes are available, operating every year from 1 March to 30 November. During winter (December – February) 200 bikes are available in the system.

Wrocław possesses a [scooter-sharing system](/wiki/Scooter-sharing_system) of [Lime](/wiki/Lime_(transportation_company)), [Bird](/wiki/Bird_Global), [Bolt](/wiki/Bolt_(company)) and [Hive Free Now](/wiki/Free_Now_(service)) – [motorised scooter](/wiki/Motorized_scooter) rental is available using a mobile application.

A [gondola lift](/wiki/Gondola_lift) over the Oder called Polinka began operation in 2013. Wrocław also has a [river port](/wiki/Inland_port) on the Oder and several [marinas](/wiki/Marinas).

## Demographics

See also: [Urban demographics of Poland](/wiki/Demographics_of_Poland#Largest_metropolitan_and_urban_areas)

Wrocław population pyramid in 2021

In December 2020, the estimated population of Wrocław was 641,928, of which 342,215 were women and 299,713 were men. Since 2011, the population has been steadily rising, with a 0.142% increase between 2019 and 2020, and a 2.167% increase in the years 2011–2020. In 2018, the crude [birth rate](/wiki/Birth_rate) stood at 11.8 and the [mortality rate](/wiki/Mortality_rate) at 11.1 per 1,000 residents. The median age in 2018 was 43 years. The city's population is aging significantly; between 2013 and 2018, the number of [seniors](/wiki/Senior_citizen), per [Statistics Poland](/wiki/Statistics_Poland) – men aged 65 or above and women aged 60 or above, surged from 21.5% to 24.2%.

Historically, the city's population grew rapidly throughout the 19th and 20th centuries. In 1900, approximately 422,709 people were registered as residents. In 1910 the population was 512,105 of whom according to the official census 3.62% (18,538) spoke Polish, however some Polish scholars estimated that there could be even up to 50,000 (10%) people of Polish extraction in Breslau. In 1933, the population was 625,000. Between 1933 and 1939 the population declined to 620,976 on 17 May 1939. The strongest growth was recorded from 1900 to 1910, with almost 100,000 new residents within the city limits. Although the city was overwhelmingly German-speaking, the ethnic composition based on heritage or place of birth was mixed.

Around 43% of all inhabitants in 1910 were born outside Silesia and migrated into the city, mostly from the contemporary regions of [Greater Poland](/wiki/Greater_Poland) (then the [Prussian Partition](/wiki/Prussian_Partition) of Poland) or [Pomerania](/wiki/Pomerania). Poles and Jews were among the most prominent active minorities. Simultaneously, the city's territorial expansion and incorporation of surrounding townships further strengthened population growth.

Following the end of the Second World War and [post-1945 expulsions](/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans) of the remaining pre-war population, Wrocław became again predominantly [Polish](/wiki/Polish_language)-speaking. New incomers were primarily resettled from [areas in the east which Poland lost](/wiki/Eastern_Borderlands) ([Vilnius](/wiki/Vilnius) and [Lviv](/wiki/Lviv)), or from other provinces, notably the regions of Greater Poland, [Lublin](/wiki/Lublin), [Białystok](/wiki/Bia%C5%82ystok) and [Rzeszów](/wiki/Rzesz%C3%B3w). At the end of 1947, the city's population was estimated at 224,800 individuals. In the following years, Wrocław had the largest [rate of natural increase](/wiki/Rate_of_natural_increase) among the five largest Polish cities. German nationals who stayed were either resettled in the late 1940s and 1950s, or assimilated, though a [cultural society](/wiki/German_Socio-Cultural_Organisation_in_Wroc%C5%82aw) now exists to promote German culture in the still-existing German minority. In the 1950s, [Greeks](/wiki/Greeks), [refugees of the Greek Civil War](/wiki/Refugees_of_the_Greek_Civil_War), also settled in the city.

Wrocław currently has one of the highest concentration of foreigners in Poland alongside [Warsaw](/wiki/Warsaw) and [Poznań](/wiki/Pozna%C5%84). A significant majority are migrant workers from [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine); other significant minorities include people from [Italy](/wiki/Italy), [Spain](/wiki/Spain), [South Korea](/wiki/South_Korea), [India](/wiki/India), [Russia](/wiki/Russia) and [Turkey](/wiki/Turkey). No exact statistic exists on the number of temporary residents from abroad. The city is home to a large population of foreign students.

### Religion

See also: [Four Denominations District](/wiki/Four_Denominations_District)

[Wrocław Cathedral](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Cathedral), which dates back to the 13th century

Wrocław's population is predominantly [Roman Catholic](/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church), like the rest of Poland. The [diocese](/wiki/Diocese) was founded in the city in 1000. It was one of the first dioceses in the country at that time. Now the city is the seat of a [Catholic Archdiocese](/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Wroc%C5%82aw).

Prior to World War II, Breslau was mostly inhabited by [Protestants](/wiki/Protestants), followed by a large Roman Catholic and a significant Jewish minority. In 1939, of 620,976 inhabitants, 368,464 were Protestants ([United Protestants](/wiki/United_Protestants); mostly [Lutherans](/wiki/Lutherans) and minority [Reformed](/wiki/Reformed_tradition); in the [Evangelical Church of the old-Prussian Union](/wiki/Prussian_Union_of_churches)), 193,805 Catholics, 2,135 other Christians and 10,659 Jews. Wrocław had the third largest Jewish population of all cities in Germany before the war. Its [White Stork Synagogue](/wiki/White_Stork_Synagogue) was completed in 1840, and rededicated in 2010. In 2014, it celebrated its first ordination of four rabbis and three cantors since the [Holocaust](/wiki/Holocaust). The Polish authorities together with the German Foreign Minister attended the official ceremony.

The [White Stork Synagogue](/wiki/White_Stork_Synagogue), initially opened in 1829

Post-war resettlements from Poland's ethnically and religiously more diverse former eastern territories, known in Polish as *[Kresy](/wiki/Kresy)*, and the eastern parts of post-1945 Poland (*see [Operation Vistula](/wiki/Operation_Vistula)*) account for a comparatively large portion of [Greek Catholics](/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church) and [Orthodox](/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church) Christians of mostly [Ukrainian](/wiki/Ukrainians_in_Poland) and [Lemko](/wiki/Lemkos) descent. Wrocław is also unique for its "Dzielnica Czterech Świątyń" (Borough of Four Temples) — a part of [Stare Miasto](/wiki/Stare_Miasto,_Wroc%C5%82aw) (Old Town) where a synagogue, a [Lutheran church](/wiki/Lutheranism), a Roman Catholic church and an Eastern Orthodox church stand near each other.

Other Christian denominations present in Wrocław include Seventh Day Adventists, Baptists, Free Christians, Reformed (Calvinist), Methodists, Pentecostals, Jehovah's Witnesses and members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints. Non-Christian congregations include Buddhists. There are also minor associations practicing and promoting [Rodnovery neopaganism](/wiki/Slavic_neopaganism).

In 2007, the Roman Catholic Archbishop of Wrocław established the Pastoral Centre for English Speakers, which offers Mass on Sundays and Holy Days of Obligation, as well as other sacraments, fellowship, retreats, catechesis and pastoral care for all English-speaking Catholics and non-Catholics interested in the Catholic Church. The Pastoral Centre is under the care of [Order of Friars Minor, Conventual (Franciscans)](/wiki/Conventual_Franciscans) of the Kraków Province in the parish of [St Charles Borromeo (Św Karol Boromeusz)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko%C5%9Bci%C3%B3%C5%82_%C5%9Bw._Karola_Boromeusza_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu).

## Education

Main article: [List of universities and colleges in Wrocław](/wiki/List_of_universities_and_colleges_in_Wroc%C5%82aw)

The main building of the [University of Wrocław](/wiki/University_of_Wroc%C5%82aw), built on the site of a medieval castle

The [Wrocław University of Technology](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_University_of_Technology) – Faculty of Architecture

Wrocław is the third largest educational centre of Poland, with 135,000 students in 30 colleges which employ some 7,400 staff.
The city is home to ten [public colleges and universities](/wiki/Public_university). The [University of Wrocław](/wiki/University_of_Wroc%C5%82aw) (*Uniwersytet Wrocławski*) has over 47,000 students, and was ranked fourth among public universities in Poland by the *[Wprost](/wiki/Wprost)* weekly ranking in 2007. The [Wrocław University of Technology](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_University_of_Technology) (*Politechnika Wrocławska*) has over 20,000 students, and was considered the best university of technology in Poland by the *Wprost* in 2007.

The [Wrocław University of Economics](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_University_of_Economics) (*Uniwersytet Ekonomiczny we Wrocławiu*) has over 18,000 students, and is ranked fifth best among public economic universities in Poland by the *Wprost* weekly ranking in 2007. The [Wrocław University of Environmental and Life Sciences](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_University_of_Environmental_and_Life_Sciences) (*Uniwersytet Przyrodniczy we Wrocławiu*) has over 13,000 students, and ranked the third best among public agricultural universities in Poland by the *Wprost* weekly ranking in 2007.

Institutions include the [Wrocław Medical University](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Medical_University) (*Uniwersytet Medyczny we Wrocławiu*), the [University School of Physical Education in Wrocław](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akademia_Wychowania_Fizycznego_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), the [Academy of Fine Arts in Wrocław](/wiki/Academy_of_Fine_Arts_in_Wroc%C5%82aw) (*Akademia Sztuk Pięknych we Wrocławiu*), the [Karol Lipiński University of Music](/wiki/Karol_Lipi%C5%84ski_University_of_Music) (*Akademia Muzyczna im. Karola Lipińskiego we Wrocławiu*), the [Ludwik Solski Academy for the Dramatic Arts](/wiki/Ludwik_Solski_Academy_for_the_Dramatic_Arts), Wrocław Campus (*Państwowa Wyższa Szkoła Teatralna w Krakowie filia we Wrocławiu*), and the [Tadeusz Kościuszko Land Forces Military Academy](/wiki/Tadeusz_Ko%C5%9Bciuszko_Land_Forces_Military_Academy) (*Wyższa Szkoła Oficerska Wojsk Lądowych*).

Private universities include the Wyższa Szkoła Bankowa (University of Business in Wrocław), the [University of Social Sciences and Humanities](/wiki/University_of_Social_Sciences_and_Humanities) (*SWPS Uniwersytet Humanistycznospołeczny*), the University of Law (Wyższa Szkoła Prawa), and Coventry University Wrocław (a campus branch of the [Coventry University](/wiki/Coventry_University), UK).
Other cultural institutions based in Wrocław arę the [Alliance Française](/wiki/Alliance_fran%C3%A7aise) in Wrocław, the Austrian Institute in Wrocław, the [British Council](/wiki/British_Council) in Wrocław, the Dante Alighieri Society in Wrocław, and the [Grotowski Institute in Wrocław](/wiki/Grotowski_Institute_in_Wroc%C5%82aw).

## Culture and landmarks

### Old Town

See also: [Ostrów Tumski, Wrocław](/wiki/Ostr%C3%B3w_Tumski,_Wroc%C5%82aw); [Market Square, Wrocław](/wiki/Market_Square,_Wroc%C5%82aw); and [Wrocław Old Town](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Old_Town)

[Wrocław Old Town](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Old_Town)

[Ossolineum](/wiki/Ossolineum)

[Cathedral Island](/wiki/Cathedral_Island,_Wroc%C5%82aw) - [Tumski Bridge](/wiki/Tumski_Bridge) and [Collegiate Church of the Holy Cross and St. Bartholomew](/wiki/Collegiate_Church_of_the_Holy_Cross_and_St._Bartholomew,_Wroc%C5%82aw)

[Two houses known as *Jaś i Małgosia*](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamienice_Ja%C5%9B_i_Ma%C5%82gosia_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu)

The Old Town of Wrocław is listed in the [Registry of Objects of Cultural Heritage](/wiki/Registry_of_Objects_of_Cultural_Heritage) and is on Poland's prestigious [list of National Monuments](/wiki/List_of_Historic_Monuments_(Poland)). Several architectural landmarks and edifices are one of the best examples of [Brick Gothic](/wiki/Brick_Gothic) and [Baroque](/wiki/Baroque_architecture) architecture in the country. Fine examples of [Neoclassicism](/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture), *[Gründerzeit](/wiki/Gr%C3%BCnderzeit)* and [Historicism](/wiki/Historicism) are scattered across the city's central precinct. The [Wrocław Opera House](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Opera), [Monopol Hotel](/wiki/Monopol_Hotel), [University Library](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gmach_Biblioteki_Uniwersytetu_Wroc%C5%82awskiego_przy_ul._Karola_Szajnochy_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), [Ossolineum](/wiki/Ossolineum), the [National Museum](/wiki/National_Museum,_Wroc%C5%82aw) and the [castle](/wiki/Castle)-like District Court are among some of the grandest and most recognisable historic structures. There are several examples of [Art Nouveau](/wiki/Art_Nouveau) and [Modernism](/wiki/Modernist_architecture) in pre-war retail establishments such as the [Barasch-Feniks](/wiki/Barasch_Brothers%27_Department_Store), [Petersdorff-Kameleon](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dom_Handlowy_Kameleon) and [Renoma](/wiki/Renoma_(Wroc%C5%82aw)) department stores.

The [Ostrów Tumski](/wiki/Ostr%C3%B3w_Tumski_in_Wroc%C5%82aw) (Cathedral Island) is the oldest section of the city. It was once an isolated islet between the branches of the Oder River. The [Wrocław Cathedral](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Cathedral), one of the tallest churches in Poland, was erected in the mid 10th century and expanded over later centuries. The island is home to five other Christian temples and churches (including the [Collegiate Church of the Holy Cross and St. Bartholomew](/wiki/Collegiate_Church_of_the_Holy_Cross_and_St._Bartholomew,_Wroc%C5%82aw), whose [scholaster](/wiki/Scholaster) was [Nicolaus Copernicus](/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus), considered the city's second most important Catholic church, and the Church of Saint Giles, the city's oldest extant [Romanesque](/wiki/Romanesque_architecture_in_Poland) church), the [Archbishop's Palace](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pa%C5%82ac_arcybiskupi_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), the Archdiocese Museum, a 9.5-metre 18th-century monument dedicated to Saint [John of Nepomuk](/wiki/John_of_Nepomuk), historic tenements and the steel [Tumski Bridge](/wiki/Tumski_Bridge) from 1889. A notable attraction are 102 original [gas lanterns](/wiki/Gas_lighting) which are manually lit each evening by a cloaked [lamplighter](/wiki/Lamplighter).

The early 13th-century Main Market Square (Rynek) is the oldest medieval public square in Poland, and one of the largest. The area of the main square together with the auxiliary square is 48,500 m2. It features the ornate Gothic [Old Town Hall](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Town_Hall), the oldest of its kind in the country. In the north-west corner of the square is [St. Elisabeth's Church](/wiki/St._Elisabeth%27s_Church,_Wroc%C5%82aw) (Bazylika Św. Elżbiety) with its 91.5-metre-high tower and an observation deck at an altitude of 75 metres. Beneath the basilica are two small medieval houses connected by an arched gate that once led into a [churchyard](/wiki/Churchyard); these were reshaped into their current form in the 1700s. Today, the two connected buildings are known to the city's residents as "Jaś i Małgosia", named after the children's fairy tale characters from [Hansel and Gretel](/wiki/Hansel_and_Gretel).

House under the Golden Sun, home of the *[Pan Tadeusz](/wiki/Pan_Tadeusz)* Museum

Former residence of Kings [Augustus II the Strong](/wiki/Augustus_II_the_Strong) and [Augustus III of Poland](/wiki/Augustus_III_of_Poland) during their stays in Wrocław

North of the church are so-called "[shambles](/wiki/Slaughterhouse)" (Polish: *jatki*), a former meat market with a [Monument of Remembrance for Slaughtered Animals](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomnik_Pami%C4%99ci_Zwierz%C4%85t_Rze%C5%BAnych). The Salt Square, now a flower market, which opened in 1242, is located at the south-western corner of the Market Square. Close to the square, between Szewska and Łaciarska streets, is the domeless 13th-century [St. Mary Magdalene Church](/wiki/St._Mary_Magdalene_Church_(Wroc%C5%82aw)), which in 1523 during the [Reformation](/wiki/Reformation) was converted into Wrocław's first Protestant temple.

The [Cathedral of St. Vincent and St. James](/wiki/Cathedral_of_St._Vincent_and_St._James,_Wroc%C5%82aw) and the [Holy Cross and St. Bartholomew's Collegiate Church](/wiki/Collegiate_Church_of_the_Holy_Cross_and_St._Bartholomew,_Wroc%C5%82aw) are burial sites of [Polish monarchs](/wiki/List_of_Polish_monarchs), [Henry II the Pious](/wiki/Henry_II_the_Pious) and [Henry IV Probus](/wiki/Henry_IV_Probus), respectively.

The *Pan Tadeusz Museum*, open since May 2016, is located in the "House under the Golden Sun" at 6 Market Square. The manuscript of the national [epos](/wiki/Epic_poem), *[Pan Tadeusz](/wiki/Pan_Tadeusz)*, is housed there as part of the [Ossolineum National Institute](/wiki/Ossolineum), with multimedia and interactive educational opportunities.

### Tourism and places of interest

See also: [Wrocław's dwarfs](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw%27s_dwarfs)

[Wrocław Multimedia Fountain](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Fountain)

The Tourist Information Centre ([Polish](/wiki/Polish_language): *Centrum Informacji Turystycznej*) is situated on the [Main Market Square](/wiki/Main_Market_Square,_Wroc%C5%82aw) (Rynek) in building no 14. Wrocław has been visited by over 6 million tourists in 2024. Free [wireless Internet (Wi-Fi)](/wiki/Hotspot_(Wi-Fi)) is available at a number of places around town.

Wrocław is a major attraction for both domestic and international tourists. Noteworthy landmarks include the [Multimedia Fountain](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Fountain), [Szczytnicki Park](/wiki/Szczytnicki_Park) with its [Japanese Garden](/wiki/Japanese_Garden_(Wroc%C5%82aw)), miniature park and [dinosaur park](/wiki/List_of_dinosaur_parks), the [Botanical Garden](/wiki/University_of_Wroc%C5%82aw_Botanical_Garden), Poland's largest railway model *Kolejkowo*, [Hydropolis Centre for Ecological Education](/wiki/Hydropolis_Centre_for_Ecological_Education), [University of Wrocław](/wiki/University_of_Wroc%C5%82aw) with Mathematical Tower, [Church of the Name of Jesus](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko%C5%9Bci%C3%B3%C5%82_Imienia_Jezus_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), [Wrocław water tower](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_water_tower), [the Royal Palace](/wiki/Royal_Palace,_Wroc%C5%82aw), [ropes course](/wiki/Ropes_course) on the Opatowicka Island, [White Stork Synagogue](/wiki/White_Stork_Synagogue), the [Old Jewish Cemetery](/wiki/Old_Jewish_Cemetery,_Wroc%C5%82aw) and the [Cemetery of Italian Soldiers](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cmentarz_%C5%BBo%C5%82nierzy_W%C5%82oskich).

An interesting way to explore the city is seeking out *Wrocław's dwarfs* – over 800 small bronze [figurines](/wiki/Homunculus) can be found across the city, on pavements, walls and lampposts. They first appeared in 2005.

A [Japanese garden](/wiki/Japanese_garden) within the city's [Szczytnicki Park](/wiki/Szczytnicki_Park)

The [Racławice Panorama](/wiki/Rac%C5%82awice_Panorama) is a monumental [cycloramic](/wiki/Cyclorama) [painting](/wiki/Painting), done by [Jan Styka](/wiki/Jan_Styka) and [Wojciech Kossak](/wiki/Wojciech_Kossak), depicting the [Battle of Racławice](/wiki/Battle_of_Rac%C5%82awice) during the [Kościuszko Uprising](/wiki/Ko%C5%9Bciuszko_Uprising) in 1794. The 15×114 meter panorama was originally located in [Lwów](/wiki/Lw%C3%B3w) and following the end of World War II it was brought to Wrocław.

[Wrocław Zoo](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Zoo) is home to the *[Africarium](/wiki/Africarium)* – the only space devoted solely to exhibiting the [fauna of Africa](/wiki/Fauna_of_Africa) with an [oceanarium](/wiki/Oceanarium). It is the oldest [zoological garden](/wiki/List_of_zoos_by_country) in Poland, established in 1865. It is the third-largest zoo in the world in terms of the number of animal [species](/wiki/Species) on display.

Small passenger vessels on the Oder offer river tours, as do historic [trams](/wiki/Tramways_in_Wroc%C5%82aw) or the converted open-topped historic buses [Jelcz](/wiki/Jelcz) 043. In 2021, the Odra Centrum has opened, an educational centre on the river which is offering workshops, a library and kayak rentals.

The [Centennial Hall](/wiki/Hala_Stulecia_(Wroc%C5%82aw)) (*Hala Stulecia*), designed by [Max Berg](/wiki/Max_Berg) in 1911–1913, is a [World Heritage Site](/wiki/World_Heritage_Site) listed by [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) in 2006.

#### Museums

Archaeological Museum

The [National Museum](/wiki/National_Museum,_Wroc%C5%82aw) at Powstańców Warszawy Square, one of Poland's main branches of [the National Museum](/wiki/National_Museum_of_Poland) system, holds one of the largest collections of contemporary art in the country.

[Ossolineum](/wiki/Ossolineum) is a National Institute and Library incorporating the [Lubomirski Museum (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Ksi%C4%85%C5%BC%C4%85t_Lubomirskich_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), partially salvaged from the formerly Polish city of [Lwów](/wiki/Lw%C3%B3w) (now Lviv in [Ukraine](/wiki/Ukraine)), containing items of international and national significance. It has a history of major World War II theft of collections after the invasion and takeover of Lwów by [Nazi Germany](/wiki/Nazi_Germany) and the [Soviet Union](/wiki/Soviet_Union).

Major museums include the [City Museum of Wrocław (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Miejskie_Wroc%C5%82awia), Museum of Bourgeois Art in the [Old Town Hall](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Town_Hall), [Museum of Architecture](/wiki/Museum_of_Architecture,_Wroc%C5%82aw), [Archaeological Museum (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Archeologiczne_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), [Museum of Natural History at University of Wrocław](/wiki/Museum_of_Natural_History_at_University_of_Wroc%C5%82aw), [Wrocław Contemporary Museum](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Contemporary_Museum), [Archdiocese Museum (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Archidiecezjalne_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), [Galeria Awangarda](/wiki/Galeria_Awangarda), the [Arsenal](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsena%C5%82_Miejski_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), [Museum of Pharmacy (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Farmacji_Uniwersytetu_Medycznego_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), [Post and Telecommunications Museum (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Poczty_i_Telekomunikacji_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), [Geological Museum (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Geologiczne_im._Henryka_Teisseyre_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), the [Mineralogical Museum (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Mineralogiczne_im._Kazimierza_Ma%C5%9Blankiewicza_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu), [Ethnographic Museum (pl)](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzeum_Etnograficzne_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu). Recent openings of museums were the Historical Centrum Zajezdnia (opened in 2016), the OP ENHEIM Gallery (opened in 2018), and the Museum of Illusions (opened in 2021).

#### Entertainment

[Świdnica Cellar](/wiki/Piwnica_%C5%9Awidnicka) (*Piwnica Świdnicka*), one of the oldest restaurant establishments in Europe.

The city is well known for its large number of [nightclubs](/wiki/Nightclub) and [pubs](/wiki/Pub). Many are in or near the [Market Square](/wiki/Market_Square,_Wroc%C5%82aw), and in the Niepolda passage, the [railway wharf](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estakada_kolejowa_we_Wroc%C5%82awiu) on the Bogusławskiego street. The basement of the [old City Hall](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Town_Hall) houses one of the oldest restaurants in Europe—*[Piwnica Świdnicka](/wiki/Piwnica_%C5%9Awidnicka)*, operating since 1273, while the basement of the new City Hall contains the [brewpub](/wiki/Microbrewery) *Spiż*. Next to the market there is also brewpubs *Browar Staromiejski Złoty Pies*, while in other parts of the city two others brewpubs - *Browar Stu Mostów* and *Browar Rodzinny Prost*.

Mostly on the second weekend of June, the [Festival of Good Beer](/wiki/Festival_of_Good_Beer) takes place. It is the biggest beer festival in Poland.

In November and December the [Christmas market](/wiki/Christmas_market) is held at the Main Market Square.

### In literature

The history of Wrocław is described in minute detail in the monograph *[Microcosm: Portrait of a Central European City](/wiki/Microcosm:_Portrait_of_a_Central_European_City)* by [Norman Davies](/wiki/Norman_Davies) and [Roger Moorhouse](/wiki/Roger_Moorhouse). A number of books have been written about Wrocław following World War II.

Wrocław philologist and writer [Marek Krajewski](/wiki/Marek_Krajewski) wrote a series of [crime novels](/wiki/Crime_novel) about detective [Eberhard Mock](/wiki/Eberhard_Mock), a fictional character from the city of Breslau. Michał Kaczmarek published *Wrocław according to Eberhard Mock – Guide based on the books by Marek Krajewski*. In 2011, appeared the 1,104-page Lexicon of the architecture of Wrocław and in 2013 a 960-page Lexicon about the greenery of Wrocław. In 2019, Wrocław was recognised as a [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO) [City of Literature](/wiki/City_of_Literature). Wrocław was designated as the [World Book Capital](/wiki/World_Book_Capital) for 2016 by [UNESCO](/wiki/UNESCO).

### Films, music and theatre

[National Forum of Music](/wiki/National_Forum_of_Music)

[Wrocław Opera](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Opera)

Wrocław is home to the Wroclaw Feature Film Studio (Wytwórnia Filmów Fabularnych), the Film Stuntman School, ATM Grupa, Grupa 13, and Polot Media (formerly Tako Media).

Film directors [Andrzej Wajda](/wiki/Andrzej_Wajda), [Krzysztof Kieślowski](/wiki/Krzysztof_Kie%C5%9Blowski), [Sylwester Chęciński](/wiki/Sylwester_Ch%C4%99ci%C5%84ski), among others, made their film debuts in Wrocław. Numerous movies shot around the city include [Plague](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaraza_(film)), [Ashes and Diamonds](/wiki/Ashes_and_Diamonds), [The Saragossa Manuscript](/wiki/The_Saragossa_Manuscript_(film)), [Sami swoi](/wiki/Sami_swoi), [Lalka](/wiki/The_Doll_(1968_film)), [A Lonely Woman](/wiki/A_Lonely_Woman), [Character](/wiki/Character_(film)), [Big Shu](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wielki_Szu), [Consul](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konsul_(film)), [Aimée & Jaguar](/wiki/Aim%C3%A9e_%26_Jaguar), [Avalon](/wiki/Avalon_(2001_film)), [A Woman in Berlin](/wiki/A_Woman_in_Berlin_(film)), [Suicide Room](/wiki/Suicide_Room), [The Winner](/wiki/The_Winner_(2011_film)), [80 Million](/wiki/80_Million), [Run Boy Run](/wiki/Run_Boy_Run_(film)), [Bridge of Spies](/wiki/Bridge_of_Spies_(film)), [Hiszpanka](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiszpanka_(film)), [Breaking the Limits](/wiki/Breaking_the_Limits).

Numerous Polish TV-series were also shot in Wrocław, notably *[Świat według Kiepskich](/wiki/%C5%9Awiat_wed%C5%82ug_Kiepskich)*, *[Pierwsza miłość](/wiki/Pierwsza_mi%C5%82o%C5%9B%C4%87)*, *[Bodo](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodo_(serial_telewizyjny))*, *[Belfer](/wiki/Belfer_(TV_series))*, *[The Mire](/wiki/The_Mire)*, *[High Water](/wiki/High_Water_(TV_series))*, *[Breslau](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breslau_(serial_telewizyjny))*, *[Black Death](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czarna_%C5%9Bmier%C4%87_(serial_telewizyjny))*, *[Killing Miss](/wiki/Agnieszka_Kotlarska_(model))*.

Also some episodes *[Four Tank-Men and a Dog](/wiki/Four_Tank-Men_and_a_Dog)* and [More Than Life At Stake](/wiki/More_Than_Life_At_Stake) were filmed in Wrocław.

There are several theatres and theatre groups, including [Polish Theatre](/wiki/Polish_Theatre_in_Wroc%C5%82aw) (Teatr Polski) with three stages, and Contemporary Theatre (Wrocławski Teatr Współczesny). The International Theatre Festival Dialog-Wrocław is held every two years.

Wrocław's opera traditions are dating back to the first half of the seventeenth century and sustained by the [Wrocław Opera](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Opera), built between 1839 and 1841. Wrocław Philharmonic, established in 1954 by Wojciech Dzieduszycki is also important for music lovers. The [National Forum of Music](/wiki/National_Forum_of_Music) was opened in 2015 and is a notable landmark, designed by the Polish architectural firm, [Kurylowicz & Associates](/wiki/Stefan_Kury%C5%82owicz).

## Sport

[Wrocław Stadium](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Stadium) – [Euro 2012](/wiki/Euro_2012) Stadium

[Olympic Stadium](/wiki/Olympic_Stadium_(Wroc%C5%82aw))

The area of Wrocław is home to many [professional sports](/wiki/Professional_sports) teams. The most popular sport is [football](/wiki/Association_football) ([Śląsk Wrocław](/wiki/%C5%9Al%C4%85sk_Wroc%C5%82aw) club – Polish Champion in 1977 and 2012), followed by [basketball](/wiki/Basketball) ([Śląsk Wrocław Basketball Club](/wiki/%C5%9Al%C4%85sk_Wroc%C5%82aw_(basketball)) – award-winning men's basketball team and 17-time Polish Champion).

[UEFA Euro 2012](/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2012) fan zone in Wrocław

Matches of [Group A](/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2012_Group_A) [UEFA Euro 2012](/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2012)'s were held at Wrocław at the [Municipal Stadium](/wiki/Stadion_Miejski_(Wroc%C5%82aw)). Matches of [EuroBasket 1963](/wiki/EuroBasket_1963) and [EuroBasket 2009](/wiki/EuroBasket_2009), as well as [2009 Women's European Volleyball Championship](/wiki/2009_Women%27s_European_Volleyball_Championship), [2014 FIVB Volleyball Men's World Championship](/wiki/2014_FIVB_Volleyball_Men%27s_World_Championship) and [2016 European Men's Handball Championship](/wiki/2016_European_Men%27s_Handball_Championship) were also held in Wrocław. Wrocław was the host of the [2013 World Weightlifting Championships](/wiki/2013_World_Weightlifting_Championships) and will the host World Championship 2016 of [Duplicate bridge](/wiki/Duplicate_bridge) and [World Games](/wiki/World_Games) 2017, a competition in 37 non-Olympic sport disciplines.

The [Olympic Stadium](/wiki/Olympic_Stadium_(Wroc%C5%82aw)) in Wrocław hosts the [Speedway Grand Prix of Poland](/wiki/Speedway_Grand_Prix_of_Poland). It is also the home arena of the popular [motorcycle speedway](/wiki/Motorcycle_speedway) club [WTS Sparta Wrocław](/wiki/WTS_Sparta_Wroc%C5%82aw), five-time [Polish Champion](/wiki/Team_Speedway_Polish_Championship).

A [marathon](/wiki/Marathon) takes place in Wrocław every year in September. Wrocław also hosts the [Wrocław Open](/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw_Open), a professional tennis tournament that is part of the [ATP Challenger Tour](/wiki/ATP_Challenger_Tour).
