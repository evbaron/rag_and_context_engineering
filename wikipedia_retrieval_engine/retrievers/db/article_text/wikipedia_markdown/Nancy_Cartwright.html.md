**Nancy Jean Cartwright** (born October 25, 1957) is an American actress. She has served as the long-time voice of [Bart Simpson](/wiki/Bart_Simpson) on *[The Simpsons](/wiki/The_Simpsons)*, which won her a [Primetime Emmy Award](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award) for [Outstanding Voice-Over Performance](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award_for_Outstanding_Voice-Over_Performance) and an [Annie Award](/wiki/Annie_Award) for Best Voice Acting in the Field of Animation. Cartwright also voices other characters for the show, including [Maggie Simpson](/wiki/Maggie_Simpson), [Ralph Wiggum](/wiki/Ralph_Wiggum), [Todd Flanders](/wiki/Todd_Flanders), and [Nelson Muntz](/wiki/Nelson_Muntz). She is also the voice of [Chuckie Finster](/wiki/List_of_Rugrats_characters) in the Nickelodeon series *[Rugrats](/wiki/Rugrats)* and its spin-off *[All Grown Up!](/wiki/All_Grown_Up!)*, succeeding [Christine Cavanaugh](/wiki/Christine_Cavanaugh).

Cartwright was born in [Dayton, Ohio](/wiki/Dayton,_Ohio). She moved to Hollywood in 1978 and trained under voice actor [Daws Butler](/wiki/Daws_Butler). Her first professional role was voicing Gloria in the animated series *[Richie Rich](/wiki/Richie_Rich_(1980_TV_series))*, which she followed with a starring role in the television movie *[Marian Rose White](/wiki/Marian_Rose_White)* (1982) and her first feature film, *[Twilight Zone: The Movie](/wiki/Twilight_Zone:_The_Movie)* (1983). In 1987, Cartwright auditioned for a role in [a series of animated shorts](/wiki/The_Simpsons_shorts) about a [dysfunctional family](/wiki/Dysfunctional_family) that was to appear on *[The Tracey Ullman Show](/wiki/The_Tracey_Ullman_Show)*. Cartwright intended to audition for the role of [Lisa Simpson](/wiki/Lisa_Simpson), the middle child; when she arrived at the audition, she found the role of Bart—Lisa's brother—to be more interesting. Series creator [Matt Groening](/wiki/Matt_Groening) allowed her to audition for Bart and offered her the role on the spot. She voiced Bart for three seasons on *The Tracey Ullman Show*, and in 1989, the shorts were spun off into a half-hour show called *The Simpsons*.

Besides *The Simpsons*, Cartwright has also voiced numerous other animated characters, including Daffney Gillfin in *[Snorks](/wiki/Snorks)*, Mellissa Screetch in *[Toonsylvania](/wiki/Toonsylvania)*, [Rufus](/wiki/Rufus_(Kim_Possible)) in *[Kim Possible](/wiki/Kim_Possible)*, [Mindy](/wiki/Buttons_and_Mindy) in *[Animaniacs](/wiki/Animaniacs)*, Pistol in *[Goof Troop](/wiki/Goof_Troop)*, the Robots in *[Crashbox](/wiki/Crashbox)*, Margo Sherman in *[The Critic](/wiki/The_Critic)* and Todd Daring in *[The Replacements](/wiki/The_Replacements_(TV_series))*. In 2000, she published her autobiography, *[My Life as a 10-Year-Old Boy](/wiki/My_Life_as_a_10-Year-Old_Boy)*, and four years later, adapted it into a [one-woman play](/wiki/One-woman_show). In 2017, she wrote and produced the film *[In Search of Fellini](/wiki/In_Search_of_Fellini)*.

## Early life

Nancy Jean Cartwright was born on October 25, 1957, in Dayton, Ohio. She was the fourth of six children born to Frank (1927–2020) and Miriam (née Wendel; 1929–1978) Cartwright. She grew up in [Kettering, Ohio](/wiki/Kettering,_Ohio), and discovered her talent for voices at an early age. While in the [fourth grade](/wiki/Fourth_grade) at the school of St. Charles Borromeo, she won a school-wide speech competition with her performance of [Rudyard Kipling](/wiki/Rudyard_Kipling)'s *[How the Camel Got His Hump](/wiki/Just_So_Stories)*. Cartwright attended [Fairmont West High School](/wiki/Fairmont_High_School_(Ohio)), and participated in the school's theater and marching band. She regularly entered public speaking competitions, placing first in the "Humorous Interpretation" category at the National District Tournament two years running. The judges often suggested to her that she should perform cartoon voices. Cartwright graduated from high school in 1976 and accepted a scholarship from [Ohio University](/wiki/Ohio_University). She continued to compete in public speaking competitions; during her sophomore year, she placed fifth in the National Speech Tournament's exposition category with her speech "The Art of Animation".

In 1976, Cartwright landed a part-time job doing voice-overs for commercials on [WING](/wiki/WING) radio in Dayton. A representative from [Warner Bros. Records](/wiki/Warner_Bros._Records) visited WING and later sent Cartwright a list of contacts in the animation industry. One of these was [Daws Butler](/wiki/Daws_Butler), known for voicing characters such as [Huckleberry Hound](/wiki/Huckleberry_Hound), [Snagglepuss](/wiki/Snagglepuss), [Elroy Jetson](/wiki/The_Jetsons), [Spike the Bulldog](/wiki/Spike_and_Tyke_(characters)), and [Yogi Bear](/wiki/Yogi_Bear). Cartwright called him and left a message in a Cockney accent on his answering machine. Butler immediately called her back and agreed to be her mentor. He mailed her a script and instructed her to send him a tape recording of herself reading it. Once he received the tape, Butler critiqued it and sent her notes. For the next year, they continued in this way, completing a new script every few weeks. Cartwright described Butler as "absolutely amazing, always encouraging, always polite".

Cartwright returned to [Ohio University](/wiki/Ohio_University) for her sophomore year, but transferred to the [University of California, Los Angeles](/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) (UCLA) so she could be closer to Hollywood and Butler. Her mother, Miriam, died late in the summer of 1978. Cartwright nearly changed her relocation plans but, on September 17, 1978, "joylessly" left for [Westwood, Los Angeles](/wiki/Westwood,_Los_Angeles).

## Career

### Early career

[Daws Butler](/wiki/Daws_Butler) was Cartwright's mentor and helped her become a voice actress.

While attending UCLA, which did not have a public speaking team, Cartwright continued training as a voice actress with Butler. She recalled, "every Sunday I'd take a 20-minute bus ride to his house in [Beverly Hills](/wiki/Beverly_Hills,_California) for a one-hour lesson and be there for four hours ... They had four sons, they didn't have a daughter and I kind of fitted in as the baby of the family." Butler introduced her to many of the voice actors and directors at [Hanna-Barbera](/wiki/Hanna-Barbera). After she met the director [Gordon Hunt](/wiki/Gordon_Hunt_(director)), he asked her to audition for a recurring role as Gloria in *[Richie Rich](/wiki/Richie_Rich_(1980_TV_series))*. She received the part, and later worked with Hunt on several other projects. At the end of 1980, Cartwright signed with a talent agency and landed a lead role in a pilot for a [sitcom](/wiki/Sitcom) called *In Trouble*. Cartwright described the show as "forgettable, but it jump-started my on-camera career". She graduated from UCLA in 1981 with a degree in theater. During the summer, Cartwright worked with [Jonathan Winters](/wiki/Jonathan_Winters) as part of an improvisation troupe at [Kenyon College](/wiki/Kenyon_College) in [Gambier, Ohio](/wiki/Gambier,_Ohio).

Returning to Los Angeles, Cartwright won the lead role in the television film *[Marian Rose White](/wiki/Marian_Rose_White)*. [Janet Maslin](/wiki/Janet_Maslin), a critic for *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times)*, described Cartwright as "a chubby, lumbering, slightly [cross-eyed](/wiki/Strabismus) actress whose naturalness adds greatly to the film's impact". Cartwright replied by sending Maslin a letter insisting she was not cross-eyed, and included a photograph. Later, Cartwright auditioned for the role of Ethel, a girl who becomes trapped in a cartoon world in the third segment of *[Twilight Zone: The Movie](/wiki/Twilight_Zone:_The_Movie)*. She met with director [Joe Dante](/wiki/Joe_Dante) and later described him as "a total cartoon buff, and once he took a look at my resume and noticed Daws Butler's name on it, we were off and running, sharing anecdotes about Daws and animation. After about twenty minutes, he said, 'considering your background, I don't see how I could cast anyone *but* you in this part!'" It was her first role in a feature film. The segment was based on *[The Twilight Zone](/wiki/The_Twilight_Zone_(1959_TV_series))* television series episode "[It's a Good Life](/wiki/It%27s_a_Good_Life_(The_Twilight_Zone))", which was later parodied in *The Simpsons* episode "[Treehouse of Horror II](/wiki/Treehouse_of_Horror_II)" (1991).

Cartwright continued to do voice work for projects including *[Pound Puppies](/wiki/Pound_Puppies_(1986_TV_series))*, *[Popeye and Son](/wiki/Popeye_and_Son)*, *[Snorks](/wiki/Snorks)*, *[My Little Pony](/wiki/My_Little_Pony_(TV_series))* and *[Saturday Supercade](/wiki/Saturday_Supercade)*. She joined a "[loop group](/wiki/Dubbing_(filmmaking))", and recorded vocals for characters in the background of films, although in most cases the sound was turned down so that very little of her voice was heard. She did minor voice-over work for several films, including *[The Clan of the Cave Bear](/wiki/The_Clan_of_the_Cave_Bear_(film))* (1986), *[Silverado](/wiki/Silverado_(film))* (1985), *[Sixteen Candles](/wiki/Sixteen_Candles)* (1984), *[Back to the Future Part II](/wiki/Back_to_the_Future_Part_II)*, and *[The Color Purple](/wiki/The_Color_Purple_(1985_film))* (1985). Cartwright also voiced a shoe that was "dipped" in acid in *[Who Framed Roger Rabbit](/wiki/Who_Framed_Roger_Rabbit)* (1988), describing it as her first "off-screen death scene", and worked to correctly convey the emotion involved.

> Once I had graduated from UCLA, I decided that as long as I was an actress, I was going to find related work in the industry. There were plenty of opportunities. And fortunately, I am just pushy enough to find and get myself in touch with those who can provide such opportunities.

— Nancy Cartwright, *My Life as a 10-Year-Old Boy*

In 1985, she auditioned for a guest spot as Cynthia in *[Cheers](/wiki/Cheers)*. The audition called for her to say her line and walk off the set. Cartwright decided to take a chance on being different and continued walking, leaving the building and returning home. The production crew was confused, but she received the part. In search of more training as an actress, Cartwright joined a class taught by Hollywood coach [Milton Katselas](/wiki/Milton_Katselas). He recommended that Cartwright study *[La Strada](/wiki/La_Strada)*, a 1956 Italian film starring [Giulietta Masina](/wiki/Giulietta_Masina) and directed by [Federico Fellini](/wiki/Federico_Fellini). She began performing "every imaginable scene" from *La Strada* in her class and spent several months trying to secure the rights to produce a stage adaptation. She visited Italy with the intention of meeting Fellini and requesting his permission in person. Although they never met, Cartwright kept a journal of the trip and later wrote a one-woman play called *In Search of Fellini*, partially based on her voyage. The play was co-written by Peter Kjenaas, and Cartwright won a [Drama-Logue Award](/wiki/Drama-Logue_Award) after performing it in Los Angeles in 1995. In a 1998 interview, she stated her intention to make it into a feature film, which she [succeeded in doing in 2017](/wiki/In_Search_of_Fellini).

### *The Simpsons*

Cartwright in 2007

Cartwright voices the character [Bart Simpson](/wiki/Bart_Simpson) on the long-running animated television show *[The Simpsons](/wiki/The_Simpsons)*. On March 13, 1987, she auditioned for a series of animated shorts about a dysfunctional family that was to appear on *[The Tracey Ullman Show](/wiki/The_Tracey_Ullman_Show)*, a sketch comedy program. Cartwright had intended to audition for the role of [Lisa Simpson](/wiki/Lisa_Simpson), the elder daughter. After arriving at the audition, she found that Lisa was simply described as the middle child and at the time did not have much personality. Cartwright became more interested in the role of Bart, described as "devious, underachieving, school-hating, irreverent, [and] clever". Creator [Matt Groening](/wiki/Matt_Groening) let her try out for Bart and gave her the job on the spot. Bart's voice came naturally to Cartwright, as she had previously used elements of it in *My Little Pony*, *Snorks*, and *Pound Puppies*. Cartwright describes Bart's voice as easy to perform compared with other characters. The recording of the shorts was often primitive; the dialog was recorded on a portable tape deck in a makeshift studio above the bleachers on the set of *The Tracey Ullman Show*. Cartwright, the only cast member to have been professionally trained in voice acting, described the sessions as "great fun". However, she wanted to appear in the live-action sketches and occasionally showed up for recording sessions early, hoping to be noticed by a producer.

In 1989, the shorts were spun off into a half-hour show on the [Fox network](/wiki/Fox_Broadcasting_Company) called *The Simpsons*. Bart quickly became the show's [breakout personality](/wiki/List_of_breakout_characters) and one of the most celebrated characters on television—his popularity in 1990 and 1991 was known as "Bartmania". Bart was described as "television's brightest new star" by Mike Boone of *[The Gazette](/wiki/The_Gazette_(Montreal))* and was named 1990's "entertainer of the year" by *[Entertainment Weekly](/wiki/Entertainment_Weekly)*. Despite Bart's fame, however, Cartwright remained relatively unknown. During the first season of *The Simpsons*, Fox ordered Cartwright not to give interviews, because they did not want to publicize the fact that Bart was voiced by a woman. Cartwright's normal speaking voice is said to have "no obvious traces of Bart", and she believes her role is "the best acting job in the world" since she is rarely recognized in public. When she is recognized and asked to perform Bart's voice in front of children, Cartwright refuses because it "freaks [them] out". Bart's catchphrase "Eat My Shorts" was an ad-lib by Cartwright in one of the original table readings, referring to an incident from her high school days. Once while performing, members of the Fairmont West High School marching band switched their chant from the usual "Fairmont West! Fairmont West!" to the irreverent "Eat my shorts!" Cartwright felt it appropriate for Bart, and improvised the line; it became a popular catchphrase on the show.

In 2000, Bart, along with the rest of the Simpson family, was awarded a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame.

Cartwright voices several other characters on the show, including [Nelson Muntz](/wiki/Nelson_Muntz), [Ralph Wiggum](/wiki/Ralph_Wiggum), [Todd Flanders](/wiki/List_of_recurring_The_Simpsons_characters#Todd_Flanders), [Kearney](/wiki/List_of_recurring_The_Simpsons_characters#Kearney_Zzyzwicz), and [Database](/wiki/List_of_recurring_The_Simpsons_characters#Database). She first voiced Nelson in the episode "[Bart the General](/wiki/Bart_the_General)" ([season one](/wiki/The_Simpsons_season_1), 1990). The character was to be voiced by [Dana Hill](/wiki/Dana_Hill), but Hill missed the recording session and Cartwright was given the role. She developed Nelson's voice on the spot and describes him as "a throat-ripper". Ralph Wiggum had originally been voiced by [Jo Ann Harris](/wiki/Jo_Ann_Harris), but Cartwright was assigned to voice the character in "[Bart the Murderer](/wiki/Bart_the_Murderer)" ([season three](/wiki/The_Simpsons_season_3), 1991). Todd Flanders, the only voice for which Cartwright used another source, is based on Sherman (voiced by [Walter Tetley](/wiki/Walter_Tetley)), the boy from *[Peabody's Improbable History](/wiki/Mister_Peabody)*, a series of shorts aired on *[The Rocky and Bullwinkle Show](/wiki/The_Rocky_and_Bullwinkle_Show)*.

Cartwright received a [Primetime Emmy Award for Outstanding Voice-Over Performance](/wiki/Primetime_Emmy_Award_for_Outstanding_Voice-Over_Performance) in 1992 for her performance as Bart in the episode "[Separate Vocations](/wiki/Separate_Vocations)" and an [Annie Award](/wiki/Annie_Award) in 1995 for Best Voice Acting in the Field of Animation. Bart was named one of the [100 most important people of the 20th century](/wiki/Time_100:_The_Most_Important_People_of_the_Century) by *[Time](/wiki/Time_(magazine))*, and in 2000, Bart and the rest of the Simpson family were awarded a star on the [Hollywood Walk of Fame](/wiki/Hollywood_Walk_of_Fame), located at 7021 Hollywood Boulevard.

Until 1998, Cartwright was paid $30,000 per episode. During a pay dispute in 1998, Fox threatened to replace the six main voice actors and made preparations for casting new actors. The dispute was resolved, however, and Cartwright received $125,000 per episode until 2004, when the voice actors demanded $360,000 an episode. A compromise was reached after a month, and Cartwright's pay rose to $250,000 per episode. Salaries were re-negotiated in 2008 with the voice actors receiving approximately $400,000 per episode. Three years later, with Fox threatening to cancel the series unless production costs were cut, Cartwright and the other cast members accepted a 25 percent pay cut, down to just over $300,000 per episode.

### Further career

> It is quite a curiosity being a celebrity that nobody knows. I ask you, how many celebrities would you *not* recognize were they to walk down the street? ... I can think of no one—besides my fellow cast members and me. The anonymity factor is such a unique aspect of this job. I must admit, sometimes I wish it were different.

— Nancy Cartwright, *My Life as a 10-Year-Old Boy*

In addition to her work on *The Simpsons*, Cartwright has voiced many other characters on several animated series, including Chuckie Finster in *[Rugrats](/wiki/Rugrats)* and *[All Grown Up!](/wiki/All_Grown_Up!)*, Margo Sherman in *[The Critic](/wiki/The_Critic)*, [Mindy](/wiki/Buttons_and_Mindy) in *[Animaniacs](/wiki/Animaniacs),* and [Rufus](/wiki/List_of_Kim_Possible_characters#Rufus) the [naked mole-rat](/wiki/Naked_mole-rat) in *[Kim Possible](/wiki/Kim_Possible)*. For the role of Rufus, Cartwright researched mole-rats extensively, and became "a font of useless trivia". She was nominated for a [Daytime Emmy Award](/wiki/Daytime_Emmy_Award) for Outstanding Performer in an Animated Program in 2004 for her work on the show. In 2001, Cartwright took over the *Rugrats* role of Chuckie Finster when [Christine Cavanaugh](/wiki/Christine_Cavanaugh) retired. Cartwright describes Rufus and Chuckie as her two most difficult voices: "Rufus because my diaphragm gets a workout while trying to utilize the 18 vocal sounds a mole makes. Chuckie because ... he's an asthmatic with five personalities rolled into one—plus I have to do the voice the way [Cavanaugh] did it for 10 years." Other television shows that have used her voice work include *[Galaxy High](/wiki/Galaxy_High),* *[God, the Devil and Bob](/wiki/God,_the_Devil_and_Bob),* *[Goof Troop](/wiki/Goof_Troop),* *[Mike, Lu & Og](/wiki/Mike,_Lu_%26_Og),* *[The Replacements](/wiki/The_Replacements_(TV_series)),* *[Pinky and the Brain](/wiki/Pinky_and_the_Brain)* and *Timberwolf*. Cartwright has appeared on camera in numerous television shows and films, including *[Fame](/wiki/Fame_(1982_TV_series))*, *[Empty Nest](/wiki/Empty_Nest)*, *[The Fresh Prince of Bel-Air](/wiki/The_Fresh_Prince_of_Bel-Air)*, *[Flesh and Blood](/wiki/Flesh_and_Blood_(1985_film))*, *[Godzilla](/wiki/Godzilla_(1998_film)),* and *[24](/wiki/24_(season_6))*.

In 2000, Cartwright published her autobiography, *[My Life as a 10-Year-Old Boy](/wiki/My_Life_as_a_10-Year-Old_Boy)*. The book details her career (particularly her experiences as the voice of Bart) and contains stories about life behind the scenes of *The Simpsons.* Laura A. Bischoff of the *[Dayton Daily News](/wiki/Dayton_Daily_News)* commented that the book was the "ultimate insider's guide to *The Simpsons*". Critics complained that the book lacked interesting stories and was aimed mostly at fans of *The Simpsons* rather than a general audience.

Cartwright adapted *My Life as a 10-Year-Old Boy* into a [one-woman play](/wiki/One-person_show) in 2004. Cartwright has performed it at a variety of venues, including the August 2004 [Edinburgh Festival Fringe](/wiki/Edinburgh_Festival_Fringe) in Scotland. The play received modest reviews, including criticism for a lack of inside stories about *The Simpsons*, and its "overweeningly upbeat" tone. David Chatterton of *The British Theatre Guide* described the show as "interesting and entertaining, but not really a 'must see' even for Simpsons fans".

Cartwright has shown an interest in [stock car racing](/wiki/Stock_car_racing) and as of 2007 was seeking a [NASCAR](/wiki/NASCAR) license. In 2001, she founded a production company called SportsBlast and created an online animated series called *The Kellys*. The series is focused on racing; Cartwright voices a seven-year-old named Chip Kelly.

In 2016, Cartwright launched Spotted Cow Entertainment, her own film and television production company, with Peter Kjenaas, Monica Gil and Kevin Burke. With a focus on international audiences, Spotted Cow is seeking "to finance, produce and acquire live action and animated films, television series, as well as entertainment for digital platforms with budgets up to $15M." With Spotted Cow, Cartwright made her first film as a screenwriter and producer, *[In Search of Fellini](/wiki/In_Search_of_Fellini)*, which was released on September 15, 2017. Based on her own journey to [Italy](/wiki/Italy) in 1985 in a bid to meet the famed director [Federico Fellini](/wiki/Federico_Fellini), the film fulfilled Cartwright's longtime vision of turning her 1995 one-woman play *In Search of Fellini* into a film.

## Personal life

Cartwright met real-estate agent Warren Murphy, 24 years her senior, on her birthday in 1988 and married him two months later. In her book, she describes Murphy as her "personal laugh track". The couple had two children, before divorcing in 2002.

Cartwright was raised a [Roman Catholic](/wiki/Catholic_Church) but joined the [Church of Scientology](/wiki/Church_of_Scientology) in 1991. In 2007, Cartwright, then making about $400,000 per episode, was awarded Scientology's Patron Laureate Award after donating $10,000,000 to the Church.

Cartwright is a contributor to [ASIFA-Hollywood](/wiki/ASIFA-Hollywood)'s Animation Archive Project. In September 2007, Cartwright received the [Make-A-Wish Foundation](/wiki/Make-A-Wish_Foundation)'s *Wish Icon* Award "for her tremendous dedication to the Foundation's fundraising and wish-fulfillment efforts." In 2005, Cartwright created a [scholarship](/wiki/Scholarship) at Fairmont High School "designed to aid Fairmont [graduates] who dream of following in her footsteps and studying speech, debate, drama or music" at [Ohio University](/wiki/Ohio_University). In 2005, Cartwright was given the title of Honorary Mayor of [Northridge, California](/wiki/Northridge,_Los_Angeles,_California) (a neighborhood of Los Angeles) by the Northridge Chamber of Commerce.

In 2007, Cartwright was in a romantic relationship with contractor Stephen Brackett, a fellow member of Scientology. Before his May 2009 death by suicide, the couple had made plans during early 2008 to marry.

In 2012, Cartwright received an honorary doctorate degree in communication from [Ohio University](/wiki/Ohio_University), where she was a student from 1976 to 1977 before transferring to UCLA.

Cartwright is also a painter, sculptor and philanthropist. She co-founded the Know More About Drugs alliance. Her stepbrother David Carpenter is the father of singer and actress [Sabrina Carpenter](/wiki/Sabrina_Carpenter).
