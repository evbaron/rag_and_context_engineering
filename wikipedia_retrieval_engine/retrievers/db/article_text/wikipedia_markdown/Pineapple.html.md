# Pineapple

For other uses, see [Pineapple (disambiguation)](/wiki/Pineapple_(disambiguation)).

"Red Pineapple" redirects here. For the apple cultivar, see [Red Pineapple (apple)](/wiki/Red_Pineapple_(apple)).

The **pineapple** (***Ananas comosus***) is a [tropical plant](/wiki/Tropical_vegetation) with an edible fruit; it is the most economically significant plant in the family [Bromeliaceae](/wiki/Bromeliaceae).

The pineapple is indigenous to [South America](/wiki/South_America), where it has been cultivated for many centuries. The introduction of the pineapple plant to [Europe](/wiki/Europe) in the [17th century](/wiki/17th_century) made it a significant cultural icon of luxury. Since the 1820s, pineapple has been commercially grown in greenhouses and many tropical plantations. The fruit, particularly its [juice](/wiki/Pineapple_juice), has diverse uses in cuisines and [desserts](/wiki/Dessert).

Pineapples grow as a small shrub; the individual flowers of the unpollinated plant fuse to form a [multiple fruit](/wiki/Multiple_fruit). The plant normally propagates from the [offset](/wiki/Offset_(botany)) produced at the top of the fruit or from a side shoot, and typically matures within a year.

## Etymology

A pine cone, which was called a *pineapple* at least two centuries before use of the name for the tropical fruit

Pineapple fruit, whole and in cross section

The word *pine apple* was used by English explorer [John Smith](/wiki/John_Smith_(explorer)) referring to the tropical fruit in 1624. The word *[apple](/wiki/Apple#Etymology)* was given during his time for any unfamiliar tree fruit that had a firm, round shape; a *pine apple* first referred to the [cone](/wiki/Conifer_cone) (fruit) of the [pine](/wiki/Pine) tree in the 14th century. Gonzalo Hernández Oviedo described the *piñas* or *pomme de pin* ("apple of pine") in [Hispaniola](/wiki/Hispaniola) around 1540.

The first reference in English to the pineapple fruit was the 1568 translation from the French of [André Thevet](/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Thevet)'s *[The New Found World, or Antarctike](/wiki/The_New_Found_World,_or_Antarctike)* where he refers to a *Hoyriri*, a fruit cultivated and eaten by the [Tupinambá people](/wiki/Tupinamb%C3%A1_people), living near modern [Rio de Janeiro](/wiki/Rio_de_Janeiro), and now believed to be a pineapple. Later in the same English translation, Thavet described the fruit as a "Nana made in the manner of a Pine apple", where he used another [Tupi](/wiki/Tupian_languages) word *nanas*. When it was first discovered by explorers, the [Tupi-Guarani](/wiki/Tupi%E2%80%93Guarani_languages) and [Carib people](/wiki/Kalina_people) used it as a [staple food](/wiki/Staple_food) with the name, *nanas*. This usage was adopted in many European languages and led to the plant's scientific [binomial](/wiki/Binomial_nomenclature) *Ananas comosus*, where *comosus* ('tufted') refers to the stem of the plant.

## Description

Pineapple in the starting stage

Pineapple [inflorescence](/wiki/Inflorescence)

A young pineapple in flower

The pineapple is a herbaceous [perennial](/wiki/Perennial), which grows to 1 to 1.5 m (3+1⁄2 to 5 ft) tall on average, although sometimes it can be taller. The plant has a short, stocky stem with tough, waxy leaves. When creating its fruit, it usually produces up to 200 flowers, although some large-fruited [cultivars](/wiki/Cultivar) can exceed this. Once it flowers, the individual fruits of the flowers join together to create a [multiple fruit](/wiki/Multiple_fruit). After the first fruit is produced, side shoots (called 'suckers' by commercial growers) are produced in the leaf axils of the main stem. These suckers may be removed for propagation, or left to produce additional fruits on the original plant. Commercially, suckers that appear around the base are cultivated. It has 30 or more narrow, fleshy, trough-shaped leaves that are 30 to 100 cm (1 to 3+1⁄2 ft) long, surrounding a thick [stem](/wiki/Plant_stem); the leaves have sharp spines along the margins. In the first year of growth, the axis lengthens and thickens, bearing numerous leaves in close spirals. After 12 to 20 months, the stem grows into a spike-like [inflorescence](/wiki/Inflorescence) up to 15 cm (6 in) long with over 100 spirally arranged, trimerous flowers, each subtended by a bract.

In the wild, pineapples are pollinated primarily by [hummingbirds](/wiki/Hummingbird). Certain wild pineapples are [foraged](/wiki/Forage) and pollinated at night by [bats](/wiki/Bat). Under cultivation, because seed development diminishes fruit quality, pollination is performed by hand, and seeds are retained only for [breeding](/wiki/Plant_breeding). In [Hawaii](/wiki/Hawaii), where pineapples were cultivated and [canned](/wiki/Cannery) industrially throughout the 20th century, importation of hummingbirds was prohibited.

The ovaries develop into [berries](/wiki/Berry_(botany)), which coalesce into a large, compact, multiple fruit. The fruit of a pineapple is usually arranged in two interlocking [helices](/wiki/Helix), often with 8 in one direction and 13 in the other, each being a [Fibonacci number](/wiki/Fibonacci_number#Nature).

The pineapple carries out [CAM photosynthesis](/wiki/Crassulacean_acid_metabolism), fixing [carbon dioxide](/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) at night and storing it as the acid [malate](/wiki/Malate), then releasing it during the day in order to reduce [photorespiration](/wiki/Photorespiration).

## History

### Precolonial cultivation

The wild plant originates from the [Paraná](/wiki/Paran%C3%A1_River)–[Paraguay River](/wiki/Paraguay_River) drainages between southern [Brazil](/wiki/Brazil) and [Paraguay](/wiki/Paraguay). Little is known about its domestication, but it spread as a crop throughout South America. Archaeological evidence of use is found as far back as 1200–800 BC (3200–2800 BP) in Peru and 200 BC – 700 AD (2200–1300 BP) in Mexico, where it was cultivated by the [Mayas](/wiki/Maya_peoples) and the [Aztecs](/wiki/Aztecs). By the late 1400s, cropped pineapple was widely distributed and a [staple food](/wiki/Staple_food) of Native Americans. The first European to encounter the pineapple was [Christopher Columbus](/wiki/Christopher_Columbus), in [Guadeloupe](/wiki/Guadeloupe) on 4 November 1493. The Portuguese took the fruit from Brazil and introduced it into [India](/wiki/India) by 1550. The 'Red Spanish' cultivar was also introduced by the Spanish from Latin America to the [Philippines](/wiki/Philippines), and it was grown to produce *[piña](/wiki/Pi%C3%B1a)* fibers that would then be used to produce textiles from at least the 17th century.

Columbus brought the plant back to Spain and called it *piña de Indes*, meaning "pine of the Indians". The pineapple was documented in Peter Martyr's *[Decades of the New World](/wiki/Decades_of_the_New_World)* (1516) and [Antonio Pigafetta](/wiki/Antonio_Pigafetta)'s *Relazione del primo viaggio intorno al mondo* (1524–1525), and the first known illustration was in [Oviedo's](/wiki/Gonzalo_Fern%C3%A1ndez_de_Oviedo_y_Vald%C3%A9s) *Historia General de Las Indias* (1535).

### Old World introduction

[Britannia](/wiki/Britannia) presented with *[cornucopiae](/wiki/Cornucopia)* including pineapples by allegories of Nature, Industry, and Science, with an [orangery](/wiki/Orangery) in the background ([frontispiece](/wiki/Book_frontispiece) of *[The Gardeners Dictionary](/wiki/The_Gardeners_Dictionary)*, 1764)

While the pineapple fascinated Europeans as a fruit of colonialism, it was not successfully cultivated in Europe until Pieter de la Court (1664–1739) developed [greenhouse](/wiki/Greenhouse) horticulture near Leiden. Pineapple plants were distributed from the Netherlands to English gardeners in 1719 and French ones in 1730. In England, the first pineapple was grown at [Dorney Court](/wiki/Dorney_Court), [Dorney](/wiki/Dorney) in Buckinghamshire, and a huge "pineapple stove" to heat the plants was built at the [Chelsea Physic Garden](/wiki/Chelsea_Physic_Garden) in 1723. In France, King [Louis XV](/wiki/Louis_XV) was presented with a pineapple that had been grown at [Versailles](/wiki/Garden_of_Versailles) in 1733. In Russia, [Peter the Great](/wiki/Peter_the_Great) imported de la Court's method into [St. Petersburg](/wiki/St._Petersburg) in the 1720s; in 1730, twenty pineapple saplings were transported from there to a greenhouse at [Empress Anna](/wiki/Anna_of_Russia)'s new Moscow palace.

1772 illustration of an *Ananas comosus* pineapple which was given the early scientific name of *Cardus brasilianus folius aloes* by Banhius in 1623

Because of the expense of direct import and the enormous cost in equipment and labour required to grow them in a temperate climate, in greenhouses called "pineries", [pineapple became a symbol of wealth](/wiki/Pineapple_mania). They were initially used mainly for display at dinner parties, rather than being eaten, and were used again and again until they began to rot. In the second half of the 18th century, the production of the fruit on British estates became the subject of great rivalry between wealthy aristocrats. [John Murray, 4th Earl of Dunmore](/wiki/John_Murray,_4th_Earl_of_Dunmore), built a hothouse on his estate surmounted by a huge stone [cupola](/wiki/Cupola) 14 metres tall in the shape of the fruit; it is known as the [Dunmore Pineapple](/wiki/Dunmore_Pineapple). In architecture, pineapple figures became decorative elements symbolizing hospitality.

### Since the 19th century: mass commercialization

Many different varieties, mostly from the Antilles, were tried for European glasshouse cultivation. The most significant cultivar was "Smooth Cayenne", first imported to France in 1820, then subsequently re-exported to the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom) in 1835, and then from UK, the cultivation spread via [Hawaii](/wiki/Hawaii) to Australia and Africa. The "Smooth Cayenne" cultivar (and sub-selections or clones of the "Smooth Cayenne") make up for the majority of world pineapple production today. Jams and sweets based on pineapple were imported to Europe from the West Indies, Brazil, and Mexico from an early date. By the early 19th century, fresh pineapples were transported direct from the West Indies in large enough quantities to reduce European prices. By the late 19th century, commercial pineapple production was specialized by region to optimize shipping times, with the Azores supplying European markets while Florida and the Caribbean dominated supply to North America.

The Spanish had introduced the pineapple into Hawaii in the 18th century where it is known as the *hala kahiki* ("foreign [hala](/wiki/Pandanus_tectorius)"), but the first commercial plantation was established in 1886. The most famous investor was [James Dole](/wiki/James_Dole), who moved to Hawaii in 1899 and started a 24-hectare (60-acre) pineapple plantation in 1900 which grew into the [Dole Food Company](/wiki/Dole_plc). Dole and [Del Monte](/wiki/Del_Monte_Foods) began growing pineapples on the island of [Oahu](/wiki/Oahu) in 1901 and 1917, respectively, and the [Maui Pineapple Company](/wiki/Maui_Pineapple_Company) began cultivation on [Maui](/wiki/Maui) in 1909. James Dole began the commercial processing of pineapple, and Dole employee [Henry Ginaca](/wiki/Henry_Gabriel_Ginaca) invented an automatic peeling and coring machine in 1911.

[James Drummond Dole](/wiki/James_Dole) (1877–1958) was the early promoter of the pineapple industry in Hawaii. He founded the company now known as the Dole Food Company.

Hawaiian production started to decline from the 1970s because of competition and the shift to refrigerated sea transport. Dole ceased its cannery operations in [Honolulu](/wiki/Honolulu) in 1991, and in 2008, Del Monte terminated its pineapple-growing operations in Hawaii. In 2009, the Maui Pineapple Company reduced its operations to supply pineapples only locally on Maui, and by 2013, only the [Dole Plantation](/wiki/Dole_Plantation) on Oahu grew pineapples in a volume of about 0.1 percent of the world's production. Despite this decline, the pineapple is sometimes used as a symbol of Hawaii. Further, foods with pineapple in them are sometimes known as "Hawaiian" for this reason alone.

In the Philippines, "Smooth Cayenne" was introduced in the early 1900s by the [US Bureau of Agriculture](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) during the [American colonial period](/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_(1898%E2%80%931946)). Dole and Del Monte established plantations in the island of [Mindanao](/wiki/Mindanao) in the 1920s; in the provinces of [Cotabato](/wiki/Cotabato) and [Bukidnon](/wiki/Bukidnon), respectively. Large scale canning had started in Southeast Asia, including in the Philippines, from 1920. This trade was severely damaged by [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II), and Hawaii dominated the international trade until the 1960s.

The Philippines remain one of the top exporters of pineapples in the world. The Del Monte plantations are now locally managed, after Del Monte Pacific Ltd., a Filipino company, completed the purchase of Del Monte Foods in 2014.

### Phytochemistry

Pineapple fruits and peels contain diverse [phytochemicals](/wiki/Phytochemical), among which are [polyphenols](/wiki/Polyphenol), including [gallic acid](/wiki/Gallic_acid), [syringic acid](/wiki/Syringic_acid), [vanillin](/wiki/Vanillin), [ferulic acid](/wiki/Ferulic_acid), [sinapic acid](/wiki/Sinapic_acid), [coumaric acid](/wiki/Coumaric_acid), [chlorogenic acid](/wiki/Chlorogenic_acid), [epicatechin](/wiki/Epicatechin), and [arbutin](/wiki/Arbutin).

Present in all parts of the pineapple plant, [bromelain](/wiki/Bromelain) is a group of [proteolytic](/wiki/Proteolytic) [enzymes](/wiki/Enzyme) present in stem, fruit, crown, core, and leaves of pineapple.

Having sufficient bromelain content, raw pineapple juice may be useful as a meat [marinade](/wiki/Marinade) and [tenderizer](/wiki/Tenderizer). Although pineapple enzymes can interfere with the preparation of some foods or manufactured products, such as [gelatin](/wiki/Gelatin)-based desserts or [gel](/wiki/Gel) [capsules](/wiki/Capsule_(pharmacy)), their proteolytic activity responsible for such properties may be degraded during cooking and [canning](/wiki/Canning). The quantity of bromelain in a typical serving of pineapple fruit is probably not significant, but specific [extraction](/wiki/Extraction_(chemistry)) can yield sufficient quantities for [domestic](/wiki/Home) and industrial processing.

Bromelain is under preliminary research, but has not been adequately defined for its effects in the human body. It is marketed as a [dietary supplement](/wiki/Dietary_supplement). Bromelain may be unsafe for some users, such as in [pregnancy](/wiki/Pregnancy), [allergies](/wiki/Allergies), or [anticoagulation](/wiki/Anticoagulation) therapy.

## Varieties

### Cultivars

Many [cultivars](/wiki/Cultivar) are known. The leaves of the commonly grown "Smooth Cayenne" cultivar and its various clones are smooth, and it is the most commonly grown worldwide. Many cultivars have become distributed from its origins in [Paraguay](/wiki/Paraguay) and the southern part of [Brazil](/wiki/Brazil), and later improved stocks were introduced into the Americas, the Azores, Africa, India, Malaysia and Australia. Varieties include:

- "Hilo" is a compact, 1.0- to 1.5-kg (2– to 3-lb) Hawaiian variant of smooth cayenne; the fruit is more cylindrical and produces many suckers, but no slips.
- "Kona sugarloaf", at 2.5 to 3.0 kg (5–6 lb), has white flesh with no woodiness in the center, is cylindrical in shape, and has a high sugar content but no acid; it has an unusually sweet fruit.
- "Natal queen", at 1.0 to 1.5 kg (2 to 3 lb), has golden yellow flesh, crisp texture, and delicate mild flavor; well-adapted to fresh consumption, it keeps well after ripening. It has spiny leaves and is grown in Australia, Malaysia, and South Africa.
- "Pernambuco" ("eleuthera") weighs 1–2 kg (2–4 lb), and has pale yellow to white flesh. It is sweet, melting in texture, and excellent for eating fresh; it is poorly adapted for shipping, has spiny leaves, and is grown in Latin America.
- "Red Spanish", at 1–2 kg (2–4 lb), has pale yellow flesh with a pleasant aroma, is squarish in shape, and well-adapted for shipping as fresh fruit to distant markets; it has spiny leaves and is grown in Latin America and the Philippines. It was the original pineapple cultivar in the Philippines grown for their leaf fibers (*[piña](/wiki/Pi%C3%B1a)*) in the traditional Philippine textile industry.
- "Smooth cayenne", a 2.5- to 3.0-kg (5- to 6-lb), pale yellow– to yellow-fleshed, cylindrical fruit with high sugar and acid content, is well-adapted to canning and processing; its leaves are without spines. It is an ancient cultivar developed by [Amerind peoples](/wiki/Amerind_peoples). In some parts of Asia, this cultivar is known as [Sarawak](/wiki/Sarawak), after an area of Malaysia in which it is grown. It is one of the ancestors of cultivars "73-50" (also called "MD-1" and "CO-2") and "73–114" (also called "MD-2"). Smooth cayenne was previously the variety produced in Hawaii, and the most easily obtainable in U.S. grocery stores, but was replaced over the course of the mid-1990s and 2000s by MD-2. The success of Del Monte's MD-2 caused Dole to [obtain & grow](/wiki/Industrial_espionage) its own MD-2 pineapples, leading to [Del Monte Fresh Produce Co. v. Dole Food Co.](/wiki/Del_Monte_Fresh_Produce_Co._v._Dole_Food_Co.)
- Some *Ananas* species are grown as [ornamentals](/wiki/Ornamental_plant) for color, novel fruit size, and other [aesthetic](/wiki/Aesthetic) qualities.

In the US, in 1986, the Pineapple Research Institute was dissolved and its assets divided between Del Monte and [Maui Land and Pineapple](/wiki/Maui_Land_and_Pineapple). Del Monte took cultivar '73–114', dubbed 'MD-2', to its plantations in Costa Rica, found it to be well-suited to growing there, and launched it publicly in 1996 as 'Gold Extra Sweet', while Del Monte also began marketing '73–50', dubbed 'CO-2', as 'Del Monte Gold'. The Maui Pineapple Company began growing variety 73-50 in 1988 and named it Maui Gold. The successor company to MPC, the Hali'imaile Pineapple Company continues to grow Maui Gold on the slopes of [Haleakala](/wiki/Haleakal%C4%81).

## Cultivation

Pineapple plantation, Brazil

In commercial farming, flowering can be induced artificially, and the early harvesting of the main fruit can encourage the development of a second crop of smaller fruits. Once removed during cleaning, the top of the pineapple can be planted in soil and a new plant will grow. Slips and suckers are planted commercially.

### Storage and transport

Some buyers prefer green fruit, others ripened or off-green. A plant growth regulator, [Ethephon](/wiki/Ethephon), is typically sprayed onto the fruit one week before harvest, developing [ethylene](/wiki/Ethylene_as_a_plant_hormone#ethylene), which turns the fruit golden yellow. After cleaning and slicing, a pineapple is typically canned in sugar syrup with added preservative. A pineapple never becomes any riper than it was when harvested since it is a [non-climacteric fruit](/wiki/Climacteric_(botany)).

### Ethical and environmental concerns

Like most [modern fruit production](/wiki/Intensive_farming), pineapple plantations are highly industrialized operations. In Costa Rica particularly, the pineapple industry uses large amounts of insecticides to protect the crop, which have caused health problems in many workers. These workers often receive little compensation, and are mostly poor migrants, often [Nicaraguan](/wiki/Nicaraguan). Workers' wages also decrease every time prices are lowered overseas. In 2016, the government declared that it would be trying to improve the situation, with the help of various other groups.

Historically, tropical fruit agriculture, such as for pineapples, has been concentrated in so-called "[banana republics](/wiki/Banana_republics)".

#### Illegal drug trade

Export pineapples from Costa Rica to Europe are often used as a cover for [narcotrafficking](/wiki/Illegal_drug_trade), and containers are impounded routinely in both locations.

#### Expansion into protected areas

In Costa Rica, pineapple cultivation has expanded into the [Maquenque](/wiki/Maquenque_National_Wildlife_Refuge), *[Corredor Fronterizo](/wiki/Border_Corridor_Wildlife_Refuge)*, [Barra del Colorado](/wiki/Barra_del_Colorado_Wildlife_Refuge) and [Caño Negro](/wiki/Ca%C3%B1o_Negro_Wildlife_Refuge) wildlife refuges, all located in the north of the country. As those are protected areas and not national parks, limited and restricted sustainable activities are allowed, however pineapple plantations are industrial operations and many of these do not have the proper license to operate in the protected areas, or were started before either the designation of the area, recent regulations or the creation of the environmental regulatory agency (Setena) in 1996. The agency has registers for around 358.5 ha (1.384 sq mi) of pineapple plantations operating within protected areas, but satellite imagery from 2018 reports around 1,659 ha (6.41 sq mi).

### Pests and diseases

Main article: [List of pineapple diseases](/wiki/List_of_pineapple_diseases)

Pineapples are subject to a variety of diseases, the most serious of which is wilt disease vectored by [mealybugs](/wiki/Mealybug) typically found on the surface of pineapples, but possibly in the closed blossom cups. Other diseases include citrus [pink disease](/wiki/Phanerochaete_salmonicolor), bacterial heart rot, [anthracnose](/wiki/Anthracnose), fungal heart rot, root rot, black rot, butt rot, fruitlet core rot, and yellow spot virus. Pineapple pink disease (not citrus pink disease) is characterized by the fruit developing a brownish to black discoloration when heated during the canning process. The causal agents of pink disease are the bacteria *[Acetobacter aceti](/wiki/Acetobacter_aceti)*, *Gluconobacter oxydans*, *[Pantoea](/wiki/Pantoea) citrea* and *Tatumella ptyseos*.

Some pests that commonly affect pineapple plants are scales, [thrips](/wiki/Thrips), mites, mealybugs, ants, and [symphylids](/wiki/Symphylid).

Heart-rot is the most serious disease affecting pineapple plants. The disease is caused by *[Phytophthora cinnamomi](/wiki/Phytophthora_cinnamomi)* and *[P. parasitica](/wiki/Phytophthora_parasitica)*, fungi that often affect pineapples grown in wet conditions. Since it is difficult to treat, it is advisable to guard against infection by planting resistant cultivars where these are available; all [suckers](/wiki/Suckers_(botany)) that are required for propagation should be dipped in a fungicide, since the fungus enters through the wounds.

### Culinary

The flesh and juice of the pineapple are used in cuisines around the world. In many tropical countries, pineapple is prepared and sold on roadsides as a snack. It is sold whole or in halves with a stick inserted. Whole, cored slices with a [cherry](/wiki/Cherry) in the middle are a common garnish on hams in the West. Chunks of pineapple are used in desserts such as fruit salad, as well as in some savory dishes, including [Hawaiian pizza](/wiki/Hawaiian_pizza), or as a grilled ring on a [hamburger](/wiki/Hamburger). Traditional dishes that use pineapple include *[hamonado](/wiki/Hamonado)*, *[afritada](/wiki/Afritada)*, *[kaeng som pla](/wiki/Kaeng_som)*, and [Hawaiian haystack](/wiki/Hawaiian_haystack). Crushed pineapple is used in yogurt, jam, sweets, and ice cream. The juice of the pineapple is served as a beverage, and it is also the main ingredient in [cocktails](/wiki/Cocktail) such as *[piña colada](/wiki/Pi%C3%B1a_colada)* and in the drink *[tepache](/wiki/Tepache)*.

In the [Philippines](/wiki/Philippines), a traditional jelly-like dessert called *[nata de piña](/wiki/Nata_de_pi%C3%B1a)* has also been produced since the 18th century. It is made by fermenting pineapple juice with the bacteria *[Komagataeibacter xylinus](/wiki/Komagataeibacter_xylinus)*.

Pineapple vinegar is an ingredient found in both [Honduran](/wiki/Cuisine_of_Honduras) and [Filipino cuisine](/wiki/Filipino_cuisine), where it is produced locally. In [Mexico](/wiki/Mexican_cuisine), it is usually made with peels from the whole fruit, rather than the juice; however, in [Taiwanese cuisine](/wiki/Taiwanese_cuisine), it is often produced by blending pineapple juice with grain vinegar.

The [European Union](/wiki/European_Union) consumed 50% of the global total for pineapple juice in 2012–2016. The [Netherlands](/wiki/Netherlands) was the largest importer of pineapple juice in [Europe](/wiki/Europe). [Thailand](/wiki/Thailand), [Costa Rica](/wiki/Costa_Rica) and the [Netherlands](/wiki/Netherlands) are the major suppliers to the European Union market in 2012–2016. Countries consuming the most pineapple juice in 2017 were [Thailand](/wiki/Thailand), [Indonesia](/wiki/Indonesia) and the [Philippines](/wiki/Philippines), having a combined consumption of 47% of the world total. The consumption of pineapple juice in [China](/wiki/China) and [India](/wiki/India) is low compared to their populations.

### Textiles

Main article: [Piña](/wiki/Pi%C3%B1a)

The 'Red Spanish' cultivar of pineapples were once extensively cultivated in the [Philippines](/wiki/Philippines). The long leaves of the cultivar were the source of traditional *[piña](/wiki/Pi%C3%B1a)* fibers, an adaptation of the native weaving traditions with fibers extracted from *[abacá](/wiki/Abac%C3%A1)*. These were woven into lustrous lace-like *nipis* fabrics usually decorated with intricate floral embroidery known as *calado* and *sombrado*. The fabric was a luxury export from the Philippines during the Spanish colonial period and gained favor among European aristocracy in the 18th and 19th centuries. Domestically, they were used to make the traditional *[barong tagalog](/wiki/Barong_tagalog)*, *[baro't saya](/wiki/Baro%27t_saya)*, and *[traje de mestiza](/wiki/Traje_de_mestiza)* clothing of the Filipino upper class, as well as women's [kerchiefs](/wiki/Kerchief) (*pañuelo*). They were favored for their light and breezy quality, which was ideal in the hot tropical climate of the islands. The industry was destroyed in the [Second World War](/wiki/Second_World_War) and is only starting to be revived.

### Houseplant

The variety *A. comosus* 'Variegatus' is occasionally grown as a houseplant. It needs direct sunlight and thrives at temperatures of 18 to 24 °C (64 to 75 °F), with a minimum winter temperature of 16 °C (61 °F). It should be kept humid, but the soil should be allowed to dry out between waterings. It has almost no resting period but should be repotted each spring until the container reaches 20 cm (8 in).
